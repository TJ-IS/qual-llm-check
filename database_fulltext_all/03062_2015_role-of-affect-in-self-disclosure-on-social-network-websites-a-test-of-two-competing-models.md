# Role of Affect in Self-Disclosure on Social Network Websites: A Test of Two Competing Models

JONGTAE YU, PAUL JEN-HWA HU, AND TSANG-HSIANG CHENG

JONGTAE YU is a Ph.D. candidate at the David Eccles School of Business, University of Utah. His primary research interests focus on online self-disclosure, privacy, and ethics. He has conducted studies to investigate online privacy concerns across different cultures and inconsistency between privacy concerns and disclosure behavior. His work has been published in International Journal of Healthcare Information Systems and Informatics and presented in several information systems conferences, including INFORMS Conference on Information Systems and Technology.

PAUL JEN-HWA HU is David Eccles Chair Professor at the David Eccles School of Business, University of Utah. He received his Ph.D. in management information systems from the University of Arizona. His research interests include information technology in health care, electronic commerce, digital government, knowledge management, and technology-mediated learning. Hu has published in Journal of Management Information Systems, Information Systems Research, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Decision Sciences, and various IEEE and ACM journals and transactions, among other venues.

TSANG-HSIANG CHENG is a professor in the Department of Business Administration at the Southern Taiwan University of Science and Technology, Taiwan R.O.C. He holds a Ph.D. in management information systems from the National Sun Yat-sen University in Taiwan, His research interests include knowledge discovery and data mining, information retrieval and text mining, knowledge management, and medical informatics. His papers have appeared in Decision Support Systems, Artificial Intelligence in Medicine, and IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics, among others.

ABSTRACT: This study examines how affect influences self-disclosure on social network (SN) websites. We test two competing models that build on direct causation theory and affect heuristic theory, respectively. In a direct effect model, affect steers self-disclosure, independent of cognitive cost–benefit appraisals. The indirect effect model instead suggests that affect influences self-disclosure by adjusting perceptions of benefits and costs. The empirical comparison of the models relies on survey data from more than 500 university students. Overall, affect influences self-disclosure indirectly by adjusting the benefits people perceive. In particular, affect toward self-disclosure and toward SN websites relate positively to self-disclosure motivators; their perceived values appear amplified in the presence of positive affect. We also offer a plausible, alternative explanation of the observed positive relationship between privacy risk and self-disclosure according to an indirect effect model, in which self-disclosure is driven mainly by motivators, whereas the effects of inhibitors depend a posteriori on self-disclosure. These findings call for a reconsideration of any exclusive focus on the direct impacts of affect on technology use, as is common in previous research, and suggest the importance of affective factors for understanding social technology uses and managing customer relationships.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: affect, dual processing approach, online privacy, online self-disclosure, social network sites.

Social network (SN) websites, such as Facebook and Google+, are popular places for socializing, which allow people to connect with family members, catch up with friends, or meet new acquaintances, regardless of their geographic locations [82]. Using these sites grants people enjoyable feelings of affiliation, belonging, and social support; similar to face-to-face contexts, they engage in social interactions and develop interpersonal relationships by sharing personal information [76]. Through self-disclosures, users also voluntarily share personal information with others, such as updates, activities, photos, whereabouts, thoughts, opinions, feelings, experiences, and preferences [18, 64, 67]. These voluntary self-disclosures are central to cultivating and strengthening interpersonal relationships [37], especially in online settings that offer great anonymity but few physical traces or nonverbal cues [67, 82]. Social network websites create an environment that encourages people to disclose personal information early in their relationship development [76], because they can disguise their identities and escape the social norms that would govern their behaviors in faceto-face settings [97]. Thus fears of rejection decline, worries about shame dwindle, and care in guarding personal information likely wanes.

Internet users often rely on self-disclosures as means to support social interactions [82, 84], and self-disclosures on an SN website differ notably from those observed in face-to-face contexts [62]. As Bazarova and Choi point out:

Public self-disclosure on [social network websites] does not conform to the classic understanding of self-disclosure as behavior confined to a closed, mostly dyadic, interaction system. . . . Thus, public self-disclosures seem to contradict conventional notions about self-disclosure based on dyadic exchanges. This discrepancy may come from the fact that challenges of managing disclosure and audiences in social media represent a new kind of self-disclosure behavior. [10, pp. 636–637]

For example, on an SN website, a person can publicly disclose personal information to a group of others who have convenient access to the provided information, including casual acquaintances. People in a conventional environment instead typically share personal information with few intimates, face to face, without the presence of third parties [62].

In previous research that examines self-disclosure in face-to-face or online contexts [50, 67, 76], identifying its key antecedents is a common focus, often with a cognitive, rational perspective. For example, people appear to make self-disclosure decisions by comparing the benefits and costs they perceive or estimate [50, 64, 67]. Perceived benefits, such as social relationships, encourage people to share personal information; the associated costs, such as privacy risks, restrain such behaviors. However, by emphasizing cognitive, rational analyses, prior research might have overlooked the role of affect and its influence on self-disclosures, which have become increasingly important to IS research and practice [38]. In general, affect refers to the specific sense of “goodness” or “badness” a person experiences, whether consciously or unconsciously; it demarcates the positive or negative quality of a stimulus, such as a behavior, object, or event [78]. Affect also determines individual decision making and behaviors [28, 32], including the use of technology [2], websites [54], or online shopping [45].

Conceivably, affect can influence behavior either directly or indirectly, such as through its impacts on an underlying cognitive evaluation. Considering affect for self-disclosure on SN websites raises crucial but challenging questions about the relationship between affect and cognition [65, 93]. An independency view suggests that affect arises independent of cognitive appraisal and influences behavior directly [94]. Most previous information systems (IS) research adopts this view and focuses on the direct impacts of affect on technology adoption and use [11], information diffusion through social media [80], health information disclosures [4],electronic word of mouth (eWOM) in social media [26], online review helpfulness [91], or negotiations via instant messaging [46]. Dual processing offers a different view that instead emphasizes the interplay of affect and cognition and suggests that human decision processes involve both analytical (i.e., what a person thinks) and experiential (i.e., how a person feels about it) thinking [78, 85]. From this view, affect has an indirect impact, because affective and cognitive modes interact to influence behavior.

Despite the significance of these questions, the influence of affect on self-disclosure on an SN website has been investigated only sparsely [32]. In examining the role of affect for self-disclosure, we consider a central question: Does it influence self-disclosure for social interaction on an SN website directly or indirectly through its impact on underlying, cognitive (analytical) cost–benefit evaluations? People use SN websites for different purposes. For this study, we focus on self-disclosures that support social interactions to develop interpersonal relationships, which constitute a primary reason to use such sites [41, 50, 76]. Affect can be categorized on the basis of its temporal duration or the source of its stimulus; we target learned affective evaluations, or the affective tendency that a person develops from previous experiences leading to the formation of attitudes for guiding her or his behaviors [95].

We build on direct causation theory and affect heuristic theory to develop two competing models of the direct and indirect impacts of affect on self-disclosure. The former focuses on the direct affect–behavior linkage, independent of cognitive processing; the latter emphasizes the indirect impact of affect through adjustments to the person’s cognitive appraisal. Our results show that affect influences selfdisclosure indirectly by adjusting people’s perceptions of its benefits, rather than steering their behaviors directly. Both affect toward self-disclosure and affect toward SN websites are positively associated with key motivators, so the perceived value of these motivators may be amplified in the presence of positive affect. However, the relationships between affect and inhibitors in the indirect effect model appear positive or insignificant, in contrast to our predictions.

Finally, we perform additional analyses to determine whether the observed effect patterns might depend on affect intensity [56]. To enhance the robustness of our results, we also assess the observed indirect impact of affect with a reverse mediation model, in which a person’s cognitive cost–benefit assessment triggers affect, which then influences self-disclosure. In addition, we test a moderating effect model in which affect moderates the impact of cognitive appraisal on self-disclosure, rather than mediating this influence.

Our study thus extends previous research in several ways. First, we investigate the role of affect for self-disclosure on an SN website, a widespread phenomenon that has received scant research attention. We analyze cognitive and affective views and empirically compare them to explain self-disclosure on SN websites; in so doing, we augment the prevailing cognitive approach and offer a more comprehensive depiction of individual self-disclosure behaviors. Our results help to clarify why people exhibit biased assessments in their rational appraisals and also suggest a reconsideration of the common emphasis on direct (independent) impacts of affect. Second, rather than approaching affect from either a cognitive or an affective perspective, our indirect effect model integrates these distinct views and considers their interactive effects. We delineate the indirect impact with ex post analyses that offer potential explanations. For example, people may consider inhibitors (e.g., privacy risk) as necessary costs to obtain the benefits of self-disclosure, so we analyze the covariance of motivators and inhibitors to determine the likelihood of such a trade-off. We also test an alternative indirect effect model in which self-disclosure is driven mainly by motivators, whereas the impacts of the inhibitors depend a posteriori on selfdisclosure. These additional analyses confirm our findings and expand existing literature.

## Background and Literature Review

## Overview of Self-Disclosure for Social Interactions

Engaging in social interactions on an SN website is easy and convenient. When socializing this way, people forge relationships by sharing personal information to initiate and strengthen social interactions, provoking attraction [76, 84], increasing reciprocity [39, 83], and reducing uncertainty [76]. Self-disclosure can increase liking and facilitate social interaction because people tend to interact with those whom they like [35]. By voluntarily disclosing personal information on an SN website, a person can draw attraction and earn liking from others, which facilitates their social interactions [67, 76, 84]. Furthermore, self-disclosures encourage reciprocity, in that people receiving others’ personal information feel a sense of obligation to share their own personal information in return [37, 83]. People thus escalate their social interactions through reciprocal self-disclosures, because reciprocity propels continued, expanded disclosures by both the discloser and recipients, which enhances their relationships [67]. Self-disclosures also reduce uncertainty. Online social interactions are often associated with substantial uncertainty [82], but on an SN website, people can mitigate this uncertainty through (mutual) disclosures of personal information [76].

In addition to rational evaluations, affect likely influences behavior, including selfdisclosure, through the impact of affectively primed information from memory [31, 32]. For example, because people tend to interact with those they like [35, 76], positive affect provides a basis for forming a sense of fellowship, which is central to social interactions for interpersonal relationship development. The presence of positive affect then might make people more likely to expand their social interactions and disclose more personal information.

## Cognitive Processing View of Self-Disclosure for Social Interaction

Most previous self-disclosure research focuses on cognitive processing: A person’s decision to share personal information, feelings, and opinions depends on a cognitive process that involves analytical calculations of the associated benefits and costs [50]. As Omarzu [64, p. 177] explains, “individuals decide what, how, and to whom they are going to disclose and . . . this decision is based on an evaluation of the possible rewards versus the possible risks of disclosing in any specific social situation.” Acting as rational agents, people seek to gain more from a relationship as their social interactions expand over time, in terms of frequency, depth, and reciprocity [81]. Empirical support for this cognitive processing view spans faceto-face and online settings [50, 67]. Self-disclosure offers benefits such as expression, self-clarification, social validation, relationship development, and social control [20, 37], though the effects of reciprocity and liking appear to be moderated by gender and personality [18].

Perceived benefits encourage people to disclose personal information [50, 67]. A study of intentions to self-disclose location information through a location-based SN service highlights the importance of personalization and connectedness benefits [96]. When interpersonal relationship development is treated as social capital, reciprocity has an indirect impact on self-disclosure through feedback [55]. Interpersonal relationship development on SN websites also requires the identification of the respective parties, which can be achieved through identifiable presentations and personal information sharing [25]. In this sense, self-disclosure provides a means for identity presentation [10]; by constructing a profile to share personal information, feelings, photos, and updates, for example, people engage in self-expression and reveal their identity to others.

When sharing personal information for social interactions on an SN website, people also incur costs though [50, 67]. Self-disclosure is the result of an optimal balance of benefit and cost, because people explore public and private boundaries to decide whether to disclose personal information on an SN website and which information to disclose [83]. A study of the use of instant messaging cites the significant, negative impacts of privacy concerns on attitudes toward the technology and its use [58]. ## Affective Processing View of Self-Disclosure for Social Interaction

People with positive affect likely disclose more personal information and appear more optimistic in evaluating their social interactions with others [31]; they also tend to disclose more personal information to those they like but withhold such information from disliked others [76]. The voluntary disclosure of personal information is therefore more likely if recipients are attractive or similar to the discloser [67, 84]. Affect can also influence social interactions by increasing self–other overlap in interpersonal relationship development [87]. For example, positive affect prompts increased understanding of new acquaintances and thereby facilitates social interactions. It also strengthens affective bonds and experiences, providing an essential basis for trusting relationships [48].

Thus, positive affect should strengthen affective bonds, prompting people to perceive others as more likable, reliable, and trustworthy than otherwise. Although studies acknowledge the significance of affect for self-disclosure, its role in relation to self-disclosure on SN websites has not been duly investigated. ## Motivation

Most previous research examines self-disclosure on SN websites by focusing on cognitive processing, without a proper consideration of affect. People seldom behave as completely rational agents or have perfect information [77]. Rather, they tend to be subject to bounded rationality, suffer processing capacity constraints, and cope with incomplete information in their decision making and judgments [1]. The limitations of a cognitive processing view are partially underscored by the disparity between the substantial privacy concerns people express and their actual self-disclosure behaviors. For example, even when deeply worried about the potential risks of disclosing personal information online, many people willingly share personal information for small or even no tangible rewards [41]. This phenomenon, referred to as the privacy paradox [1, 49], may indicate the insufficiency of a cognitioncentric view. Although several studies examine how affect influences individual selfdisclosures online [31], they tend to focus on direct effects and have limited theoretical premises. We seek to explicate self-disclosure on SN websites by considering both cognitive processing and affect.

## Theoretical Foundation

Affect represents a primitive response to stimuli and thus provides a proxy of a disposition to behave in a particular way [8]. By asking “How do I feel about it?” people interpret the meaning of a stimulus and form a sense of how to respond [72]. If they lack a strong motivation to engage in thorough cognitive processing or have incomplete information, affect offers a quick, easy, efficient means to support their decision making and behavior [30]. Incomplete information or cognitive processing capability constraints could enhance the impact of affect on behavior [1]. According to the affect priming model [14], affect is integral to cognitive appraisals of internal and external worlds. Affect automatically primes associated memories, and this priming is highly appealing when people face a cognitive task that demands the extensive use of affectively primed information in their memory [31].

## Direct Effect of Affect on Self-Disclosure

Affect can steer behavior directly [73, 79]. Direct causation theory predicts a direct impact of affect on behavior, by attributing the outcomes to the affective state a person experiences at the time he or she undertakes the behavior [8]. This view has both dispositional and evolutionary implications [8]. First, affect offers a proxy for a person’s disposition to act in a particular way [13]. It anchors a person’s action and can exert an involuntary control on behavior, such that it determines a person’s disposition and steers behavior directly [79]. Second, affect is elemental to human nature, as a response to stimuli that activate behavior, even if the person does not engage in cognitive processing to assess the behavior [8].

According to the affect infusion model [30] and affect-trust infusion model [59], people depend particularly on affect when they lack necessary information for a thorough evaluation, possess weak motivations to search for or process additional information, have limited time to assess and compare alternatives, or need to engage in deep cognitive processing due to high task complexity. In these situations, behaviors are likely directed by experienced affect related to an object or a behavior, which derives from their learned affective evaluations [30]. People often decide whether to disclose personal information without complete information about the associated benefits and costs. For example, they have limited information about the (privacy) risk of disclosing personal information on an SN website and therefore arrive at their decisions without performing a thorough, rational analysis [1]. Furthermore, they may underestimate the risk of self-disclosure, because its benefits are specific and instantaneous, whereas its risks are potential and future [1, 63]. Underestimating these risks then may reduce their motivation to search for additional information or process it in detail. Finally, to expand their social interactions, people sense an obligation to respond quickly to others’ information sharing, comments, or feedback, which creates time pressures for prompt decision making. Such pressures could favor direct impacts of affect over detailed analytical processing [32].

In an emotional process model, a stimulus can be interpreted and decoded by internal schema, using emotional registration, which prompts and activates some particular affect and influences behavior directly [24]. In this vein, behavioral outcomes are learned and accumulated as schema for subsequent retrieval to interpret a stimulus that the person encounters later. Because affect is linked to behavioral outcomes, it offers a means to respond to a presented stimulus. Positive affect arises if the accumulated behavioral outcomes are positive and can be activated in response to a stimulus to encourage repeated behaviors. The direct impact of affect on behavior can also be intrinsic and automatic because people learn from their accumulated experiences [6].

In summary, direct causation theory and the emotional process model together suggest that affect influences human decision making and behavior directly because people store and interpret their affect and tag it with the accumulated outcomes of a behavior. When encountering a new stimulus, they behave accordingly to maximize the positive affect. That is, the direct effect view suggests that affect offers a response to a detected stimulus and represents a proxy for a disposition to act in a certain way [8], especially when people lack complete information, have weak motivations to process information, or need quick responses. Therefore, people may rely on a simplified mental model or stored internal schema to decide whether to disclose personal information on an SN website.

## Indirect Effects of Affect on Self-Disclosure Through Cognitive Appraisal

Schwarz and Clore [73] highlight the weaknesses of a direct link between affect and behavior and suggest indirect impacts. According to affect heuristic theory [28], people tag a behavior, object, or event with affect, then refer to their “affective pool” for decision making and behavior. The affect that gets activated influences the person’s cognitive evaluation of the behavior. For example, activated negative affect leads people to discount the benefits of the behavior, which discourages that behavior. Activated positive affect instead amplifies these benefits and encourages the behavior. Therefore, self-disclosure is determined by rational, cognitive evaluations, in which the (perceived) benefits and costs can be adjusted by affect tagged with self-disclosure. This indirect effect view involves rationality: A person’s behavior reflects his or her cognitive cost–benefit appraisal, people are capable of assessing the outcomes of different alternatives, and they behave to optimize their utilities [57]. The indirect effect view is congruent with dual processing [78], which suggests that affect assists and adjusts the underlying cognitive appraisal and influences behavior indirectly. In this light, affect can aid value assessments associated with a behavior, in that it adjusts the underlying cognitive, analytical assessment of the behavior [28].

These theoretical premises suggest that affect provides additional information for cognitive appraisals and can alter a person’s cost–benefit assessment, as well as the ultimate decision or behavior. The dual influence of affect on behavior consists of informational and processing effects [32]. An informational effect indicates that affect guides behavior by priming access to selective, congruent memories that serve as a heuristic source of information. Affect can enable a person’s cognitive evaluation process by providing feedback and additional (affective) information [8]. The processing effect instead indicates that affect exerts a behavioral influence by guiding the way people process or deal with information [72]. According to some experimental evidence, people with positive affect perceive greater benefits than those with negative affect [28]. Self-disclosure on an SN website can thus be understood as a cognitive decision, rooted in an analytical cost–benefit comparison, in which affect assists the assessment and influences self-disclosure indirectly.

## Models and Hypotheses

Affective concepts can be categorized according to residing, temporal, and processversus-outcome dimensions [95]. The residing dimension refers to whether the meanings of the affect exist within a person, object, or behavior. The temporal dimension classifies affective concepts on the basis of their duration. For example, temporally unconstrained affect is relatively stable and applies across similar contexts, whereas temporally constrained affect denotes a temporary value that changes with the condition. Finally, the process-versus-outcome dimension indicates how affect forms, whether from an interaction process or its outcome. Object and behavior stimuli likely induce differential affect, because affect toward an object indicates “a psychological tendency expressed by evaluating a particular entity with some degree of favor or disfavor,” whereas affect toward a behavior refers to “an individual’s positive or negative feelings (evaluative affect) about performing the target behavior” [95, p. 256]. For example, a person may like the idea of a computer (object) but feel scared to use it at work (behavior).

For this study, we focus on learned affective evaluations—a temporally unconstrained disposition, stored in memory—which form attitudes that guide behavior [95]. We differentiate learned affective evaluations of SN websites (object) from learned affective evaluations toward self-disclosure on SN websites (behavior), though both should relate closely to self-disclosure on SN websites. Affect toward an SN website should also correlate with affect toward socialization partners on the site, because people typically use such sites to connect and interact with selected friends and develop interpersonal relationships online. Consistent with key factors identified by representative prior studies, we examine several self-disclosure motivators: expression, self-presentation, social acceptance, and reciprocity. These factors are also similar to those identified by the disclosure decision model [64], but with several differences. For example, we consider self-presentation instead of social control, which primarily targets socially controlled disclosures in hierarchical relationships. On an SN website, people share personal information, activities, and feelings to enable their identity presentation [74, 84], so their self-disclosures are voluntary and nonhierarchical, with a relatively symmetric power distribution. With our focus on motives to construct favorable, positive impressions through self-disclosure, we consider self-presentation in our models, rather than social control. In addition, our models feature reciprocity, which is crucial to social interactions on SN websites and interpersonal relationship development [37, 67]. However, we do not include the identity clarification factor from the disclosure decision model, which entails presenting opinions and clarifying ideas rather than continued social interactions.

Consistent with prior research [9], we consider privacy risk and social rejection as the costs of self-disclosure. People who voluntarily share personal information on an SN website could limit these behaviors if they became deeply worried about potential privacy losses and negative outcomes [47, 67], particularly if they feel threatened by severe privacy invasions or adverse outcomes that might jeopardize their autonomy, self-efficacy, or control [66]. ## Direct Effect Model and Hypothesis

Affect can exert direct impacts on behavior [56, 73], in line with a dispositional view suggesting that affect motivates people to act in a particular way [13]. The posited direct effects also reflect an evolutionary perspective: Affect is a pervasive response to stimuli that guide behavior [8]. Accordingly, people may rely on affect for decision making, to gain an overall impression, and reach an efficient decision [28], especially if they need quick responses or lack strong motivations to seek additional information and process it thoroughly [30]. The constructive, indeterminate nature of social interactions on SN websites increases the likelihood that affect drives self-disclosure directly [32]. Activation of a positive feeling state prompts people to engage in self-disclosure to reproduce that state; activation of a negative feeling state instead causes them to avoid self-disclosure.

The direct effect model does not deny the importance of cognitive appraisals; rather, it emphasizes the independence of affect from cognition because it predicts that affect can be aroused without involving cognition. Even if both were aroused simultaneously, affect and cognitive appraisal would remain independent and not interactive. That is, affect influences behavior distinctly and can produce behavioral preferences on its own [92]. Affective judgments tend to dominate [68], but appraisal information likely demonstrates recency effects. Neurophysiological and biochemical research also provide some evidence of the independence of affect from cognition [94]. Therefore, our direct effect model in Figure 1 emphasizes the direct impact of affect and includes cognitive evaluations of key motivators and inhibitors as control variables.

## Indirect Effect Model and Hypotheses

Positive affect could cause people to overestimate the merits of a behavior, discount its demerits, or both. Affect influences a person’s cognitive evaluation of expected gains and losses by altering the underlying information processing and thus behaviors [19]. People adjust their risk assessments according to their affect at the moment they make a decision [78]. Information disclosures are negatively associated with perceived privacy risks, which are influenced by affect (e.g., joy, fear) and cognitive appraisal jointly [52]. Our indirect effect model in Figure 2 thus suggests that affect influences self-disclosure indirectly, by adjusting cognitive evaluations of the perceived benefits and costs. Activation of positive (negative) affect has positive (negative) impacts on motivators but negative (positive) effects on inhibitors, which in turn determine self-disclosure behaviors.

Accordingly, the indirect effect model suggests the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2: Motivators positively affect self-disclosure and inhibitors negatively affect self-disclosure, such that self-disclosure on SN websites is positively associated with (a) expression, (b) self-presentation, (c) social acceptance, and (d) reciprocity motivators, but negatively associated with (e) social rejection and (f) privacy risk inhibitors.

Hypothesis 3: Affect toward self-disclosure and affect toward SN websites have positive effects on motivators but negative impacts on inhibitors. Specifically affect toward self-disclosures is (a) positively associated with (i) expression, (ii) self-presentation, (iii) social acceptance, and (iv) reciprocity motivators but (b) negatively associated with (i) social rejection and (ii) privacy risk inhibitors. Similarly, affect toward SN is (c) positively associated with (i) expression, (ii) self-presentation, (iii) social acceptance, and (iv) reciprocity motivators but (d) negatively associated with (i) social rejection and (ii) privacy risk inhibitors.

## Study Design

To test and compare the competing models, we collected survey data from more than 500 university students. We administered the survey at the beginning or end of regular class meetings. Before each survey, the administrator used a script to explain our objective and intended data analyses, and then explicitly addressed any questions or privacy concerns the participants might have.

## Participants

We targeted business students enrolled at a major university in southern Taiwan. Our participant selection criteria included previous experiences with SN websites (e.g., Facebook, Google+, Plurk) and current, active use of a primary SN website at the time of our study. Several faculty members teaching business classes at this university assisted with the recruitment. Participation was completely voluntary and had no effect on class performance or grades. We provided a small gift to each participant in return for his or her time and effort.

## Measurements

We measured the constructs with question items adapted from previously validated scales, with minor wording changes to fit our context. We explicitly distinguished “affect toward SN websites” and “affect toward self-disclosures” and measured each with items from Diener and Emmons [21] and Westbrook [88], respectively. We measured self-presentation with items from Walther et al. [86] and Bolino and Turnley [12]. The expression items were adapted from Ross et al. [71]; items for social acceptance were from Martin [61]; and the reciprocity items were from Posey et al. [67]. We followed the approach of Wright et al. [89] to operationalize social rejection and measured privacy risk with items from Posey et al. [67]. Self-disclosure, our dependent variable, was measured with question items adapted from Posey et al. [67].<sup>2</sup> All measurement items employed five-point Likert scales (1 = “strongly disagree” and 5 = “strongly agree”). In Appendix A, we provide each construct’s definition and measurement items, together with their sources.

We also collected personal information from participants, including Internet selfefficacy, gender, age, years at university, average time spent on SN websites, and number of friends on the primary SN website, all of which served as control variables in our subsequent data analyses. Internet self-efficacy refers to a person’s belief in his or her ability to perform Internet-based tasks and activities [23] and has been shown to influence Internet usage behaviors [42]. We measured it with items from Eastin and LaRose [23]. We controlled for gender, because several studies report different online behaviors between male and female users [41]. For example, female users seem to perceive more online privacy threats and express greater concerns than their male counterparts [41]. The number of friends a person has on his or her primary SN website helped us control for potential effects of social capital [25]. Finally, we controlled for the effects of age, years at university, and average time spent on SN websites.

## Questionnaire Survey

We conducted the survey in Chinese, the official language of Taiwan. Using a translation and back-translation method [15], we had a professional translator translate all the English-language question items into Chinese. To ensure consistent semantics, three experienced researchers, fluent in both English and Chinese and not involved in this study, reviewed the translated items individually. Their reviews indicated satisfactory and consistent semantics in the translation. The question items in Chinese were then translated back to English by another professional translator and reviewed by the same three researchers, who again indicated satisfactory semantic preservation and consistency. Our survey consisted of the final items in Chinese, a description of our study’s objective, and an explicit definition of self-disclosure on SN websites.

## Pretest

We conducted a pretest of the question items to ensure their clarity and validity. Forty-eight participants randomly drawn from the targeted student pool voluntarily took part in the pretest by answering all the question items in the survey. Analyses of the pretest data showed that the question items exhibited adequate reliability, as well as convergent and discriminant validity. Their feedback affirmed the overall clarity of the question items and indicated some minor wording issues that needed refinement. The pretest also revealed how much time participants took to complete the survey. We excluded the pretest participants from our subsequent study.

## Data and Results

Of the 1,132 students asked to participate in our study, 800 agreed to do so; among them, 242 completed the survey only partially and were removed from our sample. The vast majority of the participants (i.e., 95 percent) used Facebook; to control for potential platform effects, we excluded the 41 participants who did not use Facebook as their primary SN website. As a result, our sample consisted of 517 participants, representing a 64.6 percent effective response rate. On average, participants took 15.5 minutes to complete the survey, similar to the time used by the pretest participants. In Table 3, we report some descriptive statistics of the participants.

To examine potential nonrespondent bias, we compared the participants with the overall student pool we targeted [34]. We found no significant between-group differences in age, gender composition, or the number of years at the university. We also assessed nonrespondent bias by comparing early respondents (i.e., first 25 percent of completions) with late respondents (i.e., last 25 percent); again, we observed no significant between-group differences in age, gender composition, number of years at the university, or responses to various question items. These results suggested that nonrespondent bias was not a serious threat.

## Model Comparison Results

We compared the models at both model and path levels. The model-level comparison emphasized the fit indices closely related to confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), whereas our path-level comparisons focused on the coefficients and statistical significance of the individual paths from affect toward self-disclosure or affect toward SN websites. At the model level, we observed similar R-square values in the direct and indirect effect models (.37 and .34, respectively), suggesting comparable explanatory power for self-disclosure on SN websites. We then constructed the CFA models and used the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) and root mean square error approximation (RMSEA), rather than other fit indices, because of their relative insensitivity to sample size and model complexity [43]. A model’s SRMR and RMSEA should generally be lower than .08 [43]. Our results showed that the indirect effect model fit the data adequately, with a .063 SRMR value and .06 RMSEA value. The direct effect model did not fit well, with an SRMR value of .211.

## Hypothesis Test Results

We tested our hypotheses by examining the respective models, using partial least squares. As we show in Figure 3 and Table 7, neither affect toward self-disclosure nor affect toward SN websites had statistically significant impacts on self-disclosure; thus, our data did not support H1.

The results in Figure 4 and Table 7 show that self-presentation, social acceptance, and reciprocity were positively and significantly associated with self-disclosure, in support of H2b–d. The impact of expression on self-disclosure was statistically insignificant, which did not support H2a. The data also did not support associations between the inhibitors and self-disclosure. Social rejection had a negative and insignificant relationship with self-disclosure, and privacy risk exhibited a positive, significant association, which contrasted with H2e and H2f. Affect toward selfdisclosure and affect toward SN websites exhibited positive associations with all the investigated motivators, in support of H3a(i-iv) and H3c(i–iv). Affect toward self-disclosure also had a significant, positive impact on social rejection and privacy risk, which contrasted with our predictions in H3b(i-ii). Affect toward SN websites had a significant, positive association with privacy risk, whereas its effect on social rejection was not statistically significant. Thus, our data did not support H3d(i-ii).

## Further Ex Post Analyses

To explore other possible explanations for our findings, we performed ex post analyses to examine the positive relationship between privacy risk and self-disclosure. In this context, a risk–return trade-off may be relevant, in that people take more risks to gain more returns [27]. Thus a positive correlation arises between benefits and costs, because people realize that they must incur the risk of sharing personal information to enjoy the benefits of self-disclosure in social interactions. The results of a covariance analysis of the motivators and inhibitors, which we summarize in Table 11, indicate that social rejection positively and significantly covaried with selfpresentation and social acceptance, which suggested that the participants had ambivalent expectations of self-presentation and social acceptance. Participants seemed to believe that increasing self-disclosures could help them establish positive images for social acceptance, but doing so could also create discomfort for others and thus lead to social rejection. Privacy risk was positively and significantly associated with reciprocity, suggesting that people might become more aware of privacy risk as their social interactions expand and require more (mutual) selfdisclosures. Finally, social rejection was negatively associated with reciprocity, implying that people expected greater acceptance when their social interactions expanded through increased self-disclosures. Overall, the covariance analyses yielded evidence that people must take the risk of being rejected when they seek self-presentation and social acceptance through self-disclosure. The reciprocity of self-disclosure appears to necessitate privacy risks; however, people do not regard social rejection as a cost of gaining reciprocity. In this risk–return trade-off, people understand that they must take risks to gain benefits, and they perceive privacy risk as a necessary cost of self-disclosure.

## Discussion

Our results have several implications for research. First, affect influences selfdisclosure indirectly through adjustments to people’s cognitive processing. We observe positive relationships between affect and self-disclosure motivators, suggesting that people with positive affect tend to amplify or overestimate the benefits of self-disclosure. This causal chain, from affect to cognitive appraisal adjustment to behavior, is in line with affect heuristic theory. The activation of positive affect determines a person’s value assessment of motivators but perhaps not inhibitors; people appear to recognize the benefits of self-disclosure for social interactions on an SN website when they have positive affect and thus are more likely to disclose personal information than those with negative affect. Our findings call for a reconsideration of the direct impacts of affect on technology use or online behaviors. Most previous information systems research focuses on such direct influences, independent of cognition [11, 26, 91], but our results reveal that affect may influence people’s cognitive assessments of benefits and costs, which in turn influences their adoption of a technology, a recommended product or service, or an online review. For example, positive affect toward a particular system could encourage individual adoption of that system, by leading people to overestimate its merits, such as perceived usefulness or ease of use. Accordingly, our findings reveal the need to investigate this potential “biased effect” of affect on people’s cognitive appraisals of the benefits and costs associated with technology use.

Second, the results of the indirect effect model reveal marginal or even positive associations between affect and self-disclosure inhibitors. For example, the positive relationship of affect toward self-disclosure with privacy risk or social rejection appears counterintuitive, as does the link of affect toward SN websites and privacy risk. Yet affective ambivalence offers a plausible explanation. A common, implicit assumption is that people express unidimensional affective states, such that any activated affect occupies a particular point on a bipolar negative–positive continuum. In actuality, people seldom exhibit unidimensional affective responses to a stimulus [29] and instead have mixed affective states. Opposite affect can coexist simultaneously [51], such as when people feel both happy and sad when they graduate from college or watch a sad but well-acted movie. Many behaviors, objects, and events involve both positive and negative aspects, so when they activate affect to assess these stimuli, people likely access multiple affect states. However, an exclusive linkage might arise between negative affect and negative consequences [7]. Thus a person’s subjective well-being, which is a positive consequence, can be predicted by both positive and negative affect, but distress, a negative consequence, can be predicted only by negative affect. If people sense ambivalent affect toward selfdisclosure or SN websites, both positive and negative affect might influence their assessments of motivators, whereas their inhibitor assessments would be predicted by negative affect alone. The positive relationships we have observed between affect and inhibitors provide some suggestive evidence of the simultaneous coexistence of positive and negative affect toward an object or behavior. People with positive affect toward an SN website, due to its search capabilities and ease of use, may expect similar functionalities in other aspects of the site, such as multimedia streaming; they would be disappointed if their expectations were not met and thus could develop ambivalent affect toward the site. These different affects logically could be activated by any particular stimuli.

Third, the observed relationship between privacy risk and self-disclosure contrasts with our hypothesis. The risk–return trade-off provides a plausible explanation, supported by our ex post analysis, which revealed the significant, positive covariance of motivators and inhibitors. People seemingly consider risks as the costs necessary to enjoy the benefits of self-disclosure. The results of our alternative, indirect effect model provide another plausible explanation: Because of their preference for the present over the future, people tend to underestimate the risk of sharing personal information with others on an SN website and instead make their self-disclosure decisions mostly on the basis of the value they perceive in the motivators, rather than privacy risk considerations. To some extent, our results affirm this time-inconsistent discounting of risk, such that benefits seem instantaneous and definitive, whereas the risks are distant and potential [1, 63]. We recommend a careful reconsideration of the significance of motivators and inhibitors. People appear to consider inhibitors, such as privacy risk, relatively less important and attend to them only when self-disclosure escalates.

Fourth, our model comparison results suggest that self-disclosure involving cognitive processing requires prompt responses. In the indirect effect model, people are aware of the benefits and costs of self-disclosure and consciously make self-disclosure decisions according to their cognitive appraisal, as adjusted by affect. Selfdisclosure demands careful management of decision making because it can lead to rejection or loss of control [32]. Our findings suggest that self-disclosure on SN websites constitutes an intended behavior, driven by affect-adjusted cognitive processing rather than automatic responses to stimuli. Our additional investigation of the observed effect patterns in three alternative models reinforces the observed role of affect. That is, we propose that the impact of affect on self-disclosure is independent of affect intensity [cf. 56]. In general, affect has insignificant effects in the relationship between motivators (inhibitors) and self-disclosure, and it does not moderate the impacts of motivators (inhibitors) on self-disclosure.

Our findings also have implications for practice. For example, the indirect impact of affect through benefit assessments highlights the importance of creating easily recognized, conveniently evaluated benefits. Thus SN website providers need to adopt affectively appealing features and employ interaction modes to facilitate users’ cognitive processing. We observe different, simultaneous types of affect and their impacts on cognitive processing, so it appears that people may have a distinct affect toward an object and a related behavior. For example, when deciding whether to purchase a product from an online vendor, a customer’s value assessment may depend on affect toward the product and toward the vendor. Firms need to identify the types of affect critical to customers’ cognitive appraisals and effectively manage them for improved conversion. These distinct types of affect vary in their sources and outcomes; affect toward the product likely stems mostly from product features, whereas affect toward a vendor may result from prior customer–firm interactions. Overall, our findings suggest the importance of considering affective factors for social technologies and managing customer relationships. For example, firms should enhance their affective image by providing affectively appealing services in their marketing activities and ads. As SN websites become increasingly critical channels to connect and interact with customers, firms should strive to improve their customer relationships by designing effective interactions on salient SN websites [70]. Similarly, SN websites need to emphasize the affective component of their platforms, such as design features and images, to induce positive affect toward the websites. In turn, the affective component of design could influence individuals assessments and uses of the website. Our results indicate the importance of selfpresentation, social acceptance, and reciprocity as essential motives for people to socialize on an SN website. Accordingly, SN websites need to provide functionalities (e.g., attractive avatars) so that people can express themselves in various ways and interact with others more easily.

## Conclusion

This study examines the impact of affect on self-disclosure on SN websites. Using direct causation theory and heuristic affect theory as anchoring lenses, we develop two competing models that delineate the direct versus indirect impacts of affect on self-disclosures. Overall, our data support the indirect effect model; affect toward self-disclosure and affect toward SN websites are positively associated with motivators of self-disclosure, but their impacts on inhibitors seem insignificant or positive. That is, affect guides self-disclosure by adjusting cognitive appraisals of motivators, instead of steering the behavior directly.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the role of affect for self-disclosure on SN websites. Affect has an essential role in human decisions and behavior; this study advances an understanding of how it influences people’s selfdisclosures on SN websites. Unlike previous research that follows either a cognitive or an affective approach, we test two competing models to compare alternative views on how affect influences self-disclosure, which produces a more comprehensive depiction of voluntary self-disclosure of personal information on SN websites. In addition, our study contributes to extant literature by revealing how affect influences cognitive appraisals of self-disclosure and testing alternative explanations of why people exhibit biased assessments in their cost–benefit assessments. Noting the positive association of privacy risk and self-disclosure, we specify that people appear to consider risks the necessary cost of enjoying the benefits of self-disclosure, though they also engage in time-inconsistent discounting of risks. Overall, our findings emphasize the need to challenge the conventional wisdom that people decide whether to disclose through a logical comparison of the benefits and costs of self-disclosure.

This study also has several limitations that suggest research directions. First, we consider positive affect and examine its impacts on self-disclosure; negative affect might also influence behavior, perhaps in a different manner [56]. Simultaneous considerations of the influences of positive and negative affect could reveal a more holistic role of affect for self-disclosure on SN websites. Second, we focus on immediate, rather than expected, affect, because people can seldom develop accurate probability and value estimates of (future) alternatives or outcomes. If they could though, expected affect might directly determine behavior, a prediction that further studies could test. Along similar lines, we focus on affect toward self-disclosure and affect toward SN website, but further research should include other types of affect (e.g., playfulness, fun), affect toward users of the SN website, or affect toward a specific activity or event on the SN website (e.g., professional networking). Third, we used bipolar scales, as is common in previous research, but they might suffer from systematic response biases, and their reliability cannot be readily assessed. Multidimensional scales may measure affective states more accurately; we call for continued research to examine the ambivalence of affect toward motivators and inhibitors using multidimensional scales. Fourth, our study is limited with regard to the participants and SN websites. We surveyed business students at a Taiwanese university and took the results only from Facebook users. Thus, our findings might not generalize to other user groups or cultures, and we call for research that targets different users and addresses cultural issues that might influence self-disclosure behaviors [58]. Similarly, though all SN websites, including Facebook, seek to facilitate connection, interaction, and socialization, they differ in their intent, such as general socialization, professional networking for career development, or knowledge sharing. Continued research should consider other SN websites. We examined self-disclosure motivators and inhibitors that we identified from previous research; additional research might consider alternative factors that could foster or hinder selfdisclosure. Finally, we did not consider the amount, depth, or duration of selfdisclosure [64]. Such elements need to be investigated to clarify the impacts of affect even further.

## Appendix A. Question Items

Affect toward SN sites: Positive feeling state with respect to SN websites [78]; items from Diener and Emmons [21] and Westbrook [88].

1. Social network sites give me good feelings.

2. I love to go to social network sites.

3. I enjoy engaging in social networking activities, such as sharing photos, meeting new friends, and chatting with others.

4. Social network sites are fun.

5. I have good experiences with social network sites.

Affect toward self-disclosure: Positive feeling state with respect to self-disclosures [78]; items from Diener and Emmons [21] and Westbrook [88].

1. Disclosing my personal feelings and information online gives me a good feeling.

2. I have positive experiences in disclosing personal feelings and information online.

3. It is fun to share my own ideas and opinions online.

4. I am afraid of sharing my personal things online with others.

5. Sharing my intimate, personal things and feelings online makes me feel joyful.

Self-presentation: Actions or process taken by people who attempt to control selfrelevant images seen by real or imagined others [22]; items from Walther et al. [86] and Bolino and Turnley [12].

1. Disclosing my personal feelings and information online creates a better impression for others.

2. By disclosing my personal information online, I can make people aware of my experiences and qualifications.

3. Fully revealing myself online makes people aware of my accomplishments.

4. Disclosing my intimate, personal things online makes people perceive me as friendly.

5. Talking about myself online makes people see me as dedicated to the relationships.

Reciprocity: Mutually contingent exchange of personal information [3]; items from Posey et al. [67].

1. When I talk about my idea or opinion online, I expect similar responses from others.

2. When others talk about themselves online, I believe that they expect me to do the same.

3. Other online users trust me to return the favor of sharing their personal information.

4. People disclose information about themselves; therefore, it is only fair if I do the same.

Expression: A person’s getting relief of psychological distress by disclosing unplea sant feelings or negative emotions that people normally wish to avoid [71].

1. By expressing my personal feelings online, I feel comfortable in times of trouble or frustration.

2. When I feel tired, I could escape the distress by talking about my feelings or situation online.

3. When I am sad, I feel consoled by expressing my feelings online.

4. When I feel lonely, I could get a sense of companionship by sharing my feelings with others online.

5. When I am worried about things, I could get relief by sharing my situation online.

6. When I feel tense and anxious, I could get relief by telling people my feelings online.

Social acceptance: Acceptance or recognition by a peer or social group through engagement in social activities [53]; items from Martin [61].

1. When I disclose my personal information to others online, the content that I disclose often is different, dependent upon their personality, social position, or closeness with me.

2. I believe disclosing personal information and feelings online is useful to get along with others and be liked.

3. I rarely share my personal information and feelings online because I think others would feel uncomfortable about it.

4. I share my personal, intimate information or feelings online to make a good impression on people who express a poor opinion of me.

5. Before disclosing my personal things online, I normally consider disclosure to be “proper” in social situations.

Social rejection: Rejection by a peer or social group [53]; items from Wright et al. [89].

1. I believe that people could treat me unfavorably if I disclose personal information online.

2. I think that people may avoid me if I disclose personal things online.

3. I am worried that people could take advantage of what I tell them about myself online for fun or profits.

4. I believe that people may feel uncomfortable if I disclose personal feelings online.

Privacy risk: The expectation that a high potential for loss of privacy is associated with the release of personal information to others in their electronic communities [60]; items from Posey et al. [67].

1. In general, it is risky to give my private information to others online.

2. There is a high potential for loss when I share my personal information online.

3. Providing others with my private information online could create unexpected problems.

4. I feel safe when sharing my personal information and feelings with others online.

## Self-disclosure: Disclosing information to others, including thoughts, opinions, feelings, or experiences [67].

1. I talk about myself online frequently.

2. I often disclose intimate, personal things online without hesitation.

3. Once I get started, I fully reveal myself online.

4. My online self-disclosures usually are accurate reflections of who I really am.

5. When I talk about my feelings or experiences online, I present them honestly.

6. When I express my personal feelings online, I am always aware what I am doing and what I am saying.

7. When I am self-disclosing online, I am consciously aware of what I am revealing.

8. I often disclose positive things about myself online, but seldom about negative things.

9. I normally express my “good” feelings about myself online.

Internet efficacy: A person’s self-judgment of his or her ability to use the Internet to produce overall attainments [23].

I feel confident

1. understanding terms (words) related to the hardware of the Internet.

2. understanding terms (words) related to the software of the Internet.

3. trouble-shooting problems I have when using the Internet.

4. using the Internet to gather data.

5. learning skills from Internet-based programs.

6. turning to an online discussion group (forum) when I need help.
