# Identifying and Testing the Inhibitors of Technology Usage Intentions

Ronald T. Cenfetelli

Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia, Vancouver, British Columbia V6T 1Z2, Canada, cenfetelli@sauder.ubc.ca

Andrew Schwarz

E. J. Ourso College of Business, Louisiana State University, Baton Rouge, Louisiana 70803, aschwarz@lsu.edu

n important area of information systems (IS) research has been the identification of the individual-level beliefs that enable technology acceptance such as the usefulness, reliability, and flexibility of a system. This study posits the existence of additional beliefs that inhibit usage intentions and thus foster technology rejection rather than acceptance. We theorize that these inhibitors are more than just the antipoles of enablers (e.g., the opposite of usefulness or reliability) and so are distinct constructs worthy of their own investigation. Inhibitors are proposed to have effects on usage intentions beyond that of enablers as well as effects on enablers themselves. We report on a series of empirical studies designed to test the existence and effects of inhibitors. A candidate set of six inhibitors is shown to be distinct from enablers. These inhibitors are subsequently tested in a field study of 387 individuals nested within 32 different websites. Effects at both individual and website unit levels of analysis are tested using multilevel modeling. We find that inhibitors have negative effects on usage intentions, as well as on enablers, and these effects vary contingent upon individual or website unit levels of analysis. The overall results support the existence and importance of inhibitors in explaining individual intent to use—or not use—technology.

Key words: usage intentions; inhibitors; nonacceptance; technology rejection

## Introduction

Information technology (IT) has earned an “infamous” reputation for being dysfunctional and prone to failure (Keil et al. 1998, Nelson 2007). Such failures are often the result of individuals choosing not to adopt systems (Lyytinen 1988), despite significant investments of time and money spent in developing those systems. One case in point: only about half of the intended users of enterprise resource planning systems actually use them (Wailgum 2009). Rejection of IT is also commonly seen online, where many websites seem to purposely drive away their users (Flanders 2009). A recent study of retail websites found that shoppers abandoned a site as much for reasons of poor website design and usability, as for price (iPerceptions 2009).

Understanding why users reject technology is important so as to avoid its occurrence. Achieving this understanding requires the identification of those factors that inhibit technology acceptance, and we refer to these factors as inhibitors. Although a large body of information systems (IS) research has helped us to understand the factors that enable adoption, there has been relatively little research into adoption inhibitors.

Dominant IS theories such as the technology acceptance model (TAM) (Davis 1989, Davis et al. 1989), the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) (Venkatesh et al. 2003), and user satisfaction (DeLone and McLean 1992, 2003) focus exclusively on adoption enablers, such as the usefulness, reliability, and flexibility of a system. These are vital theories that have helped us to understand why users adopt and the factors that enable adoption. However, enablers and inhibitors may not simply be opposites on the same continuum. As we will demonstrate in this study, the distinction between the factors that enable usage and those that inhibit usage is important and necessary. A frequently pursued research question in the IS literature has been “Why do individuals choose to use technology?” If we instead asked “Why do individuals choose not to use technology?,” we may discover factors that are fundamentally different from simply the opposite or lack of factors that enable usage. For example, Venkatesh and Brown (2001) asked that very question of those households that chose not to purchase a PC. The answers to the “why not” question were often different from those given to the “why” question. As a result, a more general focus on inhibitors could identify previously overlooked antecedents to IT failure, nonadoption of technology, and/or website abandonment.

The purpose of our paper is to investigate the inhibitors of technology usage intentions. More specifically, we seek to address the following research questions: Why are inhibitors distinct from enablers? What are the specific instantiations of inhibitors? What are the effects of inhibitors? To answer these questions, we review the pertinent literature and propose a research model. This is followed by a description of a series of empirical studies designed to (a) explore and identify specific instantiations of inhibitors that are distinct from those of enablers and (b) test our research model. The paper concludes with a discussion of the results, implications for research and practice, and recommendations for future research.

## Prior Research

Increasing attention has been focused on inhibitors and their role in technology acceptance. There have been a number of investigations of negatively oriented antecedents to acceptance or to its analogues (e.g., website purchase intention). Such antecedents include website download delays (Galletta et al. 2004, Rose and Straub 2001), dissatisfiers (Zhang and vonDran 2000), website presentation flaws (Everard and Galletta 2006), anxiety (Brown et al. 2004), resource barriers (Mathieson et al. 2001), loss of control (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet 2008), lack of knowledge (Chau 2001, Venkatesh and Brown 2001), lack of IT support (Chau 2001), distrust (McKnight et al. 2003), risk (Grazioli and Jarvenpaa 2000, McKnight et al. 2002, Pavlou 2003), and job overload (Ahuja and Thatcher 2005, Thatcher et al. 2003). User resistance has also been studied for its role in inhibiting technology adoption (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005, Lapointe and Rivard 2005) and has been explicitly identified as a usage inhibitor (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet 2007, 2008).

Regardless of whether these studies have explicitly identified any of these factors as an inhibitor, they still clearly demonstrate the value in investigating factors that inhibit usage, not just those that enable usage. For example, Galletta et al. (2004) note that download delays act as a hindrance to Web usage. In studying dissatisfiers, Zhang and vonDran (2000) were motivated by the poor interface functionality found on many websites that resulted in user abandonment. Even more pertinent data about motivating an explicit focus on inhibitors come from Everard and Galletta (2006). They experimentally manipulated websites to be with or without flaws. While nearly all subjects correctly observed flawed websites as they occurred, flaw-free sites were frequently perceived as flawed even when no objective flaw was present.

Several other studies that have investigated specific individual usage inhibitors have found each to be distinct from those of an enabler or have found each to have a distinct effect. Parthasarathy and Bhattacherjee (1998) compared continuing adopters with discontinuers in the context of online services. They found that interpersonal influence, such as negative word of mouth, was asymmetrically more predictive of discontinuance than continuance. We previously referred to the PC adoption study by Venkatesh and Brown (2001): they found that the decision of a household not to purchase a PC was influenced by barriers of rapid change, high cost, and lack of knowledge. These barriers contributed to rejection, but their absence did not enable adoption. Carroll et al. (2002) also found that cost played a strictly negative role in mobile phone appropriation by being a repellent if cost were high but not an attractor if cost were low. These studies support the observation that inhibitors can exist independently from enablers, as well as behave differently than enablers in influencing adoption or rejection.

An investigation of inhibitors may be especially important in light of what research in other fields conveys about the greater impact of the negative over the positive. Prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky 1979) holds that individuals are more sensitive to losses than gains. For example, the loss of \$100 is felt more strongly than the gain of \$100. In other words, there is an asymmetry between negative and positive attributes despite their having a similar magnitude. Negative attributes are not only more memorable than the positive but also individuals tend to more quickly assess and perceive negative attributes as compared to positive ones. As a result of these biases, negative attributes overshadow positive attributes when a target object with those attributes is evaluated (Skowronski and Carlston 1987). Relatedly, research shows that negative word of mouth can be more persuasive than positive word of mouth (Parthasarathy and Bhattacherjee 1998); learning is more effective through punishment than reward (Greitemeyer and Kazemi 2008); individuals respond more quickly to negative versus positive stimuli (Gauggel et al. 2000); and negative information is processed more thoroughly and given more cognitive attention than the positive. Indeed, it is often the case that “bad is stronger than good” (Baumeister et al. 2001).

This stronger influence of negative over positive serves an adaptive purpose because negative events, attributes, or stimuli often signal that something is awry in the environment that needs attention and a response (Baumeister et al. 2001). In contrast, positive events, attributes, or stimuli signal a benign environment that requires no action. An ability to attend to, perceive, and respond to threats is critical to survival.

This begets a need for rapid, intense, and attentive responses to the negative that is not required for the positive. Systemic failure can often be ensured in the event that any individual component fails. For example, if a hard drive, processor, memory, or software fails, the computer as a whole fails. Yet, ensuring the success of any individual component cannot ensure systemic success.

There are thus grounds for distinguishing between the bad versus the good, both as independent concepts as well as having distinct effects. With this in mind, two inferences can be made with respect to technology adoption. First, given the greater influence of bad versus good, an individuals’ willingness to adopt a technology may depend as much or more on the presence of inhibitors as the presence of enablers. Second, given the greater and faster attention given to negative over positive attributes, individuals may process and note inhibitors more readily, and inhibitors may drown out the enablers, biasing one’s overall perceptions about a technology and his or her intentions of using it more toward the inhibitors, not the enablers.

Taken together, the implication of these potential differential effects of inhibitors and enablers is that IS research needs to consider both so as not to overlook the role and importance of some key unique factors influencing technology acceptance. Moreover, it suggests that our field’s prescriptions for practice are also incomplete. For example, managers need to be aware not only of good design but also of avoiding bad design, because that bad design may jeopardize the evaluations that individuals make of otherwise well-designed technology features. We next propose a model for the effects of inhibitors and justify why they are distinct from enablers.

## Research Model

Our research model (Figure 1) depicts the simultaneous and independent role of both enablers and inhibitors in predicting usage intentions. We discuss each component of this model below.

The Enablers of Technology Usage Intentions. We first identify and describe the effects of enablers on usage intentions as a basis for our eventual focus on inhibitors. The specific enablers we focus on, to contrast with inhibitors, are those beliefs identified in the seminal work of DeLone and McLean (1992, 2003). Their user satisfaction model identifies beliefs reflecting system quality, such as reliability, flexibility, and responsiveness, as well as beliefs reflecting information quality, such as accuracy, currency, and completeness. A focus on these beliefs is ideal because they include some of the most widely studied antecedents to use and usage intentions.

System quality is a user’s evaluation of the technical capabilities of the system and its usability. Information quality is a user’s evaluation of the system’s conveyance of semantic meaning and/or communication of knowledge. Both represent a user’s objectbased beliefs, rather than his or her behavioral beliefs. Wixom and Todd (2005) clarify an important distinction between object-based and behavioral beliefs in their integration of the TAM and user satisfaction models. Object-based beliefs correspond to the system itself and its attributes, whereas behavioral beliefs correspond to the action and consequences of usage behavior. The system and information beliefs identified in the user satisfaction model provide a widely used, relevant, and representative set of the domain of object-based beliefs (Wixom and Todd 2005). Usefulness and ease of use are similarly representative of the domain of behavioral beliefs. We chose to limit our focus in this study to object-based enablers (and inhibitors), because they allow for the identification of the IT artifact and design leverage points that are relevant to academics and practitioners alike (Benbasat and Barki 2007, Benbasat and Zmud 2003, Wixom and Todd 2005). Object-based beliefs describe critical attributes of the IT artifact itself—such as the responsiveness of the system or the currency of information provided (Wixom and Todd 2005). As a result, object-based beliefs provide specific prescriptive guidance for better IT design. In our pursuit of inhibitors, the identified attributes would then be prescriptive of design features to avoid.<sup>1</sup>

Several hundred studies have supported the influence of information and system quality on use and usage intentions (see DeLone and McClean 2003 for a review). The role of quality in influencing behavioral intentions is also widely supported in the marketing context, where quality has been shown to be highly predictive of a customer’s intentions to purchase— an analogous behavioral outcome to usage (Zeithaml et al. 1996, 2002). Information and system quality identify a set of salient attributes associated with the technology the user interacts with and evaluates. A user forms beliefs about these attributes, and these beliefs represent the user’s evaluation of the subjective probability that the object has those given attributes (Ajzen and Fishbein 2000). More positively oriented evaluations increase the likelihood that the user will intend to make use of the technology toward achieving a desired outcome. A user’s assessment of information quality acts to guide a user’s intentions by signaling that the target technology will provide valid information. Similarly, system quality signals that the target technology will perform properly with adequate functionality. Both information and system quality describe attributes that will contribute to outcomes that mesh with the user’s objectives (Agarwal and Venkatesh 2002, Nicolaou and McKnight 2006). Users who perceive that a system is of high quality will tend to assume that the system has positive attributes and these attributes are a signal that the system is worth using (McKnight et al. 2002). Simply put, technologies that are reliable and flexible and provide accurate and complete information are more likely to be used. In line with this reasoning, and consistent with prior studies, we hypothesize that system quality and information quality will have a positive impact on usage intentions and, thus, act as enablers.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> Information quality (enabler beliefs) will have a significant positive influence on usage intentions.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> System quality (enabler beliefs) will have a significant positive influence on usage intentions.

The Inhibitors of Technology Usage Intentions. We now address our research questions regarding why inhibitors are distinct from enablers, as well as the effects of inhibitors. We first propose that inhibitors and enablers are dual-factored constructs. Dual-factored constructs are related in nature but opposite in valence. Dual-factored constructs exist as two independent unipolar continua rather than a single bipolar continuum with a unitary midpoint. Another distinguishing feature of dual-factored con structs is that either construct in the pairing may have different, but not necessarily opposite, and potentially asymmetric effects on the same outcome. Although rare in the IS literature, dual-factored constructs have frequently been studied in other areas of research. One of the earliest applications of dual-factored constructs was in the investigation of factors that led to employee job satisfaction or dissatisfaction (Herzberg et al. 1966). In the decades since this research, dualfactored constructs have been applied to numerous other contexts, such as positive versus-negative emotions (Diener et al. 1995), consumer purchase satisfaction (Swan and Combs 1976), customer service employee performance (Johnston 1995, Sirdeshmukh et al. 2002), website satisfaction (Zhang and vonDran 2000), and interpersonal trust versus distrust (Lewicki et al. 1998). Trust and distrust provide a useful illustration. Lewicki et al. (1998) argue that distrust and trust are not mutually exclusive opposites but are instead dual-factors. Trust and distrust have distinct expectations: trust is the confident positive expectations of another’s “virtuous intentions,” whereas distrust is a fear of another’s “sinister intentions” (Lewicki et al. 1998, p. 439). Furthermore, being distinct means that trust and distrust can coexist between two parties (e.g., “trust but verify”). Finally, trust and distrust can have differing effects. Distrust has been shown to predict the likelihood of high-risk online behavior, whereas trust does not (McKnight et al. 2004).

Considering enablers and inhibitors as dualfactored constructs has three implications. One, inhibitors are not the opposite of enablers and can exist independently of enablers (Cenfetelli 2004). This independence recognizes that evaluations of a system are not monolithically good or bad but diverse combinations of positive and negative attributes. This is particularly the case for typically complex systems. A second related implication is that an individual can hold perceptions of both inhibitors and enablers simultaneously. Many objects or behaviors can be simultaneously evaluated both favorably and unfavorably, such as smoking (Petty and Cacioppo 1996). Users can similarly make both positive and negative evaluations of a target technology. For example, the process of interacting with a system may be frustrating, but the system’s output allows the person to be more effective in their job. The third implication is that inhibitors and enablers can have independent effects on intentions. Inhibitors can act to hinder intentions despite the presence of enablers facilitating those same intentions.

Additional support for the distinction of inhibitors from enablers derives from greater negative differentiation, which posits a greater number of negative, as compared to positive, psychological phenomena (Rozin and Royzman 2001). For example, there are a larger number of negative than positive words and emotions. Greater negative differentiation implies that there exist negative phenomena with no correspondingly positive antipole. Examples include trauma, risk, accident, and catastrophe (Baumeister et al. 2001, Rozin and Royzman 2001).<sup>2</sup> Similarly, there are likely inhibitors of technology usage intentions for which there is no corresponding enabler antipole. This leads to a more precise definition of an inhibitor as a belief held by an individual that acts to impede usage intentions when present but has no effect when absent. An inhibitor can be distinguished from an enabler as being a belief for which there is no corresponding positively valenced antipole that is psychologically meaningful.

Given our focus in this study on object-based beliefs, we posit that object-based inhibitors also exist as parallel but distinct system and information factors salient to usage intentions but with a negative influence on intentions. As with information and system quality, information and system inhibitors can act as a signal by which users determine future actions with respect to a target technology. However, in the case of inhibitors, these negatively oriented attributes signal that something is wrong with the target technology and so contribute to user rejection, not acceptance. The unique effects of inhibitors on usage intentions—above and beyond enablers—arise from a violation of a user’s norms or expectations that, if absent, would otherwise be taken for granted (Cenfetelli 2004). For example, pop-up advertisements on websites are almost universally condemned by users, but a website that does not have such ads is not necessarily viewed favorably as a result of that absence. System inhibitors jeopardize the interaction between user and system. Information inhibitors jeopardize the conveyance of meaning to the user. The greater the level of system and information inhibitors that a system possesses, the less likely a user will intend on using that system.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> Information inhibitors will have a significant negative influence on usage intentions above and beyond enablers.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4</sup> <sup>(H4).</sup> System inhibitors will have a significant negative influence on usage intentions above and beyond enablers.

We also theorize that inhibitors will influence enablers. Individuals observe, evaluate, and process incoming information from their environments and use this information to form beliefs. Inhibitors are inherently negative beliefs, and several theories explain why negative beliefs may serve as a bias on positive beliefs. We earlier cited prospect theory, which holds that losses are more salient than like magnitude gains (Kahneman and Tversky 1979). Kahneman and Miller (1986) extend this principle to the role of negative beliefs on impressions and decision making. They assert that in comparison to positive beliefs, negative beliefs garner more cognitive attention, are more memorable, and instigate greater degrees of information processing. Losses loom larger than gains primarily as an adaptive response by individuals to their environment. Negative events—such as losses—require an individual’s attention, problem solving, and/or corrective action so as to ensure survival. Positive events— such as gains—are benign with no attendant need for attention or action for survival.

Extending prospect theory to the technology acceptance context, enablers are analogous to gains or potential gains. Inhibitors are analogous to losses or the potential for losses. Pop-up ads, for example, are a potential loss of time and attention. The implication is that inhibitors are more salient than enablers and so more attended to cognitively by technology users. The greater attention paid to inhibitors vis-à-vis enablers combines with the dissonance of valence between these two types of beliefs to negatively bias an individual’s subjective evaluations of other attributes, even if these attributes are otherwise objectively positive (Cenfetelli 2004).

The biasing effect of inhibitors on enablers can be further explained by cue diagnosticity theory (Skowronski and Carlston 1987), which posits that negative beliefs are highly diagnostic. Certain cues are more useful in categorizing objects as either good or bad; for example, a person who tells a single lie is far more likely to be judged as dishonest than a person who is often witnessed telling the truth is likely to be judged as honest. Social judgment theory also supports the position that negative beliefs are more informative than positive beliefs (Yzerbyt and Leyens 1991). Because inhibitors are uniquely negative beliefs, they may provide a diagnostic basis for system evaluation, such as in terms of information and/or system quality enabler attributes. Inhibitors are less ambiguous and uncertain than enablers (Mizerski 1982), and inhibitors can be cues on which users may base enabler evaluations, negatively so.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5</sup> <sup>(H5).</sup> Information inhibitors will have a significant negative influence on information quality.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>6</sup> <sup>(H6).</sup> System inhibitors will have a significant negative influence on system quality.

## Methodology

We next describe a series of empirical studies with two goals in mind: (a) explore and identify specific instantiations of inhibitors that are distinct from those of enablers and (b) test our research model. To achieve these goals, we conducted a two-phase investigation of inhibitors. In the first phase of our investigation, we conducted a series of exploratory qualitative studies to identify a candidate set of object-based inhibitors. In the second phase of our investigation, we used a field study both to validate the candidate inhibitors and to test our research model. In Table 1, we have outlined the two phases of our methodology.

## Phase 1: An Exploration and Identification of Object-Based Inhibitors

We collected qualitative data involving users in three different technology contexts. The extensive but unstructured data were then analyzed to find emergent patterns and constructs, resulting in the identification of six candidate inhibitors. We next describe our data collection approach, followed by a description of the data analysis.

Qualitative Data Collection. We began our empirical research into inhibitors by investigating three different and specific usage contexts: e-business websites, personal digital assistants (PDAs), and e-mail. We chose these contexts as being a diverse set of systems so as to enhance generalizability as well as to produce more vivid responses. Table 2 provides an overview of these usage contexts. We made use of the critical incident technique (CIT) (Flanagan 1954), as it is particularly well suited to explore new research areas, as is the case for inhibitors (Gremler 2004). The purpose of CIT is to content analyze and inductively classify data gathered from individuals, such as through answers to questions posed by the researcher. We asked participants open-ended questions surrounding the rejection and/or discontinuance of the target technology: descriptions of their experiences with the technology, why they chose to reject or discontinue using the technology, and what actions they did or did not take as a result. Responses ranged from one or two sentences to several paragraphs. We divided the openended responses into textual units that captured a single concept. Typical units were one to three sentences in length, and the majority preserved the original verbatim statements of the participants. Redundant units were culled resulting in a total of 164 unique textual units.

Data Analysis: Structured Conceptualization of Inhibitor and Enabler Constructs. We used structured conceptualization (Trochim 1989) to find common patterns in the 164 textual units toward inductively developing candidate inhibitors. We recruited both students and individuals from the population at large to act as judges (Table 3), but no one was a part of the CIT studies. We asked these judges to sort and categorize the textual units from one of the three usage contexts. We did not specify a number of categories or possible identification of categories; this was left up to each judge.

The primary result from the textual unit categorization was an implicit comparison of all possible pairs of units and whether a judge perceived the two units within a pair as similar (e.g., the units were placed in the same category). We analyzed these data using hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) (Arabie and Hubert 1994). HCA is a data reduction technique that finds the optimal grouping of similar items. We provide a description of the technique and our specific use of it in Online Appendix $\mathrm { F } _ { \cdot } ^ { 3 }$ The HCA produced nine clusters for the ebusiness context, five clusters for the PDA context, and five clusters for the e-mail context.

The final step was to determine the meaning of each of the 19 clusters and so identify emergent constructs. To assist in this process, we recruited judges from the population at large who had no prior involvement with our study. We divided these individuals across the three usage contexts and asked each to read the textual units in each cluster and to label the cluster with a word or phrase that best captured its meaning. We then requested the assistance of two IS academics who are experts in technology usage but who had no other involvement with our study. We asked these experts to read the textual units in each cluster as well as the various cluster labels generated by the judges. We also provided them with a complete list of the enabling object-based and behavioral beliefs previously identified in the literature. This was done to guide the appropriate level of abstraction for construct identities, as well as provide a benchmark for identifying unique constructs. We asked the experts to generate their own labels for each cluster, as well as to compare and contrast their identified constructs against the known enabler constructs, including any antipoles (e.g., unreliable, inaccurate).

## Phase 2: Inhibitor Validation and Model Testing

Field Study Design. We next validated the distinction between inhibitors and enablers as well as test our research model. We conducted a field study and recruited participants via e-mail invitation to

5,000 members of a panel, which we obtained from a marketing research firm. Participation was encouraged via cash honoraria. The study was conducted online, and the number of unique visits to the server hosting the study was monitored to determine an effective distribution rate. A total of 1,176 unique visits were logged with a total response of 401 participants for an effective response rate of 34%. We audited the responses for the time spent by participants to complete the assigned task, as well as the time spent to complete a subsequent survey. We excluded responses where the time spent on either the task or survey was minimal. Obvious data runs were also excluded. Fourteen respondents were removed, resulting in a final usable sample of 387. The average age of the participants was 35, and 51% were female; 36% of the participants completed college or some graduate schoolwork, 16% completed graduate school, and the remainder (48%) reported completing high school or some college. The demographics of the field study sample were not statistically different from those for the general Internet population (Lenhart et al. 2003).

Field Study Design. Participants were given a task asking them to investigate a travel itinerary for a friend using a travel website. Online Appendix C depicts the task description. Participants were randomly assigned to one of a pool of 32 websites to use to investigate candidate itineraries to meet the task requirements. We chose websites that excluded wellknown sites (e.g., Expedia) but that allowed booking of both flight and hotel reservations. Participants were asked to create an optimal itinerary within the task constraints. Participants were then asked to provide details of the itinerary they created. Beyond a base honorarium, participants could earn additional monies dependent on the quality of their itinerary. This promoted active engagement with the site and a thoughtful evaluation of the site when completing a subsequent survey.

The study was designed with several factors in mind. As it happens, there are literally hundreds of travel websites that are not generally known to the public. This helped to ensure that users had no prior exposure to their assigned site, so as to create a context of technology acceptance, rather than usage or continuing usage. More than 96% of the participants reported that they had no prior exposure or experience with their assigned website. The use of real websites enhanced the potential for instigating effects that may not be feasible with just a single system or with simulated or otherwise artificially manipulated interfaces. Because participants were randomly assigned to the websites, the potential confounds related to individual differences were reduced. By monitoring the time spent by participants on both the assigned task as well as completion of the subsequent survey, we could assure that respondents spent sufficient time using their assigned website, and that they reported their evaluations and perceptions of the website immediately after use. This reduced the potential errors from typical retrospective usage evaluations (Bolger et al. 2003, Kahneman et al. 2004). Finally, interaction with the website as a target system could be accomplished in an efficient manner for the participant while still allowing them to fully explore the features and design of the system.

Field Study Measurement Items. After completing the itinerary task, the participants were asked to complete a survey consisting of measurement items intended to tap the constructs of interest in the model. Measures for the following constructs were readily available from prior literature: enablers, usefulness, ease of use, and usage intentions (Davis 1989, DeLone and McLean 2003, Venkatesh 2000, Wixom and Todd 2005). The inhibitor measures were primarily derived from the constituent statements used in the structured conceptualization, with the exception of deceptiveness, which was instead adapted from Grazioli and Jarvenpaa (2000). All items were measured using 7-point Likert agreement scales. We followed a reflective measurement approach for all constructs (Petter et al. 2007). The instrument items, descriptive statistics, and loadings are in Online Appendix A. The majority of items met the recommended 0.70 threshold for construct loading (Hair et al. 1998). We dropped seven items from further analysis.<sup>4</sup>

Inhibitor Validation Results. As a means of testing that inhibitors were distinct constructs from those of enablers, we performed an oblique (correlated) exploratory factor analysis (EFA) of the enabler and inhibitor items. The EFA allowed all items to freely load on any factor, and the number of factors was determined by an eigenvalue greater than 1.00 (Kaiser 1970). The results of the EFA are in Online Appendix B. Eighteen factors were extracted. In general, items loaded highly on their intended target construct and minimally with nontargeted constructs. Furthermore, the results of the EFA generally had a clean and simple structure (Nunnally and Bernstein 1994, Thurstone 1947). Interestingly, the exceptions involved cross-loadings between ease of use and usefulness. More importantly, none of the inhibitors cross-loaded on an enabler, thus supporting that the six inhibitors were distinct and separate constructs.

Model Testing. To test our research model, we took a multilevel approach. Multilevel modeling parses the separate effects of different levels of analysis.<sup>5</sup> Failing to consider multilevel effects may lead to statistical issues because of a lack of independence among level 1 units nested within the same level 2 unit (Hoyle et al. 2001). This can lead to overly large test statistics and increased Type I error (Krull and MacKinnon 2001). Also, inferences made to the relationships among level 1 variables may be fallacious and the result of unconsidered higher-level effects (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007).

The potential for multilevel effects in this study is a result of participant users reporting on different websites. The 387 users are nested within 32 different websites, with a range of 9 to 16 users reporting on a given website. There may be shared website effects among users reporting on the same site, hence, the potential for nonindependence among observations. To assess nonindependence, we calculated the intraclass correlation (ICC) for each construct to assess to what degree user perceptions varied between websites, as compared to within a website (Hayes 2006).<sup>6</sup> Table 6 provides a summary of each construct’s ICC. Several constructs exhibited a significant website effect. For example, more than half of the variance in intentions is the result of a website effect. Even in those cases where website effects were smaller, there is not yet clear guidance on maximum thresholds for ICC to unequivocally state independence (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007, Hayes 2006). Minimal ICC values can jeopardize findings if group-level variance is not considered (Hayes 2006, Julian 2001). Hence we considered website-level effects in testing all proposed relationships.

To model both user and website-level effects, we used hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) (Bryk and Raudenbush 1992), as implemented in HLM software version 6.06. The users are at level 1, and the websites are at level 2. We used HLM to perform a series of regression equations to test the hypothesized relationships at both levels. Issues associated with sample size, mean centering, and level 2 aggregation are discussed in Online Appendix H. The level 1 model for predicting usage intentions is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {INTENTION} _ {i j} = \beta_ {0 j} + \beta_ {1 j} \text {ENABLER} _ {i j} \\ + \beta_ {2 j} \text {INHIBITOR} _ {i j} + \beta_ {3 j} \text {CONTROL} _ {i j}, \\ \beta_ {0 j} = \gamma_ {0 0} + \mu_ {0 j}. \end{array}
$$

The above is generalized such that INHIBITOR, ENABLER, and CONTROL are proxies for several terms: a user’s reported intention (INTENTION 5 is a function of the relevant quality enabler (information or system quality), the three relevant information or system inhibitors, the relevant control variable (PU for information enabler/inhibitors, PEOU for system enabler/inhibitors), and a website group mean $( \beta _ { 0 j } ) . ^ { 7 }$

The subscript i is indexed to each user (i = 1 to 387), and j is indexed to each website (j = 1 to 32). The $\beta$ coefficients are regression coefficients similar to ordinary least squares (OLS) and represent the strength of association between predictor and dependent variables (DVs).<sup>8</sup>

The level 1 model for the effects of inhibitors on enablers is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {ENABLER} _ {i j} = \beta_ {0 j} + \beta_ {1 j} \text {INHIBITOR} _ {i j}, \\ \beta_ {0 j} = \gamma_ {0 0} + \mu_ {0 j}. \end{array}
$$

The level 2 models are as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {INTENTION} _ {j} & = \beta_ {0 j} \\ & = \gamma_ {0 0} + \gamma_ {0 1} \text {ENABLER} _ {j} + \gamma_ {0 2} \text {INHIBITOR} _ {j} \\ & \quad + \gamma_ {0 3} \text {CONTROL} _ {j} + \mu_ {0 j}, \\ \text {ENABLER} _ {j} & = \beta_ {0 j} = \gamma_ {0 0} + \gamma_ {0 1} \text {INHIBITOR} _ {j} + \mu_ {1 j}. \end{array}
$$

The level 2 models test the effects of the relevant predictors on the 32 website group means (note the single j subscript for INTENTION, ENABLER, and CONTROL above). The primary question to be addressed by the first equation in the level 2 model is whether enablers and/or inhibitors explain usage intention differences between websites. The second equation addresses between-website effects for inhibitors on enablers. The $\gamma _ { 0 x }$ terms in the level 2 equations are slope coefficients and represent the strength of the relationship between the level 2 predictors and the INTENTION or ENABLER DVs. Similar to the level 1 models, we specified that $\mu _ { 0 j }$ and $\mu _ { 1 j }$ be estimated by HLM and allowed to vary between websites.

## Discussion

The results of our study provide several key findings regarding the distinction of inhibitors from enablers, as well as the role of inhibitors in technology acceptance. Toward addressing our first research question as to why inhibitors are distinct, we built upon theories of dual-factored constructs and greater negative differentiation to argue why inhibitors can be more than the opposite of enablers. To address our second question, as to what some of the specific instantiations of inhibitors are, we identified six inhibitors: intrusiveness, process uncertainty, effort redundancy, irrelevant requests for information, information overload, and deceptiveness. A factor analysis of items reflecting these inhibitors and a content valid domain of enablers was conducted to compare these inhibitors with enablers. The results supported that these inhibitors were distinct and independent constructs, and hence, were not merely antipoles of an enabler already identified in the literature (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet 2007, Cenfetelli 2004).

To address our third question on understanding the effects of inhibitors on either usage intentions or on enablers, we tested our research model using a multilevel technique. This allowed the parsing of variance across both individual and system (website) levels, as well as the testing of relationships across both levels of analysis. The website level of analysis showed significant effects of enablers on usage intentions in support of Hypotheses 1 and 2. There were also significant effects of enablers on usage intentions at the individual level of analysis, although these effects were weaker than at the website level. These results are consistent with the extant literature supporting the positive effects of information and system quality on use intentions (DeLone and McLean 2003).

The multilevel analysis also showed that there were significant differences between websites for both usage intentions and enablers, as indicated by the high values of ICC for these constructs. The ICCs can be interpreted as a measure of the level of agreement among users of a given website (Glick 1985). Thus, there was strong agreement among the users of a given website as to the degree of enablers that are present in that site. There was also strong agreement for intentions: users of a given website tended to have similar usage intentions as other users of that site. We infer that the most likely source of that shared agreement is the design and attributes of the website itself. The participants in the study were not otherwise associated with one another, other than through the common experience of using the same website.<sup>10</sup> The relationship of enablers on usage intentions is largely a websitelevel effect, and so, it is explainable by the objective differences between websites.

For Hypotheses 3 and 4—the relationship between inhibitors and usage intentions—the results indicate that inhibitors behave differently in terms of their effects, compared to enablers. Inhibitors were not explanatory of differences in usage intentions between websites, but they were explanatory between individuals. All six inhibitors had a significant negative effect on usage intentions for users within a given website. Assessing agreement as we did for enablers, the ICC values for the six inhibitors ranged from 0.04 to 0.13, which suggests that there was a much smaller degree of agreement about the degree of inhibitors, compared to that of enablers. If a high degree of agreement is supportive of the influence of objective website differences, we can infer that inhibitors are the result of a more subjective and individually derived assessment. The variance in inhibitors is driven by individual-level differences, not by websitelevel differences. However, a user who perceives an inhibitor is still less likely to use the system.

## Implications for Research

We reviewed many of the inhibitors previously identified in the IS literature. One goal of our study was to extend this prior research by theorizing why inhibitors are distinct from enablers. The primary implication for IS research is that there is a broader array of beliefs salient to usage intentions than has been typically considered. A focus on only positive antecedents, as is often taken in technology acceptance research, may lead to an incomplete set of factors. This can lead to an “error of exclusion” in which theoretically important phenomena are omitted from the research model (Benbasat and Zmud 2003). The results of this study lend support to our proposition that there are additional antecedents to usage intentions beyond those identified in the dominant usage theories, such as UTAUT and user satisfaction.

Another implication for IS research is that by distinguishing between inhibitors and enablers, it is possible to identify antecedents to usage intentions that would not otherwise be evident from previously identified enablers. As stated in the introduction, the answers to the question “Why do individuals choose not to use technology?” may be distinct and different from what research has found in response to the commonly posed question of why individuals choose to use. The inhibitors found in this study, such as information overload or deceptiveness, are quite different in nature from enablers and likely not derivable from those enablers. The results of our research have helped to counter the inherent if explicitly unstated assumption that the antecedents of technology rejection are simply the opposite of the enablers. This behooves IS researchers to ask not just why but also to ask why not. A further implication is that because inhibitors are distinct constructs, inhibitors and enablers can simultaneously coexist as user-held perceptions. This supports the thesis that inhibitors and enablers are dual-factored constructs and thus lends empirical support to Cenfetelli’s (2004) dualfactored model of IT usage.

We found that inhibitors and enablers can act separately and simultaneously on usage intentions, although with effects that were altered with differing unit levels of analysis. The implication is that inhibitors have unique effects, compared to enablers. Usage intentions are a function not only of enablers but also of inhibitors as well. Another unique effect of inhibitors is that they can act to bias enablers. Drawing from prospect theory, cue diagnosticity theory, and social judgment theory, we proposed that inhibitors would bias enablers, given the tendency of the negative to be stronger than the positive. We found partial support for this effect. The results suggest that the biasing effect may be contingent upon specific inhibitors such as deceptiveness. In the case of deceptiveness, in addition to making usage intentions less likely, deceptiveness will also jeopardize the other positive aspects of the system being evaluated. The implication of such occurrences is that an enabler does not act as a counterbalance against an inhibitor but is itself negatively affected.

We also found inhibitors and enablers exerted their influences at different unit levels of analysis. There was more variance among individuals in how they perceive the negative attributes of a given website (greater variance within groups), whereas there was more similarity among individuals in how they perceive the positive attributes (greater variance between groups).

The research implication is that negative attributes may prompt more cognitive analysis than neutral or positive attributes (Taylor 1991). As a greater degree of cognitive analysis ensues, the evaluation of negative attributes inherently becomes more subjective. Different individuals have different experiences with a given website, and they bring these different experiences to bear on their cognitive analysis. This results in more variance in their perceptions of these attributes, as compared to that of other users of the same site. Conversely, because positive attributes prompt less cognitive analysis, these attributes are perceived more simply and thus are less subjective. Individuals will not bring to bear their full experience with the site when evaluating these attributes and thus leads to less variance among those using the same site.

There are two broader implications for research from this study. One, the concept of inhibitors may inform contexts beyond individual usage such as interorganizational systems (King and Teo 1996, Teo et al. 2006). Two, this study helped to demonstrate multilevel modeling and its applicability to contexts where multiple target systems are studied. Multilevel modeling is still a rather new technique to our field and, as Burton-Jones and Gallivan (2007) have ably pointed out, failing to incorporate multilevel analysis risks drawing inappropriate inferences.

## Implications for Practice

Object-based beliefs can be important for the diagnosticity of system design and give managers distinct leverage points for insuring successful system design and implementation (Wixom and Todd 2005). The identification of the object-based inhibitors in this study may provide similar prescriptive value. Insuring accurate and reliable systems is important, but it is also important to avoid designing systems with inhibiting features, such as leaving users uncertain as to procedural step completion (process uncertainty) or forcing users to repeat steps (effort redundancy). Although complete information has been identified in the literature, information overload has not been. Assessing only the completeness of information would not likely capture whether too much information has been provided. The set of six inhibitor beliefs identified in this study potentially adds to the repertoire of attributes important to system design.

The effects of inhibitors on enablers implies that it may not be sufficient to offer better system design features in the hopes of compensating for any unforeseen design flaws. In some cases, the flaws will not only have a negative influence on their own accord: they may also jeopardize the evaluations that individuals make of otherwise positive design features. The multilevel modeling results shed further light on the situation; we suggested that the website-level effects on enablers and usage intentions were the result of objective differences between websites. Although some variance derives from differences between individuals, most of the explainable variance is the result of website-level effects and, by inference, the design aspects of those sites. Sites that provide greater degrees of information and system quality are rewarded with users who are more likely to intend to return for future business. However, this is less the case for inhibitors. The effects of inhibitors on usage intentions were largely at an individual level. This may support the contention of Cenfetelli (2004) that firms do not purposely engage in poor design. Inhibitors do not tend to be systematic issues; they likely occur through accident or error. When inhibitors do occur, they may tend to occur to a few unlucky users of a given system, and those users are less likely to intend to use the system in the future as a result. This is all the more the case in our use of real-world websites. A systematic presence of an inhibitor that affects each and every user would likely lead to most if not all users rejecting the site leading to its eventual demise. These are naturally removed from the pool of available sites.

## Limitations and Future Research

In the process of eliciting inhibitors, we explored both initial and continued usage contexts. However, our field study design precluded the capturing of actual usage behavior and thus was limited to an acceptance context only. We acknowledge as a limitation of our study the differences in the contexts used for elicitation versus testing of inhibitors. It may be that certain inhibitors are only salient to certain contexts, such as continued usage. We opted not to remove any inhibitors from our field testing in the interests of maintaining a broad list of inhibitors. It would be beneficial for future research to be conducted that incorporates actual usage behavior. This could be especially important for corporate contexts where significant IT project failures still occur as a result of a lack of employee buy in or outright abandonment (Keil et al. 2008, Nelson 2007). Such a postmortem of a failed system may reveal inhibitors heretofore unconsidered. On that point, we also cannot claim that the six inhibitors identified in this study are exhaustive. An exploration of other usage contexts may identify additional object-based inhibitors. We looked exclusively at individual consumer usage of technology. A study of corporate, small group, and educational contexts may augment our findings significantly. An inhibitor perspective may be particularly relevant for newer areas of IS research such as cloud computing. Health-care IT may be another particularly relevant context, as there have been frequent comments on how much this area has lagged behind other industries in the world in terms of leveraging IT for better patient care and lower costs. The benefits seem obvious, but it may be that the focus of research should shift from the enablers to the inhibitors. The work by Bhattacherjee and Hikmet (2007, 2008) on physician resistance to health-care IT is one exemplar of this approach, but there are likely many other possibilities as well. Our model may be useful for identifying specific inhibitors for these and other context and so provide practical guidance in these contexts for issues to avoid.

We purposely limited our investigation of inhibitors to object-based beliefs. Investigating different belief targets will likely identify other inhibitors. For example, the resistance inhibitor (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet 2007) is not object based but is instead an individual characteristic. Likewise, job overload (Ahuja and Thatcher 2005) targets a facilitating condition (or lack thereof). Different belief targets beckon further research into other specific inhibitors and our model could provide a framework for these investigations. Other belief targets worth pursuing include individual characteristics, social norms, role perceptions, characteristics of the organization, and/or facilitating conditions. These belief targets have been studied as antecedent to usage but under the assumption that they are strictly positively oriented in nature. We assert that despite our focus on only object-based inhibitors, our research model was posited at a level of abstraction such that the constructs and relationships should continue to hold for other belief targets.

## Conclusion

This study investigated the inhibitors of technology usage intentions. Inhibitors are individual-level beliefs that foster technology rejection rather than acceptance. We theorized the reasons for inhibitors to exist as constructs independent of the extensive array of enablers that have been previously identified in the literature as antecedent to intentions. We further posited why and how those beliefs may have unique effects on usage intentions. Through an inductive and exploratory process, a broad set of generally applicable inhibitors was identified. We then empirically tested these inhibitors and found that they did exist separately from enablers. This supported the idea that there are additional predictive beliefs beyond what has been previously identified in the literature. A focus solely on enablers excludes other relevant factors to technology acceptance. The findings also supported the idea that inhibitors have negative effects on usage intentions and on enablers. Inhibitors have important implications for our understanding of why individuals do not accept technology, as well as being implications for technology design.
