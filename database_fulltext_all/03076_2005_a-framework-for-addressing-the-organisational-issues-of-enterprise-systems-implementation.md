# A framework for addressing the organisational issues of enterprise systems implementation

John Ward<sup>a,</sup>\*, Christopher Hemingway<sup>a</sup>, Elizabeth Daniel<sup>b</sup>

Information Systems Research Centre, Cranfield University, School of Management, Bedford, MK43 0AL, Bedford, UK

<sup>b</sup>Open University Business School, Walton Hall, Milton Keynes, MK7 6AA.

Available online 13 May 2005

## Abstract

Over the last 10 years many organisations have made significant investments in Enterprise-wide Systems (ES), particularly Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) and Customer Relationship Management (CRM) software packages. Whilst in most cases technical implementation is relatively successful, many of the initiatives have failed to deliver the benefits expected. Research studies have identified a wide range of factors, that can affect the success of ES implementations, and the general consensus is that organisational issues are more difficult to resolve than technical ones.

This research set out to synthesise a framework, from prior research, for analysing and understanding these organisational issues and to apply and refine the framework by studying four ES initiatives in different organisational and industry contexts. The findings from the case studies suggest that the framework can help understand how different approaches to managing ES implementations both address and influence the behaviours of key interest groups and hence the achievement of the benefits expected from the investment.

Keywords: Enterprise systems; Enterprise resource planning; Organisational issues

## 1. Introduction

Enterprise Systems (ES) are configurable, off-the-shelf software packages that provide an integrated suite of systems and information resources for operational and management processes across a broad range of business activities. The range of ES available is growing and includes enterprise resource planning (ERP), customer relationship management (CRM), supply chain management (SCM) and, most recently, enterprise portals. Success and failure in implementing ES have been documented in the information systems literature (Scheer and Habermann, 2000; Krumbholz and Maiden, 2001; Hong and Kim, 2002; Sarker and Lee, 2003). Unfortunately, the proportion of failed projects is significant (Bingi et al., 1999), in some cases leading to a reduction in earnings or decline in profits (Stedman, 1999). In the worst cases, ES projects have been identified as factors in organisational bankruptcy and collapse (Davenport, 1998).

Given the high stakes involved, it is imperative for organisations embarking on ES projects to be aware of the success and failure factors, and understand how to address them. There is a growing consensus that organisational issues are more critical than technical considerations, since the benefits are typically realised through extensive changes to business practices and decision making (Markus et al., 2000; Davenport, 2000; Bancroft et al., 1998). Relatively little is known, however, about how the ES project team might address these organisational issues and how this can affect the success of ES implementation.

This research aimed to address this gap in current understanding of ES implementation. In particular, this study sought to understand the organisational issues of ES implementation by considering two important dimensions: the interactions between a project team’s management approach and stakeholder modes of behaviour. A synthesised framework was developed from existing literature that addresses management approaches to implementing change (Ury et al., 1993) and stakeholder rationalities regarding IT implementation (Kling, 1980; Kumar et al., 1998). Case studies were used to apply and refine the framework.

The paper is organised as follows: Section 2 reviews the literature and Section 3 develops the theoretical framework; Section 4 describes the research methods adopted for the empirical study; Section 5 describes the case studies; Section 6 discusses the key findings; and Section 7 draws conclusions from the work in terms of its practical relevance and theoretical implications.

## 2. Organisational issues of ES implementation

Previous research has differentiated between technological, economic and organisational explanations for ES success and failure (Sarker and Lee, 2003). Technological factors include technical properties (e.g. field lengths, record IDs) that are unsuitable in some contexts (Soh et al., 2000) and software version control (Markus et al., 2000). Economic factors concern the accuracy and strength of the business case for the ES investment. In particular, many organisations were motivated to invest in ES as a means of ensuring Year 2000 compliance (Sia et al., 2002) or to reduce the business risks of nonintegrated and failure prone legacy systems (Worthen, 2002; Hirt and Swanson, 1999). It has been suggested that, in such cases, the financial and strategic implications of ES implementation did not receive enough attention (Markus et al., 2000).

The term ‘organisational issues’ refers to the effects of organisational culture, structure, governance, communication and conflict between stakeholder groups (Krumbholz et al., 2000; Krumbholz and Maiden, 2001; Soh et al., 2000; Scott and

Vessey, 2002; Besson and Rowe, 2001) and reflects the growing use of this term in related strands of research (Doherty and King, 2001; Lederer and Nath, 1991). Recent research suggests that an ES project team’s ability to address these issues, by managing multiple stakeholders with divergent interests, can play a vital role in ES implementation success (Sarker and Lee, 2003).

Whilst the focus of this paper is on the organisational issues that arise during implementation, it should be noted that there are other important areas of research that pertain to the adoption and use of such systems. These include studies on the appropriation of technology (see for example, Venkatesh et al., 2003); the division of benefits between the organisation and the individual (Jurison, 1996) and the time dependency effects of IT (Brynjolfson, 1993).

## 2.1. Communication and negotiation between ES project stakeholders

ES implementations usually affect a diverse range of interest groups. The ways in which stakeholder groups resolve or reconcile their different interests have been found to affect ES implementation success. Effective communication between groups, in particular, has been identified as a significant factor in enhancing co-operation and reducing resistance to change (Besson and Rowe, 2001), whilst its absence has been proposed as a risk factor in ES implementation (Sumner, 2000).

Consistent with the broader IS literature, senior management and ES project managers are considered to play an important role in securing the co-operation of diverse stakeholder groups (Koh et al., 2000; Willcocks and Sykes, 2000). A recent study, however, contends that management must go beyond communication and contribute to negotiating change and mediating disagreements between interest groups (Chen, 2001). ES project managers have also been recommended to remain in constant communication with affected business stakeholders, particularly at the design stage (Bancroft et al., 1998; Parr and Shanks, 2000). Empirical studies suggest that communication during the initial phase of an ES project, in order to establish a consensus about the investment objectives and achievable benefits is a valuable precursor to stakeholders agreeing how implementation will proceed (Markus et al., 2000). Effective negotiation is also seen as a key means of realising the business benefits of ES following implementation (Truex et al., 1999). To date, however, there has been little research exploring how ES project managers’ styles of communicating with stakeholders and managing conflicts between stakeholders’ interests contribute to the success or failure of ES implementation.

## 3. Research framework

Recognising that the behaviours of stakeholders and the ES project team’s approach to managing or accommodating those behaviours will both have a significant effect on the ES implementation, we propose a framework for analysis that considers and combines these two dimensions.

## 3.1. Types of stakeholder interaction

Theories of information systems have been characterised as taking either a system rationalism or a segmented institutionalism perspective (Kling, 1980). The former assumes that all stakeholders in an organisation focus on maximising the organisation’s efficiency and effectiveness through the use of technology, whereas the latter regards stakeholders as pursuing their private interests, even at the expense of the organisation. More recently, a third perspective, trust-based rationalism, was proposed as a means of recognising that, whilst protecting one’s interests can be expected and may lead to conflict, stakeholders also collaborate with one another to seek joint gains, based on relationships of trust (Kumar et al., 1998). The importance of establishing trust among all the parties involved in information systems developments has been emphasised in prior research, including recent studies of ERP implementations (Scott and Kaindl, 2000; Gefen, 2002, 2004; Sarker and Lee, 2003). The specific issues associated with trust and relationships were not the main focus of this study and are not discussed in depth in this paper. (A more detailed consideration of the notion of trust as presented in this model is to be the subject of another paper, based on this work and a subsequent research project, in which a number of CRM implementations were studied).

The three rationalities are not mutually exclusive but represent different ways of describing stakeholder behaviour surrounding IS implementation. Empirical ES researchers have observed behaviour characteristic of all three rationalities (see Table 1).

Implementation activities reflecting system rationalism include project planning, systems analysis and design, and quantifying expected benefits. Thus, it might be expected that an ES project may begin with an emphasis on system rationalism, due to the extensive use of planning and design methods, but exhibit more characteristics of trust-based rationalism and/or segmented institutionalism as planning activity gives way to the practicalities of effecting organisational change.

## 3.2. ES project team’s approach to resolving potential stakeholder conflicts

As our focus was on how the ES project team addresses organisational issues that are potential areas of conflict between stakeholders, negotiation research addressing conflict between interest groups was chosen as the basis for exploring the possible management approaches of the ES team.

Reviewing the literature on negotiations between stakeholders with divergent interests, we identified the widely used power, interests and rights framework for dispute resolution (Ury et al., 1993) as an appropriate typology. It is a generic typology that could be expected to cover the majority of types of negotiation approaches that an ES project team might need to adopt. This framework proposes three approaches:

† Power-based, financial, political and other sources of power are used to coerce stakeholders to agree to a preferred option. A power-based approach usually results in a win–lose solution and may also reduce the willingness of some stakeholders to engage constructively with one another in the future.

† Interest-based, the needs, desires and concerns of all stakeholders are considered in order to agree a way of creating value for all parties. An interest-based approach attempts to deliver a win–win solution.

† Rights-based, divergent interests are dealt with in a contractual or legalist way. A rights-based approach usually results in a win–lose solution, as benefits to one stakeholder are traded for benefits to another.

Empirical studies confirm that all three approaches are used in practice (Ury et al., 1993; Brett et al., 1998). Much negotiation research, however, has focused on interestbased strategies for identifying outcomes that are mutually beneficial to all stakeholders, and on ways of avoiding the rights and power based approaches, which may exacerbate conflict between stakeholders (Bazerman and Neale, 1992; Fisher et al., 1991). Recent research, however, suggests that either rights or power based strategies can be effective if they include: a specific and detailed demand; a specific negative consequence of the identified outcome not being achieved; or a specific positive consequence if the identified outcome is achieved (Lytle et al., 1999).

That each of the three strategies can be used to good effect in different circumstances, suggests that an ES project team’s ability to select and use an appropriate negotiation strategy when managing potential stakeholder conflict would be one approach to effectively resolving organisational issues during ES implementation.

## 3.4. Implementation phases

The model of Markus et al. (2000) proposes that ES implementation is comprised of four phases: chartering, project, shakedown, and ‘onwards and upwards’, with each phase involving distinct activities and having different measures of success. The first three phases are driven by an ES project team and are, therefore, the focus of this study.

According to this model, the chartering stage involves establishing clear business objectives for the ES investment. The project stage is defined as delivery of the ES software package and changes to operational processes to agreed time, scope and budget. The shakedown phase is mainly concerned with restoring business performance, which often drops following ES implementation. It often involves diagnosing business process problems, managing negative reactions to the new system and maintaining support for its continuing operation. The differences between each phase suggest that the organisational issues an ES project team must address will vary across the implementation process. We therefore, recorded changes along each dimension of Fig. 1 during the charter, project and shakedown stages of the ES implementations studied.

## 4. Research method

The management approaches and modes of behaviour that are the focus of this study are highly complex and context-dependent. For this reason, research methods that allow such complexity to be observed and recorded within its unique setting are required (Hoskisson et al., 1999). The study was exploratory, rather than confirmatory in nature, seeking to apply and refine the proposed framework, suggesting the need for an interpretive approach (Galliers, 1992; Walsham, 1995). An in-depth case study approach was adopted (Yin, 1994; Eisenhardt, 1989) using semi-structured interviews as the main data collection instrument.

To populate the framework with sufficient evidence of the range of possible interactions between the management approaches and stakeholder behaviours required a multiple case study approach and, since our synthesised framework was not industry-specific, case studies were selected from different industries (Hussey and Hussey, 1997). The use of multiple cases and the collection of data through semi-structured interviews reduced the possibility that evidence, of relevance to the research, was not ascertained (Eisenhardt, 1989).

Table 2 shows the four case studies undertaken, their industry sectors, and other characteristics of the organisations studied.

Since the focus of the study was on the interactions and negotiations of the project team with other groups within the organisation, interviews were undertaken with project staff. In all four cases project staff from both IS and business departments were interviewed. Interviews with multiple informants in each project team increased the internal validity of the data collected (Easterby-Smith et al., 2001; Miles and Huberman, 1994). In total thirteen people were interviewed in nine separate interviews. Interviews lasted between one and three hours and were tape recorded and transcribed. A summary of the interview transcriptions was produced for each of the four case study organisations. These summaries were reviewed by the staff interviewed to ensure accuracy and completeness. In addition, relevant business and project documentation in each case study organisation was examined to confirm the factual information about the projects. Data collection, summary and review took place during 2002.

Analysis was performed on each interview using content analysis (Weber, 1990) in order to identify comments relating to management approaches adopted by the ES team and the behaviour of stakeholder groups for each phase of the project. The reasons for these approaches and behaviours and apparent consequences were also noted. Subsequently, for each case, the evidence from the analysis of the individual interview transcripts was reconciled and summarised, to provide a full description of the management approaches demonstrated and the stakeholder modes of behaviour exhibited in each case.

## 5.1. Implementing ES in CTel (case A)

CTel provides systems and hardware for broadband internet protocol (IP) networks to integrate voice and data communications both within and between businesses. It has operations in over 70 countries, divided into two regions: North America and EMEA (Europe, the Middle East and Africa). CTel’s corporate headquarters are located in North America and the EMEA headquarters are in the UK.

The ES implementation studied consisted of two distinct phases. The first phase was an organisation-wide deployment of an ERP system across both regions. The second phase consisted of the implementation of SAP Service Management and Business Warehouse applications within the EMEA region alone.

## 5.1.1. Phase 1. Project genesis: a global ERP implementation

Phase 1 was a costly global implementation that was technically acceptable but failed to deliver any significant business benefits. Progress through phase 1 and the project outcome can be understood by considering how the management approaches and organisational behaviours evolved over the course of the project (Fig. 2).

In late 1998, the executive management of the EMEA region tasked a project team with evaluating options for implementing an ERP system. This team recommended the adoption of SAP, since it was being successfully used within a recently acquired semiconductor business. A clear mandate for the ERP implementation and hence the project team’s role was established, based upon a rational analysis of the benefits to be realised and organisational changes required to deliver them. When staff in CTel’s headquarters in North America learned of this project, several senior managers argued that the benefits of improved business integration through ERP would be even greater if CTel deployed SAP organisation-wide. Accepting this argument, the Board approved a single, global implementation project (step 1: top down/rational).

A complex project management structure, reflecting the regional and business unit structure, was created with the project management role being distributed across four separate project managers. Each project manager was given responsibility for implementation in specific business units, with a requirement to co-operate on technical matters. This was described by the EMEA project manager (interviewee A1) as encouraging a ‘premature focus on technical issues’, rather than clearly evaluating the business benefits that might be expected and the changes to organisational processes and working practices that would be needed to realise them. Although the project had, by this time, moved beyond initial planning, it remained essentially top-down, with the project managers failing to engage with key stakeholder groups, particularly line managers who would be needed to make resources available for implementation. Without a clear understanding of the benefits that they might expect, the line managers were reluctant to commit their resources, preferring to protect their own interests, which concerned maintaining the smooth running of their respective units (step 2: top down/self-interest).

As implementation progressed (interviewee A4):

“the project was dogged by schedule delays caused by different approaches and constantly shifting priorities”.

The project team therefore chose to modify the project plan in two ways. First, they would reduce the time taken to present the system to user groups and incorporate their suggestions into the final implementation. Second, they would replace the planned staged rollout with a ‘big bang’ approach, in which both regions (N. America and EMEA) and all lines of business would begin using the new system on the same day. To achieve this, the project managers were obliged to try and build relationships with groups of line managers to persuade them to accept the system despite the lack of consultation and also to provide sufficient resources for the ‘big bang’ implementation. Recognising that due to the backing of the senior management and the mandate of the project team, that the ‘project was going to happen anyway’ (interviewee A4), the line managers somewhat begrudgingly provided resources, but remained concerned about operational problems and business risks in their own units (step 3: coalitions/self-interest).

Whilst cooperation was eventually gained from line managers and their staff, the reduction in user consultation was regarded as a sign of a technology-centred approach to implementation that neglected SAP’s impact upon core business processes and working practices:

“To many of those in the business, it felt like SAP was being done to them, rather than they were involved or part of it” (interviewee A2).

As the ‘go live’ date approached, even members of the project team acknowledged that their overriding concern became “don’t screw up the business” (interviewee A1), rather than delivering business benefits. To avoid business problems during implementation, it was necessary for the four project managers to agree a consistent, low risk approach to introducing the new applications in each business. This was achieved by negotiating detailed written agreements with each of the key stakeholder groups (step 4: negotiation/self-interest). However, lacking common business objectives for the implementation, the project managers avoided conflict by negotiating only where they had some common ground—the technical standards for implementation. Fortunately, achieving this consistency regarding technical issues meant that the SAP software was implemented without significant problems. Nevertheless, the failure to establish common business goals at any point in the project resulted in few stakeholder groups believing that they had benefited from the ERP implementation, as described by interviewee A1:

“it’s our old business on a new system—but the new or extra benefits that we expected have not been realised.”

## 5.1.2. Phase 2: Service Management Implementation in EMEA

The organisational modes and management approaches witnessed during phase 2 were significantly different from those that characterised phase 1 (Fig. 3). Whilst the geographic scope and the number of business activities affected were less than in phase 1, the modules implemented affected the most complex parts of the business and the version of the software installed had not, at that time, been installed in Europe by any other organisation.

As with phase 1, the project commenced with the EMEA senior management establishing a vision of why and how the service organisation had to change, and how this could be achieved by implementing the new SAP modules. There was an explicit statement of the intended benefits and an understanding that these would result from a combination of organisational, process and system changes. This top-down vision allowed the project team to develop a comprehensive business case and agree a clear framework for detailed planning, design and implementation with line managers (step 1: top down/rational).

As the project moved to the implementation stage the project team presented the business case at workshops where stakeholder groups were extensively involved in discussing how the changes would be implemented and benefits realised. This stage, which lasted for over half the duration of phase 2, included developing a system prototype for demonstration to key business users and gaining their feedback about how implementation could be refined. Once technical and business changes were agreed, responsibility for achieving them was explicitly assigned to cross-functional teams, involving both field engineers and central service management staff, who committed to co-operate with each other to deliver them (step 2: coalitions/trust), as described by interviewee A1:

“twelve key business change requirements were identified at these workshops—and action plans were developed and responsibilities agreed in order to achieve these changes.

In contrast to phase 1, phase 2 was managed by a single senior business manager who also had access to sufficient financial and other resources. As the change over to the new system approached, key stakeholders were becoming concerned about the details of implementation and changes within their own areas. The project manager was able to discuss the implications with each stakeholder group and provide extra resources, where needed, to affect the necessary changes, thus avoiding any deterioration in services to customers (step 3: negotiation/self-interest).

A formal post-implementation review of the project gave a detailed account of the benefits achieved, both from a financial perspective and according to the views of key stakeholders. Almost all the intended benefits had been achieved and, equally importantly, the field engineers declared the system a great success in terms of ease of use.

The project commenced at the end of 1998 and the urgency of the need to replace the non-compliant systems caused the executive management to mandate the project, that is to adopt a top down approach, with authority delegated to the project team. The project team selected PeopleSoft and Siebel as the packages that would be used, and agreed with senior management that ‘plain vanilla’ (interviewee D2) versions should be implemented, and where necessary, business processes should be changed to suit the new systems. The organisation traditionally has a highly consensual style arising from its public sector background and the large number of academic and highly qualified research staff. In this environment, rather than have solutions mandated, stakeholders expected each other to work in ways that are mutually beneficial (step 1: top down/trust).

The same management approach and mode continued into the project phase. The project team used its strong mandate and the widespread concern over Y2K to gain the interest and cooperation of the necessary stakeholders. These stakeholders trusted the project when they gave the assurance that changes would be limited and benefits significant (step 2a: top down/trust).

However, reliance on the consensual culture of the organisation led to limited evaluation and consultation concerning the business benefits that could be realised from the new systems, and also the changes to processes and working practices that might be required. It soon became clear that a plain vanilla implementation would require more significant business changes than functional managers had been led to believe. As a result, according to interviewee D1:

“Some key business staff refused to cooperate with the Board’s decision. They wouldn’t make the changes to business processes that were required—or at least not as quickly as was required.” (step 2b: top-down/self-interest)

Consequently, with the agreement of senior management, the project team had to abandon the plan to implement a ‘plain vanilla’ solution and, instead, undertake extensive software customisation (over 50%), in order to meet the specific demands of the functional managers (step 3: negotiation/self-interest).

In spite of the difficulties, the system went live on time at Christmas 1999. However, the shakedown phase was protracted and highly problematic. In order to ensure the Y2K deadline was met, insufficient attention had been paid to user training. As described by interviewee D2:

“It was assumed that since the modifications had been modelled on the system that had been replaced, users would need little training.”

However, the system was not as intuitive as expected and for some weeks the help-desk was overwhelmed by users asking what the new system did and how it worked. Gradually, as problems were removed, confidence in using the system increased. Business staff began to appreciate the operational improvements that were now possible and the project team sought to build coalitions of interest groups to identify where specific business changes could deliver expected benefits that had not been realised or additional benefits—‘to build a value chain that works’—interviewee D1 (step 4: coalitions/rational). Overall the project was viewed as reasonably successful. However, the package was highly customised, increasing the cost of the project and suggesting increased future maintenance and upgrade costs.

## 6. Discussion of the case study findings

The case studies undertaken illustrate different combinations of organisational modes and management approaches, resulting in alternative paths through the framework proposed in Fig. 1. These combinations and routes through the framework need to be considered in the context of the evolution of the ES implementation process. For this reason, the findings are summarised in terms of the model of Markus et al. (2000). In this discussion, evidence from the two further cases, summarised in the Appendix is included where relevant.

Charter stage: To quote Markus et al. (2000, p. 263), ‘only senior executives (not project managers and team members) can address pre-existing organisational challenges that threaten ERP success’, in the charter stage. In three of the four cases (cases A, B, C), the charter stage had active involvement from the senior business management team to develop the business case for investment and frame key decisions, such as package selection. Executive management effectively set the agenda for business integration and change, providing a clear mandate for the role of the ES project team (top-down/rational).

In case D senior management did not believe the ES implementation was significant in terms of the future business strategy, but wanted to ensure accountability for the delivery of the efficiency improvements and cost savings expected from the replacement of legacy systems. Responsibility was delegated to the project team to plan the implementation (topdown/trust).

Project stage: The combinations of organisational mode and management approach occurring at the project stage varied considerably, and in most cases changed several times during implementation as can be seen in Figs. 2–4, A1 and A2. Only in the CTel Service Management project (case A phase 2) was there no stage during which significant conflicts arose between the project team and stakeholder groups. In this case the project team worked with coalitions of users to define the combinations of systems functionality and business changes that would produce the benefits, prior to agreeing implementation responsibilities (coalitions/trust). In all the other cases there were periods when some combinations of stakeholder behaviours and the project teams’ approaches were associated with delays to the project and changes to the project plans and scope of either the business changes or system implementation.

In all cases (A phase 1, B, C and D) when project teams proceeded to the project stage before gaining acceptance of the organisational changes required and responsibility for delivering the benefits, implementation was characterised by significant periods during which the top-down/self-interest combination dominated the project teams’ relationships with key interest groups. In these cases the project teams were forced into dealing with individual interest groups, or coalitions of groups, to agree the business changes that would be made in order to achieve the benefits in the business case. In two cases (A phase 1 & C) the project teams invoked their ‘top-down’ mandate to force through the project plan, although this resulted in an increased need to deal with the different and specific concerns of many stakeholders. The consequence in each case was to increase the extent of software customisation to offset the resistance to making business changes. This led inevitably to cost overruns and delays, but also to a reduction in the benefits realised.

Where the risk of running over time and/or budget became a serious concern for the project team (for example in cases A1, B and C), pressure increased to make reductions in project scope and to defer some of the more difficult organisational changes. In cases A1 and C, the project teams strove to bring the project back as close as possible to schedule, by seeking trade offs amongst different interest groups in attempt to prevent further delays (coalitions/self-interest), although this reduced the benefits achieved from the initial implementation.

In case B, the team first attempted to negotiate scope reductions with the affected interest groups (negotiation/rational). Where groups perceived such a reduction in scope would result in a concomitant loss of benefits, negotiations became protracted, thereby increasing costs and delays still further. Eventually the team responded by changing the management approach, in an attempt to achieve a more effective trade off between software and business changes and thereby retain a higher proportion of the benefits. The negotiation/trust combination proved to be a more inclusive and productive approach to dealing with the need for scope reduction. The project team worked with key interest groups to develop agreement about the organisational (not group) level impacts of alternative ‘cut-backs’, and then negotiated openly with these groups about which alternatives would be taken. This led to greater acceptance of the need for scope reduction by the main stakeholders. The project team was then restructured to facilitate shared learning and problem solving amongst multiple interest groups, to agree how much change was feasible and whether the potential benefits were justified in relation to the business changes required (coalition/rational).

Shakedown stage: The route taken through the charter and project stages affected the nature and duration of the shakedown stage and the potential scope for further developments. In the two cases which entered the top-down/self-interest ‘box’ early in the project stage (phase 1 of case A and case C), the shakedown phase proved difficult and extended, owing to a lack of consensus on how to address problems and make business changes. Eventually, as problems were identified and resolved the shakedown stage was followed by successful further developments, as coalitions of line managers emerged who now understood and could agree how to realise additional benefits following ‘baseline implementation’. In cases C and D, the organisations effectively re-entered the project stage, and adopted different management approaches (coalitions/trust and coalitions/rational, respectively), which succeeded in realising benefits that depended on business changes but which had not been achieved by the original largely technically focussed implementation.

## 6.1. Implications of the findings

The four case studies revealed very different investment intentions and definitions of success, ranging from replacing non-integrated legacy systems, transforming an organisation’s core business processes, to innovation in service delivery and customer relationship management. These different intentions, described by Robey and Boudreau (1999) as alternative rationalities for innovating with IT, imply that different approaches are needed to planning and carrying out the implementation, in terms of configuring the technical solution and defining and managing organisational change (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997). Consequently, ES implementations are likely to experience a number of the combinations of organisational modes and management approaches presented in the framework. The modes and approaches which actually occur will be influenced by a variety of factors including the investment intent, how effectively the organisational vision is communicated, the extent to which areas of potential conflict are identified and resolved in the charter stage (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997; Markus et al., 2000), the degree to which technological and process change issues can be addressed concurrently (Robey et al., 2002), and, as found in this research, the management approach adopted and how well it accommodates the evolving modes of behaviour, and avoids provoking adverse behaviours, of key stakeholder groups.

The choice of management approach is largely within the control of senior management and the ES project team. The organisational mode, however, is influenced by the broader organisational context, pre-existing relationships between interest groups, and the actions and reactions of those groups over the course of implementation. Equally, interest groups will exhibit different modes of behaviour during the implementation and, unless the project team adopts a management approach which is sensitive to those behaviours, further organisational issues are likely to arise.

The framework appears to explain why certain issues had arisen, why they had or had not been dealt with effectively by the project team, and how this had influenced the future evolution and eventual outcome of the project. While the framework cannot adequately reflect all possible combinations of organisational behaviours and management approaches, it appears to help to describe and understand some of the more commonly occurring organisational issues of ES implementations.

The following observations, which are based on the combinations of behaviours and approaches witnessed in the cases studied, and the consequent different ‘routes’ through the matrix (as shown in Fig. 6) have practical implications and also add to existing knowledge gained from previous research. The first two are consistent with the findings of Robey et al. (2002) with regard to the two different approaches to ES implementation they observed— piecemeal and concerted. The third is in line with findings by Ramiller and Swanson (2003) regarding the organisational or collective ‘know-how’ required to innovate with IT.

1. Phase 2 of case A was the only ES project studied that achieved all of the intended business benefits without encountering organisational problems. Clearly a number of lessons had been learned from the earlier global ERP project. The Service Management project began with a top-down/rational charter stage, continued with a coalition/trust approach at the project stage, which enabled a benefit-based change plan to drive the technical implementation, and had a shakedown phase characterised by negotiation/selfinterest (solid line, Fig. 6). The project had combined objectives of improving performance by changing business processes and using the new ES application capabilities. The evolution of management approach successfully accommodated the organisational implications of achieving both types of objective, by developing coalitions of interest groups to determine the nature of the changes needed and take responsibility for achieving them.

2. In the cases where initial attempts were made to implement organisational change topdown, the project phase was often characterised by periods when top-down/trust, topdown/self-interest and coalition/self-interest combinations dominated the project relationships (illustrated by the dashed line in Fig. 6). When this occurred, it usually resulted in a defensive or low-risk approach to organisational change with interest groups eventually focusing on their own local benefits. This led to the business changes being reduced in scope and, consequently, fewer benefits than originally planned being realised. Where the project proceeded into the top-down/self-interest ‘box’, normally due to the project team underestimating the importance of existing relationships, very few benefits were achieved due to the refusal of some stakeholders to make the necessary business changes. Alternatively the project team undertook extensive customisation of the software, mainly to replicate existing business practices, in order to avoid conflicts that could have resulted in complete failure of the project (negotiation/self-interest). This suggests that although a topdown management approach to the charter stage is appropriate, it has limitations when applied throughout the project. It can cause behaviours that inhibit the organisation’s ability to define and implement process and practice changes, and hence preclude the realisation of many of the available benefits.

3. Where the ES projects required substantial organisational change but the exact nature of change was not jointly understood and agreed through discussions amongst the project team, senior management and key interest groups in the charter stage, the project phase encountered significant delays and costs as these ambiguities were resolved. Such ambiguities can be the result of either a lack of a common understanding of the purposes of changes or different perspectives on how to affect them successfully (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997; Ramiller and Swanson, 2003).

In the cases studied, these delays often increased the pressure on the project team to meet its time and budget commitments. In most cases the project team’s response was to attempt to regain control of the project components that would, in its view, increase its ability to carry out its mandate, by negotiating reductions in scope with individual interest groups (negotiation/rational). When this occurred, the business benefits resulting from these projects were often significantly less than originally planned, even though the benefits were, in principle, always achievable. Only when the project teams facilitated the exchange of knowledge among existing interest groups or new coalitions of key stakeholders, allowing them to collectively resolve the trade offs, did the balance between changes and benefits favour the achievement of organisational, as opposed to merely local benefits (negotiation/trust or coalitions/rational, as illustrated by the dotted line in Fig. 6). In two of the cases this only became possible after the initial implementation, in part due to the lack of benefits realised and business problems which emerged in the shakedown phase.

## 7. Conclusions and implications for future research and practice

The purpose of this research was to develop, apply and refine a framework for understanding and analysing the organisational issues involved in ES implementation.

Previous research suggested that the perceived and actual impacts on groups within an organisation have a significant effect on ES success (Sarker and Lee, 2003; Sumner, 2000). This study sought to understand two important dimensions of these organisational issues: the interactions between a project team’s management approach and stakeholder modes of behaviour. The synthesised framework considers combinations of management approaches to implementing change (Ury et al., 1993) and stakeholder rationalities regarding IT implementation (Kumar et al., 1998).

From the evidence provided by applying the framework to the case studies, insights can be drawn that may enable practitioners to understand and address some of the organisational issues that can have a significant impact on ES implementations:

† Given the scope, complexity and timescale involved in ES implementation, all three management approaches will probably be needed at different stages of the project, in order to accommodate and influence the organisational behaviours that already exist or emerge as the implementation proceeds. Ensuring that appropriate management approaches are adopted over the course of an ES project can enable and encourage the desired modes of behaviour and thereby increase the likelihood of achieving some or all of the intended benefits.

† Whilst ‘top-down’ management appears beneficial in the charter phase, sustaining this approach during the project phase may damage existing interest group relationships and their relationships with the project team. This can lead to an increasing tendency for stakeholders to focus on self-interest, which in turn can create or increase resistance to change.

† Consistent with previous research by Scott and Kaindl (2000) and Gefen (2002, 2004), we found that understanding when and how to utilise existing trust based relationships or create new coalitions of interest groups is important to the success of many ES implementations. This can affect a project team’s ability to deliver to cost and time, but may be essential to achieving the organisational changes needed to realise the investment benefits.

This research attempted to build on and synthesise findings from prior work, in order to extend our knowledge of how organisational issues affect ES success. We believe this framework could be developed and refined further, to include dimensions from other change management and behavioural models, for example, the change management control systems proposed by Simon (1995), and organisational theories which may explain the outcome of ES implementations, as suggested by Robey and Boudreau (1999). Further applied research using the framework could lead to more understanding of how specific actions taken by the project team, senior management and other stakeholders during the project affect the realisation of the particular intended benefits of the ES investment. This would be most effectively achieved through longitudinal case studies, but it might also be possible to develop an instrument to test a number of more detailed hypotheses regarding the interactions of the management approaches and modes of behaviour.
