# Platform Competition in the Sharing Economy: Understanding How Ride-Hailing Services Influence New Car Purchases

YUE GUO, XIAOTONG LI, AND XIAOHUA ZENG

ABSTRACT: Ride-hailing services provide not only alternative transportation for passengers but also job opportunities for potential drivers, resulting in both negative and positive effects on new car purchases. Our study assesses the impact of ride-hailing platforms’ market entry on new car purchases in the presence of platform competition. Our data is a monthly panel data on new car registration plates from 2013 to 2015, during which two leading ride-hailing platforms (Didi Chuxing and Uber) rolled out their services across select cities in China. We find that, while the entry of a single ridehailing platform led to a decline in new car purchases, platform competition mitigated the negative impacts of platform entries. Our explanation is that the two competing platforms may have provided subsidies to drivers such that more people purchased new cars in order to sign up as drivers. By leveraging brand heterogeneity, our analysis finds supporting evidence that platform competition has resulted in increased sales of those car brands that are commonly adopted by ride-hailing drivers. Our study contributes to the literature on pricing strategies and subsidy allocation for two-sided markets by providing empirical evidence from the ride-hailing market. It suggests that companies’ competitive strategies need to account for consumer expectations and learning in the presence of strong network effects.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: causal inference, competitive strategies, consumer expectations, Didi Chuxing, ride-hailing, staggered difference-in-differences, synthetic control methodology, Uber, car market, car-riding platforms.

## Introduction

Internet-enabled sharing platforms, such as Uber and Airbnb, have achieved rapid global expansion, generating revenues of US\$18.6 billion worldwide in 2017, according to Statista [38]. These platforms create two-sided markets. For example, ride-hailing platforms, such as Uber and Lyft, connect consumers who need a ride with private drivers who offer rides in exchange for payment via an app. The introduction of ride-hailing platforms has caused disruptions to the new car market. On the one hand, ride-hailing apps provide a medium for passengers to call for cars effortlessly and may discourage private car ownership [16]. On the other hand, ridehailing platforms provide flexible job opportunities for private drivers and thus increase the value of car ownership. The effects from the passenger side and the driver side produce opposing predictions for new car purchases, with the net effect remaining to be tested.

The net effect is further complicated by the intense competition between ride-hailing platforms. Ride-hailing platforms’ competitive strategies are often driven by network effects in the two-sided market. Drivers prefer the ride-hailing platform with the most consumers to achieve a higher utilization rate, while consumers prefer the platform with more drivers to minimize the time they have to wait for a ride. Moreover, with more users on both sides, a ride-hailing platform can implement more effective analytics to align supply with demand [30]. Thus, building market dominance is a key objective for competing platforms. However, a crucial strategic question is which side of the market (drivers vs. passengers) a platform should subsidize. Depending on which side is subsidized, platform competition may increase new car purchases (if drivers are subsidized) or decrease new purchases (if passengers are subsidized).

In this study, we aim to assess the effects of ride-hailing platforms on new car purchases in the presence of platform competition. Our study is relevant to the sharing economy where ride-hailing platforms play a prominent role and intense competition in two-sided markets is not uncommon. We examine a sample of 100 prefecture-level cities<sup>1</sup> in China where Didi Chuxing and Uber China (the two major competing platforms from 2013 to 2015) had gradually entered into a few of the same cities. Using the staggered difference-in-differences (DiD) method, we compare the sales trends in treated cities that introduced the ride-hailing platforms and in control cities without a ride-hailing platform. We also adopted a recent identification approach, synthetic control method (SCM), as a robustness check for our model.

Our findings show that following the entry of ride-hailing platforms, the sales of new cars fell markedly in those treated cities relative to the control cities. Interestingly, platform competition may have mitigated this negative effect. While one plausible explanation for the competition effect is the diminished car demand, we suggest that it is because competing platforms made major efforts to recruit ride-hailing drivers such that more people purchase cars to sign up as drivers. Two results support this explanation. First, the mitigation effect was stronger for cities with higher competition intensity, suggesting that the effect was mainly driven by platform competition. Second, by leveraging brand heterogeneity, our analysis shows that platform competition has increased the sales of budget-friendly brands that are commonly used by ride-hailing drivers, confirming that the increase in new car purchases mainly comes from the driver side. In contrast, the demand for luxury car brands has been largely unaffected by platform competition.

Our results are supported by anecdotal evidence that both Didi Chuxing and Uber have offered subsidies and bonuses to drivers during their market expansions [24]. While competing in the Chinese market, Uber and Didi Chuxing have spent ludicrous amounts on driver subsidies. Uber has reportedly spent US\$1 billion each year in the Chinese market. Didi Chuxing spent even more. The driver subsidy takes various forms. For example, for every order a driver completes they might receive a bonus, which could be one or two times the amount of the passenger’s payment. Once a driver completes a number of orders, there is an additional bonus. Platform bonuses further increase for peak times and for those drivers who receive high customer ratings. While both platforms have also provided subsidies to passengers, the main effort seems to be on the driver side. One important reason is that drivers are viewed by platforms as a more limited resource [50]. Thus, platforms strive to reach critical mass quickly such that they can gain significant new demand driven by positive network feedback [33-35].

Several methods are used in our study to draw causal insights related to the effects of ride-hailing platforms on new car purchases. Our research setting has some unique features, including two companies (and thus two treatments) and differences in the timing of their entries across cities. Thus, we need to use a staggered DiD model to address the issue of different entry timing [17, 46]. In addition, SCM is adopted in our study to provide a more flexible way of estimating the counterfactual outcome of the treated unit and to provides a useful robustness check to our DiD model. SCM is a recent identification strategy that has been used in the economics literature [2] but has not yet been adopted by information systems researchers, to the best of our knowledge. Both methods are used to draw causal inferences by comparing the sales performances of the treatment and control groups. Under the assumption that the two groups resemble each other in all aspects except for the intervention of platform entry, the differences in new car purchases between the two groups can then be attributed to the intervention. A key step of our analysis is to construct the control group. For the staggered DiD, we first used propensity score matching (PSM) to identify those cities that have a similar probability of receiving the treatment for inclusion in the control group. In contrast, the SCM constructs a weighted combination of cities as controls. These approaches have wide applicability for studying the impact of new technologies in the digital economy.

To the best of our knowledge, only a few studies have examined the impact of ride-hailing platforms on incumbent firms. Burtch et al. [8] examine the entry effect of the gig economy (Uber and Postmates) on entrepreneurial activity as measured by campaign launches on Kickstarter. Greenwood and Wattal [26] explore the social benefits of ride-hailing services, such as reducing the alcohol related motor vehicle fatalities. Guo’s et al. [27] study is the only paper published thus far to examine the influence of ride-hailing services on new car purchases.

Our study differs from Guo et al. [27] in two ways. First, while they examined the effects of one ride-hailing platform’s entry, our paper focuses on platform competition. We expand the investigative timeframe from one year to three years to cover the rollout of Didi Chuxing and Uber in various Chinese cities. Thus, we can measure not only the impact of a single platform’s entry, but also the additional effects induced by platform competition. Moreover, by examining the differential effects for luxury and budget-friendly cars, we can explore the mechanisms underlying the competition effects. Second, our longer investigative timeframe allows us to account for the influence of consumer expectations. For those potential ridehailing drivers who planned to register newly-purchased cars through ride-hailing platforms, a deferral option was associated with their car purchase decisions. This option is valuable due to the significant uncertainty surrounding the timing of platform entries, platform registration requirements, platform competition, and the intensity of special dealer promotions immediately following such entries. Thus, a one-year timeframe cannot fully capture the impacts of potential car buyers’ expectations and options thinking in making new car purchase decisions.

## Literature Review

Recent studies have highlighted the increasingly intense competition faced by incumbent firms in several industries connected directly to the sharing economy [7, 14, 23]. Researchers have investigated the consequences of the emerging sharing economy using analytical and empirical methods [47, 48, 51]. It has been demonstrated that popular online two-sided platforms can substitute and complement the goods or services offered by the incumbent firms [15, 37]. As a prominent example of two-sided platforms that are quickly gaining popularity in the sharing economy, ride-hailing apps have recently begun to attract the attention of several researchers [13, 41, 42]. While the implications of the ride-hailing business for controlling pollution, improving traffic congestion, and reducing transportation costs have long been investigated [10, 12, 22], few studies have investigated the implications of ride-hailing apps for new car purchases.

There are numerous antecedents and consequences related to decision-makers’ participation in online two-sided platforms [4, 25, 29]. Thus, the impacts of the emergence of two-sided platforms on demand for the related goods or services are fairly complex [29, 32]. Guo et al. [27] contribute to this stream of research by empirically examining and providing evidence of the positive impact of Didi Chuxing’s entry on new car purchases in dozens of cities across China. They point out that Didi Chuxing’s dominant market position generates strong crossside network effects that offer individuals strong incentives to buy new cars and register as drivers on its platform. This result is consistent with prior views suggesting that once a large network reaches critical mass it can rapidly generate significant new demand because of positive network feedback [33-35] .

The extant literature on two-sided platforms has suggested that platform competition is usually very intense and that prolonged platform subsidies are fairly common [39, 43]. Practices seem to vary across industries. For example, video game companies often charge game developers and subsidize the gamers as loss leaders, while operating system platforms do the opposite by charging consumers (see [43]). It is suggested that platforms should subsidize the more price-sensitive side and charge the side whose demand increases more strongly in response to growth on the other side [20]. However, answers for the ride-hailing market are less clear-cut. Both sides of this market are individual consumers, and factors such as price sensitivity are not immediately apparent. While the sharing economy is an example of a two-sided market, the traditional research on twosided markets may not apply to sharing platforms due to assumptions made about the forms of utility and interaction across the markets [21]. The firm’s choice of which side to subsidize has different implications for new car purchases, which is the focus of our study.

Furthermore, previous information systems (IS) studies have highlighted the role of individuals’ expectations in influencing their decision-making [5, 34]. Since carsharing has long been considered a greener alternative to private car ownership, many potential car buyers’ expectations may be affected by mechanisms such as herding or informational cascade [19, 35]. A key insight from the real options literature is that the deferral option is invaluable when there is significant uncertainty about a new technology [34, 36]. In the face of significant uncertainty surrounding the ride-hailing business model, many potential car buyers may defer their purchases to allow this uncertainty to be resolved over time, which also suggests that the investigative timeframe for ride-hailing platforms’ entries needs to be expanded.

## Data and Research Design

Our research context is based on the rollout of two leading ride-hailing platforms in China: Didi Chuxing and Uber. China’s sharing economy is nascent but already impressive in scope, offering opportunities for unique natural or quasi-experiments that enable researchers to derive valuable causal insights for the digital economy [40]. During the three-year period from 2013 to 2015, we observed 50 prefecture-level cities offering ride-hailing services, including 32 cities where only Didi Chuxing entered and 18 cities where both Didi Chuxing and Uber entered. Based on this timeframe, we are able to investigate not only the entry effect of Didi Chuxing but also the coexistence (or competition) effect when Didi Chuxing and Uber both operate in the same city.

There are some empirical challenges to establishing a causal link between platform entry and new car purchases in our context. First, endogeneity could arise because of a platform’s selection bias. It is possible that city-specific characteristics may jointly determine platforms’ decisions to enter a city and new car purchases. To address this issue, our model has included many city-specific characteristics as well as city-specific fixed effects and time-specific fixed effects. Time variant city-specific effects are approximated with online search data that reflect market demand. We employ the PSM method to construct a comparable control group. As a robustness check, we also use the SCM method that provides a more flexible way of constructing the control group. Second, the setting for a standard DiD analysis is that all cities receive one treatment at the same time. However, in our case there are two platforms, and entry occurred in different cities at various times. Thus, we employ a variant of DiD analysis called staggered DiD analysis. We describe the PSM method below and explain the details of staggered DiD in the next section.

The purpose of the PSM method is to select treatment and control group cities that are similar to each other in all the relevant characteristics except that the treatment group cities have ride-hailing platforms and the control group cities do not. Therefore, the differences in new car purchases between the two groups can be attributed to the platform entry. By ensuring the comparability of the treated group and the untreated group, the PSM method helps mitigate the endogeneity concern [31]. We considered the 50 prefecture-level cities with the ride-hailing services to be the treated group. To construct the untreated (control) group, we identified 50 prefecture-level cities that had a similar probability (or propensity score) of becoming treated cities (although they did not become treated cities in the period under investigation).

To calculate the propensity score, we included factors that reflect the demographic, social, and economic status of a city because these factors might influence the entry decisions of ride-hailing platforms. Specifically, we included population, average wage, and the number of registered unemployed, since they reflect the economic status of each city. The numbers of mobile phone subscribers and Internet subscribers may influence the accessibility of the ride-hailing apps. We also include factors such as highway passenger traffic, the number of buses and taxis, and the annual volume of passengers transported by buses, each of which can influence the use of ride-hailing as an alternative mode of transportation. We then conduct the propensity score analysis using a logistic model. Based on the propensity scores of the cities, we employ the nearest-neighbor matching algorithm with replacement and caliper matching (0.05). The theory behind matching is that the more treated and untreated cities are similar (based on the observed characteristics), the less likely they are to differ in unobserved ways.

Our final dataset is national monthly panel data for the 50 treatment cities and 50 control cities from January 2013 to December 2015. The number of new car purchases for each city is based on the number of new car registration plates purchased from the vehicle administrative office in China. The entry month of the platform in each city was manually collected from news releases made publicly available on the Internet. We then created two dummies indicating whether Didi Chuxing or Uber had been introduced in a city in a given month. Data on the social and economic variables were obtained from the China City Statistical Yearbook, which is an annual official statistical report that summarizes key indicators of economic and social development in China. All variables are available at the national and local level (province, autonomous region, and municipality under the direct control of the central government).

In addition, to control for other time-variant events at the city level that could influence their respective car sales, we collected Baidu Index numbers that reflect market demand. Baidu is by far the most popular search engine in China. The Baidu Index is similar to Google Trends, which has been widely used in the literature to predict demand in various industries, including the automobile market [18]. Like Google Trends, the Baidu Index tracks the queries that consumers type into Baidu’s search box and generates volume indices by geography (e.g., city) and by time (e.g., month). A larger Baidu Index number reflects greater consumer interest in relation to that keyword. We manually obtained the Baidu Index data for two keywords related to new car purchases: “Xin Che” (meaning “new car”) and “Mai Che” (meaning “buy a car”). We collected data on these Baidu Index trends by month and by city.

Table 1 provides descriptive statistics of the variables used in our analyses. The dependent variable — the number of new car registration plates — is a count variable, and thus we log-transformed it to approximate more closely the assumption of normally-distributed residuals. We also log-transformed the independent variables, as they exhibit skewed distributions.

Note that our study focuses on the new car market and does not consider the used car market. In China, ride-hailing platforms usually have car age requirements. For example, Didi Chuxing requires that the cars used on its platform are less than six years old.<sup>2</sup> Given that the supply of used cars within six years is limited, only a small percentage of drivers would buy used cars to join the ride-hailing platforms. In other markets where older cars can easily be registered to offer ride-hailing services, it would be important to account for the impact of entry on the used car market. In those contexts, because ride-hailing drivers could also purchase used cars to register on ride-hailing platforms, considering new car purchases alone would not reflect the true demand from drivers.

## Econometric Specification and Results

## Effect of a Single Ride-Hailing Platform’s Entry

This will be our baseline model and we will build on it with an extended form to address twofirm competitive entries. The effect of a single platform’s entry has been examined by Guo et al. [27] using a shorter timeframe (one year) and a slightly different sample of cities. We demonstrate that a longer investigative timeframe can lead to different results since factors such as consumer expectations can be better captured.

We adopt a variant DiD model because of the different timing of the platform entry for each city. The standard DiD setting includes a treatment group (e.g., cities with Uber entry) and a control group (e.g., cities without Uber entry), where the intervention occurs for all the units in the treatment group at the same time. Then, the standard DiD analysis relies primarily on two variables: the treatment dummy (with or without entry), the time dummy (pre-entry vs. post-entry), and their interaction (treatment × time), or the DiD variable. In this paper, the situation is different because units in the treatment group receive the treatment at different points in time. To deal with that situation, previous studies [17, 46] have proposed a staggered DiD model that uses two-way fixed effects:

$$
\ln \left(\text { NewCarSale } _ {c t}\right) = \theta_ {0} + \theta_ {1} \text { DidiEntry } _ {c t} + \text { City } _ {c} + \text { YearMonth } _ {t} + Z _ {c t} + e _ {c t},\tag{1}
$$

where c represents city and t refers to time (from January 2013 to December 2015). $N e w C a r S a l e _ { c t }$ is the number of new car registration plates for city c at time $t . D i d i E n t r y _ { c t }$ is a binary variable that indicates the entry of Didi Chuxing, where $D i d i E n t r y _ { c t } { = } 1$ if Didi Chuxing was available in city c at time $t ,$ otherwise $D i d i E n t r y _ { c t } { = } 0$ . Thus, the coefficient $\theta _ { 1 }$ is the DiD estimator representing the impact of Didi Chuxing’s entry on new car sales; $C i t y _ { c }$ is a vector capturing city-level fixed effects; and YearMonth<sub>t</sub> is a vector capturing time-wise fixed effects. Here, the two-way fixed effects $C i t y _ { c }$ and YearMonth<sub>t</sub> are similar to the time-wise and treatment variables in a standard DiD model. $D i d i E n t r y _ { c t }$ is equivalent to the interaction term Treatment × Time in the standard DiD model.

Furthermore, $Z _ { c t }$ is a vector capturing city demographical features and socioeconomic indicators, such as average wage, unemployment, number of mobile phone subscribers, and numbers of buses and taxis. It also includes online search volume that reflects timevariant events at the city level. Finally, $e _ { c t }$ is the stochastic error term.

In Table 2, we report the results for the baseline model (Column 1) and the model that adds search trends (Column 2). The results consistently show that the entry of Didi Chuxing has a significant negative impact on new car purchases (e.g., −0.153 in Column 2).

Note that a ride-hailing service could have both negative and positive effects on new car purchases. On the one hand, ride-hailing platforms can provide consumers with an alternative mode of transportation, thus reducing their incentive to buy new cars. On the other hand, ride-hailing platforms can provide drivers with new job opportunities, thereby encouraging them to buy new cars in order to offer the ridehailing service. The negative effect of Didi Chuxing’s entry implies that the passenger-side effect dominates the driver-side effect in this case.

The other parameters in the model generally exhibit the expected signs. For example, the registered number of unemployed persons is negatively correlated with new car purchases, while the average wage is positively correlated with new car purchases. The effects of search trends on car sales are significantly positive.

Our results provide an interesting contrast to the previous findings. Guo et al. [27] found a positive effect on new car sales, but this paper identifies a negative impact. One reason why the inclusion of two more years of data yields the opposite results is due to the consumer expectation effect. Guo et al.’s [27] data contain a short time period (i.e., half a year) before the entry of the platform. If the news was publicly announced earlier and consumer expectations led to a faster reaction (which we will demonstrate using a relative time model later), one year of data was not sufficient to capture the entry effect. Furthermore, Guo et al. [27] examined 50 cities that Didi Chuxing entered during their one-year study period (2015), but Uber also entered some of those cities during the same period and this produced a confounding effect. To e remove the effect of Uber, in this analysis we consider just 32 cities where only Didi Chuxing entered during the period under investigation, plus the cities in the control group.

We extend our analysis for the impact on new car purchases when two ride-hailing platforms (Uber and Didi Chuxing) compete in the same cities. Our analysis includes all 50 treated cities and the 50 untreated cities. We modified Equation 1 as follows:

## Explaining the Competition Effect

## Analysis 1: The Effect of Competition Intensity

We interpret the coefficient of DidiUberEntr $\rangle _ { c t }$ as the competition effect. One alternative explanation is the economic principle of diminishing marginal value. The entry of the first ride-hailing platform provides substantial value to consumers as they now have the option to replace car ownership with a ride-hailing service. The marginal value provided by the entry of the second ride-hailing platform is much lower as it only marginally increases the convenience of a ride-hailing service. Therefore, the positive effect of platform coexistence may not be due to competition.

To exclude this alternative explanation, we examine whether the platform coexistence effect varies for competition intensity in the ride-hailing market. The idea is that if the platform coexistence effect is driven by competition, we should see a stronger effect in the cities with higher competition intensity. To measure the competition intensity of a city, it would be ideal to obtain the market share of the two ride-hailing platforms. However, since data on the number of users of each platform is not available, we use the online search volume for each platform as a proxy of their market shares. We obtained the Baidu Index results for the key words “Didi” and “Uber” (in both English and Chinese) to capture consumer interest in the two platforms, respectively, and to approximate the demand for each platform. Then, we computed the Herfindahl– Hirschman index (HHI), a common measure of market concentration, for each city for the time period when the two platforms entered. We then divided all the cities into quartiles based on their competition intensity. For instance, the first quartile (Q1) includes cities with the lowest market concentration (or highest competition), while the fourth quartile (Q4) includes cities with the highest market concentration (or lowest competition). We then examined the differential effects of competition for the quartiles.

The results are reported in Table 4. Interestingly, the positive effects of platform coexistence was stronger as the competition intensity increases (betas = 0.171, 0.173, and 0.252) and became insignificant for cities in the last quartile (the lowest competition) (beta = −0.008). This implies that the coexistence effect occurred only if the competition in ride-hailing services was high in the city but not if the city was not competitive enough.

## Analysis 2: Which Side of the Market to Subsidize?

A further question to consider is why platform competition had a positive impact on new car purchases? Given that the ride-hailing market is two-sided, competing platforms could incentivize either the passenger side or the driver side — or both. This strategy leads to opposite predictions for new car purchases, since the incentive for drivers will encourage them to purchase cars, whereas the incentive for passengers will discourage them from buying new cars. Our finding of a positive competition effect suggests that the platforms provided subsidies mainly to drivers. In fact, anecdotal evidence shows that Didi Chuxing and Uber have invested hundreds of millions of dollars in subsidies to undercut each other’s prices [12]. As a result, many people have purchased new cars that enable them to work as ride-hailing drivers.

To confirm the aforementioned conjecture, we ran separate analyses for car brands that vary in popularity among ride-hailing drivers. If the competition effect is primarily determined by the driver side, we should observe a stronger effect for the brands that were more popular among ride-hailing drivers and a weaker effect for the brands that were not typically used to offer ride-hailing services. We identified four luxury brands — Audi, BMW, Mercedes-Benz, and Buick — all of which were largely for personal use and seldom used to provide ride-hailing services. We also identified three popular brands, commonly used to provide ride-hailing services — Kia, Great Wall, and BYD — all of which were budget-friendly and widely used by ride-hailing drivers.<sup>3</sup> We reanalyzed the competition model (Equation 2) for these two groups of brands.

Table 5 (Column 1) shows the estimates for the luxury brand group.

Contrary to our previous results, platform competition had no effect on the sales of these brands. In addition, the entry of either Didi Chuxing or Uber had no effect on these brands’ sales, perhaps because consumers who bought these luxury brands do not usually consider ride-hailing services as a substitute for their luxury cars. Table 5 (Column 2) shows the estimates for the budget-friendly brand group. These results are consistent with those reported in Table 3 (for all car brands). Interestingly, the magnitude of the competition effect (beta = 0.263) exceeded that of Didi Chuxing’s entry (beta = −0.047) and Uber’s entry (beta = −0.112), indicating that intense competition between the two ride-hailing platforms can meaningfully boost the sales of budget-friendly automotive brands. These empirical results are consistent with our conjecture that the two platforms may have made great efforts (e.g., through subsidies) to quickly expand their networks of ride-hailing drivers in response to intense competition driven by network effects.

Another alternative explanation for the dual entry effect is “multi-homing” (or consumers register more than one platform at the same time). From the perspective of consumers, the two ride-hailing apps are complementary because they offer more channels to reach passengers that increase their productivity. From the perspective of platforms, multi-homing could have intensified the competition. Because consumers could easily switch between the apps, platforms needed to use more aggressive pricing strategies to attract drivers. For example, Didi Chuxing rewarded drivers with an extra 100 RMB after they completed five orders.<sup>4</sup> This type of practice is very common among ride-hailing platforms. To fulfil the minimum number of orders to obtain the subsidies offered, drivers have less incentive to register with multiple ride-hailing platforms at the same time. Moreover, recall that the dual entry effect increased as competition intensity increased (Table 4), suggesting that the dual entry effect was related to competition. If it were only the complementary effect due to the dual channel, we should have observed the same effect across cities regardless of their competition intensities, because they all had two platforms. In other words, the complementary effect from the consumer perspective was very likely but there must have been an additional effect due to competition.

## Consumer Expectations

We have examined the impacts on new car purchases after the ride-hailing apps launched in the cities under examination. However, because news about the entry of a ride-hailing platform is announced to the public a few months in advance, consumers might have formed expectations and acted accordingly before the actual entry. Therefore, we examined the lead-lag effect of ride-hailing apps using a relative time model, as suggested by Autor [6]. Relative time models have been used in the literature to examine the lead-lag effect [11, 26].

We first modified the no-competition model (Equation 1) by adding time dummy variables $D i d i E n t r y _ { c t } ^ { j }$ for up to eight months before Didi Chuxing’s entry and for four months after Didi’s entry, where j represents $- 8 , - 7 , . . . , 3 , 4 .$ , and onward, indicating whether month t was the $j ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ month since Didi Chuxing became available in city c. To simplify the estimation, we used “4 and onward” to capture the overall effect in Month 4 and all the months afterwards. We included a slightly longer period before Didi Chuxing’s entry in case the effects started earlier. For example, while the news was often announced half a year before actual entry, consumers may have developed expectations based on observations in neighboring cities and thus acted sooner. The number of months included in the lead-lag effect will have a minimal effect on our conclusions. Our baseline period includes about two years before Didi Chuxing entered most cities in China, which is sufficiently long. Thus, including a few more months allows us to see when the effects started to take off. The relative time model is shown as follows:

$$
l n (N e w C a r S a l e _ {c t}) = \mu_ {0} + \Sigma_ {j} \mu_ {j} D i d i E n t r y _ {c t} ^ {j} + C i t y _ {c} + Y e a r M o n t h _ {t} + Z _ {c t} + v _ {c t}\tag{3}

Specifically, we can see a significantly negative impact on new car purchases for up to five months prior to Didi Chuxing’s entry and the effect remained until the end of our observation period (December 2015). This finding offers an interesting contrast with the work of Guo et al. [27], which found that Didi Chuxing’s entry led to a positive effect on new car purchases. Our results suggest that in the presence of consumer expectation it was important to include in the analysis a sufficiently long period of time before the entry.

To understand these results, it is worth noting that Uber was the first ride-hailing platform entering the Chinese market. Therefore, Chinese consumers needed some time to begin to understand this new business model and to form expectations about the usefulness of car-sharing. This learning effect may explain why, unlike Didi Chuxing, Uber only had an impact on new car purchases for a few months after its official entry. By contrast, Didi Chuxing entered most of the cities in which it operates in early 2015, by which time consumers had already become familiar with the ride-hailing business model and could thus take actions in advance by postponing or cancelling new car purchases. As a result of consumers’ expectations that the entry of a major ride-hailing platform such as Didi Chuxing could make car sharing more cost-effective and convenient, the negative impacts on new car purchases took place a few months before Didi Chuxing’s official entry month.

As discussed previously, to quickly expand their networks of drivers, the two competing platforms incentivized potential ride-hailing drivers to purchase new cars. Since a new entrant wants to gain market share and an incumbent wants to deter the entrant, these promotional efforts often occur a few months before and after the second platform’s entry, suggesting a strong competition effect during this window of time (consistent with the results of our relative time model). It also is worth noting that the competition effect may be further strengthened by the options thinking of potential platform drivers. Facing significant uncertainty about the ridehailing business model and platform competition (from a new entrant), many potential platform drivers may have retained their deferral options to wait for the market uncertainty to be resolved. Therefore, if they could foresee the entry of a major competing platform in the near future, many of them might have postponed getting new cars long before the competing platform’s official entry. Consequently, the accumulated demand from those potential platform drivers could have further strengthened the competition effect in the short run.

## Robustness Checks

## Synthetic Control Method (SCM)

Our main analyses are based on the DiD design. To check the robustness of our results, we used SCM, a recently developed empirical methodology [1-3] to assess the effects of ride-hailing apps. Similar to a DiD design, SCM exploits the differences in treated units and untreated units across the event of interest. However, SCM does not give all untreated units the same weight. Rather, SCM creates a weighted average of the untreated units (or synthetic counterpart) that resembles the treated unit over the pre-treatment period (i.e., the construction of a counterfactual). In our case, this synthetic counterpart is an objectively-weighted combination of the 50 cities without any ride-hailing apps. The weights were calibrated so that the synthetic counterpart mimics the treated cities before any ridehailing apps were introduced. By comparing the actual sales of new cars in the treated cities to that of the synthetic counterpart after the introduction of ridehailing apps, we were able to estimate the magnitude and direction of the treatment effects.

We applied SCM to assess the competition effect of the two rival platforms. Figure 1 shows the new car purchases for the cities with ride-hailing apps (solid line) and their synthetic counterpart (dotted line) around the time of competition. The x-axis indicates the month relative to the platform competition time, thus time = 0 indicates when the two ride-hailing platforms started to compete. The y-axis indicates the log form of new car purchases. The new car purchases of the two groups were parallel to each other before the any platform entries. Then, the sales of new cars declined in treated cities relative to the synthetic counterpart after one platform entered the market, but the sales difference between the two groups diminished after two platforms started to compete. This pattern is consistent with our findings that platform competition mitigates the negative effect of ride-hailing service on new car purchases.

## Reverse Causality

One source of endogeneity is reverse causality: the dependent variable may influence the key explanatory variables. To address this concern, we performed an analysis to examine whether our dependent variable (new car purchases) and control variables significantly influenced the likelihood of Didi Chuxing’s entry and Uber’s entry into a city. We estimated a discrete time (logit) hazard model [8, 45]. In this model, the units of observation are city and month, and the dependent variable is a dummy indicating whether Didi Chuxing (Uber) had entered the city. For each city, we used observations before the entry and entry month and discarded observations after the entry month. The results are shown in Table 9. Most importantly, the dependent variable (new car sales) did not significantly influence either Didi Chuxing’s or Uber’s entry decision.

## Serial Correlation

In the static panel data, we controlled for serial correlation by clustering the standard errors at the city level [49]. This approach is most effective when the panel data involve large N and small T. There are 36 months and 100 cities in our data. One might consider that the number of time periods is not much smaller than the number of cross-sections, and there may still be serial correlation issues in the models. As a robustness check, we reran our analysis using only 24 months from January 2014 to December 2015. Because platform entry occurred in most cities between August 2014 and June 2015, this shortened period still allowed us to account for the effects of consumer expectation while reducing the concern of serial correlation. The results are consistent with the previous findings, as shown in Table 10.

## Discussion and Conclusion

The rollout of two ride-hailing platforms across Chinese cities from 2013 to 2015 provided us with a unique opportunity to examine the effects of ride-hailing services in the presence of platform competition. Our study offers empirical evidence showing that the entry of a single ride-hailing platform negatively impacted new car purchases. More interestingly, our analysis suggests that intensified competition resulting from the entry of two major competing platforms was able to alleviate the negative impact on new car purchases in the short run. In other words, while car sharing may disincentivize passengers’ purchases of new cars and promote more sustainable travel behavior, platform competition in the market was able to boost new car demand and consequently alleviate the decline in new car purchases driven by platform entries.

Our study contributes to the growing body of literature on the interactions between traditional industries and digital start-ups in the sharing economy [15, 28, 29]. While previous studies have shown that ride-hailing services have created disruption for incumbent firms in several industries, few studies have focused on the implications of ride-hailing platform competition. Our research is the first study to empirically document the impacts of ride-hailing platform competition on new car purchases. There are several alternative explanations to the dual entry effect, such as the diminished demand for cars, and the complementary effects due to multi-homing. However, we have shown that the dual entry effect varied for markets with different competition intensities, suggesting that it was mainly driven by platform competition. Exploring the heterogeneity of brands, our analysis also has shown that the dual entry effect predominately influenced the sales of those brands that were popular among ride-hailing drivers, implying that platform subsidies were mainly distributed to the driver side of the market.

The findings of our research have important theoretical implications. The previous literature discusses pricing strategies and subsidy allocation for two-sided markets. Because both sides of the market prefer a larger size for the other side, platforms commonly use subsidies to encourage participation and to intensify the positive network feedback they generate. Typical examples of two-sided markets analyzed in previous studies are video game companies and operating system platforms. Factors such as price-sensitivity and demand elasticity have been suggested to determine a firm’s choice of which side to subsidize [20]. Ride-hailing platforms are an example of two-sided markets with some unique features. For example, both sides consist of individual consumers, and therefore, the two sides are not completely separated. The decision-makers’ utility functions are also distinct because there is a tradeoff between the benefits of sharing and the benefits of personal usage [21]. Therefore, it is unclear whether some of the previous findings based on traditional two-sided markets can be generalized to ride-hailing platforms. Therefore, our study contributes to the literature by providing empirical evidence from this emerging and distinct two-sided market.

Our results also demonstrate the importance of accounting for consumer expectations in the presence of strong network effects. The negative impact of Didi Chuxing’s entry on new car purchases occurred as early as five months prior to its official entry month. We have pointed out that this early impact was likely driven by consumer expectations, and those people who retained their valuable options by deferring their car purchase decisions. On the one hand, our results suggest that consumers’ expectations about future platform entries and their real options thinking may have negatively influenced their new car purchases several months before the official entry month. On the other hand, because of the fierce competition driven by cross-side network effects, the platforms’ promotional efforts and other incentives also may have significantly boosted new car purchases to mitigate the negative impact — at least in the short run. These observations are consistent with insights from previous studies demonstrating that expectations, learning, competition, and uncertainty play important roles in markets subject to strong network effects [19, 34].

This research has several limitations. First, a key assumption in our analysis is that the entry timing of ride-hailing platforms was exogenous with respect to new car purchases. It is important to note that the entry decisions of Didi Chuxing and Uber were not random. While we have employed several methods to alleviate such concerns, these steps may not have completely resolved the issue of endogeneity. Second, since the existing data on new car purchases are currently aggregated at the city level, we were not able to distinguish between new car purchases made by passengers for private use and new car purchases made by drivers for ride-hailing services. Thus, obtaining more detailed data, such as the number of drivers who registered with the platforms and the magnitude of the subsidies provided to them, will allow future researchers to gain a deeper understanding of the competition effect. Third, we note that the magnitude of some of the effects observed in our study was large. These results are based on 100 prefecture-level cities in China that are relatively large in size. In addition, the three-year data period is fairly short and, as a result, the observed effects are likely transitory.

To conclude, this research assessed the impact of ride-hailing platforms’ market entry on new car purchases in the presence of platform competition. We analyzed the data on new car purchases made during the rollout of Didi Chuxing and Uber across selected cities in China. The findings show that while the entry of a single ride-hailing platform led to a decline in new car purchases, platform competition mitigated this negative impact in the short run. Our research contributes to the literature on pricing strategies and subsidy allocation for two-sided markets by providing empirical evidence from the ride-hailing market. It also suggests that companies’ competitive strategies need to account for consumer expectations and learning in the presence of strong network effects.
