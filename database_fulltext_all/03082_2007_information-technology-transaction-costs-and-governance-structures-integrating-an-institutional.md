# Information technology, transaction costs and governance structures: integrating an institutional approach

## Abstract

Transaction cost concepts have been deployed in information systems to analyze the impact of information technology on the organization of economic activity in markets and hierarchies. It has been widely observed that there is a general trend toward markets. It has also been observed that the transaction cost approach cannot explain empirica observations where the choice of governance has more to do with power and behavioral attributes of transacting actors than with minimizing transaction costs. Information systems researchers have overcome the shortcomings of the transaction cost approach to a limited extent by complementing it with political economy, resource-dependency and network theories. However, these complementary perspectives cannot easily analyze the interactions between power and efficiency and cannot handle the impact of the institutional environment on the choice of governance structure. This research exposes the shortcomings of these complementary perspectives in the light of a range of institutionalist studies drawn from economics, sociology and anthropology. The research points out that an institutional approach is essential for understanding and overcoming the complications that may arise as IT-enabled moves toward markets are launched in organizations that are situated in institutional environments that are at present not compatible with market-oriented exchange arrangements. We demonstrate this by the application of three ‘tools’ to an exemplary case where information systems in the British National Health Service were intended as a tool to move one aspect of the hierarchical structure toward a market structure.

## Transaction costs and information systems

rom the early 1980s, many researchers have recognized that transaction cost economics could inform a number of information systems issues that involved exchange of goods and services within or across the boundaries of the firm. These issues ranged from systems analysis and inter-organizational relationships to the ITenabled transformation of firms and public administration (Ciborra, 1981, 1983). From late 1980s onwards, information systems researchers intensified their efforts to uncover the impact of information technology on the organization of markets and hierarchies. Ciborra (1987) pointed out that information technology would reduce transaction costs, thereby enabling the emergence of more efficiently organized markets and hierarchies. Malone et al. (1987) predicted that increasing adoption of IT would not only enable greater use of electronic markets and hierarchies but would also lead to an overall move toward markets. Clemons et al. (1993) argued that the lower relationship specificity of IT investments and the lower cost and better monitoring capability of IT would create a move toward greater outsourcing but this move would be a ‘move towards the middle,’ that is, more outsourcing, but from a reduced set of stable partnerships.

Gurbaxani and Whang (1991: 72) used transaction cost concepts to show that IT could result in an increase or decrease in either the horizontal or vertical dimension of firm size. They pointed out that the impact of IT would vary from one firm to another depending on business functions, market conditions and industry characteristics. Others applied transaction cost concepts involving asset specificity and information asymmetry to analyze the various risks that arise as a result of outsourcing IT operations (Loh and Venkatraman, 1992; McFarlan and Nolan, 1995; Earl, 1996; Lacity et al., 1996). Bakos and Brynjolfsson (1993) pointed out that reductions in the number of suppliers was not just due to developments in information technology but also due to an increasing demand for quality and the consequent need for manufacturers to relinquish part of their power in order to create an incentive for the suppliers to invest in the relationships. Holland and Lockett (1994) proposed a ‘mixed mode’ argument where firms simultaneously adopted different forms of market and hierarchical relationships with different partners depending on market complexity and business strategy.

By early 1990s, the trend toward greater outsourcing and reduction of suppliers had greatly increased the strategic importance of inter-organizational information systems involving electronic data interchange and electronic markets (Johnston and Vitale, 1988; Rockart and Short, 1989). Such systems had been widely adopted in various industries including airlines, health care, the automotive industry, banking and retailing (Chicktong, 1991; Short and Venkatraman, 1992; Cavaye, 1995; Webster, 1995; Reekers and Smithson, 1996). Transaction cost concepts involving information asymmetry, asset specificity and the construction of bilateral resource dependencies were deployed to analyze the interactions between firms using electronic data interchange systems and between buyers and sellers in electronic markets. Bakos (1991) argued that by reducing search costs information technology would allow buyers to search more extensively, reduce sellers’ ability to obtain monopolistic profits, increase competition and lower prices. Zaheer and Venkatraman (1994) found that the IT-induced asset specificity has a significant effect on the degree of electronic integration in the US property and casualty insurance industry.

Transaction cost concepts were also deployed to analyze the impact of information technology on the organizational structures of firms and public organizations. Applegate (1994) showed that information technology enhanced the efficiency of hierarchies by ‘flattening the information curve,’ that is, by simultaneously facilitating downward delegation of decisions and the monitoring by top management of the quality of decision making at lower levels. Ciborra (1987) observed that advances in information technology would enable firms and public organizations to improve their efficiency by deploying the hierarchical, market and clan structures in more effective combinations. He also pointed out that information technology could enhance the effectiveness and quality of working life in teams as well as increase the transparency of markets and the competitiveness of business hierarchies (Ciborra (1993) and in a modified form in Ciborra (2002)). In public administration, transaction cost concepts were deployed to analyze the feasibility of delivering public services such as health care, education and social services through quasimarkets (Le Grand and Bartlett, 1993).

## Limitations of the transaction cost approach

Researchers who used transaction cost concepts to predict the impact of information technology on the organization of markets and hierarchies frequently observed that the transaction cost approach could not take into account various factors involving power and behavioral attributes of transacting actors. Bakos (1991: 303) pointed out that information technology may not lead to the creation of more efficient markets in cases where there are small number of sellers and large numbers of buyers, and the sellers distort the market in their favor by emphasizing product rather than price information. This implied that in industries where markets were not sufficiently competitive, transactions might have been aligned with the governance structure that suited the more powerful transacting side rather than with the governance that minimized transaction costs incurred by the two sides for that transaction. Behavioral issues involving consensus and cooperation were particularly significant in inter-organizational relationships (Johnston and Vitale, 1988; Clemons and Row, 1992; Meier, 1995). This was especially so in cases where inter-organizational information systems enabled the dominant firm to intensify its control of its subcontractors and satellite firms (Hollands, 1991; Mackay, 1992; Webster, 1995; Reekers and Smithson, 1996). In some cases, it was the suppliers who gained from the asymmetrical dependence of the clients on them (Korczynski, 1994).

Transaction cost economics provided little guidance on power and behavioral issues. Power was considered a transient process that was more appropriate for political science/organizational science to study than for transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1993, 1996). It was assumed that power was largely irrelevant for transaction cost economics since efficiency considerations would dominate the choice of governance structure in the long run, that is, over a decade (Francis et al., 1983: 7). Such an assumption, however, could not always be deployed in information systems research on transactions where the impact of information technology often had to be analyzed over 1-year or 2-year periods and in environments where competitive pressures were not sufficiently large to eliminate power (Ghoshal and Moran, 1996).

With respect to behavioral issues, Williamson acknowledged that trust reduces transaction costs yet did not elaborate on the processes with which trust develops, delegating these to sociology (Williamson, 1993). After all, he was primarily concerned with explaining why different goods and services were exchanged using different governance structures. Analyzing why individuals who differed in their behavioral attributes exchanged the same good or service using different governance structures was not as high a research priority in transaction cost economics as it was in information systems.

Information systems researchers overcame the shortcomings of the transaction cost approach to a limited extent by importing various theories and concepts from other disciplines. Bensaou and Venkatraman (1996: 85) argued that a political economy perspective can contribute to the analysis of exchange relationships by highlighting that governance structures are embedded within the sociopolitical characteristics of the exchange relationships, which involve power and control as well as cooperation. Reekers and Smithson (1996) relied on resource dependence and network perspectives to analyze issues involving power and the relationships of transacting actors with third parties. Resource dependence theory was used to analyze empirical observations where a firm tried to minimize their dependence on other firms and to maximize the dependence of other firms on itself. The network perspective was deployed to analyze empirical observations where the exchange behavior of transacting parties with each other were influenced by the relationships transacting parties had with other actors. Behavioral programs involving trust and cooperation were also deployed to explain transactional behavior (Clemons and Row, 1992; Webster, 1995).

## Shortcomings of contemporary IS research on transactions

The manner in which information systems researchers deployed multiple perspectives on transaction issues had a number of drawbacks. First, the researchers could not integrate power and efficiency considerations by viewing the monetary benefits and losses associated with an increase or a decrease in power as reductions or increases in transaction costs. The standard transaction cost definition was not broad enough to enable them to do that. On the one hand, they deployed resource dependence theory to explain empirical observations involving power and on the other, they relied on Williamson’s theory to account for exchange behavior that resulted from efficiency considerations. This meant that in cases where governance alignments were influenced simultaneously by both power and efficiency considerations, the relative significance of power and efficiency considerations in determining the alignment outcome could not be compared beyond highly contextual qualitative terms.

In addition, the information systems researchers who deployed network perspectives or focused on the behavioral attributes of transacting actors did so without taking into account the institutional environment in which such network relationships and behavioral attributes are embedded. This is a significant obstacle for making generalizations from such studies because the governance structure that is appropriate for a transaction in one institutional environment may not be as appropriate or feasible in another environment. Numerous studies in economics and sociology have pointed out the various ways in which institutions in the environment influence governance alignments by shaping and constraining the opportunities, incentives and motivations of the actors (Granovetter, 1985; North, 1990). Many transactions that are carried out within firms in Western societies take place within networks of firms in Asian societies (Biggart and Hamilton, 1992). Significant governance variations are also observed within Western countries such as the United

States, Germany and Sweden and within Asian countries such as Japan, South Korea and Taiwan (Aoki, 1990; Biggart, 1991; Sako, 1992; Orru, 1993; Hamilton and Feenstra, 1995).

These governance variations are partly due to differences in institutional factors involving family structures and ethnic networks (Steers et al., 1989; Granovetter, 1995). Nationally distinct social institutions that regulate individualism, trust between non-kin and authority have a significant impact on the organization of economic activity (Whitley, 1992; Dijck and Groenewegen, 1994; Hill, 1995; Lane, 1996). This impact is particularly pronounced in environments where the absence of wellfunctioning legal systems leads transacting actors to rely on reputation and on kinship and ethnicity (Zucker, 1986: 54, 62). In these environments, actors prefer longer term, closer business relationships with smaller number of partners (Rademakers, 1998).

The security of private property rights also influences governance structures (North, 1990). In many third world and former Soviet countries, property rights are not secure and legal systems are not effective. The powers of the state are abused by the incumbent politicians and their businessmen allies to extract rents from other business groups (Evans, 1989; Granovetter, 1995: 122). In such environments, businessmen buffer themselves against the economic and political uncertainties by avoiding highly asset-specific investments. They also establish longer term, more hierarchical relationships with smaller number of trusted partners and customers in order to adapt more easily to rapid changes in economic and political circumstances.

Institutions’ influence on behavior is greater in organizations that conform to rules and regulations to receive support and legitimacy than in organizations whose survival is determined by how efficiently they compete in markets (Scott and Meyer, 1994). For example, the activities of schools, hospitals and public utility companies are guided by institutions to a greater extent than the operations of manufacturing firms, restaurants or health clubs. The implication for governance structures is that organizations where institutions exert significant influence on operations may choose a particular governance structure as a result of ‘institutional isomorphism,’ that is, they may adopt practices that become standard in their sector so as not to appear backward or out-of-touch (Di Maggio and Powell, 1983; Fligstein, 1990).

Another way in which institutions influence the governance choices of actors is by shaping their cognitive and computational processes (Douglas, 1986; North, 1990). Behavioral programs, which underlie many key behavioral attributes, take the form of habits, heuristics, values and cognitive frames. These programs help actors to come to grips with the complexities of the real world within the narrow confines of their bounded rationality and limited information processing capabilities (Simon, 1984). Some behavioral programs are so deeply ingrained that individuals do not even realize that institutions do their ‘thinking’ on their behalf (Douglas, 1986). Behavioral programs influence the governance choices of transacting actors by shaping their behavior on issues such as trust, opportunism and solidarity. These programs are constructed partly as a result of experience and partly acquired through socialization in family and at work. Both the history of economic relationships and the national, occupational and organizational cultures exert equally important influence on the formation of values and ideologies (North, 1990; Ouchi, 1993; Trice, 1993). Internalization of performance norms is an integral part of the education of professionals such as doctors and teachers whose performance cannot be easily measured (Coleman, 1990).

In addition to influencing governance choices, the behavioral attributes of actors also influence the evolution of institutions in the transactional environment. Actors’ resources, social relationships and behavioral programs all play key roles in influencing which institutions are supported and which institutions gradually decline. Institutions survive as long as they are perceived as beneficial or as part of the natural order (Douglas, 1986; Coleman, 1990). At the same time, institutions that are supported by a greater number of actors or by more resourceful actors are more likely to survive than others (Knight, 1992). Behaviors can become institutionalized depending on the extent to which individuals who experience the positive or negative externalities created by the actions of an actor are willing and capable of providing collectively rewards or punishments to that actor (Coleman, 1990).

However, institutionalization or de-institutionalization of behaviors is not just a matter of resources and collective action. Douglas (1986) points out that the directions in which new norms and behavioral programs evolve depend significantly on their compatibility with existing institutions. Douglas (1986: 112) observes that ‘institutions gain legitimacy by distinctive grounding in nature and in reason. They afford to their members a set of analogies with which to explore the world and with which to justify the naturalness and reasonableness of the instituted rules.’ Furthermore, Douglas (1986: 112) emphasizes that ‘an institution controls the memory of its members, causing them to forget experiences incompatible with its righteous image, and bringing to their minds events which sustain the view of nature that is complementary to itself.’ She points out that it is a lot easier to change a given institution in directions where the emergent institution will gain its own legitimacy in a manner that is compatible with, or at least does not contradict, the ways in which the other institutions justify their own naturalness and reasonableness.

## Institutions and IT-enabled move toward markets

Coleman (1990) observes that the transaction cost reductions brought about by institutions may vary substantially according to the nature of the transactional action and the identity of the involved actors. This observation provides a crucial insight on institutions, which is not apparent in the more macro-oriented new institutional economics literature. It shows that if a transaction is moved from one governance structure to another, the reductions brought about by institutions in the transaction costs of an actor may vary if the nature of the transactional actions undertaken by the actor and the set of transacting partners with whom he is transacting change as a result of this change in governance.

Do the nature of the transactional actions undertaken by economic actors and the set of transacting partners with whom they transact change as a transaction is moved from one governance to another? They often do because a move toward a shorter-term contract (or vice versa) often implies a change in the size and composition of the set of transacting partners with whom an actor has exchange relationships as well as possible changes in the nature of the exchanged items. These changes, in turn, lead to changes in the exchange-related activities of the actor which, in turn, cause changes in the extent to which institutions reduce his transaction costs. For example, measurement-related behavioral programs regarding the reliability of transacting partners are likely to make smaller contributions toward reducing the transaction costs of actors in more market-like governance structures than in hierarchical ones. This is because in such structures transacting actors often interact with larger numbers of transacting partners for shorter periods and consequently have limited opportunities to establish behavioral programs regarding the reliability of each of their partners. Similarly, solidarity norms, which help weaker transacting actors selling similar products or services to band together against more powerful actors, are also likely to make smaller contributions toward reducing the transaction costs of actors in more market-like governance structures than in hierarchical ones. This is because in such structures, actors who sell similar products or services have greater scope to take business away from each other and consequently are more likely to evade solidarity norms. This is graphically illustrated in the detailed description of transactions within Enron (McLean and Elkind, 2004).

The fact that the transaction cost reductions brought about by institutions may vary substantially depending on the governance structure the transaction is aligned with has crucial implications for IT-enabled realignments of transactions with more market-like governance structures. In spite of the measurement cost reductions brought about by information technology, transacting actors may exhibit reluctance to move to more market-like governance structures if they perceive that institutions will contribute less toward reducing their transaction costs in such structures. This is likely to arise in transactions where actors rely heavily on measurement-related behavioral programs involving reputation and trust, rather than on law, to keep their enforcement costs at low levels. The reluctance to move toward markets is also likely to be observed in transactions where actors such as workers and small suppliers rely on solidarity norms to counterbalance the high bargaining power of large firms.

Another issue that has a direct bearing on IT-enabled realignments toward market-like governance structures is the compatibility of market-oriented transactional activities with the organizational and occupational institutions upheld by transacting actors. This issue is particularly important for employment transactions in areas such as health care, education and the military, where professionals often measure and enforce their own performance standards through internalized norms. Market-oriented governance structures tend to emphasize short-term results rather than long-term performance and are much more quid pro quo than hierarchical structures. This contrasts with the unselfish and caring attitude that professionals assume after internalizing performance norms and may alienate them from market-oriented employment contracts. This implies that IT-enabled realignments toward marketoriented governance structures may be hindered in those industries where organizational and occupational institutions upheld by transacting actors do not favor markets.

Coleman’s observations on the institutionalization of behaviors and Simon’s observations on behavioral programs suggest that the realignment of a transaction with a more market-like governance structure may undermine the capacity of transacting actors to carry out strategic institutionalizations, that is, to influence the institutions in ways that fit their interests. As Coleman points out, an actor’s capacity to establish and maintain a transactional norm depends on the extent to which he can obtain the cooperation of third parties who can deliver the required sanctions and rewards on relevant transacting partners. This in turn depends on whether the transacting actor has assets with attributes that appeal to the third parties, what the production costs are for transforming the transacting actor’s existing assets into exchange items with attributes that appeal to the third parties, and whether the measurement, enforcement and asset specificity activities concerning the exchanges between the transacting actor and the third parties can be achieved at low costs. This points out that the stability provided by the longer-term contracts in the hierarchical governance structures can be an advantage for transacting actors in their strategic institutionalization activities.

In addition, Simon’s observations on behavioral programs show that transacting actors’ capacity to influence strategically the behavioral programs of their current and prospective transacting partners depends on the extent to which they can exert influence on which events get placed into and how prominent they become in their cognitive environments. This suggests that the extended window of interaction provided by the longer-term contracts in the hierarchical governance structures can be an advantage for transacting actors in their activities aimed at strategically manipulating the cognitive environments of their transacting partners. It is for these reasons that IT-enabled realignments toward more market-like governance structures may be hindered if transacting actors perceive that the resource dependency and interaction patterns in such structures will undermine their capacity to undertake strategic institutionalizations.

## Empirical analysis

Three analytic tools complement our application of Williamson’s theory in IT-enabled realignments toward markets. They show how relative positions affect strategic choices.

## Analytic tool A1

The institutional approach demarcates transaction costs by actor and deploys a taxonomy to categorize costs into measurement, enforcement and asset specificity costs. When the measurement cost reductions brought about by changes in information technology are considered, the governance structure that emerges as the least transaction costly alternative for one actor may not be considered as such by the other actor because of concerns over enforcement and asset specificity costs. In such cases, the more powerful actor may try to impose its choice of governance on the weaker one if competitive pressures are not high enough to deter it from doing so.

## Analytic tool A2

Losses in bargaining power may result in higher enforcement costs, and transaction cost reductions brought about by institutions may vary from one governance structure to another and promote clashes between competing structures. In environments where transacting actors rely heavily on measurement-related behavioral programs such as reputation, rather than law, to reduce their enforcement costs, actors on both sides of the transaction may be reluctant to realign their transactions with more marketoriented structures in spite of the measurement cost reductions brought about by information technology. In transactions where actors on one side of the transaction rely on solidarity norms to counterbalance the high bargaining power of the actor on the other side of the transaction, weaker actors may not be willing to shift their transactions to more market-oriented structures if they fear that such a move will undermine their solidarity and bargaining power, and increase their enforcement costs.

## Analytic tool A3

In some IT-enabled realignments, transacting actors may exhibit reluctance to move to a market-like governance structure if they perceive that the resource dependency patterns in the new structure will undermine their capacity to undertake strategic institutionalizations.

Observing the relationships among institutions, changes in information technology and the transaction–governance realignments are fraught with difficulties including differentiating an institutionalized transactional action from an un-institutionalized one. Similarly, differentiating firmly institutionalized behaviors from weakly institutionalized ones requires observing the extent to which actors are either open to questioning, or are themselves engaged in judging the utility of the program they follow. Judging the relative magnitude of the measurement, enforcement and asset specificity costs that transacting actors incur in each governance structure requires observing in detail the attributes of the exchanged items, the uncertainties faced by transacting actors, etc.

## Transforming UK National Health Service payment systems

These problems can be overcome and common research methods can be used to collect the evidence necessary to perform thorough institutional analysis of transactions. Here we work through an exemplary case, involving payment systems in the United Kingdom National Health Service (NHS) for nursing staff, to demonstrate the utility of the approach. To begin this study, extensive informal discussions on transactional issues and a thorough review of reports, technical descriptions of the systems and published literature preceded the conduct of 19 in-depth interviews. The interviewees were chosen to balance staff in terms of grade, specialty and length of work experience. Our acceptance rate for invitations to participate was 60% and we feel that the participants adequately represent the range of opinions widely held (methodological details are available in Islamoglu, 2000).

The NHS introduced an internal market between health care purchasers (district health authorities) and providers (hospitals and clinics) in April 1991. The functionality of both information technology and health care costing procedures such as diagnosis-related groupings seemed to make this move feasible, and one major attraction was the supposed ability to enable the NHS to devolve human resource decisions to individual provider units. This prepared the ground for realigning the employment transactions of nurses with more market-oriented governance structures involving local pay. Given the huge size of the Service, this was one of the largest IT-enabled realignments from hierarchies to market-like governance structures in the world. It did so by aiming to align two broad classes of transactions: those between parts of the NHS that commissioned health care and those that provided it. They did this by creating a ‘purchaser–provider split’ with district health authorities and fund-holding general practices<sup>1</sup> on the demand side and the hospitals (restructured as quasi-independent NHS ‘trusts’) and other public and private health care providers on the supply side. A substantial part of the savings envisaged was to be obtained from the employment transactions of more than 450,000 nurses whose salaries comprised about 30% of the running costs of the provider units. As this happened, NHS trusts gradually moved their employment transactions toward market-like structures and away from the previous civil service model of employment contracts.

Let us now see the effect the IT-enabled realignment had on employment transactions of nurses when they began to fear that the process would be used as an opportunity to undermine their bargaining power and to reduce salaries. From a transaction cost perspective, the consequences of the hierarchical governance structure for the government and the nurses were as follows. In terms of measurement costs, the classification of the myriad of different nursing jobs into six national grades made it simpler for the government and the nursing unions to compare workloads and remuneration across medical specialties and geographical regions. This reduced the measurement costs of both the government and the unions during the national collective bargaining process, guided resolutions of workload disputes and provided one factor to compare the attractiveness of alternative job offers throughout the NHS.

However, the national system made it difficult for the government to measure the effectiveness with which nurses were being deployed by providers at the local level. It also created a conflict of interest for district health managers because they were responsible both for the overall performance of the provider units and for purchasing care from them. As purchasers they had to pressure the managers to provide maximum health care. As providers they relied on the same managers for improving the productivity of the district in relation to other districts, and for this they relied only on quantitative performance indicators.

The move to market gives powers to general practitioners, who have first-hand experience of both the needs of the patients and the medical consequences of their treatment. It also implies that the government could bring about more effective utilization of the workforce by subjecting the provider units to competitive pressures, but there are both losses and gains in terms of enforcement costs. In terms of asset specificity costs, the national system of grade-based salaries was more advantageous for nurses than the local pay arrangements and the move to market suddenly exposed the allocative inefficiencies that had developed over decades. Many providers expected to incur considerable asset specificity costs as they adjusted to the demands of the purchasers by scrapping supposedly uncompetitive facilities, giving them an incentive to shift some of these asset specificity costs onto nurses. Just as there were gains and losses in terms of enforcement costs, there were also ambiguities about who fared well in terms of asset specificity costs and upon introduction, neither the government nor the nursing unions had any empirical evidence to substantiate their claims regarding national and local pay systems.

A key component of the local pay bargaining machinery was information on provider output and nursing workload. This information was obtained from case-mix management systems and nursing information systems where diagnosisrelated statistics were used to analyze expected and actual costs for each class of patient. Nursing information systems helped to plan patient care, coordinate rostering and keep track of workloads. Both systems were fed by the main patient administration system and were linked to other systems such as payroll, supplies and clinical audit, but the resulting figures did not reflect the variations that the severity of patients’ medical conditions created in the intensity of nursing care and in the required level of nursing skills and experience. The government tried to be swift but cautious in introducing the changes, but the unions suspected that local pay was a means of compensating for the government’s under-funding of the NHS by cutting back on nursing paybill behind the fac¸ade of the internal market.<sup>2</sup>

The implementation of the new system met with much resistance and consequently was very slow, amounting to only about 20% of the activity over the next 4 years (1995– 1999). There were also indicators that providers would lower the skill mix of their nursing workforce in order to decrease costs and remain competitive in the internal market.

What we observe can best be explained in terms of the role institutions played in effectively blocking the move toward more market-like governance structure. Nurses believed that the hidden agenda of the transformation was to cut their pay by stealth and so they formed a response to the measurement-related elements. In interviews, nurses were opposed not so much to performance measures as the uses to which such measurements were put. As one senior district nurse put it:

Nurses’ performance has always been measured by their superiors, and we have always recorded our output during out visits, but at the same time we never had to deal with the kind of aggressive pressure which some purchasers, especially fundholding GPs, now exert on providers to purchase more health care without more pay.

## Evaluating the utility of the approach

The transaction cost model that currently prevails in the discipline of information systems predicts that generally transactions will be realigned with more market-like governance structures as advances in information technology reduce the measurement costs in such structures to levels that are lower than those in more hierarchical structures. However, IT-enabled realignment initiatives may fail if one transacting side fears that such a realignment may reduce their bargaining power and thereby increase their enforcement and asset specificity costs. The model also fails to recognize that realignments toward more market-oriented structures may be difficult to achieve if such structures clash with the organizational and occupational institutions upheld by transacting actors.

Our NHS study shows that changes in information technology and in health care costing techniques reduced the measurement costs of both the government and the nurses in employment transactions. However, nurses refused the move toward markets on the grounds that such a move would increase their enforcement and asset specificity costs. Furthermore, nurses felt uncomfortable with more market-like structures such as those involving performance bonuses because they perceived such governance structures as incompatible with their occupational values. These factors led to the failure of the local pay initiative, at considerable cost to the government. These costs could have been minimized if the impact of the institutional environment on the IT-enabled moves toward markets was recognized at the outset.

Analytic tool A1 helped reveal through empirical observations that there were substantial governance preference differences between the government and the nurses and among nurses themselves. It would not have been possible to account for this without the distinction the institutional approach makes between the transaction costs incurred by each of the involved actors.

Analytic tool A2 allowed for a more systematic comparison between the measurement, enforcement and asset specificity institutions before and after the transformation. We could observe how nurses became aware that features of transactional institutions such as job security and predictability, performance norms and the influence of hierarchies on reported measures would be undermined by the move to markets. Our interviewees were clearly split between those who did not perceive a significant threat to their enforcement of asset specificity costs and therefore supported the move toward the ‘local contract option,’ and those who opposed the move because of their stated fears over enforcement and asset specificity issues. These and similar empirical observations would be extremely difficult to analyze from a transaction cost perspective without the institutional approach. It is together that they help explain that the transaction cost reductions brought about by solidarity norms may disappear with more market-oriented governance structures when those structures are incompatible with organizational and occupational norms. We can see, for example, that IT-enabled realignments toward markets are more likely to succeed in systems where collective bargaining plays a smaller role in determining pay levels. Managers could prepare employees in such circumstances by moves to ensure that features associated with market-bargaining, such as salami-slicing and shifting standards are monitored by independent bodies and disassociated with the move away from hierarchies.

Analytic tool A3 allows us to accommodate the ways in which institutions are strategically perceived in cases such as this where nurses feared that it would be more difficult to rally public support against cost-cutting moves within the new governance structure. In interviews, they stated that the de-standardization of nursing jobs would provide the government cover for cutting the overall spending on health care while dressing up such cuts as efficiency savings. Transacting actors can be seen to prefer aligning their transactions with governance structures that allow them strategically to create transactional institutions which serve their purposes.

## The need for an institutional approach

The various institutional studies, which have been drawn from economics, sociology and anthropology, suggest that competitive and institutional factors may restrict the extent to which information technology can be utilized to realign transactions with more market-oriented governance structures. At present, however, there is little empirical evidence in information systems to suggest that competitive and institutional factors may restrict the extent to which transacting actors can benefit from the measurement cost reducing powers of information technology through realignments toward markets. This lack of awareness on institutional issues poses two grave dangers for information systems research on IT-enabled realignments toward markets.

First, some transacting actors may derive misleading conclusions from case studies on successful IT-enabled realignments and hastily embark upon similar realignments even though the institutional environment in which their transactions are embedded is not conducive to more market-oriented arrangements.

Secondly, actors who transact in environments where institutions favor more hierarchical governances may remain unaware of the link between having pro-market institutions and their capacity to benefit from advances in information technology through realignments. Institutional environments can be gradually modified to make them more conducive toward more market-like forms of governance. Solidarity norms and professional values can be made more accommodating toward markets and legal systems can be strengthened. However, changing these institutions takes time, resources and a keen awareness about the economic consequences of institutional realignment.

An institutional approach that complements Williamson’s theory may contribute toward information systems research on IT-enabled realignments in two ways. First, it may enable actors to make more accurate comparisons between their institutional environments and those in which successfully realigned transactions were embedded. This may help actors to draw up more realistic realignment initiatives and minimize failed realignments and associated conflicts. Furthermore, such an institutional approach may help raise awareness about the importance of strategically influencing institutions in ways that are conducive to more marketoriented forms of exchange.

An institutional approach that complements Williamson’s theory will make especially valuable contributions toward analyzing IT-enabled moves toward markets in the public sector where there is limited competition, in the developing and formerly socialist countries where legal enforcement mechanisms are ineffective, in the employment transactions of large private and public organizations where pay is determined through collective bargaining, and in the employment transactions in sectors such as health care and education where professionals regulate themselves heavily through internalized performance norms.

## About the authors

Mahmet Islamoglu has a B.Sc. in engineering and an M.Sc. in technology management from Cornell University and a Ph.D. in Information Systems from the London School of Economics. He then returned to northern Cyprus where he currently is an assistant professor of Business Administration. His research interest is in organizational information systems.

Jonathan Liebenau has a B.A. in science policy from the University of Rochester and a Ph.D. in History and Sociology of Science from the University of Pennsylvania. He teaches at the London School of Economics in the Department of Management, information systems group. He specializes in two areas: fundamental concepts of information, and the problems and prospects of information and communication technology in economic development. He has previously worked in academic administration, technology policy and the economic history of science-based industry, all positions in which he has emphasized the use of information in organizations.
