# Same technology, different outcome? Reinterpreting Barley’s Technology as an Occasion for Structuring

Starling David Hunter III

Carnegie Mellon University in Qatar, Doha, Qatar

## Abstract

In the last few decades, several studies have found the same technology implemented in highly similar organizational settings to be associated with very different consequences for structure and process. The seminal study in this stream of research is Barley’s (1986) Technology as an Occasion for Structuring, which reported that two similarly composed radiology departments implemented the same technology (computerized tomography scanners), yet experienced very different structural outcomes. In this paper I re-analyze the original study’s data under three different statistical assumptions. First, I performed an arcsine transformation on the dependent variable where the original study used the raw probabilities. Second, I specified a power regression model in which the original study employed a linear regression. Finally, I user fewer dummy variables in the ‘combined’ regression models to determine the distinct phases through which the two hospitals evolved. Taken together, these assumptions produce very different results from the original study. Specifically they indicate that the radiology departments did not decentralize at different rates and did not do so over a different number of distinct phases. From my analysis come three specific recommendations for research investigating the consequences of information technology in similarly constituted organizations: (1) exchange the default assumption of homogeneity of outcomes with one of heterogeneity; (2) explicitly account for both the observable properties of technology and the context of its use; and (3) state clearly and a priori the standard used to classify structural and organizational outcomes as ‘different’.

## Introduction

There is a long-standing body of empirical research concerned with the consequences of information technology for organizational structure and processes (e.g. Meyer, 1968; Klatzky, 1970; Whisler, 1970a, b; Blau et al., 1976; Pfeffer & Leblebici, 1977; Robey, 1981; Carter, 1984; Barley, 1986; Robey & Rodriguez-Diaz, 1989; Zeffane, 1989, 1992; Huber, 1990; Orlikowski, 1992, 1993; Brynjolfsson et al., 1994; Leidner & Elam, 1995; Robey & Sahay, 1996; Hitt & Brynjolfsson, 1997; Black et al., 2004; Jones & Karsten, 2008; Poole, 2009). Several of those studies have reported that the same technology, when implemented in similar organizational settings, can be associated with vastly different, even diametrically opposing, organizational consequences (e.g. Barley, 1986; Orlikowski, 1992, 1993; Robey & Sahay, 1996).

Among those studies reporting this same-technologydifferent-outcome finding, Barley (1986) may be the seminal work. Through its examination of changes in the structure of radiological work ‘occasioned’ by the implementation of computerized tomography (CT) scanners, the study sought to challenge the technological and organizational imperatives (Markus & Robey, 1988) that had long dominated the debate on research of the technology-structure relationship. It did not do so by taking sides in the long-running ‘centralization debate’ (George & King, 1991). Rather, it sought to explain decades of conflicting predictions by ‘embracing’ divergent outcomes, by accepting contradictory findings ‘as a matter of course’ (Barley, 1986, p. 78), and by applying ‘alternate theoretical frameworks’ such as structuration (Giddens, 1979) and negotiated-order (Strauss, 1978, 1982) theories, as well as other perspectives that viewed structure as ‘patterned action, interaction, behavior, and cognition’ (Barley, 1986, p. 79).

The study’s research methodology (ethnography), quasi-experimental design (comparing the same technology in two highly similar settings), unique conceptualization of technology (as an ‘occasion for’, rather than determinant of structure), strong social science grounding (negotiated-order and structuration), and counterintuitive finding (that the same technology occasioned different structural outcomes) made it a welcome departure from previous studies of the technology-structure relationship. Not surprisingly, the paper is very frequently cited in the literature on the organizational consequences of information technology and elsewhere in the field of management studies. A Social Sciences Citation Index<sup>s</sup> search performed in September 2009 reported over 200 citations for the paper, nearly 30 times the expected level for a publication by a management scholar (Long et al., 1998). While over half of these citations appear in research related to the consequences of hospital and managerial information systems and other technologies on organization structure, processes, and performance, it has also been frequently cited by researchers in the areas of jobs, skills, expertise, and careers; theory building and research methods; institutionalization and social behavior in organizations; organizational learning, culture, and change; structuration, routines, and sense-making.

The study involved the examination of the implementation of CT scanners by similarly constituted radiology departments of two community hospitals. Its principal finding was as follows: (1) ‘far more’ decentralization in one department than the other and (2) a difference between departments in the number of distinct ‘phases of structuring’ through which each ‘evolved’ (ibid, p. 105). This same-technology-different-outcome finding is perhaps the most widely cited of the study’s conclusions (e.g. Leonard-Barton, 1988; Markus & Robey, 1988; Robey & Rodriguez-Diaz, 1989; Weick, 1990; George & King, 1991;

Orlikowski, 1992; DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Pentland, 1995; Robey & Sahay, 1996; Anderson & Aydin, 1997; Morrill & Fine, 1997; Sahay, 1997; Pinsonneault & Rivard, 1998; Vendelo, 1998; Orlikowski & Barley, 2001). In this paper I reinterpret the data from the original study under three different statistical assumptions. First, I perform an arcsine transformation on the dependent variable in which the original study used the raw probabilities. Second, I specify a power regression model in which the original study employed a linear regression. And third I user fewer dummy variables in the ‘combined’ regression models to determine the distinct phases through which the two hospitals evolved. I end the paper with a discussion of the practical and theoretical implications that my findings have on our understanding of the original study, in particular, and of the information systems-organization relationship, more generally.

## Research design, methods, and findings of original study

The setting for the original study was the radiology department of each of two Massachusetts community hospitals. The author named them ‘Urban’ and ‘Suburban’. As their names suggest, the two hospitals differed with respect to geography but were otherwise considered to be highly similar. Each department employed the same number of radiologists and related staff, performed the same ‘standard’ range of radiological procedures, and purchased and began use of the same model of CT scanner in the same year (Barley, 1986, p. 84). Despite these obvious similarities, the original study reported as its major findings: (1) ‘far more’ decentralization in Suburban’s radiology department than in Urban’s and (2) a difference between departments in the number of distinct ‘phases of structuring’ through which each ‘evolved’ (ibid, p. 105).

Table 1 provides a description of the eight regression models that were developed in the original study and are the subject of my analysis. The dependent variable in all models was an index of centralization, operationalized as ‘the percentage of (nine) decisions made by a radiologist during the course of a scan’ (ibid, p. 86). The nine decisions were: (1) when to start a patient; (2) where to start scanning; (3) how far to scan; (4) what techniques to use; (5) whether to reposition the patient; (6) whether to inject contrast; (7) what windows and centers to use; (8) whether the radiologist should view the scans; and (9) when to end the exam. The import of this measure is that two people were able to make operational decisions concerning the performance of radiological scans: radiologists, who were physicians, and technicians, who were not physicians. Because radiologists had higher rank than technicians, the higher the number of the nine operational decisions made by radiologists, the more centralized was the decision making considered to be.

The independent variables in the models were of two kinds: continuous and categorical (dummy) variables. In the Suburban-Linear and Urban-Linear models, the only independent variable was the number of days since the start of use of the CT scanner (DAYS). The Suburban-Quadratic and Urban-Quadratic models had two independent variables: DAYS and a quadratic term, DAYS<sup>2</sup>. In each of the last four regression models – Suburban-Suburban, Urban-Urban, Suburban-Combined, and Urban-Combined – up to four dummy variables were used to represent the number of distinct phases of structuring experienced at each hospital. In the Suburban-Suburban model, the sole independent variable was a single dummy representing the first of that hospital’s two phases. The Urban-Urban model utilized three dummy variables, one for each of the first three of Urban’s four hypothesized phases of structuring. The two ‘combined’ models each had four dummy variables representing the three phases of structuring at Urban and the single phase at Suburban. Phases differed with respect to starting dates and duration and were named according to the nature of the interaction between radiologists and technicians, as indicated in Table 2.

Finally, the two ‘combined’ models, Suburban-Combined and Urban-Combined, each contained four dummy variables – one from Suburban and three from Urban. For these models it was reported that ‘in neither case did the combined model substantially increase the proportion of variance explained by the hospital’s own model’ (ibid, p. 105). In other words, the Suburban-Combined and Urban-Combined model did not explain a significantly greater proportion of the variance than the Suburban-Suburban and Urban-Urban models, respectively. On the basis of the results of these four models, it was concluded that the data were ‘consistent’ with the claim that Suburban experienced two phases of structuring but that Urban experienced four such phases. Thus, the first four models were used to establish that the rate of decentralization differed in each hospital while the last four models were intended to show that the two hospitals experienced a different number of distinct phases of structuring.

## Reinterpreting the original results

As noted above, the original study examined the appropriation of CT scanners in radiology departments of two community hospitals and reported that they experienced different structural outcomes: (1) ‘far more’ decentralization in one department than the other and (2) a difference between departments in the number of distinct ‘phases of structuring’ through which each ‘evolved’ (ibid, p. 105). In this section of the paper I reinterpret the data underlying these two findings with a power, rather than linear, model with the dependent variable arcsine transformed and with a reduced number of dummy variables in the ‘phases of structuring analysis.

All analyses in this section were performed with data taken directly from the graphs of the decision proportions (centralization index) appearing in Figure 3 (ibid, p. 103) of the original study as depicted in Figure 1 of this study. It is observed that the plot for Suburban contained 45 observations while that for Urban had 42. The positions of each of the 87 observations along the two coordinate axes were estimated by this author and by a research assistant. The average value of the two sets of estimates was used for all subsequent regression analyses.

To determine how faithfully the estimated data represented the original data, estimated proportions were regressed on estimated days and compared to the results of the same regression shown in Table 1 of the original study. As can be seen in Table 5, the estimated and the original values of the intercept (b ), the sole regression coefficient (b ), and the coefficient of determination (R<sup>2</sup>) are almost identical.

## Transforming the dependent variable

Recall that the original study’s dependent variable was the proportion of the nine operational decisions made by radiologists. As a rule, transformations of independent or dependent variables are employed to correct for any of the following violations of the requirements of regression models: (1) nonlinearity among variables; (2) heteroscedasticity or instability of error variance; and (3) nonnormality of the distribution (Hair et al., 1995). There are several transformations that correct for each violation and some that correct for more than one. Distributions based on proportions or percentages are especially susceptible to being guilty of the all three violations (Cohen & Cohen, 1983, p. 266) and their need for transformation has been long recognized (e.g. Bartlett, 1937; Bliss, 1937; Fisher & Yates, 1938).

The arcsine transformation can be employed to correct for both the heteroscedasticity (Bartlett, 1947; Snedecor & Cochran, 1967; Winer, 1970; von Eye & Schuster, 1998) and the non-normality violations (Hair et al., 1995). It is defined as twice the angle (A) whose sine equals the square root of the percentage (p) being transformed or A ¼ 2 arcsine $\left( \boldsymbol { p } \right) ^ { 1 / 2 ; }$ . If the percentage, ${ p , }$ is equal to 0.0 or to 1.0 then a different transformation is used: $\mathrm { A } _ { 0 } = 2$ arcsine $( 1 / 4 d ) ^ { 1 / 2 }$ and $\mathrm { A } _ { 1 } = 3 . 1 4 1 6 \mathrm { - } \mathrm { A } _ { 0 } ,$ , where d equals the denominator of the fraction that generated the percentage (Walker & Lev, 1953; Owen, 1962; Snedecor & Cochran, 1967; Cohen & Cohen, 1983). In our case, d ¼ 9.

The results of analyses performed with data subjected to an arcsine transformation are not necessarily different than those obtained with untransformed percentages (Hair et al., 1995). When ‘nearly’ (Snedecor & Cochran, 1967, p. 328) or ‘almost’ (Cohen & Cohen, 1983, p. 266) all of the observations lie within the range between 0.25 and 0.75, the arcsine transformation produces little or no noticeable change in the results of statistical analyses and is not needed. If, however, a large number of observations lie outside this range then estimated regression coefficients are unreliable and likely to be invalid, that is biased. A sizeable portion of the data for both hospitals lies outside the prescribed (25–75%) range. Almost 70% (31/45) of Suburban’s observations lie outside of it, with eight of them being equal to 0.0. For Urban, 43% (18/42) lie outside of the range, with five being equal to 1.0. The resulting distributions are, thus, highly skewed. Figure 2 provides a frequency distribution of both the raw and the arcsine-transformation of the centralization index for Suburban. It is evident that the effect of the arcsine transformation is to make the distribution of the centralization index more nearly normal. The same effect was observed for Urban’s data as well.

Also, recall that in the original study, the coefficients of the quadratic term, DAYS<sup>2</sup>, was highly significant (t ¼ 2.88; Po0.01) for Suburban, but only marginally so for URBAN (t ¼ 1.83, Po0.10). This was taken to indicate that centralization decreased at ‘different rates’ in the two radiology departments, that is ‘geometrically declining’ at Suburban and ‘gradually, in a linear fashion’ at Urban (ibid, p. 104). Transforming the data reveals that this finding still holds, albeit less strongly. Now, the quadratic term for Suburban is less highly significant $\mathrm { ( \bar { b } = 5 . 0 7 E { - } 0 5 ; ~ t = 2 . 5 8 , ~ } P { < } 0 . 0 5 )$ while the significance level for Urban is unchanged $( \mathrm { b } = 3 . 1 1 \mathrm { E } { \cdot } 0 5 ; ~ \mathrm { t } = 1 . 8 6 ,$ Po0.10). Thus, the rates of decentralization do still differ across the two sites, provided we rely upon the results of quadratic models.

There are, however, important reasons to reconsider a reliance on the quadratic models to indicate differences in rates in decentralization. First of all, the quadratic model does not closely parallel the unfolding pattern of interaction between radiologists and technicians that is described in the study. Specifically, neither narrative associated with the two radiology departments described a dynamic between radiologists and technicians that would have seen centralization beginning to increase about $2 / 3$ of the way through the implementation and returning to its starting levels just 3–6 months after the end of the observation period. This is, however, what the quadratic models predict. When quadratic model’s estimates are used to predict the level of centralization beyond the end of the observation period, we find that for Urban, the model predicts that after 170 days centralization began to increase and that by 384 days, barely 6 more months after the end of the observation period, the level of centralization would have returned to its original levels. The same holds for Suburban. Its quadratic regression predicts that centralization would begin to increase only after 150 days into the implementation and would return to its initial level by day 349. That is a mere 100 days after the end of the observation period for this hospital. These predictions are the exact opposite of what the original study tells us transpired at both hospitals, that is that centralization declined rapidly (even ‘geometrically’ at Suburban) and then stabilized.

As seen in Table 6, it can be observed that the power model, that is a model of the form $\gamma = b _ { 0 } \times x ^ { b _ { 1 } } = b _ { 0 } \times$ $\mathrm { D A Y S } ^ { b _ { 1 } }$ , explained 4–5% more of the variation in the dependent variable than does the comparable quadratic model for each hospital. This is as it should be because power models are the appropriate choice for modeling data that display geometrical declines or increases (Neter et al., 1996). As shown in Figure 3, the slope coefficient for DAYS in power models for both hospitals is negative and highly significant. However, as what the case in the original study, the magnitude of Suburban’s slope coefficient $( \mathbf { b } _ { 1 } = - 0 . 2 7 4 , \mathrm { t } = - 5 . 3 4 , P < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ is double of that for Urban $( \mathsf { b } _ { 1 } = - 0 . 1 4 1 , \mathsf { t } = - 4 . 2 4 , P { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$

Non-parametric multiple regression is another approach to dealing with non-normally distributed data. Although not widely available at the time of the original study, non-parametric multiple regression is now preferred to the addition of polynomial terms to the regression equation (Fan & Gijbels, 1996; Fox, 2000). Whereas both Generalized Linear Model and Ordinary Least Squared regression assume that the dependent variable is a linear function of the independent variables, nonparametric regression relaxes this assumption. Specifically, it fits a smoothed curve to a scatterplot of the data instead of a straight line (Garson, 2009). Thus, the model is derived inductively, that is from the data, rather than deductively, that is from prior expectations – an ideal approach for quantitative ethnographies.

This apparent difference in slopes may not actually be an indicator that each department decentralized at different rates, however. A careful examination of Figure 3 reveals, among other things, that the values of centralization for Suburban run consistently below those for Urban throughout most of the observation period. Although such a test was not performed in the original study, it can be readily determined whether the mean level of the centralization index is in fact higher for Urban than for Suburban. After determining that the two hospitals did not have different variances in their centralization scores (F ¼ 1.21, P ¼ 0.27), a two-sample t-test assuming equal variances was performed. It indicated that the mean levels (of the arcsine transformed measure) of centralization for Suburban (1.05) and Urban (1.92) were significantly different (t ¼ 7.09, Po0.001). Figure 4 displays the regression lines for two power models: the same one for Urban that is shown in Figure 3, plus another for Suburban but with a constant value of 0.867 added to it. This value is the difference between the mean level for Urban and Suburban. And although it is hard to tell, Figure 4 does contain the plots of two regressions lines. What we see is that when the mean difference is accounted for, the two regression lines appear to be one. Moreover, the slope coefficients are almost identical: the intercept and slope coefficients for Urban are 3.221 and –0.149, respectively, while those for Suburban are 3.212 and –0.147. The only appreciable difference between the two power models that remains is that Urban’s model explains a smaller proportion of the variance (30.4%) than does Suburban’s (45.2%).

Taken together, the reinterpreted results relate a different but complementary story about decentralization between the two radiology departments. Later in the paper I take up this matter in greater depth. First, however, I attend to the question of the reported difference in the number of phases of structuring – the other half of the ‘different structural outcomes’ finding of the original study.

The Urban-Urban model utilized three dummy variables, one for each of the first three of Urban’s four hypothesized phases of structuring. The coefficients for the dummy variables representing phases 1 and 3 were highly significant (Po.001 and Po0.01, respectively), but that for phase 2 was not. This result also is consistent with the results reported in the original study but does not confirm the existence of four distinct phases, however, since phase 2 did not differ from phase 4. Thus, there is only support for the conclusion that Urban experienced three, rather than four, phases while Suburban experienced only two. This fact was overlooked in the original study.

In order to demonstrate that Urban did experience four phases, two additional ‘combined’ regression models were also tested in the original analysis. The justification for these models that was provided reads as follows:

Since Suburban was said to have experienced two phases and Urban four, Suburban’s data were regressed on one dummy variable representing the first phase of structuring while Urban’s data were regressed on three variables representing Urban’s first three phases. Each site’s data were then regressed on all four dummy variables in a combined analysis. If each site’s phasing was adequately defined then the combined model should predict radiologists’ involvement no better than the model constructed to depict the site’s own phases of restructuring. (Barley, 1986, p. 105, emphasis added)

There is another approach to depicting each site’s phases. As a rule, dummy variables are used to render information on membership in one of k mutually exclusive and cumulatively exhaustive categories (Hair et al., 1995). This is accomplished by defining k-1 dichotomous variables. For example, since Suburban had two (k ¼ 2) phases it was represented by one (k-1 ¼ 1) dummy variable. Urban, with its four (k ¼ 4) hypothesized phases, was represented by three (k-1 ¼ 3) dummy variables.

The k phases through which each hospital was believed to have evolved constitute a mutually exclusive and cumulatively exhaustive set for that hospital only. Each of the k-1 dummy variables for each hospital expresses one, and only one, meaningful aspect of group membership: for example, phase 1 or phase 2 in the case of Suburban; phase 1 or phase 2 or phase 3 in the case of Urban. In a regression model, the interpretation of any of the k-1 dummy variables is always in relation to the (undefined) kth category – the reference category. For example, in the Urban-Urban model the positive and statistically significant slope coefficient for dummy variable Urban\_Phase\_3 is interpreted as indicating that phase 3 has a higher level of centralization than phase 4, the reference category.

The two combined models each have a total of four dummy variables representing Urban’s phases 1, 2, and 3, and Suburban’s phase 1. However, combining both Suburban’s single and Urban’s three dummy variables into the same model violates the requirements of exhaustiveness and exclusivity. In such a model no interpretation of any of the dummy variables is possible and thus, neither of the combined models can be construed as supporting the existence of four phases of structuring for Suburban. What can be done, however, is to determine whether or not modeling four phases for Urban provided the best fit of the data.

The 3rd–15th rows in Table 7 contain the results of a series of regressions of all remaining combinations of Urban’s four phases. Two obvious trends emerge from this analysis. The first is that the best fit (adj-R<sup>2</sup> ¼ 40%) is provided by a model with variables for phases 1 and 3. The positive sign of their coefficients suggests that, as was the case with the original study, these two phases experienced much higher centralization than phases 2 and 4. This is further supported by observing the sign and significance of the coefficients in the model containing dummies for phases 1, 3, and 4. Again, the coefficients for phases 1 and 3 are significant and positive while that for phase 4 is positive but not significant. The second important observation is that all models containing three phases explain a nearly identical proportion of the variance. This would be the case because either phase 2 or phase 4 must be included as a dummy variable or the comparison category in all such models. Thus, while there was evidence to support the claim that Urban and Suburban evolved through a different number of phases, the support is weaker than originally reported. This analysis makes clear that centralization differed across three phases at Urban experienced and across two of them at Suburban. This is a different number of phases, to be sure. But the difference between two and three phases seems less noteworthy when we recall that each department’s first phase lasted about 25 days in length and that the majority of the decentralization at each site occurred within those first 3–4 weeks.

## Recap

The results of the preceding section complement the results of the original in an important way. First, recall that the original study reported that ‘Suburban’ decentralized far more than the ‘Urban’ radiology group. My interpretation is that both decentralized significantly but with an important caveat: Urban had a higher staring point or initial condition and ended at a higher point. The next section of the paper discusses why accounting for the initial and persisting difference in the level of centralization is important. Also, I found that the rates at which they decentralize are different – just as originally reported – but not different in a statistically significant sense. Second, recall that the original study reported the two groups evolving through different numbers of distinct phases of structuring – four for Urban and two for Suburban. The reinterpreted results find a smaller difference – three at Urban and two at Suburban. Moreover, the bulk of the decentralization at both hospitals occurred during the first 3–4 weeks.

## An alternate approach

As noted above, the data for the two sites appear to be separated by a constant difference over the entire observation period (see Figure 3). This suggests the presence of an ‘intercept shift’, a difference that can be captured statistically by the addition in a regression model of N-1 dummy variables representing the N categories into which observations can be classified. In the case under consideration, capturing the intercept shift would have required adding a single dummy variable for hospital to a regression model that utilized observations pooled from both sites, as shown in Eq. 1.

$$
Y _ {i} = b _ {0} \times b _ {1} ^ {\text { DAYS }} + b _ {2} \times \text { HOSPITAL }\tag{1}
$$

where HOSPITAL is a dummy variable coded 1 for Urban and 0 otherwise. Thus, for Urban, the model is

$$
Y _ {i} = b _ {0} \times b _ {1} ^ {\mathrm{DAYS}} + b _ {2} \times 1 = b _ {0} \times b _ {1} ^ {\mathrm{DAYS}} + b _ {2}\tag{2}
$$

and for Suburban is

$$
Y _ {i} = b _ {0} \times b _ {1} ^ {\mathrm{DAYS}} + b _ {2} \times 0 = b _ {0} \times b _ {1} ^ {\mathrm{DAYS}}\tag{3}
$$

Employing such a model affords one particular advantage over separate regressions: it allows us to simultaneously assess the impact of both classes of explanations for the variation in structure – the technological and the contextual. This, in turn, makes it possible to assess whether one factor mediates the other; whether one factor provides a stronger explanation of variation in structure than the other; and whether the two factors together explain more of the variation in the structure than is left unexplained.

Models 1, 3, and 4 indicate that the standardized slope coefficient for the technological variable, DAYS, is both negative and highly significant (-0.572 o bo-0.527; -8.30 oto-5.71; Po0.001) thereby indicating that the locus of decision making regarding scans declined significantly as the number of days the CT scanner was in use increased. The significance of the dummy variable for hospital in Model 3 indicates that we can readily reject the strongest form of the technological imperative, that is technology is the only determinant of structure.

Both are highly significant predictors of structure, but the latter is clearly much more so.

Thus, neither form of a context as mediator hypothesis is supported by the results of the original study. Similarly, the insignificant value of the coefficient for the interaction term between HOSPITAL and DAY (b ¼ -0.160, P ¼ 0.687) in Model 4 indicates there is also no support for a context as moderator hypothesis, that is that there were differences in outcomes between the two departments, particularly differential rates of decentralization.

Finally, the results of the first three models taken as a group provide strong support for another hypothesis that could have been tested only with pooled data – that is, that consideration of both technology and its context of use provides a better explanation of the observed variation in structure than either does alone. The data suggest that this hypothesis is supported in both an absolute and a relative sense. In relative terms, we note that the adjusted $\cdot R ^ { 2 }$ of Model 3 (64.3%), one that includes both the number of days in use (DAYS) and a variable representing the context (HOSPITAL), is much higher than the $R ^ { 2 }$ for Models 1 (26.9%) and 2 (35.8%). This is a clear indication that the joint consideration of both technology and its context provide a superior explanation of the observed variation in structure than either variable does alone. In absolute terms we can note that the $R ^ { 2 }$ of the combined model (64.3%) is well in excess of 50%. This indicates that the proportion of variance explained by the model, which jointly considers technology and its context (Model 3), is greater that the proportion of variance left unexplained.

## Another word about dummy variables

There are two interpretations of the coefficient of the dummy variable HOSPITAL in Models 2–4 above. Each deserves careful consideration. First, there is an understanding of a strictly statistical kind in which the estimate of the slope coefficient for HOSPITAL is seen as an intercept shift, as the expected difference in centralization across the two sites. This is confirmed by observing that in Model 3 the non-standardized value of the coefficient for the dummy variable, HOSPITAL, took on a value of 0.867, the amount that I earlier showed was the difference between the mean levels of centralization and Urban and Suburban. This value translates into a difference of about 1.4 additional decisions being made by radiologists at Urban vs radiologists at Suburban. What the value of coefficient of the dummy variable for hospital in the pooled regressions does not and cannot tell us, however, is why such a difference existed. That issue is addressed by our second interpretation, one that treats dummy variables as controls for unobserved heterogeneity, that is for the presence of unobserved factors that could be expected to affect the outcome variable. Controlling for unobserved heterogeneity by specifying dummy variables is a very common practice in quantitative empirical research in management and others social sciences. Doing so greatly reduces the possibility of misinterpreting results attributable to observable characteristics (Heckman, 1981). Examples of entities for which unobserved heterogeneity has been shown to exist include industries, firms, strategic business units, test subjects and sites, schools, and patent classes. They are almost invariably found to explain significant proportions of variation in the dependent measures regressed upon them (Heckman et al., 1985).

The ‘fixed effects’ method of controlling for unobserved heterogeneity associated with N categories involves specifying N-1 dummy variables (Hsiao, 1986). In our case, the factor of interest is the hospital wherein the technology was deployed. Since there were only two (N) hospitals, then only one (N-1) dummy variable was required to control for unobserved heterogeneity. What we observed in Models 3 and 4 was that the dummy variable HOSPITAL was positive and highly significant, indicating thereby that a substantial proportion of the observed variation in centralization was attributable to site-specific factors, to possibly long-lived sources of unobserved heterogeneity across the two departments. This point relates directly to an important contribution of the original study – and others like it – which heretofore has gone unrecognized.

Imagine for a moment that the data from the original study had been obtained by way of survey methods or other secondary sources. Imagine further that the author never visited either of the two radiology departments, never observed scans taking place, and never witnessed the patterns of interaction between radiologists and technicians. Had this been the case, it would have been quite difficult to know why there were differences in the mean level of centralization across the two sites. The statistics would have revealed that the difference did exist, but it would have been left to conjecture as to what this difference could be attributed. Fortunately for us, this is not how the data were collected. Rather, it was gathered in such a way that makes it easier to ascertain the sources of variation between the two sites, sources that would have otherwise remained unobserved. These were, as we now know, each site’s ‘specific historical processes’ in which the technology was embedded and the ‘relative distributions of expertise’. The detail that was gathered through the ethnographic approach provided valuable insight as to why a dummy variable for hospital would have been highly significant – had the data been pooled and such a variable been included.

## Conclusion

Recall that the original study concluded that the same technology occasioned different structural outcomes – different rates of decentralization and a different number of phases of structuring. These different outcomes were attributed only to differences in the CT scanners’ context-of-use at each site and not to the objective measures of the technology’s use. In this study I have shown

(1) that there were no differences in the rate of decentralization, but rather in the mean level of the measure of structure;

(2) that this difference in the mean level was constant at all levels of the independent variable (DAYS) and was thus captured by a dummy variable representing unobserved heterogeneity between sites;

(3) that the likely source of this unobserved variation was the differences in each site’s processes and expertise, as was suggested in the original study;

(4) that there was no evidence of an interaction between technology and context;

(5) that both the technology and context each explained significant proportions of the variation in structure;

(6) that both technology and context together explained more variation in structure than either did alone;

(7) that there was no evidence of technology or context mediating one another (each preserved the strength of its influence in the presence of the other);

(8) that taken together, the two factors explain more variance than they left unexplained; and finally

(9) that the difference in the number of phases of structuring was smaller than originally reported.

The implications of these findings for the understanding of the IT-organization relationship are many. The first concerns the need for research on the organizational consequences of IT to make clearer what is meant by ‘different structural outcomes’. The standard implied by the original study – a difference in rates of decentralization – is not one that other studies would be advised to emulate. I say this because it used possibly the lowest possible threshold for establishing differences. Obviously, the claim of different structural outcomes would have been most strongly supported if reported significant changes in opposite directions, that is one site centralized while the other decentralized. Support would have also been strong, although less so, if one site changed significantly (in either direction) while the other did not. Instead, what was reported was that both sites experiencing changes in the same direction but at different rates. To give credit where it is due, the original study was the first of its genre that offered data on its dependent measures. This is what distinguishes it from other published ethnographic studies that have reported diametrically opposing outcomes. Without data of these kind, the matter of what constitutes different outcomes will prove difficult to resolve.

A second implication of these findings concerns the need for a greater recognition of the role of unobserved heterogeneity in explaining variation in structure, especially in similarly constituted organizations. To date, the notion that variation in dependent measures can depend as much on variation in the unobserved measures as on observable ones is not widely recognized by researchers at the intersection of technology and institutions. Neglecting to control for unobserved heterogeneity when investigating the impacts of technology on similarly constituted organizations would appear to increase the chance of reaching erroneous conclusions about the absolute and relative influence of technology and context. Replacing the current ‘homogeneity assumption’, that is that similarly constituted organizations should not exhibit variation in outcomes associated with technology, with one cognizant of unobserved heterogeneity will require that researchers now assume (1) that no matter how similar two organizations may be in observed ways, there will also be unobserved differences among them and (2) that unobserved differences may account for variation in structure both independently of technology, as well as through an interaction with it.

These assumptions dovetail nicely both with Orlikowski & Barley’s (2001, p. 145) call for more ‘interaction’ and ‘cross-fertilization’ between information technology and organization studies’ and with Jones & Karsten’s (2008, p. 150) call for ‘more attention’ on the ‘interaction between technology and human action’. More specifically, the former have argued that ‘organization studies can benefit y by y taking material properties of technologies into account’. The question that remains is what form that ‘cross-fertilization’ and ‘interaction’ should take. If the ‘cross-fertilization’ between IS and organization studies is translated into considering technology and context as co-determinants of technology, then much stands to be gained. If the ‘interaction’ they recommend extends not only to the paradigmatic level, but to the measurement level as well, even greater will be the benefits. If scholars of the technology-organization relationship combine their recommendations with the ones put forth here concerning unobserved heterogeneity and operationalizing ‘different structural outcomes’, the result could be a bumper crop.
