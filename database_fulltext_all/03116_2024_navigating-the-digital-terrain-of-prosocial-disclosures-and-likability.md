# NAVIGATING THE DIGITAL TERRAIN OF PROSOCIAL DISCLOSURES AND LIKABILITY<sup>1</sup>

Xue (Jane) Tan Information Technology & Operations Management, Cox School of Business, Southern Methodist University, Dallas, TX, U.S.A. {janetan@smu.edu}

Lu (Lucy) Yan Operations & Decision Technologies, Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, IN, U.S.A. {yanlucy@indiana.edu}

Alfonso J. Pedraza-Martinez IT, Analytics, and Operations, Mendoza College of Business, University of Notre Dame, Notre Dame, IN, U.S.A. {apedraz2@nd.edu}

When people share their prosocial behavior on social media, they face a dilemma. By making prosocial disclosures, they risk being perceived as self-promoting or even selfish and thus less likable. By staying silent, they fail to spread awareness about prosocial activities they value. Drawing on attribution theory, we study the digital reaction of likes to the self-disclosure of prosocial activities that involve multiple stakeholders to understand the affordances and constraints of social media. Leveraging field data on a social networking site, we found that reporting a higher level of self-effort increases the digital reaction of likes. To our surprise, expressing gratitude for the sponsor’s efforts also increased likes for such selfdisclosures. Through lab experiments that emulated this context, we dissected this paradox by separating the positive effect of expressing gratitude and the negative effect of sharing credit with other stakeholders on likes. Further, we underscore the salience of expressing gratitude, even when gratitude was extended to sponsors with bad reputations. Our study contributes to the design of a more humane digital world where individuals share and promote prosocial activities without compromising their likability and offers valuable insights for navigating the evolving landscape of social networking platforms.

Keywords: User-generated content, braggart’s dilemma, likability, prosocial behavior, attribution theory

## Introduction

User-generated content (UGC), defined as organic content created by the general public on social media, has demonstrated both affordances and constraints in collective engagement (Vaast et al., 2017). In charitable fundraising, individual contributors can self-disclose their prosocial activities and mention the organizers and corporate sponsors of the prosocial activities to serve as their “wingmen” and increase their visibility. Such prosocial disclosures are critical to awareness building for the organizers, as 39% of millennial donors are inspired to give by social media (Ripley, 2018) and

30% of traffic to nonprofits’ websites comes from social media (Strub, 2020). Prosocial disclosures are also essential to endorsing corporate sponsors, who could suffer from public cynicism if they self-disclose their socially responsible efforts (Capriotti, 2011; Cho et al., 2017; Wongpitch et al., 2016).

People face the “braggart’s dilemma” when making prosocial disclosures: by disclosing their prosocial acts, individuals may be perceived negatively as self-promoting or even selfish, defeating the purpose of content generation; by staying silent, they fail to portray a socially preferable image and share their valued causes (Berman et al., 2015). The braggart’s dilemma is both a theoretical riddle and a practical challenge because it could lead to people’s silence about their prosocial efforts and the failure of social media to reach its full potential in communicating valuable causes, rewarding socially responsible corporate sponsors, and raising awareness for charitable activity organizers.

We focus on an emergent digital design that addresses the braggart’s dilemma by mitigating the negative perceptions of selfishness and stimulating the positive perceptions of selflessness when people self-disclose prosocial efforts that involve organizers and corporate sponsors on social media (Majchrzak et al., 2016). The digital process of self-disclosure is often operationalized with a pre-designed message from facilitators of this activity—for example, “I donated to UNICEF, please join me in supporting their great cause,” that participants can post to social media with a single click following their prosocial acts. Other users can react to such prosocial disclosures with the digital reaction of likes to reinforce the self-disclosure behavior (Batenburg & Bartels, 2017; Fulk et al., 1987).

We draw on attribution theory to understand the perception of self-disclosures, as reflected in the digital reaction of likes (Kelley & Michela, 1980). Attribution theory concerns how people make causal inferences by ascribing causes to events and has been widely used to understand social media content about prosocial participation (Dunn & Harness, 2018). In all studies, we utilized the context of a mobile distance tracking application (app) that connects users who run, walk, or bike to a sponsor who promises to donate a fixed amount of money to a nonprofit organization (e.g., Feeding America, Stand Up to Cancer, Autism Speaks) for every mile users complete. This app encourages users to share the physical exercise they perform to benefit a charity organization on social media and prompts them to share a pre-designed message after completing each workout benefitting a charity to ease the social sharing process—for example, “I ran 2.5 miles for @RedCross.”

We used a mixed methods approach in performing four studies to understand prosocial disclosures from the perspective of shared social responsibility (SSR), an improved corporate social responsibility (CSR) model where organizers and sponsors of prosocial activities engage individuals to cocreate value (Gneezy et al., 2010). We collected field data about users’ self-disclosure of participation in a distancetracking app on Twitter.<sup>2</sup> Study 1 shows that a higher level of self-effort leads to more likes on UGC. Study 2 shows that expressing gratitude for sponsors’ efforts leads to more likes on UGC. While it is intuitive that higher individual effort leads to more likes, it is counterintuitive that acknowledging the contribution of others may also lead to the same outcome. In Study 3, we dissected this paradox by designing a lab experiment to separate the effects of mentioning and thanking another contributor, who could either be an organizer or a sponsor. We found that an increase in likes only occurred when gratitude was expressed for the other contributor and that the increase in likes was stronger when gratitude was communicated to the organizer versus the sponsor. When simply mentioning others’ efforts without expressing gratitude, the individual received fewer likes than when not mentioning others’ efforts at all. In Study 4, we further examined the role of sponsor reputation. We found that thanking a sponsor with a good reputation led to more likes than thanking a sponsor with a bad reputation. Interestingly, our findings indicate that expressing gratitude to a sponsor is better than not mentioning the sponsor at all, even when the sponsor has a bad reputation.

Our study contributes to the understanding of the collective affordances and restrictions of UGC that disclose an individual’s prosocial activities. Past information systems studies have mostly focused on the affordance of technology for individuals or small groups, while our study goes beyond individual affordance to incorporate multiple stakeholders (corporate sponsors and organizers) and analyzes the collective affordance of social media (Vaast et al., 2017). By contrasting the impact of disclosing self-efforts and efforts made by others and by analyzing the framing of the UGC, we answer the call of Majchrzak et al. (2016) to investigate the nuanced impact of incorporating other stakeholders in the selfdisclosure of prosocial activities. Our findings provide solutions to the braggart’s dilemma and shed light on the social media strategies that lead to a more humane digital world where individuals can effectively share and promote their prosocial acts without being denigrated as braggarts. This speaks to social media’s “affect” element to benefit the charitable fundraising ecosystem with grassroots power (Briones et al., 2011; Kane et al., 2014), which is becoming increasingly important in light of the major transformations that the social media landscape is undergoing.

## Theoretical Foundation

## The UGC of Prosocial Behavior

User-generated content (UGC) is defined as content “created outside professional routines and practices” (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010, p. 61). Following the advent of Web 2.0,

UGC has become one of the key byproducts of interactive media. It takes various forms, including Twitter tweets, Facebook statuses, YouTube videos, online answers to questions, and online product reviews (Duan et al., 2008; Gunarathne et al., 2018; Li & Hitt, 2008; Susarla et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2021). UGC is considered an important information and communication technology (ICT) artifact capable of impacting the success of crowdfunding projects (Hong et al., 2018), people’s investment decisions (Qiu & Kumar, 2017), and charitable giving decisions (Tan et al., 2021). While past studies typically focus on the positive impact of UGC to highlight the affordances of social media, research has identified its negative impact to address the constraints of social media (Chan et al., 2016). Researchers have also extracted different textual features of UGC (e.g., benevolence, credibility, subjectivity, readability, sentiment, and length) to understand its qualitative features (Archak et al., 2011; Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006; Sonnier et al., 2011), but no prior study has examined the role of effort by the user and others in prosocial disclosures to understand the digital design of such messages.

Research has shown that users generate content on social media based on both intrinsic and image-related motivations (Shi et al., 2014; Toubia & Stephen, 2013) and that content quality is positively affected by the digital reaction of likes (Qiu & Kumar, 2017). The digital reaction of “likes” is critical in UGC, as it reflects endorsement and approval, which are essential to the likability of the content generator. People’s likability has a long-lasting impact on their development, success, relationships, and happiness (Prinstein, 2017). Likable individuals are more likely to show compassion and build personal connections with others (Stoler, 1963). In the workplace, likable employees are more likely to get better service evaluations (Jayanti & Whipple, 2008). Therefore, it is a human tendency to disclose one’s socially preferable characteristics, such as altruism, generosity, and kindness, to improve likability (Leary et al., 1995). Likability is also considered a persuasion tactic and a scheme of self-presentation (Reysen, 2005). Due to its asynchronous nature, social media allows individuals to spend sufficient time crafting messages to express positive attitudes and portray desirable attributes (Arnhold, 2008; Buffardi et al., 2008). As such, social media is a desirable platform for users to portray a likable image (Batenburg & Bartels, 2017). As evidence, Sekhon et al. (2015) consider microblogging messages to be part of an autobiographical narrative for the attainment of positive selfpresentation (Geva et al., 2019). Female politicians were found to leverage interpersonal self-framing effectively to showcase a likable attribute on Twitter (Fountaine, 2017). Brandtzaeg and Haugstveit (2014) found that self-enhancing social posts on Facebook lead to more likes and comments.

Several studies have explored the factors that govern the digital reaction of likes to content. Lee et al. (2019) and Lee et al. (2018) examine the factors that drive likes in marketergenerated content (MGC), which is content created by marketers on behalf of their organizations to engage consumers (Goh et al., 2013). By construction, MGC is intended to serve external causes (e.g., monetary returns) (Choi et al., 2017; Goh et al., 2013; Scholz et al., 2013; Song et al., 2019), making it distinct from UGC, which is usually driven by internal causes (e.g., altruism). We contribute to the literature by analyzing UGC that self-discloses prosocial behavior through the lens of attribution theory.

## UGC and Attribution Theory

Attribution theory concerns “how people make causal explanations, about how they answer questions beginning with ‘why’” (Mizerski et al., 1979, p. 123). It has been widely applied in social and economic circumstances to understand how the social perceiver explains the causes of behavior and events from information input (Kelley & Michela, 1980; Mizerski et al., 1979), focusing, for example, on support for women with postpartum depression (Ruybal et al., 2017) and people’s response to social transgression in the COVID-19 pandemic (Nguyen et al., 2021; Yao & Siegel, 2021). It has also been used to understand businessrelated actions such as consumers’ purchase decisions (Mizerski et al., 1979), consumers’ perceptions of advertiser credibility (Hansen & Scott, 1976), and consumers’ perceptions of companies’ strategies in reputational crises (Zamani et al., 2015). Given the growing social connectedness on digital platforms, causal attribution has great potential to guide the design of digital platforms where people actively disclose opinions, beliefs, and behaviors.

The self-disclosure of prosocial behavior could be attributed to internal causes such as altruism (Unger, 1991) or selfpromotion (Berman et al., 2015). Such an attribution process is dispositional, as the behavior is attributed to personality, intrinsic motives, or beliefs. The objective of this study is to understand UGC designs that stimulate the positive perception of altruism and mitigate the negative perception of self-promotion (Jones & Pittman, 1982), bragging (Berman et al., 2015), and boasting (Brown & Levinson, 1987). Scopelliti et al. (2015) documented these negative perceptions, finding that perceivers, especially friends, are very likely to feel annoyed by self-promotion. The reasons are threefold. First, self-promotion is considered a negative personal trait in most cultures (Ze’ev, 1993). Second, selfpromotion often leads to social comparison, which causes psychological discomfort for the recipient (Hoorens et al.,

2012). Third, the negative perception becomes worse when a person self-promotes prosocial behavior. To be perceived as more likable, one’s prosocial behavior needs to be based on selflessness. However, self-promotion may actually be based on the furtherance of one’s self-interest, making such self-enhancing activities self-defeating (Berman et al., 2015). Self-disclosure content could be designed to augment the attribution to external causes to reduce negative perceptions. For example, an individual’s participation in a prosocial activity could be attributed to the activity being well-organized or the involvement of a specific sponsor. Such an attribution process is situational because the behavioral cause is assigned to external or environmental features.

We draw on attribution theory to explain the audience’s digital reaction of likes to the self-disclosure of prosocial behavior (Nisbett et al., 1975). First, according to Jones and Davis (1965), choices play an important role in dispositional attribution. Individuals choose among a set of candidate actions. If a person chooses one action voluntarily, this action contributes more to the attribution process. Second, commonality is a critical factor of attribution. For each action, there could be multiple possible causes. When an action corresponds to fewer causes, this action is more useful for inference. For example, if a person prefers to go to the Caribbean instead of Brazil on vacation, hardly any inference can be made about the cause. If this person chooses the Caribbean over Nepal, inferences can be drawn that this person likes the beach. Third, the desirability of the action also matters. The more undesirable an action is, the more likely it is that the action will be used for inference. For example, if a person sits on a bus seat, nothing can be inferred from this normal behavior. However, if this person sits on the bus floor, inferences can be drawn from this unusual behavior. Fourth, salience also plays an important role in attribution. In McArthur and Post (1977), one of two interacting individuals in an image was made more salient by being more brightly illuminated. Perceivers attributed the more salient person’s behavior to dispositional factors. Fifth, Kelley (1987) proposed the augmentation principle, which posits more attribution of a behavior-correspondent disposition for contraindicated behavior; the less expected behavior is taken as revealing a stronger correspondent disposition because it is less likely to be caused by situational pressures. Finally, Kelley and Michela (1980) emphasized the role of consistency in attribution. When people behave in a consistent manner, the number of observations needed to make a judgment decreases. For example, in their experiment, consistency in time and modality permits perceivers to access information about performers’ competence.

## Hypothesis Development

Drawing on attribution theory, we develop a system of four hypotheses concerning the digital reaction of likes to selfdisclosure of prosocial behavior on social media. In our first two hypotheses, we predict that likes are positively associated with users’ self-effort and negatively associated with others efforts. In our third hypothesis, we highlight the importance of message framing in the self-disclosure of prosocial efforts. We predict that expressing gratitude for others’ efforts leads to the positive reaction of likes. In our fourth hypothesis, we predict that the sponsor’s reputation affects the social reaction of likes. These hypotheses center on the shared social responsibility framework, which involves multiple stakeholders of the fundraising ecosystem.

We used the principle of desirability to predict that higher selfeffort in a prosocial activity leads to more likes. As stated in the section on theoretical foundation, an undesirable action can be more readily used for inference (Kelley & Michela, 1980). Indeed, according to the literature on charitable giving, efforts made by fundraisers that cause discomfort may result in more prosocial contributions by the self and others (Olivola & Shafir, 2013; Olivola & Shafir, 2018). People deliberately seek uncomfortable experiences when fundraising. Fundraising activities such as charity walk-a-thons and marathons require fundraisers to exert physical effort; some fundraising activities even require people to dump icy water on their heads (Sohn, 2017), endure fasting for more than 24 hours, or walk barefoot on broken glass (Olivola & Shafir, 2013). However, when individuals engage in moderate exercise to benefit a charity, their efforts may be attributed to self-interest because moderate exercise is beneficial for their health (Newman & Cain, 2014; Simon, 1993). Thus, we anticipate that a high level of effort is needed for exercise benefitting a charity to be attributed to altruistic motivations because higher fundraising effort is associated with higher perceptions of altruism due to higher perceptions of sacrifice (Simon, 1993). Since positive internal causes for prosocial activity (e.g., altruism) are rewarded in social networks (Berman et al., 2015; Nowak & Sigmund, 2005), we predict that self-disclosure reporting a higher level of effort leads to more likes.

H1: Reporting a higher level of self-effort in one’s selfdisclosure of prosocial behavior increases the digital reaction of likes.

We further use the principles of commonality to predict a decrease in likes when other participants’ efforts are acknowledged. A prosocial activity usually involves multiple stakeholders, such as organizers who design and organize the activity, corporate sponsors who financially support it, and individual participants who devote effort or money to the cause. When individuals self-disclose their participation, if they also mention the efforts made by the organizers or sponsors, their self-disclosure not only communicates their own prosocial behavior but also generates awareness for the organizer and boosts the reputation of the sponsor, possibly improving their business performance (Capriotti, 2011; Cho et al., 2017; Varadarajan & Menon, 1988). This type of UGC communicates the value proposition of the organizer and the social consciousness of the sponsor more effectively than direct communication from the organizer or the sponsor, which is often treated skeptically (Cho et al., 2017; Wongpitch et al., 2016).

Despite these desirable features of this type of UGC, acknowledging the efforts made by the organizer and the sponsor conveys alternative causes for the individuals’ prosocial acts. According to Kelley and Michela (1980, p. 462), “the intention underlying a voluntary act is most clearly evident when it has a small number of effects that are unique to it.” Instead of being perceived to do so for internal causes such as altruism, the individual could be suspected of participating because the organizer spent dedicated efforts managing the activity or because the sponsor provided great support. Either way, acknowledging other parties’ efforts reduces the uniqueness of the cause to the actions undertaken by the individual, leading to situational attribution. This is especially the case when perceivers evaluate others because people usually attribute others’ prosocial activities to situational factors and their own prosocial activities to dispositional factors (Tetlock, 1985).

Acknowledging the efforts of others, however, could have a positive impact on likes. According to the principle of choices, the voluntary disclosure of other contributors could signal one’s selflessness. The principle of salience would also predict that mentioning others could shift the perceivers’ attention away from self-promotion. However, these mechanisms are unlikely to manifest in a dominant way because mentioning other participants is situationally appropriate and within expectation. The expected behavior is discounted as an indication of disposition. Thus, we predict that mentioning the effort of others leads to a reduced number of likes.

H2: Reporting the effort of other parties in one’s selfdisclosure of prosocial behavior decreases the digital reaction of likes.

We further develop a hypothesis regarding the expression of gratitude for others in the self-disclosure of prosocial behavior based on the principles of choice, augmentation, and salience. The expression of gratitude for others’ contributions could potentially reduce the digital reaction of likes as it also conveys alternative causes for individuals’ prosocial efforts— individuals’ participation could be attributed to the efforts made by other participants, as posited by the principle of commonality. However, in many cultures, being modest about prosocial behavior is a virtue (Lin-Healy & Small, 2013). People who express gratitude toward others are perceived to be less egocentric and thus less selfish because when highlighting (differing from simply mentioning) others contributions, they reduce the expression of their own social consciousness. Thus, according to the principle of choice, the voluntary expression of gratitude for others’ efforts strengthens the inference of selflessness, establishing one’s positive image as a modest and humble person, potentially reducing the audience’s suspicion of self-promotion and increasing likes. More importantly, the principle of augmentation predicts that an individual is more likely to hold a behavior-correspondent attitude when expressing gratitude for other contributors than when simply mentioning other contributors because the latter is more likely to be situationappropriate and expected behavior (Kelley, 1987). Further, the expression of gratitude is more likely than simply mentioning the other participants to shift people’s attention away from the individual’s self-disclosure behavior because the positive emotion associated with the expression of gratitude attracts more attention than simply mentioning other contributors (Lang et al., 2007). As such, we predict that expressing gratitude for others’ efforts positively impacts likes.

## H3: Expressing gratitude for the effort of other parties (i.e., organizers and sponsors) in one’s self-disclosure of prosocial behavior increases the digital reaction of likes.

We further discuss the role of sponsor reputation on the likability of reporting individuals. Corporate reputation is defined as the collective representation of the past images of an organization (Cornelissen, 2020). We focus on corporate sponsors because sponsors can have varying reputations, unlike organizers, who usually have good reputations. More importantly, sponsors have more motivation to participate in corporate social responsibility activities when they have a negative reputation (Werbel & Wortman, 2000).

According to attribution theory, the consistency of information plays a critical role in the attribution process (Kelley & Michela, 1980). The inference is more likely to be made when corporate sponsors make consistent choices over time (Mizerski et al., 1979). When companies with a bad reputation participate in corporate social responsibility activities such as sponsoring exercise for charity, they are more susceptible to skepticism (Bae & Cameron, 2006). This is because the lower the reputation of the company, the more likely the company will be to act to improve its reputation. This vicious circle often makes prosocial efforts seem manipulative and reputation-driven (Elving, 2010). Past research has identified the link between a bad reputation and skepticism toward a company (Elving, 2010). We go beyond the extant literature to propose that individual participants who mention sponsors’ efforts are affected by sponsors’ bad reputations because sponsors are enablers of individuals’ prosocial efforts and individuals’ endorsement of the sponsorship implies their approval of the sponsor’s attempt to improve their corporate reputation. When an individual discloses a sponsoring relationship with a corporation, this individual establishes a nonformal collaborative relationship with the corporate sponsor, allowing the negative reputation of the sponsor to spill over to the individual.

Alternatively, a lower level of likes could be directly tied to the social perceiver’s attitude toward the sponsor-related content. Muda and Hamzah (2021) identified the positive impact of attitude toward brand-related UGC on purchase intentions and the decision to share the content. Similarly, the bad reputation of a sponsor could evoke social perceivers’ negative attitudes toward the content and thus reduce likes.

H4: Acknowledging or thanking sponsors with a negative reputation negatively affects the digital reaction of likes.

## Analyses and Results

## Research Context and Design

We collected social media data and designed lab experiments based on the distance tracker mobile app Charity Miles. Charity Miles was founded in 2012 and has since raised \$3 million for nonprofit organizations and helped create more than one million user workout records. The app aims to help corporate sponsors spend their advertising budget in a revolutionary way by monetarily supporting app users who participate in exercise for charity.<sup>3</sup> When users open the app, they are asked to choose from more than 40 charities to support, including Feeding America, Stand Up to Cancer, Autism Speaks, and Wounded Warrior Project. As users walk, run, or bike, the app tracks the distance, with walkers and runners earning 25 cents per mile and bikers earning 10 cents per mile. The app also displays the corporate sponsor’s banner for the activity—for example, Timex, Johnson & Johnson, or Brooks Running (Gurkoff, 2018). After every workout, the app encourages users to share their contributions on social media. To ease the sharing process, a message template is provided that includes the type of exercise, the number of miles, and the relevant charity. An example is “I biked 15.251 @CharityMiles for @EveryMomCounts.” This message can be broadcast to any social media platform; we focused on the social networking site of Twitter. During our observation, one major template update took place. On November 11, 2013, the app permanently changed the post template to express gratitude to the sponsors with an additional sentence (e.g., “Thx2 @GNC for sponsoring me!”). This template update inspired us to examine the digital reaction of likes to users self-disclosure of exercise for charity both empirically and through lab experiments.

In the following sections, we present four studies (summarized in Table 1). The first two studies are empirical analyses based on an initial collection of 756,724 tweets<sup>4</sup> containing the keyword “@CharityMiles” between September 2012 and September 2016. The data contains the creator’s ID and the number of likes for each tweet. If a tweet did not follow the standard template by reporting miles, activity, and charity, we removed it from our sample (<0.04%). For Study 1, we used 104,636 Charity Miles tweets one year after the template change to test H1. For Study 2, we focused on Charity Miles tweets before and after the template change, with supplemented data of users’ other tweets not associated with Charity Miles. We included users who posted both types of tweets before and after the template change to guarantee comparability and ended up with 9,687 tweets from 285 Twitter users to identify the effect of thanking sponsors in the self-disclosure of prosocial behavior, testing H3. We performed two experimental studies to complement our empirical studies. Study 3 (203 participants) was designed to test H2 and H3 simultaneously by comparing the impact of acknowledging organizers and sponsors with that of expressing gratitude toward those stakeholders. Study 4 (431 participants) was designed to test H4 by examining the factor of sponsor reputation on likes for tweets generated by individuals.

## Study 1: Effort of Self

In Study 1, we leveraged the standardized template for individuals to self-disclose exercising for charity to gauge the role of efforts undertaken by the self on the digital reaction of likes. We extracted two effort-related factors from the selfdisclosure of prosocial participation: exercise type and exercise intensity. For the example above, “I biked 15.251 @CharityMiles for @EveryMomCounts,” the exercise type is “biked,” and the effort intensity is “15.251 miles.”

## Empirical Model and Identifications

We used Charity Miles tweets one year after the template change to evaluate the impact of self-effort, and this choice of time frame ensured that our analysis was not affected by the template change. We constructed three dummy variables (Ran, Walked, and Biked) for each type of exercise and a continuous variable to document the number of miles (Miles). Usually, running is perceived to require more effort than walking and biking because an average person will burn between 80 and 140 calories per mile while running, 40 and 80 calories while biking, and 60 and 100 calories while walking. Biking requires equipment and planning, but the average effort per mile is the lowest. Both the number of miles and the activity types are indicators of the effort exerted by the user. In the context of the chosen activity, higher numbers of miles correlate with greater effort and higher levels of discomfort (Kelley & Michela, 1980).

Since all tweets were generated after the template change, a corporate sponsor and a charity can be identified in every tweet. We construct a series of dummies to represent the sponsors because the public perception of these sponsors could influence the reaction of likes and the message template for each corporate sponsor is slightly different. We also identified each charity and categorized it into one of the five categories: education, environment, medical, society, and sports. We further controlled for the length of a tweet (ContentLen), the number of days since the user’s last Charity Miles tweet (DaysSinceLast), and the number of tweets generated on the user’s last active day (NumTweetsLast). We also controlled for time effects at the week level and individual-level fixed effects. The individual-level fixed effects account for any time-unvarying unobservables that may affect the number of likes—tweets generated by the same individual are likely to receive a similar number of likes (Xu et al. 2019). The weekly fixed effects account for time effects, such as an increase in followers. We summarize the variable statistics in Tables A1 and A2 of the Appendix. To model the outcome of likes, we used a negative binomial model since the number of likes is an overdispersed count variable. We used i to denote individuals and j to denote tweets. On average, biking did not significantly differ from walking in terms of likes (insignificant coefficient for Biked in Models 1 through 3). However, Model 4 reveals the difference between biking and walking. When Miles < 1.58, biking resulted in more likes than walking. Otherwise, walking led to more likes than biking. While the coefficient for Biked is positive and significant (β = 0.0826, p < 0.01), the coefficient for Biked × Miles is negative and significant (β = -0.0103, p < 0.01). That is, the choice of biking corresponds to more likes than walking, but the marginal effect of miles is lower for biking compared to walking.<sup>7</sup> The choice of biking could result in a stronger inference of altruism because biking requires the extra effort of preparation and planning. Meanwhile, the lower marginal increase of likes per mile for biking compared to walking could be justified by the lower effort required for biking, excluding the setup cost. Also, the charitable contribution sponsored per mile is strictly lower for biking. Taken together, our findings suggest that exerting more effort in exercise for charity leads to more likes, supporting our first hypothesis (H1).

After performing all checks, our results remain unchanged.

## Study 2: Effort of Others

In Study 2, we evaluated the template change to extend the individual’s self-disclosure by incorporating a thank you note to a corporate sponsor who financially supported the prosocial act. For example: “I biked 19.251 @CharityMiles for @EveryMomCounts. Thx2 @GNC for sponsoring me!” In contrast to Study 1, which emphasized self-effort, Study 2 was designed to evaluate the role of others’ effort on the perception of a focal individual’s likability.

## Empirical Model and Identifications

To show that this template change applied to all the Charity Miles-related tweets, in Figure 1, we illustrate the number of Charity Miles tweets that included (dashed line) and did not include (solid line) the keywords “Thx” and “Thank.” We clearly see that Charity Miles tweets generated before November 11, 2013, did not contain these keywords, and posts generated after that date included them.<sup>8</sup>

We first performed a before-and-after analysis with only Charity Miles tweets to assess the average effect of the template. We then constructed a difference-in-differences (DiD) model to estimate the impact of this template change by contrasting the likes for Charity Miles tweets and the likes for other ordinary tweets generated by the same set of users before and after this template update. The DiD estimator is widely used to analyze policy impacts while accounting for temporal trends (Angrist & Pischke, 2009). Following Xu et al. (2019), we modified the classic DiD setting to accommodate our research context, where we observed users’ repetitive postings of tweets. Xu et al. (2019) compared users’ answers and edits on stack overflow before and after job changes. We set individuals’ Charity Miles tweets as the treatment group and set these users’ tweets that were not associated with Charity Miles as the control group. The dummy variable CharityMiles took the value of 1 when a tweet followed the Charity Miles template and 0 otherwise. The inclusion of the control group accounted for temporal effects such as users’ increasing or decreasing the level of interaction with their followers.

Our observation is between October 21, 2013, and December 2, 2013, corresponding to three weeks before and after the template change. Since the template change is manifested immediately rather than gradually (Figure 1), the dummy variable After took the value of 1 in the post-intervention period and 0 otherwise. We chose this observation window because we wanted to avoid the holiday effects that may have occurred after December 2, 2013. Our robustness checks show that our findings are not sensitive to the choice of the time window (two weeks or four weeks before and after the template change).

Based on the common trend assumption of the DiD approach, the trend of likes should be the same for both ordinary tweets and Charity Miles tweets in the absence of the message template change (Angrist & Pischke, 2009). We illustrate the common trend in Figure 2a, where we observe clear evidence of the common trend before the template change introduced a deviation from this common trend. Note that the y-axis refers to the average number of likes per tweet for the given week. To improve robustness, we included more data points before and after the template change in Appendix Figure A1, and the common trend assumption continues to hold. In Appendix Figure B1. As in Study 1, we employed a negative binomial model to accommodate the count outcome of likes. We first ran an analysis with only Charity Miles tweets. We used the dummy variable After to identify the average effect of the template change. We then performed the DiD analysis, where the coefficient of interest was the interaction between After and CharityMiles to measure the cross-difference of the conditional expectation of the observed outcome minus the cross-difference of the conditional expectation of the potential outcome without treatment (Athey & Imbens, 2006). This coefficient reveals the causal impact of the template change by comparing the before-after difference in terms of likes for ordinary tweets and Charity Miles tweets. This model is specified in Equation (2):

$$
f \left(L i k e _ {i j} \mid \lambda_ {i j}, \theta_ {i}\right) = \frac {\Gamma \left(\lambda_ {i j} + L i k e _ {i j}\right)}{\Gamma \left(\lambda_ {i j}\right) \Gamma \left(L i k e _ {i j} + 1\right)} \cdot \left(\frac {\theta_ {i}}{1 + \theta_ {i}}\right) ^ {L i k e _ {i j}} \cdot \left(\frac {1}{1 + \theta_ {i}}\right) ^ {\lambda_ {i j}},\tag{2}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{c} \ln \lambda_ {i j} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} A f t e r + \beta_ {2} C h a r i t y M i l e s _ {i j} + \beta_ {3} A f t e r \times C h a r i t y M i l e s _ {i j} + \beta_ {4} E m o t i o n _ {i j} + \\ \beta_ {5} S e n t i m e n t _ {i j} + \beta_ {6} D a y s S i n c e L a s t _ {i j} + \beta_ {7} N u m T w e e t s L a s t _ {i j} + \beta_ {8} C o n t e n t L e n _ {i j} + \beta_ {9} W e e k _ {i j}. \end{array}
$$

We controlled for the number of days since users’ last tweet (DaysSinceLast) and the length of the tweet (ContentLen). We included weekly dummies (Week) to account for any time-level fixed effects, and we accounted for individual-level fixed effects. We further controlled for the sentiment of content (Sentiment) and the emotion of content (Emotion)<sup>9</sup> to account for the diverse content in ordinary tweets. Both the sentiment scores and the emotion scores were calculated using the NRC Word-Emotion Association Lexicon (Mohammad, 2012). The summary statistics of this dataset are reported in Appendix Table A3. Overall, about 21% of the tweets were related to Charity Miles, and about 79% of tweets were not. Tweets that received no likes decreased by about 5% for Charity Miles tweets after the template change compared to a less than 0.3% decrease for ordinary tweets. This evidence shows that the template change likely increased the number of likes.

We also learned from the negatively significant coefficient of CharityMiles that Charity Miles tweets generally received fewer likes than ordinary tweets. This could be because Charity Miles tweets followed a standard template and were less original than other content. We found that lengthy content received fewer likes (negatively significant coefficient of ContentLen) and that users who had not posted for a while received more likes (positively significant coefficient of DaysSinceLast). Further, content with negative sentiment corresponds to fewer likes, and joyful content is related to more likes (negatively significant coefficient for Negative and positive coefficient for Joy). While these findings are not central to the research focus of this paper, they offer interesting insights into the social reaction to UGC.

While the findings in Study 2 provide strong support for H3, we did not observe from the empirical context how the outcome of likes would be affected by a simple mention of others’ efforts (without expressing gratitude). In the next two studies, we used lab experiments to test H2 and H4.<sup>12</sup> The lab experiments were designed to separate the effect of mentioning other participants and thanking other participants for the prosocial act.

## Experiment Design

We recruited 250 U.S. participants via Prolific to participate in an online study in exchange for \$1 plus a bonus of up to \$1. Prolific is an online research platform that helps researchers recruit participants for online research. One author processed the experimental data, and the experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of this author’s university (protocol # 1903114361). To start, participants were presented with an introduction of the context: “Charity Run is a free app that enables you to earn money for charity whenever you run. Zenet Corporation is a corporate sponsor for Charity Run; it is a leading vitamin and dietary supplement company in the US.” In this experiment, we did not use Charity Miles but employed a synthesized app: Charity Run; similarly, we used a fake sponsor, Zenet, which does not exist in the real world. Such designs allowed us to avoid a potential perception bias people may have about Charity Miles or a real corporate sponsor (Elving, 2010; Yoon et al., 2006). Participants were then presented with the scenario of a hypothetical colleague who self-disclosed his prosocial activity on social media. They were told, “Imagine that you follow a colleague John (@JohnSmith) on Twitter. Other than talking about his life, John sometimes shares his charity run activity on Twitter.”

Following past works examining user perceptions of social media content or platform designs (Ananthakrishnan et al., 2020; Moravec et al., 2022), we presented each participant with five tweets displayed in random order: a baseline tweet (Figure 3a) and four treatment tweets (Figures 3b through 3e). All tweets included the self-disclosure of prosocial behavior, “I ran 5.178 miles for @Redcross,” which is the baseline tweet. Two tweets (Figures 3b and 3c) corresponded to the expression of gratitude, with 3b extending gratitude to the sponsor and 3c extending gratitude to the organizer. Two tweets (Figures 3d and 3e) corresponded to the mention of other stakeholders, with 3d mentioning the efforts made by the sponsor and 3e mentioning the efforts made by the organizer.

A challenge in synchronizing our empirical and experimental studies is that empirical studies measure the outcome of likes and experimental studies measure the perception of likability or the intention to like a tweet. We addressed this challenge by adapting the “trust game,” which is widely used in behavioral economics. This technique originates from prisoner’s dilemma games (Nash, 1950) and has been adopted in the humancomputer interaction (Davis et al., 2002) and information systems literatures (Ananthakrishnan et al., 2020) to predict behaviors. We followed Ananthakrishnan et al. (2020) in designing a game where participants were given \$1 worth of virtual chips that they could use to bet on which tweets would receive the most likes. They could bet on one or more tweets. They were told that their bet should reflect their best guess based on their own judgment. Not betting was not an option. They were paid a bonus equivalent to the number of chips they bet on the tweet (in addition to the participation remuneration) if the tweet was determined to have actually attracted the most likes.

![](/api/attachments/NXMKWS88/fulltext/images/9cbcfbc7f427a1ea1a70b02136941d8d60427846fa1fc9c004a59bd4193d275d.jpg)

We performed a manipulation check to ascertain that our treatment conditions reflected the underlying mechanisms regarding effort and self-promoting tendencies. Specifically, after participants made their bets, they reviewed each tweet to report their perceptions, with the tweets displayed in random order. To gauge the perceived effort, we created a two-item effort scale. Participants rated: (1) “John exerts a lot of effort in the activity he reported” and (2) “John exerts little effort in the activity he reported” on five-point scales ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. The Cronbach’s α was 0.915. The perceived effort made by the reporting individual was significantly less when the organizer was mentioned (mean = 3.65) and when the sponsor was mentioned (mean = 3.58) than the baseline condition where neither party was mentioned (mean = 4.05). The difference was significant at the level of 5%. We also created a three-item self-promotion scale, where participants rated: (1) “John likes to show off if he gets the chance,” (2) “John likes to impress others,” and (3) “John likes to be complimented” on five-point scales ranging from 1 = strongly agree to 5 = strongly disagree. This measure was adapted from Berman et al. (2015). The Cronbach’s α was 0.853. The perceived self-promoting tendency was significantly less when thank you notes were directed toward the sponsor (mean = 3.51) or the organizer (mean = 3.47) than they were in the baseline condition (mean = 3.02). The difference was significant at the level of 10%. It is worth mentioning that simply mentioning the sponsor (mean = 3.36) or the organizer (mean = 3.23) did not lead to a significantly different selfpromotion tendency than the baseline condition.<sup>13</sup>

We asked three comprehension check questions, and the failure to answer these questions correctly resulted in the termination of the study. In the first comprehension check, participants chose whether John contributed to Red Cross or Habitat for Humanity. In the second question, participants selected who sponsored the charity run: John, Zenet, or Charity Run. In the third comprehension check, participants were asked to identify the author of the post: myself, my friend John, or Zenet. In total, 203 participants correctly answered all comprehension check questions and remained in our study. Finally, we collected participants’ demographic information. The average age was 37.3, with the minimum age being 18 and the maximum being 69. Further, 100 participants were male, 102 were female, and one participant was nonbinary or a third gender.

## Findings

In this experiment, each participant split the bet of \$1 among the five conditions based on their judgment. Participants assigned an equal amount of \$0.20 to each condition if they believed that these tweets had the equivalent capability of eliciting likes. In our data, two participants chose to allocate their bet equally across conditions. Most participants chose to allocate their bet in a disproportional way to reflect their preferences. We summarize the average bet for each condition in Figure 4. Compared to the baseline condition, simply mentioning the effort of either the sponsor or the organizer reduced the likes. However, thanking either the sponsor or the organizer resulted in more likes. More interestingly, thanking the organizer resulted in a much higher increase in likes than thanking the sponsor.

We further examined the results of the experiment in a regression. We used a dummy variable Organizer to indicate whether the organizer was included in the message and a dummy variable Sponsor to indicate whether the sponsor was included in the message. When both variables took the value of 0, it referred to our baseline condition. We used an additional dummy variable Thanks to indicate whether the message was framed to express gratitude to either the sponsor or the organizer; it took the value of 1 if a thank you note was included. The regression results are reported in Table 4, with Model 9 being the main specification and Model 10 including the interaction between Thanks and Sponsor. Since each participant was restricted to the fixed budget amount of \$1, this design normalized the outcome at an individual level to remove individual heterogeneity. Consistent with the modelfree evidence above, we found from both Model 9 and Model 10 that reporting the effort of either the organizer or the sponsor led to a significant reduction in likes. This is reflected in the negative and significant coefficients for Sponsor and Organizer. Reporting the efforts of a sponsor led to a higher reduction in likes, possibly because sponsors are associated with a stronger commercial intent than organizers. However, the thank-you note significantly increased likes for the reporting individual, as reflected in the positive and significant coefficient for Thanks. The positive effect of the thank-you note on likes was stronger than the negative effect of reporting others’ efforts on likes, resulting in an overall increase in likes when gratitude to other parties was expressed.

Further, Model 10 revealed that thanking an organizer versus a sponsor led to more likes; the coefficient for Thanks × Sponsor is negatively significant. This could also be a result of the higher commercial intent associated with sponsors rather than organizers, which could activate perceivers persuasion knowledge, making the positive effect of Thanks less salient. These findings confirm both H2 and H3 that simply mentioning the effort of other stakeholders decreases likes but expressing gratitude to other stakeholders increases likes, highlighting the importance of framing in the selfdisclosure of prosocial activities.

## Study 4: The Reputation of Other Contributors

We accounted for the reputation of the sponsor in Study 1 by including sponsor dummies and in Study 3 by using a fake sponsor; in Study 4, we explicitly evaluated the role of the sponsor’s reputation. A reputable sponsor’s prosocial efforts are more likely to be considered consistent, leading to a lower level of skepticism and a higher level of likes (Bae & Cameron, 2006; Mizerski et al., 1979).

## Experiment Design

Following and extending the experimental design in Study 3, we designed a lab experiment to include both between-subject variation and within-subject variation. We recruited 500 participants from Prolific to complete our surveys; participants were paid \$1 plus a bonus of up to \$1. Comprehension checks were performed (identical to the questions in Study 3), and 431 participants passed the screening checks. Participant ages range from 18-73, with the average age being 35.72. 154 participants were male, 269 were female, five were nonbinary or a third gender, and three participants chose not to disclose their gender.

We manipulated the reputation of the sponsor between participants, following Elving (2010). Each participant was assigned to one of three conditions. Participants assigned to the control condition (152 participants) were presented with a positive description of the fake corporate sponsor Zenet: “Zenet Corporation is a leading vitamin and dietary supplement company in the US.” Participants assigned to the first treatment group (142 participants) were presented with the same message and an additional description of a recent incident: “In 2020, a civil penalty was imposed on the company for a violation of defect notification.” Participants assigned to the second treatment group (137 participants) were presented with a negative description of the sponsor: “Zenet Corporation is a leading tobacco company in the US.” Thus, these two treatment conditions both corresponded to a bad reputation, but the causes of the bad reputation were different. Specifically, the bad reputation caused by a violation of defect notification is more temporal (BadRepTemp), and the bad reputation from being a tobacco company is more permanent (BadRepPerm). The experimental design was adapted from Yoon et al. (2006).

The interaction terms in Model 12 of Table 5 illustrate how the impact of Thanks and Sponsor differ for sponsors with different reputation levels. The interaction terms between Thanks and BadRepTemp or BadRepPerm are both negatively significant, indicating that expressing gratitude for a sponsor with a negative reputation is not as favorable as expressing gratitude for a sponsor with a positive reputation (the baseline condition). However, our findings suggest that even when the sponsor has a bad reputation, expressing gratitude for others efforts is still better than not mentioning the sponsor at all because the positive coefficient for Thanks is much greater than the negative coefficient for Thanks × BadRepTemp or Thanks × BadRepPerm. Further, the effect of simply mentioning a sponsor with a bad reputation does not differ from that of mentioning a sponsor with a good reputation because the coefficients for Sponsor × BadRepTemp or Sponsor × BadRepPerm are insignificant.

Our findings in Study 4 partially support H4 because the differential impacts between a good reputation and a bad reputation only manifest when gratitude to the sponsor is expressed and not when a sponsor is simply mentioned. These results support the spillover effect. They also imply that the informal collaborative relationship between sponsors and individuals is more likely established when gratitude is expressed than when sponsors are merely mentioned.

## Discussion

## Summary of Studies and Findings

We performed four studies to understand how to best design digital templates for individuals to self-disclose their prosocial acts while generating positive spillover effects for other stakeholders, including corporate sponsors and charitable activity organizers. In Study 1, we used 104,636 tweets that followed a specific template to report individuals’ charity workouts to show that a higher reported level of self-effort leads to more likes. In Study 2, we leveraged a unique update in the template to understand the impact of including a thank you note to corporate sponsors in self-disclosures of exercise for charity, using 9,687 tweets generated by 285 Twitter users who posted both ordinary content (the control content) and Charity Miles reports (the treatment content) both before and after the template change. We discovered a positive impact of expressing gratitude to another contributor on likes. In Study 3, we performed a lab experiment where we manipulated the role of the other contributor (sponsor or organizer) and how they were mentioned (acknowledgment or expression of gratitude). From the responses of 203 participants, we found that regardless of whether the other contributor is a sponsor or an organizer, simply acknowledging the contributor’s support leads to a reduced level of likes and that framing the contribution as a token of appreciation for the contributor leads to an increased level of likes. This study allowed us to estimate the situational attribution effect of sharing credit with another party separately from the dispositional attribution effect of expressing gratitude toward another party. In Study 4, we further improved the generalizability of our study by showing that the positive impact of expressing gratitude for sponsors holds even when the sponsor has a bad reputation.

## Theoretical Implications

Our study has rich theoretical implications. While UGC has been studied as an artifact of information and communication technology (ICT) in information systems, the prior scope has been limited to product or service reviews, online communities, and electronic knowledge management practices (Duan et al., 2008; Gu et al., 2007; Sambamurthy & Subramani, 2005). UGC that discloses individuals’ prosocial participation is a unique type of content that could be co-produced by individuals and digital platforms that facilitate prosocial activities. Despite its potential for bringing together different stakeholders of the prosocial activity (i.e., individuals, sponsors, and organizers), the prevalence of such content is restricted by potentially negative perceptions, as documented by the braggart’s dilemma (Berman et al., 2015). While past literature has looked into the negative consequences of self-promotion (Moss-Racusin &

Rudman, 2010), very few works have discussed potential strategies to attenuate such negative consequences (Batenburg & Bartels, 2017). Through both empirical evidence and experimental approaches, we identify novel ways to create content that provides a potential solution to this dilemma. By disclosing significant effort by them or expressing gratitude for efforts made by other stakeholders (i.e., sponsors or organizers), individuals can alleviate the negative perceptions by increasing the perception of effort and shifting people’s attention away from self-promotion. This contributes to the emergent digital design addressing the constraints of social media.

In understanding the impact of UGC, past literature has examined the impact of content type, content valence, politeness, and various linguistic features (Lee et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2019). Effort level is a content feature that has received little attention despite its relevance in many types of UGC. For example, a respondent to an online Q&A platform can include the effort they invested in finding an answer. A reviewer can mention the effort expended to compare multiple products in a product review. In our context, exercise type and number of miles are indicators of individuals’ effort levels. This unique context allowed us to quantify and systematically study the selfeffort level. Our finding that a higher reported level of selfeffort is more socially preferable not only demonstrates the affordance of social media in communicating socially preferable attributes but also adds to the understanding of incentive designs in digital platforms to encourage user engagement (Yang et al., 2019).

Past studies in information systems have mostly focused on the positive effects of ICT artifacts, and there have been multiple calls for information systems researchers to examine unintended or negative consequences of ICT artifacts (Majchrzak et al., 2016). We found that a sponsor’s negative reputation can spill over to individuals who explicitly express appreciation for the sponsor; however, we did not find that it spills over to individuals who simply mention the contributions of others without expressing gratitude for their efforts. Such findings address the potential negative consequences of UGC that discloses individuals’ prosocial activities. More importantly, these findings shed light on when such negative consequences may occur. Platform designers should caution against these potentially negative effects on likes and optimally design the message template to encourage the sharing of prosocial activities while minimizing negative perceptions (Ketter et al., 2016). This could be done by following our application of attribution theory, which provides a lens through which findings can be better understood, enabling their broader applicability to contexts where digital platforms facilitate individuals recognizing various stakeholders.

## Practical Implications

Our study offers prescriptive suggestions for corporate sponsors. Traditional corporate social responsibility (CSR) activities include cause-related promotions, where a portion of product sales is donated to charities (Varadarajan & Menon, 1988). However, such CSR activities suffer from a low participation rate because they mainly present the company’s altruistic intention rather than the expression of an individual’s social consciousness (Gneezy et al., 2010). To combat this, Gneezy et al. (2010) proposed the strategy of shared social responsibility (SSR), which seeks to engage individuals by linking their efforts to corporate contributions. Our findings support this model by showing the link between self-effort and likes.

Our findings also shed light on the impression management of individual users on social media by showing that acknowledging corporate sponsors or organizers will not dilute the credit of individuals when the acknowledgment is properly framed to communicate socially preferable attributes. Impression management is a key component of social interaction, and past practice has focused on interpersonal communication where performers can complement the perceiver to increase their own likability. This study can be viewed as an adaptation of impression management on social media. Our finding that an individual’s expression of appreciation for another participant involved in a prosocial act could increase likability for the individual extends impression management practices to the social media environment in a way that could result in a win-win-win situation—the sponsor would gain exposure as a socially responsible entity, the organizer would gain more awareness, and the individual would be perceived as more likable.

Finally, our results generate direct implications for organizers of charitable activities. To raise awareness and encourage more charitable giving, fundraising managers usually encourage donors to share their contributions on social media through a message template (Hong et al., 2018). We offer concrete prescriptions for the design of such templates. First, these message templates need to communicate a socially preferable image of the individual. As such, it is important to prompt such content generation only when individuals have made a sufficient contribution—reporting a low level of effort may backfire and lead to negative consequences. Second, message templates should be designed to reduce perceptions of selfpromotion. Instead of focusing on one’s own contribution, the message should shift the focus to other participating parties, such as sponsors and organizers. When mentioning other parties’ efforts, framing is critical. Specifically, simply mentioning contributions results in lower likability for the reporting individual, but expressing gratitude for contributions results in higher likability for the reporting individual.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our study has certain limitations. First, we performed four studies using mixed methods to allow these studies to complement each other. Future studies could design a more unified research framework to test all hypotheses in one study (e.g., a field experiment) in order to enhance internal and external validity. Second, we examined two major antecedents of likes (i.e., perceptions of altruism and self-promotion); future studies could perform a more comprehensive investigation of antecedents for likes (e.g., the value and originality of content). While our study considers sponsors and organizers to be alternative third-party contributors, future studies could simultaneously include sponsors and organizers in the template. Moreover, we explored the outcome of likes in relation to prosocial disclosures; future studies could expand the scope of outcomes to include individuals’ subsequent decisions to share the content or perform similar prosocial activities. Further, despite our efforts to account for content exposure, we did not have data on the views of each tweet. Given the availability of view data following Elon Musk’s acquisition of Twitter, future studies could improve the control for content exposure.

In addition to these limitations, we would also like to discuss the impact of multiple transformations Twitter has gone through following Musk’s takeover of Twitter in 2022. One forecast projects that over 32 million users will exit Twitter in the two years following Musk’s acquisition (Sweney, 2022). Potential reasons for this include the mass layoff of siteperformance employees, which may lead to greater latency and even outages, as well as Musk’s new content moderation policy, which may increase hate speech on the platform (Anti-Defamation League, 2023). While Twitter is still considered the most powerful tool for social movements (Kaur, 2023), it could be substituted by other social media platforms as the social networking landscape evolves. This is an inevitable issue faced by social media studies in information systems research, and we believe a mixed methods approach can effectively address it. Since we observed consistent discoveries across Twitter data (Studies 1 and 2) and controlled lab experiments (Studies 3 and 4), our results could likely be generalized to other social networking sites where individuals self-disclose their prosocial acts and receive the digital reaction of likes. Moreover, Twitter’s policy update of the paid verification badge will potentially lead to the impersonation of brand accounts, which could increase the risk of digital marketing on Twitter. Changes in the recommendation algorithm may result in lower engagement for organizers of social movements; indeed, activists have reported greater difficulty in making connections and reaching the right audience (Kaur, 2023). In light of these changes, our findings only become more salient because prosocial disclosures represent grassroots power, which offers potential solutions for organizers and sponsors of prosocial activities seeking to combat Twitter’s policy changes. Individuals prosocial disclosures are supported by decentralized personal networks, a stark departure from verification status and recommendation algorithms, which are utilized by organizers and sponsors.

## Concluding Remarks

In social media fundraising practices, a prominent strategy has emerged where fundraisers employ a UGC template for online donors to voluntarily self-disclose their prosocial endeavors on social media to benefit a worthy cause. Drawing on attribution theory, we performed four studies to uncover the “secret recipe” for effective UGC design that not only selfdiscloses individuals’ prosocial behavior but also recognizes the contributions of other stakeholders. By following this secret recipe, individuals can present an enhanced self-image while supporting other stakeholders as “wingmen,” allowing organizers and sponsors to more effectively engage in meaningful social media conversations.

## C1. Relative Time Model

To assess the common trend assumption, we performed a relative time model (Bertrand et al., 2004; Greenwood & Agarwal, 2016). Instead of including the interactions between the dummy variable After and CharityMiles, we included interactions between the weekly dummies and CharityMiles. This resulted in six interaction terms that ranged from Time(-3) × CharityMiles to Time(3)×CharityMiles. We used Time(- 1) × CharityMiles as a baseline and examined the difference between Charity Miles tweets and ordinary tweets at a weekly level. As reported in Table C1, the coefficients for the interactions between Time(t) and CharityMiles were insignificant when ?? ∈ {−3, −2} and the coefficients for the interactions between Time(t) and CharityMiles were positively significant when ?? ∈ {1,2,3}, indicating that relative to the likes of ordinary tweets, the likes of Charity Miles tweets only increased after the template change. These findings validate our common trend assumption and corroborate our results.

## C2. Content Consistency (Cosine Similarity)

The treated tweets in our study were generated by the Charity Miles template, and the controlled tweets were ordinary tweets generated by users. Unlike the Charity Miles tweets, which were highly homogeneous, the ordinary tweets covered various topics and expressed different emotions and sentiments. In this robustness check, we first concatenated the tweets from each user before and after the template change. We obtained two documents for each user. We then used the global vectors for word representation (GloVe) model to represent each document by a word vector with 50 dimensions. GloVe is the state of the art in natural language processing model for representing documents with word embeddings and has been widely used to understand tweets (Badjatiya et al., 2017; Khatri, 2020). Next, we calculated the cosine similarity between the content before and after the template change for every user. We plotted the histogram of the cosine similarity in Figure C1. We found that the ordinary content before and after the template change was highly similar. Thus, it seems unlikely that our study suffered from a change of content that resulted in different levels of likes.
