# Can big data improve firm decision quality? The role of data quality and data diagnosticity

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Big data utilization Data quality Decision quality Data diagnosticity

## A B S T R A C T

Anecdotal evidence suggests that, despite the large variety of data, the huge volume of generated data, and the fast velocity of obtaining data (i.e., big data), quality of big data is far from perfect. Therefore, many firms defer collecting and integrating big data as they have concerns regarding the impact of utilizing big data on data diagnosticity (i.e., retrieval of valuable information from data) and firm decision making quality. In this study, we use the Organizational Learning Theory and Wang and Strong's data quality framework to explore the impac of processing big data on firm decision quality and the mediating role of data quality (DQ) and data diagnosticity on this relationship. We validate the proposed research model using survey data from 130 firms, obtained from data analysts and IT managers. Results confirm the critical role of DQ in increasing data diagnosticity and improving firm decision quality when processing big data; suggesting important implications for practice and theory. Findings also reveal that while big data utilization positively impacts contextual DQ, accessibility DQ, and representational DQ, interestingly, it negatively impacts intrinsic DQ. Furthermore, findings show that while intrinsic DQ, contextual DQ, and representational DQ significantly increase data diagnosticity, accessibility DQ does not influence it. Most importantly, the findings show that big data utilization does not significantly impact the quality of firm decisions and it is fully mediated through DQ and data diagnosticity. The results of this study contribute to practice by providing important guidelines for managers to improve firm decision quality through the use of big data.

## 1. Introduction

The advent of networked business has considerably increased the variety, volume, and velocity of data, often described via the term “big data” [18,29]. Firms are attempting to enhance data diagnosticity, which refers to gaining sophisticated and deep insights from data, by exploiting big data gathered from various sources. The goal is to ultimately improve decision making quality [45]. Recent developments in information technology have led to constant increases and quick accumulation of data [14], and have allowed firms to benefit from big data by increasing data diagnosticity, which could lead to improved firm decision quality and eventually firm outcomes.

Big data utilization may help to investigate more of the ‘why’ behind events explained with traditional analytics, which could greatly aid in decision-making [51,60]. However, some studies have found that most of the firms that invested in processing big data have not yet improved their outcomes [50]. According to Henke et al. [56], only 25% of firms reported that processing and analyzing big data has “significantly” enabled them to enhance their outcomes. A recent report found that

49% of firms assert that the greatest value of utilizing big data is to improve firm decision quality [27]. Hence, our first objective is to explore the efect of big data on firm decision quality, which eventually impacts firm outcomes.

Studies argue that poor data quality (DQ) is likely to negatively impact the generation of valuable insight from data [15,112]. As big data encompasses new characteristics, its quality may face many challenges [14,21], and thus, the quality of data becomes more critical than ever [5,43,44]. Particularly, researchers have argued that with the increasing variety, volume, and velocity of data, quality of the obtained data could be compromised [95]. Resultantly, the costs of poor DQ could be as high as 8% to 12% of firm revenues, which could lead into losses that exceed billions of dollars per year [55]. Therefore, in order to improve firm decision quality and revenue generation, we explore the impact of big data utilization on data quality and subsequently data diagnosticity.

Studies argue that many firms have not begun to collect and integrate big data as they are concerned that DQ could adversely impact data diagnosticity [14]. Although there are a number of conceptual studies regarding the impact of big data on firm DQ, there are no em pirical studies exploring this relationship. Therefore, as the quality of data is considered a critical factor in obtaining insight and intelligence from data [72,74], our second objective is to investigate DQ as a possible mediator of the relation between big data utilization and firm data diagnosticity, which could eventually impact firm decision quality. Data diagnosticity is the ultimate goal for data analysis [22,35]. As there is no validated scale to measure data diagnosticity in the litera ture, the concept is operationalized and validated.

To address the gaps identified above, Wang and Strong's [111] DQ framework and Organizational Learning Theory have been utilized. Wang and Strong [111] conducted one of the most in-depth research projects in the area of DQ where they developed a framework for data quality and suggested that the quality of data depends on four main categories of representational DQ, intrinsic DQ, contextual DQ, and accessibility DQ. Intrinsic DQ is the extent to which data corresponds to its ‘real’ value; accessibility DQ is the ease of obtaining the data; contextual DQ is defined as the quality of data based on the context in which it is used; and representational DQ refers to presenting the data in a clear manner [46,111]. As indicated in previous studies (e.g., [48, 119]) the data quality framework that is developed by Wang and Strong [111] is the most popular framework researchers have been used to measure users' data quality perceptions in diferent contexts. Thus, in this study, we used this framework and classified DQ as representational DQ, intrinsic DQ, contextual DQ, and accessibility DQ.

Organizational Learning Theory [61] is used in this study as it fo cuses on data acquisition, interpretation, and knowledge generation from the data. According to Organizational Learning Theory, after data integration, the next part of the learning process is data interpretation [61]. In order to interpret the results and generate insight from the data, the quality of data plays a critical role. In the context of this study, Organizational Learning Theory helps us understand how utilizing and processing big data impact insight generation from data, which even tually impacts firm decision quality. We adopt this theory and Wang and Strong's [111] DQ framework to argue that DQ and data diag nosticity mediate the relation between big data utilization and the quality of firm decisions. Specifically, we raise the following questions: (i) does big data utilization increase firm decision quality?; and (ii) does DQ mediate the efect of big data utilization on data diagnosticity? To address the above objectives, we collected data from 130 data analysis and IT managers. Our results provide a theory-based understanding of the efect of the big data on the quality of data, firm data diagnosticity, and firm decision quality.

## 2. Relevant literature

## 2.1. Data quality framework

Wang & Strong [111] define data quality as data that is fit for use by users. It is a critical factor for any analytics program. Firms make wrong decisions when they process data with low quality which may cause losses that could exceed billions of dollars per year [55].

Research on DQ started in the 1990s, and studies proposed diferent dimensions for DQ over the past decades [3,47,91,111,115]. One of the most in-depth research projects was done by Wang & Strong [111]. They developed a framework and argued that DQ judgment depends on four main categories of DQ, including intrinsic DQ (i.e., the innate correctness of data), accessibility DQ (i.e., the ease with which the data is obtained), contextual DQ (i.e., the quality of data based on the context), and representational DQ (i.e., showing data in a clear manner). In this framework, each category has several dimensions used to evaluate DQ. For example, data accuracy and believability are considered as intrinsic DQ dimensions, data completeness and relevance are considered as contextual DQ dimensions, ease of understanding and concise format are considered as representational DQ dimensions, and ease of accessibility is considered as an accessibility DQ dimension. This study focuses on the quality of data (i.e., quality of “raw facts”) rather than the quality of information (i.e., quality of “meaningful data”) [21,30,31]. Thus, we rely on Wang and Strong's [111] framework to classify the DQ categories used in this study.

## 2.2. Organizational learning theory

Organizational Learning Theory [61] focuses on the process of in tegrating and transferring data to generate insight and knowledge with the purpose of improving firm decision-making. This theory highlights the need for firms to build their learning capabilities [99]. Organizational learning is the process to gain new insight through utilizing as well as analyzing data [39,61,96,105]. Scholars argue that the data acquisition and insight generation from the data are critical to obtaining a competitive advantage (e.g., [23,96]). This theory is grounded in the resource-based view of the firm, which suggests that the firm's ability to learn is the only firm capability that cannot be emulated [25]. Organizational Learning Theory focuses on data acquisition, inter pretation, and knowledge generation from the data [61]. In the context of this study, Organizational Learning Theory helps us understand how utilizing and processing big data impact insight generation from data, which eventually impacts firm decision quality [97].

Organizational Learning Theory has been used in diferent contexts, such as data sharing and marketing support [62,107] and customer metrics and decision-making [6]. Failure to make efective firm deci sions stems from not having organization-wide learning capabilities [59,82,83]. In order to be competitive in a rapidly changing environment, many firms are continuously collecting data with vast velocity, volume, and variety from diferent sources [50]. Firms should make conscious decisions to change their actions in response to the changes in the environment. For example, some firms use advanced technologies (e.g., lambda architecture) to rapidly integrate and process massive amounts of data to diagnose the data and discover patterns from the data to enhance their decision-making.

According to Organizational Learning Theory data interpretation is a critical part of learning process [61]. To interpret the results and create insight from the data, the quality of data plays a vital role. For example, Homburg et al. [59] show that quality of customer informa tion, rather than customer prioritization (from most to least important), ultimately leads to higher average customer profitability. Data that is complete, accurate, timely, and easy to understand can help firms to better increase their insight generation compared to low quality data [51]. Kwon et al. [68] argue that DQ (e.g., data consistency and data completeness) is a critical condition of a firm's IT infrastructure, and a key asset necessary in appropriately analyzing data to generate knowledge, and to make firm decisions. They suggest that high DQ ensures production of reliable information and fosters its strategic and tactical usage. When the quality of data becomes an issue, due to different reasons such as flawed system design, or data input errors, firms cannot build their learning capabilities and they would not be able to make accurate decisions [68]. Therefore, the quality of data is critical for data diagnosticity gained from big data. In this study, we utilize Organizational Learning Theory to investigate the impact of processing big data on firm decision quality which is mediated by DQ and data diagnosticity.

## 2.3. Data diagnosticity

Data diagnosticity refers to the retrieval of deep and sophisticated information from data to make valid and reliable interpretations and assessments [52]. Data diagnosticity is generated by analyzing data to obtain useful and valuable information about what is going on with a particular phenomenon or situation. For example, firms analyze data to understand current events, to investigate the reason something occurred in the past, and to identify accurate predictions of future events. For instance, credit-card companies can use large data warehouses to select most likely customers prospects, or they can use a “ready-tomarket” database that allows a system to analyze an issue and make personalized ofer in milliseconds, or they can optimize ofers over time by tracking responses to predict future decisions [24]. Therefore, data diagnosticity serves as a mean for firms to make better decisions. In the context of big data, firms attempt to generate valuable information from accessing and analyzing large volumes of data to improve decision making quality and solve business problems, which lead to the generation of a wide range of intangible and tangible values for the firms [9,35]. Although data diagnosticity is the ultimate goal for data analysis [22,35], there is no validated scale to measure this variable in the literature. Thus, in this study, the concept of data diagnosticity is operationalized, conceptualized, validated and its relationship to firm decision quality is investigated.

## 2.4. Related work

Although in the context of big data, the importance of DQ has been emphasized by many papers [5,14,51], there is no empirical study that has examined the impact of big data on data quality. Hazen et al. [55] argue that with advances in technologies, most firms are able to collect, and process data with high volume, velocity, and variety. However, that data is useless for decision-making if it is of poor quality [74]. In fact, the usefulness of data for firms is determined mainly by its quality. Ghasemaghaei et al. [51] argue that although advanced data analytics tools can detect useful information from a large amount of hetero geneous data, the ultimate results and value of processing and analyzing big data will be influenced by its quality. They considered DQ as an important component of firm data analytics competency which impacts decision-making performance within firms. Kwon et al. [68] emphasize the importance of DO management for big data analytics and found that firm's intention to analyze big data can be influenced by the firm's ownership of high quality data. Cai & Zhu [14] argue that one of the main dificulties of processing and analyzing big data is data quality. They state that in the context of big data analytics, the heterogeneity of data sources brings complex data structures. They suggest that big data could impact diferent DQ dimensions (e.g., relevancy, reliability, availability, presentation quality). Clarke [21] argues about the risks of inadequate coverage of the quality of big data in the literature. The study uses quasi-empirical scenarios and explains the importance of DQ in the context of big data. Merino et al. [77] developed a data quality model and suggested that the data adequacy and timeliness of the data are critical in the context of big data. Merino et al. [77] argue that in the context of big data, the management of the quality of the data is very critical as big data by itself, regardless of the type, size, or speed, is worthless. In another study, Baesens et al. [5] state that data is the main ingredient of any analytical models and the well-known garbage in, garbage out principle is still applicable in the context of big data. They suggest that although data will never have perfect quality, the acceptable margin of error should be clarified in firms. As firms now have access to larger amounts of data, the importance of data quality has enhanced [5]. However, most often, firms consider investment in data quality to be too expensive and may thus overlook the importance of the quality of data and its impact on firm outcomes (e.g., decision making quality) [5]. Firms need to make sure about the quality of data in all steps of data processing and analysis including the initial data collection, data storage, data retrieval, and data preparation for analysis [5]. Firms with low DQ would not be able to generate valuable insights from data which may considerably reduce the quality of firm decisions [51,112]. As big data has the three main characteristics of volume, variety, and velocity, when firms process and utilize big data, extracting high quality data from the variable, massive, and complicated data sets is an important issue for firms [14]. In particular, the diversity of data sources enhances the dificulty in ensuring the accuracy of the data that it is integrated. In addition, when firms integrate large sizes of diferent types of data from various sources, it is dificult to make sure about the quality of the data [14]. Because of the increasing concern regarding the quality of the big data and as there is a lack of research in this area, in this study, we explore the efect of big data utilization on DQ which eventually could impact data diagnosticity and firm decision quality.

The concept of data diagnosticity has been mainly used in the context of online consumer decision quality [52,114] and most existing studies have included diagnosticity in their experimental design rather than directly measuring it. However, although data diagnosticity is the eventual purpose of utilizing big data, there is not any empirical study that has examined the impact of big data utilization on data diag nosticity. Based on the Organizational Learning Theory, firms need to first integrate the data (in which the quality of data is vital), and then interpret and create insight from the data. Thus, in this study, we integrate Organizational Learning Theory and Wang and Strong's [111] DQ framework to explore the mediating role of data quality and data diagnosticity on the efect of big data on firm decision making quality.

## H2. Big data utilization will increase decision quality.

Based on Organizational Learning Theory, to improve decisionmaking, firms need to first collect and integrate data, then interpret it, and then transfer that data into usable knowledge [61]. In the interpretation stage, the judgment about the quality of data could be a cri tical factor in generating insight and knowledge from the data. Data quality refers to the “quality of raw facts that reflect the characteristics of an event or entity” ([32], p. 24). In the context of big data, there are two lines of reasoning for the efect of data on DQ. The first is that the quality of data will degrade when the variety, volume, and velocity of data are constantly increasing [63]. Within the data analytics literature, studies argue that the ever-increasing variety of data and velocity of data collection will result in questionable usefulness of information that can be obtained [34]. The second line of reasoning is to understand the impact of big data on each DQ category separately. Next, we review the first line of reasoning.

Cai & Zhu [14] explained a couple of challenges firms face regarding the quality of big data. First, a variety of data sources and diferent types of data bring with them complex data structures, which enhances the dificulty of integrating data. In the past, firms mainly used data that was created within their business systems, such as inventory and sales data. However, recently, many firms are attempting to collect and analyze data from various sources such as the Internet of Things, social media, and data collected from various other industries [28]. For firms, obtaining data from diferent sources enhances concerns regarding the DQ [76]. Second, Cai & Zhu [14] argue that with the enormous amount of data it is not easy to judge the quality of data within a reasonable time frame. Particularly, it is dificult to gather, clean, and integrate high DQ within a reasonable amount of time. This has been considered a challenge given current techniques and capabilities of data processing [14]. Third, useful data is perishable. The usefulness of data changes very quickly, which further increases demands on processing technology. If firms do not gather the required data in a timely manner, they may be acting on invalid and outdated information. Analysis and processing of these data could produce misleading and useless conclusions, which ultimately lead to inaccurate firm decision-making [14]. Hence, when using big data. the challenge is to understand what is accurate and relevant and then extract that data to analyze in a timely manner. Therefore, based on this argument, it is dificult to attain unambiguously high DQ from big data.

The second line of thought is to investigate the impact of big data utilization on each DQ category separately. As explained earlier, Wang & Strong [111] developed a framework operationalizing the categories of DQ as representational DQ, intrinsic DQ, contextual DQ, and accessibility DQ. They argue that DQ judgment depends on these four main categories of DQ. Therefore, this approach has been used in this study to investigate the impact of big data utilization on DQ.

Representational DQ refers to presenting data in a clear manner. Interpretability, concise representation, and representational consistency are considered as dimensions of representational DQ [111]. In the context of big data, firms can obtain a comprehensive and deep understanding of data by integrating and analyzing diferent types of data (i.e., semi-structured, unstructured, and structured data). For example, firms can use data visualization dashboards to summarize big data in a clear manner which would help managers to get insight from data in a real-time. Hence, big data utilization could positively influence representational DQ.

Contextual DQ is classified as another major DQ category, which is defined as the quality of data based on the context within which the data is used. Appropriate amount of data, relevancy, completeness, timeliness, and value-added are considered as dimensions of contextual DQ [111]. Firms are constantly collecting a large amount of data from various sources [94] such as social network data, Web, videos, images, and sensor-based data [1,16]. Therefore, it is more likely that firms collect data that is more complete, detailed, and relevant to the task at hand. For example, many firms use Hadoop clusters to collect a huge amount of data from external and internal sources about their custo mers, which would help them to have more up-to-date, relevant, and complete data about them while making decisions. Hence, big data utilization could positively influence contextual DQ.

Intrinsic DQ is another main DQ category, which refers to dimen sions that are objective to the data such as believability, accuracy, and objectivity. Intrinsic dimensions assess the extent to which data is identical to its corresponding ‘real’ value [47,111]. By integrating big data from diferent sources, firms have realized that the large amounts of data have advantages of predicting and preventing risks, improving service quality, and understanding customer needs [14]. However, to generate value, big data needs to be based on accurate and credible data [51]. Firms are constantly collecting data from very wide sources (e.g., social media, transactional data), and they use diferent techniques (e.g., data scrubbing) to detect and remove inconsistencies and errors in data to improve the quality [14]. Lukoianova & Rubin [71] argue that collecting data from various resources increase the quality of data by decreasing errors and bias stemming from a limited amount of data. For example, to hire new employees, firms often use sophisticated techniques to collect various amount of data about their job applicants from diferent sources (e.g., social media, documents) to have more accurate, objective and more credible data about them; this helps them to make better decisions in their hiring processes. Hence, big data uti lization could positively influence intrinsic DQ.

Accessibility DQ is another main DQ category which refers to the ease of obtaining data [111]. The development of information technologies makes it easier for firms to collect data from a variety of sources. For example, firms can easily access their customers' data through the Internet of Things and online social media [28]. As firms are enabled to quickly obtain heterogeneous data using advanced in formation technologies, big data utilization is likely to increase accessibility DQ.

In summary, based on the above discussions, big data utilization is likely to have a positive impact on DQ categories. Thus:

H3a. Big data utilization will enhance representational DQ.

H3b. Big data utilization will enhance contextual DQ.

H3c. Big data utilization will enhance intrinsic DQ

H3d. Big data utilization will enhance accessibility DQ.

Poor DQ (e.g., incomplete or inaccurate data) is useless for consumption, and can have a direct influence on decreasing the quality of firm decisions [112], which has been found to have deleterious efects on firms [10,55,106]. Generally, firms perceive data to be more diag nostic in the process of their decision-making when they have high quality data. In fact, the more firms have access to complete, timely, and accurate data, the more they find such data to be helpful in gen erating valuable information and insight [36].

Although advanced information technologies and analytical tools can discover patterns in data and identify useful information [86], ul timately, insight generated from data will depend on DQ [72]. User entry redundancy, corruption, and errors, all afect the value of data [95]. Without high DQ, it would be dificult for firms to diagnose data appropriately and generate deep, and valuable information [72].

All DQ categories are likely to enhance data diagnosticity. Representational DQ (e.g., concise representation, ease of understanding) helps firms to obtain a better understanding of the data. Firms often use advanced visualization tools to represent the data in a clear manner which help them to explore, and gain valuable information and insights from data and make efective decisions [79]. Hence, representational DQ could positively influence data diagnosticity. Contextual DQ (e.g., timeliness, relevancy) is also likely to increase data diagnosticity. This could be due to the fact that when firms have more relevant, up-to-date, and complete data, they could better discover patterns in the data related to the task at hand. Merino et al. [77] argue that when firms analyze data that is related to the domain of interest, they can provide valuable insight from it. Kyoon Yoo et al. [69] state that insight generation is contextspecific, and insight that does not reflect the context at hand, holds no relevance. Intrinsic DQ (e.g., accuracy, believability) is also likely to increase data diagnosticity. This DQ category is concerned with the very nature of the data. If firms analyze inaccurate data, they will not be able to generate valuable insight from analyzing that data [58]. Wahyudi et al. [108] argue that high intrinsic DQ helps firms to successfully unlock value from data. However, the low intrinsic DQ causes the internal deficit (e.g., biased, untrusted, inaccurate data) which could reduce generating valuable insight from the data [108]. To solve this issue, firms should improve the authenticity of the datasets by pre-processing the data, assessing the accuracy of data, and rating the reliability of the data providers before start analyzing the data. Thus, high intrinsic DQ can help firms to enhance the data diagnosticity. Accessibility DQ is also likely to increase data diagnosticity. Easily access to data could help firms to quickly obtain data from internal and external sources to generate insight from it. For example, many firms use advanced technologies (e.g., lambda architecture) which help them to quickly process and integrate data to discover patterns in data and generate valuable information and insight from it.

In summary, DQ is more likely to have a positive impact on data diagnosticity. Thus:

H4a. Representational data quality will increase data diagnosticity.

H4b. Contextual data quality will increase data diagnosticity.

H4c. Intrinsic data quality will increase data diagnosticity.

H4d. Accessibility data quality will increase data diagnosticity.

In the world of big data, data diagnosticity is the crucial aim for firms [22,35]. Recent developments in information technology have led to a quick accumulation of data [14]. The continuous increase in data velocity, volume, and variety helps firms to capitalize on the premise of big data by increasing the insight generation from data which could lead to improved firm decision-making quality. For example, analyzing big data helps firms to extract patterns in data and interpret them using some data models [49] which could help them to make better decisions.

Firms can use the data they collect from internal and external sources to make a trajectory of what will happen in the future and some possible actions firms can take and their potential outcomes. With the use of advanced big data analytics technologies (e.g., Hadoop, NoSQL databases, MapReduce), better data insights can be attained, which could improve firm decision making [98]. Rothberg & Erickson [93] argue that insights generate from analyzing big data have the potential to directly impact firm decision quality. For example, firms can generate insight from their customers through accessing to various sources which would help them to improve their decisions by ofering more personalized products/services [34]. According to Organizational Learning Theory, the insight that is generated through analyzing data enables firms to enhance their decision-making. Thus:

H5. Data diagnosticity will increase decision quality.

## 5. Results

The results are explained in two parts. First, we discuss the measurement model to confirm variable validity and reliability. Then, the structural model is analyzed to test the hypothesized relationships among the constructs in the model.

## 5.1. Test of the measurement model

In this study, we use previously validated instruments (see Appendix A<sup>1</sup>) for all the constructs, except for data diagnosticity, which does not have a validated scale. Following Moore and Benbasat's [78] procedure, data diagnosticity was developed as a reflective construct (see Appendix B). In this study, three related control variables were considered. First, as the importance of collecting and analyzing big data may vary by industry, the firm industry was included. Second, firm size, which was operationalized as the number of firm employees, was considered as a control variable, because firms with larger sizes have often access to more resources [17]. Third, familiarity with data analytic tools was included, because it may influence the respondent's subjective interpretation of the impact of big data on the firm [53].<sup>2</sup>

To test the measurement model, the assessment of discriminant and convergent validity, as well as internal consistency is required. However, reflective and formative items should be treated diferently [8,116]. Formative items are the ones that result in variance in the construct being investigated [12], and they do not exhibit internal consistency [19]. Multicollinearity is the main concern for formative constructs [120]. Multicollinearity is not a threat in this study because (1) tolerance values averaged > 0.65; (2) the maximum variance inflation factor (VIF) is 1.716 which is well below the threshold of 3.3 or 5.0 [85]; and (3) none of the bivariate correlations were above 0.90 [102,118].

For formative constructs, the weights examination is suggested rather than the loadings assessment [13]. The findings show that the weightings of the items for two of the three big data measures were significant and one was marginally significant.<sup>3</sup> To retain content validity [85] all the items were kept in the research model [57].

Table 3 shows the square roots of average variance extracted (AVE) of the reflective constructs (i.e., data diagnosticity, decision quality, representational DQ, intrinsic DQ, contextual DQ, and accessibility DQ). Moreover, the of-diagonal elements of the table show the correlations between the constructs. As presented in Table 3, the square root of the AVE is larger than the correlation between each variable and any other variable; showing suficient discriminant validity [7].

For reflective constructs. the measurement item reliability was measured by calculating the loadings of each measurement item on its assigned construct. As can be seen in Table 4, all indicators loaded higher than the threshold of 0.70 [19], and they highly loaded on their assigned construct. In addition, loadings of the items on their theoretical assigned variables are higher than any other loadings and the diference is higher than the threshold of 0.10 [40]. Moreover, as illustrated in Table 4. all factors show high reliability with Cronbach's alpha and composite reliability of being higher than 0.70 [37].

Following the recommendation by Podsakof [87], Harman's onefactor test was conducted to examine the common method bias. The findings showed several factors and they all explained < 50% of total variance, which demonstrates no considerable common methods bias [100]. We also utilized the marker-variable technique recommended by Lindell & Whitney [70], Pavlou et al. [81], Malhotra et al. [73], and Xu et al. [117]. They suggested that a marker variable, which is a theoretically unrelated construct, need to be used to adjust the correlations among the main variables. In this study, gender, a theoretically unrelated variable, is identified and used as a marker variable. The existence of high correlations among principal constructs and gender show common method bias. The results show that the average correlation among gender and the other principal constructs is r = 0.05. Thus, minimal evidence exists for the common method bias.

Results from SmartPLS [92] with bootstrapping using 500 re-sam ples are illustrated in Fig. 2, which includes path coeficients and the statistical significance of the relationships in the research model.

The results show that big data utilization significantly influences data diagnosticity $( \ B = 0 . 4 5 5 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , which provides support to H1. Interestingly, the results show that big data utilization does not significantly influence the quality of firm decisions $( \beta = 0 . 1 5 8 ;$ $p > 0 . 0 5 )$ , which rejects H2. The findings indicate that big data utili zation positively impacts contextual DQ $( \beta = 0 . 4 0 4 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 ) ,$ , representational DQ $( \beta = 0 . 4 6 9 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and accessibility DQ $( \beta = 0 . 3 2 6 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ) providing suficient support to H3a, H3b, and H3d. Surprisingly, results indicate that big data utilization negatively impacts intrinsic DQ $( \beta = - 0 . 3 1 4 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , not providing support for H3c. Finally, the finding showed that data diagnosticity significantly influences decision quality $( \beta = 0 . 4 6 1 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , which supports H5. Fig. 2 shows that big data and DQ explain about 57% of the variance in the data diagnosticity. Moreover, big data and data diagnosticity explain about 31% of the variance in decision quality.

5.3. Data diagnosticity and decision quality for diferent levels of data quality and big data utilization

Following Ghasemaghaei et al.'s [50] procedure, as a post hoc exploratory analysis, we further investigated the role of big data and DQ on data diagnosticity and decision quality. Specifically, using median splits, the firms were classified based on their level of big data utilization and DQ. As can be seen in Table 5, four groups (1: low big data utilization, low DQ; 2: high big data utilization, low DQ; 3: low big data utilization, high DQ; 4: high big data utilization, high DQ) have been created using this procedure. While only 36 firms out of our sample of 130 firms have high DQ and process high degrees of big data, 46 firms have low DQ and utilize low degrees of big data. Moreover, whereas 30 firms utilize high big data and have low DQ, 18 firms utilize low big data and have high DQ. As shown in this table, firms that utilize high big data and have high DQ have the greatest data diagnosticity and decision quality means. Thus, to increase both their data diagnosticity and decision quality, firms need to utilize high quality data with enormous variety, volume, and velocity. As shown in Table 6 and Figs. 3 and 4, ANOVA analyses were also conducted to investigate whether observed diferences in data diagnosticity and decision quality for firms that classified in Table 5 into diferent groups were significant. The findings show that there are significant mean diferences for both data diagnosticity and decision quality between firms that utilize low big data and have low DQ compared to firms that utilize high big data and have high DQ. The results show the role of both DQ and big data utilization in increasing data diagnosticity and improving firm decision quality.

## 6.1. Theoretical contributions

Big data utilization helps to investigate more of the ‘why’ behind events explained with traditional analytics to increase data diagnosticity which greatly aids in improving firm decision quality [51]. We addressed this objective by utilizing Organizational Learning Theory and the DQ framework to understand the impact of processing big data on firm decision quality and the mediating role of DQ and data diagnosticity on this relationship. The Wang and Strong's [111] DQ framework classifies diferent categories of DQ, while Organizational Learning Theory focuses on the process of integrating data, and transferring that to the insight to improve firm decision-making. One of the unique contributions of this study is explaining the influence of big data utilization on each DQ category. Particularly, our findings reveal that big data utilization positively impacts contextual DQ, accessibility DQ, and representational DQ. However, interestingly, it negatively impacts intrinsic DQ. Tee [104] argues that one of the main issues with big data is the tendency for increased errors, which could reduce intrinsic DQ. This could be because firms are trying to collect and analyze big data from diferent sources, and thus there could be inconsistencies and conflicts among data collected from diferent sources [14], reducing believability of the real values of the data. This explains the negative impact of big data utilization on intrinsic DQ. In addition, the results show that while intrinsic DQ, accessibility DQ, and representational DQ significantly increase data diagnosticity, interestingly, accessibility DQ does not influence data diagnosticity. Thus, accessing data with real value, representing data in a clear manner, and obtaining data related to the task at hand play critical roles in increasing deep, and valuable information generated from data, whereas, easily obtaining data does not play an important role.

As discussed earlier, one of the key reasons firms have begun to process big data is to enhance their decision-making. Remarkably, the findings show that the impact of utilizing big data on firm decision quality is not significant and it is fully mediated through DQ and data diagnosticity. This could explain the reason that many firms that utilize big data have not yet improved their decision quality. Moreover, the results from ANOVA analyses provide unique insights about the im portance of big data utilization and DQ together in increasing data diagnosticity and improving firm decision quality. Specifically, the results show that firms that utilize data with high volume, velocity, and variety, and have high DQ have the highest data diagnosticity and decision quality compared to other firms.

In sum, the findings show that while many firms invested in processing big data, in this context, the quality of data is more critical than ever before for improving firm data diagnosticity which leads to enhancing firm decision quality. The study results provide novel insights for researchers interested in understanding the role of big data in the quality of firm decisions and the mediating role of DQ and data diagnosticity.

## 6.2. Practical contributions

The findings show that when firms quickly collect and integrate a large variety of heterogeneous data, the innate correctness of data will decrease considerably. Therefore, when firms utilize big data, they should specifically focus on increasing intrinsic DQ. For example, different tasks such as cleansing, filtering, conforming, pruning, and matching should be applied in the early stage of collecting data to increase the DQ. Moreover, our results illustrate that firms that process big data can get more relevant data, can better understand the data, as it is shown in diferent formats and types, and can have access to more data that matches their needs. As poor DQ is likely to negatively impact the generation of valuable insight from data [112], firms need to use advanced technologies to clean the data before start analyzing it. For example, managers should ensure that they use appropriate methods (e.g., data scrubbing) to identify and remove inconsistencies and errors from data in order to improve its quality. They can also provide clear policies regarding how data is collected, processed, stored, and retrieved from internal and external sources [55]. They also need to have some knowledgeable employees who have suficient skills required to measure, monitor, and control DQ [66]. Firms often attempt to use advanced information technologies to quickly obtain big data which increase accessibility DQ. However, to improve data diagnosticity, firms need to pay particular attention to other DQ categories including the intrinsic DQ, the contextual DQ, and the representational DQ.

Based on the results of this study, firms need to improve their DQ and increase their data diagnosticity to successfully utilize big data and improve their decision-making. One of the key challenges that many firms face is that many of them have not still started to collect and integrate big data as they have a concern about the negative efect of big data utilization on the quality of data which impacts obtaining valuable information from data. ## 6.3. Limitations and future research

Several limitations of this study are noteworthy and pave the way for future research. First, in this study, the participants were IT managers and data analysts from North American companies. Therefore, as culture may have potential impacts on individuals' perceptions regarding IT, future studies could conduct this research in other countries. Second, the influence of utilizing big data on firm decision quality could be mediated by factors other than the DQ and data diagnosticity. Therefore, the role of other variables (e.g., IT infrastructure capability, managerial support) on this relationship warrants future research. Third, as big data is mainly characterized by variety, velocity, and volume of the data [51], in this study utilizing the data that has high volume, velocity, and variety has been defined as big data utilization. Some studies proposed other characteristics of big data (e.g., value, and veracity) [109]. Future research should operationalize, conceptualized and validate big data using other recommended characteristics and assess whether the operationalization of big data utilization using other characteristics will significantly impact the findings. Finally, we did not have the identification of the participants as their participation in our study was anonymous, and thus, we could not conduct the results validation check with the participants. Future studies could examine the findings of this study using diferent approaches such as focus groups, and empirical observation.

## 7. Conclusion

This study addressed an important gap in the literature regarding the impact of the large variety of data, the fast velocity of obtaining data, and the huge volume of generated data on firm decision quality and the mediating role of DQ and data diagnosticity on this relationship. We used the Organizational Learning Theory and Wang and Strong's [111] DQ framework to explain the impact of utilizing big data on DQ categories as well as on firm data diagnosticity and decision quality. The results revealed that while big data utilization positively impacts the contextual DQ, accessibility DQ, and representational DQ, it negatively impacts the intrinsic DQ. ## Appendix B. Instrument development process

Data diagnosticity is an important goal for big data analysis [22,35]. However, data diagnosticity is mainly conceptualized in the consumer behavior literature and most existing studies include diagnosticity in their experimental design rather than directly measuring it. Therefore, in this study, the concept of data diagnosticity is operationalized, conceptualized, and validated, using Moore & Benbasat's [78] methodology. To do so. first the literature review has been conducted and the candidate measurement items have been created with the help of one professor, one assistant professor, and one graduate student. Second, the measurement items were sent to a panel of graduate students and asked about their opinion regarding the selected items. Third, based on the feedback we received from graduate students, the measurement items for data diagnosticity, and the items for other variables were presented in random. Fourth, we asked five PhD students to classify the items based on their similarities and identify the underlying constructs for each category. The agreement rate between the judges was 82%. Based on the feedback and comments we received from judges, the items for data diagnosticity were further refined. Fifth, we asked another five PhD students to categorize the retained measurement items based on the definition of the construct. A “too ambiguous/doesn't fit” category was also included to make sure that the judges were not obligated to consider items into a predefined category. The average agreement rate between the judges was 93%. This process helped to establishing high reliability and discriminant validity of the measurement items [78]. As shows in Table A1 in Appendix A, the final measurement items for data diagnosticity contained four items and these items were further assessed in the measurement model validation.
