# Sources of conflict between developers and testers in software development

Xihui Zhang <sup>a,</sup>\*, Thomas F. Stafford <sup>b</sup>, Jasbir S. Dhaliwal <sup>b</sup>, Mark L. Gillenson <sup>b</sup>, Gertrude Moeller <sup>b</sup>

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Conflict Interpersonal conflict Conflict sources Software development Testing

## A B S T R A C T

Conflict between software developers and testers is inevitable. Prior work on such conflict has focused on conflict management but not on its prevention. We addressed the research question: What are the source of interpersonal conflict between developers and testers in software development? To attempt to answer this, we collected and analyzed 50 developer–tester conflict scenarios from professional software developers and testers. Our results indicated that the sources of conflict fell into three major areas: process, people, and communication. Conflict sources are presented in a category–subcategory–description format. Implications for theory and practice in the proactive management of conflict sources are discussed.

Published by Elsevier B.V.

## 1. Introduction

Software development is a complex social process. On the one hand, software projects are becoming more intricate and deadlines more pressing; on the other, software applications are becoming larger and more integrated. Both require interaction between diverse individuals in different roles in order to deliver projects on time, within budget, and with all required features and functionality. One of the most important role interactions is between developers (who typically include systems analysts, designers, and programmers) and testers. Software testing involves fault-finding which possesses the potential for interpersonal conflict because the two parties have different goals, mindsets, values, preferences, backgrounds, and experience. Conflict is an omnipresent and inherent part of corporate IS development, particularly during the testing process.

Prior research has typically focused on conflict between end users and IS staff, or on conflict among IS staff. Little research has focused specifically on why conflict arises [9]. This is a shortcoming that our study sought to address.

The primary role of testers is to verify and validate software produced by developers to ensure its quality. The role of testing is to catch problems and this is inherently adversarial, resulting in interpersonal conflict between developers and testers. Developers and testers work in a highly interdependent manner, have different and sometimes conflicting responsibilities, use different methods to accomplish their goals, and must work jointly while at the same time competing for scarce resources.

Software testing is indispensable in ensuring that software quality and the critical contributions of the IT unit to the strategic objectives of the firm are met [3]. Yet, the continuing mis-match between goals of testers, developers, the IT Unit at large, and the firm threatens corporate success [12]. Testing has become fully integrated into the software development process [13]; as a result, testers are playing an increasingly important role in software development. Yet, the frequent interactions between developers and testers inevitably increase the potential for interpersonal conflict.

Some conflict in the firm is good, especially when leveraged to increase dialog between parties. But, this only pertains to moderate conflict levels; high conflict levels decrease development team satisfaction and performance [5]. These conflicts are likely to be negatively associated with software quality and job satisfaction [2,8]. The ‘‘gotcha effect’’ of tester discovery of developer errors inevitably denigrates the relationship between both parties, and poor quality software leads to unhappy end users, leading to increases in absenteeism, intention to leave, and actual turnover [6]. In view of the link between development-related job turnover, and the current and projected shortage of IT talent, this points to an additional motivation for studying developer–tester conflict. IS professionals must understand the nature of such conflict in order to mitigate its potential to be dysfunctional.

Two limitations exist in prior studies of conflict between software developers and testers: (1) the focus has not been on conflict sources, and (2) the context has generally not been developer–tester specific. Conflict is seen as an inherently dyadic phenomenon both in the management [7] and psychology literature. Aside from the lack of a dyadic perspective of the conflict process, the research has focused on the processes used to manage conflict once it arises and not on how to manage the sources of conflict to prevent them from arising [10].

Managers spend at least one fifth of their time dealing with conflict management in the firm, yet a focus on the sources rather than the effects of conflict has received very little attention, implying that some notable portion of managerial conflict management effort is suboptimal.

## 2. Prior research

## 2.1. Overview of the antecedents of interpersonal conflict

Conflict is both a state and a process. As a state, conflict can result in disagreements, negative emotions, and interfering behaviors between or among conflicting parties [1]. As a process, it results in one party perceiving that its interests are being opposed or negatively affected by another. Conflict levels include personal, interpersonal, group, intergroup, organizational, interorganizational, national, and international. In our study, the interpersonal level was our specific focus.

According to prior studies, antecedents of interpersonal conflict can be split into two broad categories: individual characteristics (which include personality, values, goals, commitment to position, stress and anger, and a desire for autonomy) and interpersonal factors (which include perceptual interface, behavior, communication, structure, and any effects of previous interactions). Perceptual interface involves belief about another’s intentions or motivations, regardless of accuracy. Conflict arises when the other is perceived to have goals or intentions incompatible with one’s own, violating norms of equity or fairness, or harboring harmful intent.

Behavioral factors that cause conflict involve harmful effects, such as control attempts by others that result in blocked goals, and threats or loss of power engendered by others. Communication between parties is a potent antecedent to reduce conflict, and this antecedent is moderated by factors such as frequency, content, context, and facility of the communicators. Conflict arises from poor communication, resulting in ineffective coordination. High levels of communication may also lead to misunderstanding, particularly in cross-cultural contexts. Structural factors are characteristics of the social environment (such as laws, norms, customs, contracts, and other formal requirements that affect interaction).

Structural and contextual factors serve as conflict antecedents; these may include group goals, organizational controls and reward structures, authority relationships, centralization, lack of formalized roles and procedures, communication barriers, and unfamiliar rules of behavior. Finally, previous interactions can exacerbate other interpersonal factors; e.g., repeated resolution failures can lead to negative stereotyping, prejudice, and self-fulfilled prophecies that result in new conflicts.

## 2.2. Conflict in software development

Rather than exploring the antecedents of conflict, research is often focused on the impact of conflict, conflict management, or associated factors on project outcomes – begging the question of how conflict arises; though some studies have considered conflict precursors, including individual and interpersonal factors, organizational, structural, and contextual factors, and communication.

## 2.2.1. Individual and interpersonal factors

What are loosely characterized as ‘‘individual characteristics’’ will typically be included in most research models of conflict among software developers. What is characterized in software testing studies as levels of participation and influence, disagreements between team members, and beliefs about other team members or the team in general are a good match for the notion of interpersonal interactions than individual values, personal goals and commitments, emotions and individual characteristics, such as personality. Hence, most prominent models of conflict in the software testing process are concerned with the dynamics of interaction among testing team members rather than personal characteristics of single individuals.

## 2.2.2. Organizational, structural, and contextual factors

These are also generally neglected in IS research. Examples include distinguishing between users and developers as members of distinct organizational cultures, the contextual nature of conflict and communication issues, and the role of scarce resources, organizational rules, and procedures, or systematic conflict due to divergent, synthesis-resistant goals which can result in distrust and conflict among team members.

## 2.2.3. Communication

This has been identified as a critical success factor for software development with conflict resulting from miscommunication and misunderstanding between stakeholders. In virtual teams, both communicating asynchronously and lack of rich media can increase the likelihood of the communication being misunderstood [4].

## 2.3. Interpersonal conflict between developers and testers

Our study focused on interpersonal conflict between software developers and testers. It has been noted that the testing process is adversarial, implying a certain inevitability of developer–tester conflict. The differing and divergent goals of developers and testers are difficult to synthesize, which reinforces the conflict-laden nature of the relationship.

Developers and testers can be very different from one another in mindsets, goals, experience, and perception of their relative importance. These differences naturally lead to conflict. Developers usually seek to maximize efficiency, or complete the process in the shortest amount of time. In contrast, testers seek to maximize effectiveness, in terms of delivering an end product with the best quality. Indeed, it is not uncommon for experienced developers to work side-by-side with less experienced testers. Finally, developers and testers may have different perceptions of their relative importance in the organization; testers feel that they have to continuously work to gain the level of respect given to developers.

A taxonomy has been established in prior studies for the comparison of testers and developers. In this, good testers are expected to have broad knowledge of many domains, learn new things quickly, focus on user needs and behavior, think empirically, and concentrate on reporting problems. In contrast, good developers are expected to have specialized knowledge of product internals, gain understanding of new things slowly but thoroughly, focus on system design, think theoretically, and concentrate on understanding problems.

However, we sought to expand the identification of conflict sources to an appropriate dyadic perspective, in keeping with the traditional focus of the psychological studies of conflict, by considering the views of both testers and developers in eliciting and evaluating a broad range of conflict sources. Several key limitations suggest important improvements to the study of conflict sources in software testing. We summarize these in Table 1.

We therefore posed the following research question for an exploratory study to build upon the currently limited information: What are the sources of interpersonal conflict between developers and testers in software development?

## 3. Research methodology

We initially used Q-sort methodology to address our major research question. This process was particularly useful for the purpose of conflict source identification. We collected 50 developer–tester conflict scenarios and used content analysis techniques to broadly sample the domain of potential constructs of developer/tester conflict. Two rounds of Q-sort analysis were used to distill underlying dimensions of conflict sources from the broad range of conflict indicators identified in the domain sampling phase. This was followed by an online survey for confirming and validating purposes.

## 3.1. Data collection

Fifty conflict scenarios were collected from participants enrolled in a software testing training course developed for IT employees of a large, global company who had over 4000 software developers and 700 testers. The sample was a good fit because all participants were either software developers or testers and actively engaged in either software development, software testing, or both.

Written conflict scenarios were collected in the following manner: before the start of the software testing training module, the training instructor asked participants to provide a written description of one software development conflict experience, including information about the issues involved and reactions of both parties. Of the 50 scenarios collected in this manner, 31 were generated by testers and 19 by developers.

## 3.2. Data analysis

The analysis process involved five sequential steps.

## 3.2.1. Step 1 – summary statements for conflict scenarios

The 50 original hand-written conflict scenarios were transcribed and double-checked to ensure accuracy. Soft copies of these transcripts were distributed to two persons meeting the cognitive and educational criteria for review of scenario content. They first independently analyzed the transcripts and then summarized each conflict scenario with a focus on conflict sources.

The initial agreement between the two on conflict sources for each of the conflict scenarios was 83% (40 out of 48). Further discussion produced full agreement. Then the summaries were reviewed and validated for content validity by an administrator of the software testing training program from which the recruits were drawn (see Appendix A for a summary of scenarios).

While analyzing and summarizing each of the conflict scenarios, the two transcript analysts found that two of the scenarios were not really conflict scenarios (Nos. 17 and 26), and another five scenarios were not about developer–tester conflict (Nos. 1, 7, 9, 34, and 48). As a result, these seven scenarios were excluded from further analysis, and the remaining 43 scenarios – 16 (37%) from developers and 27 (63%) from testers – were included in subsequent data analysis steps.

The next two steps involved Q-sorting [11] with one set of exploratory sessions and one set of confirmatory sessions in order to adequately represent the spectrum of opinions on the topic of software testing conflict. Thus the generation of the extensive list of conflict scenarios broadly surveyed the domain of the constructs that we sought to identify and explore.

An exploratory Q-sort was used to categorize and subcategorize the representative information by potential conflict source groupings. Confirmative Q-sort was then used to further refine categories and subcategories and their corresponding conflict sources. Three coders, all well-qualified for the task, were recruited for each step. In the first round of analysis, coders included a design manager who had worked as a test lead, a project analyst who was a former testing manager, and a current testing manager. In the second round, coders included a software quality assurance manager, a business application advisor responsible for quality assurance support to development teams, and an IT manager with 8 years of experience in software development.

## 3.2.2. Step 2 – exploratory Q-sort

Each of the 43 conflict summaries was printed on a 4-by-6 index card. Then, with two session facilitators present to administer the session and answer questions, each coder independently sorted the cards into conflict source categories. At the exploratory level, three Q-sort sessions were held, each with one coder. In each session, the session facilitators first introduced the coder to the objective of the Q-sort session. Then, the scenario cards were presented to the coder in a random order, and a protocol – shown in Appendix B – was read to the coder to provide guidance for the sorting process.

In each session, coders were asked to provide a category name for each pile of cards that they had sorted. After the exploratory Qsort, results were analyzed to determine which scenarios to keep and which to exclude from subsequent analysis. An inclusion rule and an exclusion rule were used for this purpose.

The inclusion rule specified that a scenario would be kept for further analysis when at least two out of the three coders placed it into a particular pile and also gave the same or a similar category name for that pile. As shown in Appendix C, 27 scenarios (63%) met this criterion. The exclusion rule specified that a scenario would be excluded from further analysis when all three coders placed it into a pile with different category names that were not conceptually related. Five scenarios (12%) met this criterion and were excluded from further analysis. The remaining 11 scenarios met neither rule and were kept for subsequent analysis. Thus, a total of 38 scenarios (88%) were carried into the next step.

In the initial Q-sort step, four categories and seven subcategories emerged for potential use in the next step. Key themes in the initial sort had to do with communication, people, processes, and organization. The process theme concerned boundaries for work, ownership of work processes and their documentation, and the organization theme related to bureaucratic and political issues in maintaining organizational procedures.

## 3.2.3. Step 3 – confirmatory Q-sort

The confirmatory Q-sort further refined the retained categories and subcategories. The remaining 38 conflict summary cards were presented to three new coders who were provided eight sorting cards on which the seven subcategories identified in the first Qsort were printed along with a ‘‘does not fit any’’ card.

Coders were asked to place each card into the conflict source category that provided the best fit, or into the ‘‘does not fit any’’ group. After the completion of the Q-sort sessions, results were analyzed to determine whether each scenario would be included in or excluded from subsequent analysis. As shown in Appendix D, 18 scenarios (47%) were placed into the same category by two of the three coders. Twelve scenarios (32%) were placed into the same category by all three coders. As a result, a total of 30 scenarios (79%) were retained for further analysis based on consensus, and 8 scenarios were excluded. The subcategory ‘‘Process, Boundaries/Ownership’’ was removed because none of the sorters assigned a scenario to it. Thus 30 conflict summaries (79% of the original group) were sorted into 4 primary categories and 6 subcategories. This information was retained for the next step.

## 3.2.4. Step 4 – online survey

The 30 scenarios from the confirmatory Q-sort step were incorporated into an online survey which was posted on www.SurveyMonkey.com. This step used multivariate analysis to check each scenario’s content validity, identifying any scenarios that might have been ambiguous and thus have the potential to cross load on multiple categories. The survey was administered by emailing the survey link to a group of about 300 IS scholars, including both IS faculty members and senior doctoral students. The sample respondents, all active IS researchers with either experience in testing and development or interests in these areas, were chosen because they met the cognitive and educational criteria for the purpose of evaluating scenario content validity. With a 33% response rate, 3/4 of respondents were developers, testers, or academic scholars affiliated with a research center specializing in software testing; the remaining participants were members of in IT association listserv who responded to a request to complete the survey.

For each scenario, respondents were asked to choose from the available categories one that provided the best fit with the scenario theme. These categories included: communication, knowledge sharing; process, documentation; people, emotions/attitude/personality; people, knowledge/experience; organization, politics; and process (compliance or noncompliance), or ‘‘does not fit any’’ (see Appendix E for the distribution of respondent category selections).

Two criteria were used to identify the resulting coded scenarios which would be included in our final conflict sources model. First, following multivariate analysis of the Q-sort results, scenarios which loaded 50% or above on one category were included in the final model: 19 scenarios met this criterion (Nos. 2, 5, 6, 14, 15, 16, 24, 27, 28, 30, 33, 37, 38, 41, 42, 44, 46, 49, and 50). Second, scenarios in which the highest factor loading value was greater than the sum of the next two highest loadings were also included. Five scenarios met this criterion (Nos. 3, 10, 12, 45, and 47). Six scenarios met neither criteria and were excluded from the final model. These are shown in Appendix F. Of the 24 scenarios meeting the criteria for model inclusion, none fell into the ‘‘Organization/Politics’’ category, which was removed from further consideration.

## 3.2.5. Step 5 – build conflict sources model

Our last data analysis step was to build a model of conflict sources. This model contained 24 scenarios that fell into 3 categories and 5 subcategories.

## 4. Result and model

Of the 50 conflict scenarios collected, 7 were found to be irrelevant. The remaining 43 went through two rounds of Q-sort analysis and an online survey. Multivariate analysis of data from this survey reduced this group of scenarios to 24, which were included in the final conflict sources model. Our analysis strongly suggested an alternative three-category conceptual structure: we think conflict arises from the testing process itself, and the people involved in the process, but we also found that communication factors and not a broader organizational factor were a key source for conflict in the testing regime. Fig. 1 depicts the relationship between the three categories as a layered subset structure.

In the final model, ‘‘Process’’ serves as the organizational context in which software development takes place. ‘‘People’’ are considered to be contained within processes because, when enforced, process constrains individual and group behavior. Finally, ‘‘Communication’’ is the innermost layer – representing the key component of human behavior, rendering it subsidiary to people-based conflict. Considering this structure from the bottom up, project team communication is performed by people, whose choices of when, how, and what to communicate are constrained and guided by established process.

The three layers of the model can then be considered in the context of five subcategories which were retained through two rounds of Q-sort analysis and a round of factor analysis supported by an online survey. Our interpretation of the category–subcategory–description information gleaned from these three analytical steps is given below.

## 4.1. Process (documentation, 3 of 24)

Two out of the three conflict scenarios were contributed by testers who indicated that developers were responsible for the conflict. Conflict arises when developers fail to document and communicate corrected defects and development changes. In the other case, a developer identified inadequate requirements as a conflict source, which resulted in tester requests for change after code completion.

## 4.2. Process (process compliance or noncompliance, 2 of 24)

In these, the conflict was explicitly attributed to process noncompliance. In the first, a tester reported the failure of a developer to follow a correct defect management process. The second illustrated another common source of developer–tester conflict: the lack of code standards. Testers may use methods that make assumptions about the code while developers program according to the detailed design, not taking code standards or testing expectations into account. For example, in scenario 47, testers criticized both the design and the code, recommending that developers comply with a coding standard. While this was a specific instance of noncompliance, the scenario also implied an additional process issue: the failure to specify a standard early in the software development process.

## 4.3. People (emotions/attitude/personality, 8 of 24)

In some cases, scenarios contributed by developers and testers revealed strong negative interactions. They can be characterized by emotional content type: behavioral (negative behavior), perceptual (including negative attributions about the other’s attitude or orientation), or some combination of these. The damaging effects of negativity are often explicit. In one scenario, a tester described an emotional outburst by a developer that results in them abandoning a prescribed level of testing: downstream testing problems and software quality issues can easily be imagined. Additional damage scenarios included developers delaying in acknowledging environmental issues, developers withholding assistance in test design, and developers refusing to provide a test tool. In other cases, poor working relationships and reduced team effectiveness were not stated but can be inferred, as in developer reluctance to work with a tester who is believed to gloat about critical defects.

## 4.4. People (knowledge/experience, 2 of 24)

Two developers reported conflict resulting from a lack of knowledge of testers. In one, invalid defects were identified due to the tester’s lack of application knowledge, and in another a tester lacked understanding of requirements. These knowledge or experience failures implied an upstream process failure in the organization, which typically mandated developer-created documentation to support development of test scripts. Reports of tester knowledge deficits raised the possibility that necessary documentation was missing, inadequate, or delivered too late.

## 4.5. Communication (knowledge sharing, 9 of 24)

Many communication conflicts between developers and testers included instances of errors not being detected because the test lead was left out of the communication loop during code maintenance, of developers delivering hardware that was not ready for testing (because testers failed to communicate their needs), and of late and/or unclear communication of new production release criteria, which led to disagreements about implementation readiness. Other issues concerned delays and reduced quality arising from a lack of proper communication. For example, failure to communicate the status of code completion or changes to code could have resulted in invalid test cases and test failures.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Summary of key findings

Our results indicate that developer–tester conflict occurs because they do not document their work or follow standardized procedures correctly; they also engage in, or are recipients of, strongly negative behaviors and consequently develop negative perceptions of each other; and they do not communicate with each other appropriately, effectively, or efficiently.

Our model is based on the collection of 43 written conflict scenarios from both software developers and testers. Our mode assumed that individual conflict resides within, and perhaps, occurs due to, individual involvement in the process framework. Our analysis indicated that the communication processes tend to have the capacity to mediate the interactions and subsequent disagreements between participants.

We believe that both our data and our model represent a significant advance from prior models. A final contrast between our study and prior research is presented by our finding that communication functions as a major source of developer–tester conflict. However, all but one of our communication-related scenarios appear to be symptoms of a lack of unified, appropriately designed and effectively applied project structure and process. Thus, we believe that this study provides a major advancement to that of prior models.

## 5.2. Implications

Our findings have important theoretical implications. Conflict appears to originate from the organizational layers, which can be perfectly mapped to interpersonal factors identified in previous studies including structure, perceptual interface, and communication. Our findings identified both people and processes as conflict sources. However, communication is an additional major source of interpersonal conflict.

The findings have also important implications for practice. Accurate identification of potential conflict sources enable IS development managers and technical leads to proactively alter management strategies to prevent dysfunctional conflict from developing, or intervening before it can escalate and damage project outcomes, software quality, and/or ongoing working relationships. Developer–tester conflict is rooted in both context and human interaction. From a practical standpoint, organizations need to take these categories into account in creating both longterm strategies and short-term tactics to manage interpersonal conflict between software developers and testers.

Clearly, the lack of effective and efficient communication causes problems. Therefore, it is critical to create an environment that facilitates effective and efficient communication between developers and testers, decreasing the opportunity for misunderstanding between them. Developers and testers should choose one or more communication channels to reduce misunderstanding caused by information equivocality and task uncertainty so that communication between them can be improved. It is important for an organization to decrease the knowledge and experience gap between its software developers and testers and create a working environment in which developers and testers trust each other and maintain a good relationship. To build trust between developers and testers, an organization may also engage developers and testers in social activities outside work.

## 5.3. Limitations

Our study had several limitations. First, it used a convenience sample from a single organization, possibly limiting the generalizability of its findings. Second, the sample size was relatively small, leading toa final collection of only 43 usable conflict scenarios. Third, the data collected did not include standard control data (e.g., age, gender, job tenure, and education) that would support identification of sub-constituencies within the groups. Last, qualitative research methodology and Q-sort method, in particular, have some inherent limitations; e.g., different raters may have different perspectives on the same conflict scenario, leading them to put the same conflict scenario into different categories or subcategories.

Future research should draw developers and testers from several different organizations to collect more conflict scenarios. In addition to addressing conflict sources and reaction of the parties involved, researchers might address conflict escalation and conflict resolution. Future studies should also collect demographic data for the purpose of providing descriptive statistics for the sample and supporting testing for potential control variables. In terms of data analysis, comparing the perceptions of developers to those of testers is encouraged in addition to identifying and categorizing the conflict sources.

## 6. Conclusion

Interpersonal conflict between developers and testers is an inevitable phenomenon in the software development process. Our findings suggest that conflict arises in three major contextual layers: process, people, and communication. Although complete elimination of developer–tester conflict is impossible, overall software development effectiveness and efficiency can be greatly improved if conflict is managed at the source. To fulfill this goal, the first step is to clearly identify and thoroughly understand the sources of conflict, for which our results have provided the basis and demonstrated its utility.

Mark L. Gillenson is Professor of Management Information Systems in the Fogelman College of Business and Economics of the University of Memphis. He received his B.S. degree in Mathematics from Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute and his M.S. and Ph.D. degrees in Computer and Information Science from the Ohio State University. He is an associate editor of the Journal of Database Management and has published in such leading journals as MIS Quarterly, European Journal of Information Systems, and Information & Management. His latest book is Fundamentals of Database Management Systems 2nd edition, 2012, John Wiley & Sons.

Gertrude Moeller is a practicing project manager and program administrator, recently retired from a globally branded logistics company headquartered in the midsouth region of the United States. She has served on the Board of PMI Memphis Chapter since 2001, including seven vears in her current position as Director of Academic Outreach.
