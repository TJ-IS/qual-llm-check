## Editorial

Welcome to the final issue of the Information Systems Journal in 2010. Our first paper of Björn Lundell, Brian Lings and Edvin Lindqvist (2010) looks at the issue of open source in the Swedish context. A qualitative telephone survey was carried out to look at perceptions of open source, open source adoption and development models in Swedish companies. Its goal was to investigate the extent to which open source has influenced business thinking, as seen from the standpoint of stakeholders. 58 telephone interviews were conducted, of these, only 6 interviews resulted in conversations with practitioners whose companies had no current involvement with open source. They analysed the implications of open source from a number of perspectives, including: business motivations and rationale; individual and organisational motivations; and tensions concerning different values (community vs. corporate values). The authors found that uptake is much higher than reported in earlier studies but, as with previous studies, activity is still concentrated in SMEs. There is increased evidence of interest beyond the simple use of open source components at the infrastructure level. Further, a significant proportion of the companies studied are supporting the open source community as well as benefiting from it. Support includes participation in existing projects and the release of new software under open source licences. Particularly striking about the study is the level to which companies are represented which have a business model strongly influenced by open source. Overall, the survey suggests that organisational involvement in open source development is a promoter of change: of perceptions, development processes and ultimately business models.

The paper of Albert Boonstra and Marjolein van Offenbeek presents a case study related to telecare. This is defined as the use of information and communication systems to facilitate care delivery to individuals in their homes. Policymakers have high expectations of telecare with many policy reports on telecare adopting an optimistic and deterministic view. These reports emphasise the medical, economic, social and political advantages by suggesting that telecare will in itself improve the lives of people needing care, while at the same time saving public money. Drawing on the work of Chu. & Smithson (2007) and Häkkinen & Hilmola (2008), amongst many others, the authors use the structurational model to develop a theory in terms of technology appropriation, institutional context and other factors before examining the case itself. The case study is an interesting description of a health care initiative which, despite the optimism for such systems, has been largely unsuccessful here as its implementation proved very complex. The authors argue that social multidimensionality complicated communication between stakeholders and this was at least a partial cause of the lack of success. The paper concludes with a preliminary proposal for more consistent telecare implementation modes.

Keng Siau, Xin Tan and Hong Sheng examine information systems professionals in team contexts, in particular identifying and understanding the important characteristics of ‘good team members in software development projects. They did so by applying the repertory grid, an established psychological technique, to guide their interviews with twenty-one experienced information systems professionals, who have had extensive experience in software development teams. The data was analyzed qualitatively using the open coding method of grounded theory. Fifty-nine unique characteristics were identified and classified into eight categories. Among them, attitude/motivation, knowledge, interpersonal/communication skills and working cognitive ability were perceived by research participants to be the most important categories. The results have significant implications for information systems recruiting, training, staffing, and human resource management. The comprehensive list of important characteristics, together with the relative importance from the perspectives of peer team members, can serve as the criteria for managers to select appropriate members for an information systems development project. Their study also supplements the research on management of information systems development teams.

David Avison, Guy Fitzgerald and Philip Powell
