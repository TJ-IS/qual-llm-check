# Do We Order Product Review Information Display? How?

Abstract: The purpose of product reviews is to help consumers make more informed purchase decisions. By integrating the information processing literature, schema theory, and “fit” notion, this research seeks to answer the following questions: Would ordered product review display (relative to random review display) be more helpful and easier to comprehend? If so, what kind of product review display order would be more effective? A set of experiments was conducted based on the review information manifested in the form of attribute-based and experience-based reviews and the product being reviewed. Our results show that, relative to random product review information display, product reviews ordered by their types (i.e., presenting an attribute/experience-based review before another type) are perceived to be more helpful and easier to comprehend. We further observed that displays of attribute-based product reviews followed by experience-based product reviews are perceived to be more helpful for a search product. Likewise, displays of experience-based product reviews followed by attribute-based product reviews are more helpful for an experience product. However, we did not observe significant improvement in the ease of review comprehension based on ordering the product review information by product type.

Keywords: product review, information display order, product type, information processing, schema theory

## 1 Introduction

Online product reviews or the product assessment posted by people who have used a product are among the most important sources of information that consumers refer to when making purchase decisions [18]. The rapid accumulation of reviews over time has resulted in the fundamental problem of information overload [20]. To help consumers make more informed purchase decisions, scholars and practitioners have called for research on identifying product review displays that are deemed effective for potential consumers [42; 49]. Review effectiveness is reflected by two variables. One variable is whether the reviews are perceived to be helpful, and the other variable is whether the reviews are easy to comprehend [2; 43]. Review helpfulness provides parameters for the degrees of information that should be included in reviews to assist consumer evaluations of focal products [45]. Ease of comprehension refers to the consumers’ perception of the cognitive effort needed to comprehend the reviews [46]. A website that can present reviews effectively to potential consumers can improve consumer satisfaction, purchase inclination, and website loyalty, which lead to sustainable online business [62].

One of the product review display approaches adopted by Amazon.com is the arrangement of reviews based on characteristics. Research on product review presentation is rare. The bulk of studies conducted on general information provision and not on product review in specific. For instance, to establish the notion that the order of information display can affect a consumer’s product choice, Bruine de Bruin and Keren [7] showed that, when a set of products are presented to consumers with different product orders (e.g., showing product A, product B, and then product C versus displaying product C, product B, and then product A), the consumers’ choice behavior is also different. Suk, et al. [68] further exemplified this point by showing that different reference prices can be formed even by ordering the prices of a single product. Previous studies have highlighted that information presentation is not trivial in the perception and subsequent decision making of a consumer [4; 8; 23; 40]; how information, particularly product review information, should be ordered to positively affect a consumer’s information evaluation is not yet well understood. Given the increasing prevalence of product review information on websites and the dearth of understanding on how such information could be more effectively presented, the present study seeks to address these questions: (1) Would consumers perceive ordered product review displays as more effective, and (2) in what manner should that review information be organized?

To answer these questions, we considered two typical types of product review information, namely, attribute-based and experience-based [39; 49; 67; 79]. An attribute-based product review discusses the evaluation of a product based on its attributes (e.g., a review of this type could be written as “the processing speed of this computer is good, it always runs quickly and keeps quiet”); whereas an experience-based product review is characterized by an overall evaluation of a focal product (e.g., such a review can be written as “this is really a good product. I am so proud of it”). On the one hand, experiencebased product reviews are characterized by the overall experience evaluation and, thus, assist a consumer in gaining an overarching understanding of a product. On the other hand, attribute-based product reviews are denoted by specific attribute assessments and, thus, facilitate a more developed assessment of a product.

Drawing on the literature of consumer information processing and integrating it with schema theory and “fit” notion, we posit that ordered reviews, based on the review types, would be more effective than randomly displayed reviews. Through empirical validation, this study extends the literature by showing how a consumer processes information and how the display order of different types of information facilitates his review evaluation [4; 11; 17]. Furthermore, by considering product types, this research provides insight regarding the moderating conditions of product types on the effects of information display of different types of review information (i.e., attribute-based and experience-based product reviews) on a consumer’s review evaluation.

## 2 Theoretical Background

Online product reviews are among the most important sources of information that help consumers make purchase decisions. However, with the development of online platforms, too much information and conflicting feedback in reviews lead to information overload [10]. Thus, to facilitate potential customers to make easier purchase decisions, scholars and practitioners focus significant attention on presenting reviews that are deemed effective to potential consumers [32; 48; 49; 80]. Review effectiveness is manifested by the following two factors: one is whether the reviews are helpful to potential consumers and the other is whether the reviews are easy to comprehend by consumers [36; 43]. That is, reviews are considered effective when the reviews are helpful and easy to comprehend. With the significance of the exploration of effective reviews, the question of how to make reviews more effective (i.e., beneficial) to consumers becomes crucial.

Prior works have explored a variety of review characteristics that affect review helpfulness. For instance, Cao, et al. [10] examined the relationship between the semantic characteristics (e.g., valence) of product review information and review helpfulness. They observed that, compared with product reviews that have mixed or neutral opinions, product reviews with extreme opinions receive more helpfulness votes. Li and Zhan [35] used the mining method to identify several review information characteristics (e.g., review length and review sentence length) that are related to review helpfulness. Furthermore, Willemsen, et al. [78] determined that review expression density and diversity significantly predict perceived helpfulness. As regards the effect of review contextual factors, Connors, et al. [14] and Forman, et al. [18] have consistently determined that the reviewers who posted the review typically influence review helpfulness. The discourse of reviewer identity influences consumer perceptions and evaluations of review helpfulness. Huang, et al. [27] also determined that reviewer identity discourse does influence consumer psychological distance, which leads to different evaluations of review helpfulness. Despite the differences in the theoretical lens of prior works, a commonality exists: the reviews should be presented in a manner that consumers can adequately process.

Figure 1 shows the theoretical lens adopted in this research. Specifically, we anchor on three theoretical underpinnings: (1) information processing theories to theorize the review information display (Section 2.1), (2) schema theory to theorize the product type (Section 2.2), and (3) theoretical “fit” notion to connect review information display and product type (Section 2.3).

## 2.1 Information Processing Perspective of Review Information Display

Information processing theories suggest that the order of information display influences the impression formation [3; 15; 25], judgment [7; 41], and decision making of customers [23; 40]. In particular, two information processing strategies are used, namely, the top-down and bottom-up strategies, which represent the concept of “part” and “unity,” respectively [6; 50; 57]. The top-down processing strategy refers to the information processing approach in which a consumer starts with the evaluation of a product’s overall performance and then assesses the performance of each specific attribute of the product. In contrast, a consumer who uses the bottom-up information processing strategy typically examines the product’s individual attributes before evaluating the overall performance of a product.

The conceptualization of top-down and bottom-up information strategies can be observed in several earlier works. For instance, researchers in the field of system development have developed two system design strategies, namely, top-down and bottom-up development [58; 60]. Organization management scholars proposed the top-down and bottom-up management styles in different situations to improve management efficiency [12; 37], whereas market researchers observed that these two strategies are suitable for different markets in terms of resources, flexibility, and adaptability [61; 66]. Although different conceptualizations are utilized in various research initiatives, the central thesis is that the top-down approach focuses on the analysis or process from the unity to the part, whereas the bottom-up approach emphasizes analysis from the part to the unity, which influences consumer decision making differently [24; 52].

Relating the product review information display forms to the two information processing strategies suggests that a customer first depends on the experience-based product review information to gain an overall understanding of the product if he or she adopts the top-down information processing strategy. Then, the consumer utilizes the attribute-based product review to concentrate on assessments based on individual product attributes. Conversely, if a consumer adopts the bottom-up information processing strategy, then he/she first uses the attribute-based product review information to understand the details of a product and then uses the experience-based product review to gain a summarized picture. As exemplified in a previous study, the adopted processing strategy affects the effectiveness of a consumer’s judgment [26], but questions remain regarding which strategy is more feasible for the processing of product review information or which strategy particularly enables consumers to view review information as effective. For example, Park and Smith [50] determined that consumers use the top-down information processing strategy when faced with a product choice set characterized by noncomparable attributes. This finding means that a consumer evaluates the overall performance of alternative options (i.e., general assessment) prior to the assessment and a comparison of their attributes (i.e., the details) is more effective. However, Bolton and Reed [6] contend that the bottom-up information processing strategy is more effective than the top-down processing strategy, especially when product information display deviates from the consumers’ prior knowledge-based schema. We refer to schema theory to understand the type of processing strategy a consumer would adopt in our specific research context.

## 2.2 The Schematic Perspective of Product Type

Schema theory posits that prior knowledge is organized in dominant or abstract mental structures, termed as schemas, and how an individual interacts and processes the externally given information depends on how these mental structures are formulated [9; 16]. Relating this schema theoretical perspective to the discussion of the top-down and bottom-up information processing would suggest that the processing approach is contingent on the formed schemas in the consumers, which is the fundamental product type that is reviewed. For example, Lynch and Schuler [38] applied schema theory to examine the match between a spokeperson and the product type that he is supposed to speak for. The authors conceptualized that a product type is mentally represented for a spokeperson as a schema. Perceptual outcomes are contingent on the two schemas. Our study focuses on information processing based on a specific display approach, which may not ex ante construe a schema, but the product type that we are considering does. A good understanding of the product type is imperative. Before we delve into the theoretical “fit” notion that allows us to theorize the interaction betweeen information processing display and product type, we draw on the liteature about the nature of product type.

A product can be categorized as either a search type or an experience type [13]. The essential difference between a search product and an experience product exists in the consumer’s judgment of the product’s quality or performance before his/her purchase or consumption [29; 47]. For a search product, such as a notebook computer, a consumer can evaluate the product based on information on its attributes, such as the hard drive and the processor, before experiencing it (pre-established knowledge-based schema). In other words, a consumer knows about a search product’s performance based on its specifications and other attributes. In contrast, for an experience product, such as a dress, a consumer will have difficulty judging the product before experiencing it despite the availability of attribute information. The nature difference between a search product and an experience product leads consumers to formulate different schemas in using processing strategies, i.e., top-down vs. bottom-up.

## 2.3 The “Fit” Notion of Review Information Display and Product Type

Based on schema theory, we can deduce that a product type could construe a specific schema and, to incorporate this consideration with the top-down versus bottom-up information processing strategies. To understand how different types of reviews presented with two specific order, we refer to the theoretical “fit” notion. The $" { \sf f i t } ^ { * }$ notion is a generalized concept from two related theories: (1) cognitive fit theory, which centers around the interaction (fit) between an individual and the demands of a given task [72], and (2) task–technology fit, which suggests a fit between the demands of a task and the functionality of technology [22; 69; 81]. In our context, the essential thesis of the $" \mathrm { f i t } " $ notion is that the match between information display approach and a task (i.e., product type in our case) could lead to a consumer responding more favorably [26; 65]. Prior studies have suggested that the fit between “information need” and “information supply” significantly improve people’s evaluation and performance [70]. For instance, based on resource matching theory, Tan, et al. [70] suggested that the fit between the available information and the information required for decision making affects the decision performance, whereas Hong, et al. [26] determined that a fit between the shopping task and the information display format ensures favorable performance and evaluation.

In relation to our case, we reiterate that a “fit” between review information display and product type could positively affect review effectiveness. In other words, we reiterate that the effects of review information display on a consumer’s review evaluation could be contingent on the product type that the reviews described. This $" \hbar ^ { * } \hbar ^ { * }$ can be identified based on the shared characteristics between the review information display and the product type. Attribute-based reviews enable consumers to learn the attributes of a search product, whereas experience-based reviews, which are closely related to other consumers’ evaluations of overall performance, enable consumers to learn more about the product experience and, thus, would be more closely related to an experience product [5; 31]. This finding suggests that attributebased review information is always primarily expected before overall performance is examined when a consumer looks at a search product. Thus, the bottom-up information processing strategy would be more likely to be “fitting”. Conversely, when a consumer buys an experience product, considering the obscure characteristics of its attributes, overall performance-related review information is more likely to be primarily examined before other information [5]. Thus, the top-down information processing strategy would be more likely to be activated when a consumer buys an experience product.

## 3 Research Model and Hypotheses Development

Figure 2 depicts the research model. The effect of review information display on review effectiveness is contingent on the product type. We look at three types of review information display, namely, (1) the randomly displayed mixture of attribute-based and experience-based reviews, (2) the ordered display of attribute-based reviews followed by experience-based reviews, and (3) the ordered display of experience-based reviews followed by attribute-based reviews. For product type, we consider search and experience products. As the outcome, review effectiveness is reflected by perceived review usefulness and ease of comprehension.

We develop the hypotheses in the following order: First, we establish (H1) that ordered review information display is better than random review information display. Second, we consider the moderation role of product type (H2) on the effects of review information display (i.e., random vs. ordered) on review effectiveness. Third, we focus on the ordered review information display by theorizing which form of the two ordered review displays (i.e., attribute-based followed by experience-based reviews or experience-based followed by attribute-based reviews) could lead to better review effectiveness (H3 and H4).

## 3.1 Ordered Versus Random (Benchmark) Review Information Display

A consumer’s evaluation of review effectiveness is affected by how fluently one perceives information processing. Prior studies have argued that processing fluency positively influences an individual’s favorable evaluation and judgment regarding information, which is in line with information processing theories [55; 77]. Our argument of relationship between the review information display (ordered versus random) and review effectiveness can be elaborated based on the concept of chunk [64]. A chunk refers to a set of elements that are strongly associated among themselves but weakly associated with other chunk elements [21]. Based on this definition, we conceptualized that attribute-based or experience-based reviews are two different types of chunks. Prior studies have argued that a consumer would typically spend a significant amount of cognitive effort to comprehend them when processing information belonging to different types of chunks, which leads to poor fluency in the information comprehension process [54; 56]. Extending this notion to the current research, we expect that, when the reviews are randomly displayed, a consumer requires constant switching between various processes when dealing with different types of information chunks, which need a significant amount of effort to understand. In contrast, when the reviews are presented in a typical order (i.e., attribute-based followed by experience-based reviews or experiencebased followed by attribute-based reviews), a consumer processes such types of reviews much more fluently because he/she engages in less process switching among the different chunks. Therefore, we posit that:

H1: The ordered product review information display is perceived to be more effective than the random product review information display, as reflected by (i) higher perceived helpfulness and (ii) greater ease of comprehension.

## 3.2 Effect of Review Information Display as Moderated by Product Type

As mentioned previously, the search and experience products have different natures and, thus, trigger different schemas. A consumer may have a relatively easier time evaluating a search product because it possesses clear attribute information, whereas for an experience product, considering the obscure nature of its attributes, overall performance-related information is more suitable for consumer evaluation [1; 19]. That is, when a consumer evaluates an experience product, grasping the quality of the product through the introduction of its attributes and overall performance compared with the evaluation of a search product is relatively difficult. This finding is consistent with the definition of search and experience products, which suggests that a consumer will consider it relatively difficult to assess an experience product before experiencing it herself, compared with a search product [29; 76]. Based on this finding and the chunk concept, we further develop H1 by arguing that, compared with a search product, the transfer from one type of chunk (i.e., attribute-based reviews) to another type of chunk (i.e., experience-based reviews) for an experience product would need more cognitive effort. Therefore, we propose that, for different types of products, the effects of product review information display (i.e., ordered versus random review information displays) on review effectiveness differ, posited as follows:

H2: The product type moderates the effect of product review information display on review effectiveness. Specifically:

i. The effect of ordered product review information display (versus random product information display) on perceived review helpfulness is stronger for a search product than for an experience product.

ii. The effect of ordered product review information display (versus random product information display) on perceived ease of comprehension will be stronger for a search product than for an experience product.

## 3.3 Comparative Effect of Ordered Review Information Displays as Moderated by Product

## Type

The argument presented previously focuses on theorizing why the ordered (i.e., attribute-based followed by experience-based reviews and experience-based followed by attribute-based reviews) and random product information displays have different consequences on review effectiveness evaluation. To advance theorization further, we compare the effects of two ordered product review information displays (i.e., attribute-based followed by experience-based reviews and experience-based followed by attributebased reviews) on consumer evaluation by considering, at the same time, the product type described by the review information. Based on schema theory, we deduce that different types of schemas would be activated when consumers seek to buy different types of product, that is, the top-down (bottom-up) information processing strategy could be activated when shopping for an experience (a search) product. Further, applying the notion of “fit” [26; 34; 74], we argue that, when the product review information display (e.g., the ordered reviews) matches with the product type, the review evaluation would be favorable. In detail, when the reviews are presented in the order of attribute-based followed by experience-based review information display sequence, which is a bottom-up processing strategy, consumers focusing on search products would express higher review effectiveness. Conversely, when the reviews are presented in the order of experience-based followed by attribute-based review information display sequence, which is a topdown processing strategy, consumers looking for experience products would report higher review effectiveness. Accordingly, we posit that:

H3: Product type moderates the effect of ordered product review information display on the consumer’s evaluation of review helpfulness. Specifically:

i. For a search product, the reviews presented in the order of attribute-based followed by experiencebased information display sequence would be perceived as more helpful than the reviews presented in the order of experience-based followed by attribute-based information display sequence.

ii. For an experience product, the reviews presented in the order of attribute-based followed by experience-based information display sequence would be perceived as less helpful than the reviews presented in the order of experience-based followed by attribute-based information display sequence.

H4: Product type moderates the effect of the ordered product review information display on the consumer’s ease of review comprehension. Specifically:

i. For a search product, the reviews presented in the order of attribute-based followed by experiencebased information display sequence would be perceived to be easier to comprehend than the reviews presented in the order of experience-based followed by attribute-based information display sequence.

ii. For an experience product, the reviews presented in the order of attribute-based followed by experience-based information display sequence would be perceived as less easier to comprehend than the reviews presented in the order of experience-based followed by attribute-based information display sequence.

## 4 Methodology

Considering the benchmark of the randomly displayed mixture of two types of reviews, we designed a 3 (review information display order: randomly displayed mixture, ordered display with attributebased reviews followed by experience-based reviews, and ordered display with experience-based reviews followed by attribute-based reviews) × 2 (product type: search versus experience) full-factorial experiment to empirically validate the hypothesis (for the treatments, see Table 1). We used this design to test (1) whether the reviews with a specific display order (i.e., attribute-based followed by experience-based and experience-based followed by attribute-based) would be perceived as more effective than those that are randomly presented and (2) whether these two kinds of display are moderated by the focal product described by the reviews. Before conducting the experiment, we first conducted two pretests to identify the products that represent the search and experience types and to validate our argument regarding the specific sequences of the product review information used in the top-down and bottom-up information processing strategies.

## 4.1 Pretest 1

This pretest aims to confirm whether the products used in our experiment are considered search or experience products. This step is performed because prior research suggests an inconsistency in product classification. For instance, a digital camera is considered a search product in some studies but an experience product in others [19; 29; 45]. To generalize our findings, we decided to select two products for each category in our study. We performed the following steps: First, we identified the products considered as either a search product or an experience product through a review of the existing literature [20; 29; 45; 76]. Then, we selected the products that were commonly used, that is, taking the digital camera and notebook computer as search products and clothing and shoes as experience products.

## 4.2 Pretest 2

We used the top-down and the bottom-up information processing strategies as the foundation from which we theorized regarding the effect of review display order on consumer evaluation. Although we cannot determine how the processing strategy is undertaken during information processing, we measured consumer expectations regarding the information display sequence. In the second pretest, we recruited 10 students to test their information expectation sequence. We asked them to answer the following two reverse items: (1) “Generally, when I read product reviews, I search for information on related product attributes before I evaluate the product’s overall performance/quality” and (2) “Generally, when I read product reviews, I search for information on the product’s overall performance before I go through its attribute information.” The mean value of consumers who answered the first question is 4.30, and the mean value for consumers who answered the second question is 4.10. No significant difference was observed (p > 0.5) when compared with the middle value, namely, 4.0. This result suggests that consumers have no significant preferences regarding the two types of information display sequence.

To understand whether product type influences the information expectations of consumers, we designed a scenario and asked the subjects to answer the following questions: (1) “I search for information related to product attributes before I evaluate the product’s overall performance when I am planning to buy a notebook computer/clothes” and (2) “Generally, I search for information on the product’s overall performance before I go through its attribute information when I am planning to buy a notebook computer/clothes.” When the subjects sought to buy a notebook computer, the results were significantly different for the answers of the first and second questions $( m e a n _ { H i r s t } = 5 . 3 0 ; m e a n _ { s e c o n d } = 3 . 4 0 ; t = 4 . 7 3 4 ; p <$ 0.01). Similar results were obtained when the subjects sought to buy clothes. The means of the answers for the first and second questions were significantly different (mean<sub>first</sub> = 3.30; mean<sub>second</sub> = 5.30; t = −4.714; p < 0.01). The same findings were also identified in the examination of the digital camera and shoes. Overall, the results support the argument that, without a specific product type in mind, the subjects have no preference regarding the information display sequence. However, when the subjects have a clear idea about what type of product they are evaluating, they formulate an expectation regarding the order of information display.

## 4.3 Subjects

A total of 192 university students were recruited for the experiment. University students are appropriate for this study because the comprehension behavior of students are not significantly different from the broader population in this research context [14]. We recruited the students from a large university to participate in the experiment by announcing it on posters and through the public announcement of teachers. The students were then randomly and equally assigned to six treatment groups. Participation was voluntary, and each subject received a token of appreciation in the amount of RMB 30 (approximately USD 5.00). Finally, excluding the subjects who did not finish the whole experiment, we collected 186 valid data samples. The average age of the participants was 21.41 years old, and 52.15% of the subjects were female.

## 4.4 Independent variables

For the experiment, we extracted hundreds of reviews for each product from a large online platform by using the Locoy Spider software. We screened the reviews based on the following five steps: First, two research assistants conducted an initial round of screening to ensure that the reviews were related to the products. We deleted the reviews that talked about logistics. Second, based on the proposed definitions of the two types of reviews, four research assistants were asked to classify the reviews into attribute-based and experience-based groups. Third, with regard to the number of reviews we should use in the experiment, a pretest was conducted to confirm the number of reviews to be included. Consumers in this sample pool usually read 20 reviews. Fourth, to extract the fixed number of reviews for each treatment in the experiment, we performed the following steps: (1) we screened 50 reviews which were either attribute-based or experience-based for each product; (2) to ensure that the reviews were of similar lengths [51], we deleted extremely long or short reviews; and (3) from the screened samples, we chose the same number of reviews for each treatment. Considering that the industrial report suggests that most of the reviews be positive (i.e., at approximately 90%)2, we (4) added two negative reviews to the design in each treatment and (5) to ensure the robustness of this classification, we used card sorting [44]. We recruited four more students to classify the reviews into two groups based on their understanding of the definitions of attribute-based and experience-based reviews. The reviews were correctly categorized, and the inter-coder reliability was determined as follows: notebook computer = 100%; clothing = 97.5%; digital camera = 100%; and shoes = 96.5%.

## 4.5 Dependent variables

In line with previous studies, we used the following items to measure the perceived review helpfulness [14]: (1) The reviews improved my ability to make a decision on whether or not to buy this product; (2) The reviews provided me insight into whether I would like this product; and (3) The reviews contained helpful information about this product. As regards the measurement of the ease of comprehension, we adopted the following items from Igbaria, et al. [30] and Lee and Aaker [33]: (1) The reviews were easy to comprehend; (2) Grasping the meaning of the reviews was easy for me; (3) Understanding the reviews was easy for me; and (4) I find that the reviews were expressed well and not difficult for me to understand. All the items are reflective indicators measured on a seven-point Likert scale from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” We used the committee approach to translate the questionnaire [73] because the respondents were native Chinese speakers. A four-step revision procedure was followed to ensure the validity of the measurement items3.

## 4.6 Experimental Procedure

We conducted our study in an electronic commerce laboratory. The subjects were randomly assigned to each treatment (see Table 1). For each treatment, we presented a mock-up online shopping website to the subjects (for a screenshot, see Figure 3). The website had two main parts, as follows: (1) the first module showed the description of the product, including an introduction of its various characteristics, and (2) the second module presented product reviews with different display orders. Such types of settings are similar to other mundane online shopping websites [36]. When the subjects were seated, they were asked to read the instructions carefully before proceeding with the experiment. Based on their initial understanding of the instructions, the subjects were given several minutes to ask questions about the experiment. Afterward, the subjects were asked to start the experiment. In the experiment, we used a scenario to enable the subjects to become more involved in the experience of a real online shopping task. This scenario is common in consumer behavior studies [59]. Meanwhile, this scenario is also consistent with the consumer information seeking and decision making behavior scenarios [49].

## 5 Data analysis

To gauge the significance of the examined variables better, we performed statistical tests on the demographic characteristics of the participants. The control check suggests that no significant differences exist across demographic information, such as age $( F = 0 . 3 6 3 , \ p > 0 . 5 )$ , gender $( F = 0 . 0 6 4 , \ p > 0 . 5 )$ shopping experience $( F = 3 . 0 4 8 , \ p \ > \ 0 . 1 )$ , product display sequence $( F = 0 . 5 2 8 , \ p > 0 . 5 )$ , product knowledge $( F = 0 . 0 1 4 , \mathfrak { p } > 0 . 5 )$ and product price $( F = 0 . 1 9 2 , \mathfrak { p } > 0 . 5 )$ . However, we observed differences in the consumers’ information processing involvement $( F = 1 0 . 6 3 6 , \ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , which suggests that the different degrees of the consumer’s review comprehension attention influence their evaluations of the review effectiveness. Other than the control check, we also performed manipulation check. We performed two types of manipulations, as follows: one is the manipulation of the reviews as either ordered or a randomly displayed mixture and the other is, under the condition of ordered reviews, manipulation of the reviews as attribute-based followed by experience-based or experience-based followed by attribute-based. Considering that the attribute-based and experience-based reviews are separately presented in different pages in the ordered treatment (i.e., Treatments 3, 4, 5, and 6 in Table 1), we asked the subjects to judge whether they read the same type of review information in the first page and the review in the second page. The results revealed that the reviews in the treatment of the random mixture $( m e a n = 5 . 6 5 0 , S D = 0 . 9 8 6 )$ typically gained a significantly higher evaluation $( t = 4 . 3 6 6 ; \mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ than the ordered reviews (mean = 4.910, SD = 1.145).

For the second manipulation (i.e., comparing Treatments 3, 4, 5, and 6 in Table 1), following the method of Xia and Bechwati [79], we asked the subjects to answer the following questions: (1) “The reviews I read on the first page are more related to the product attributes and characteristics”; and (2) “The reviews I read on the second page are more related to the product attributes and characteristics”. The results suggest that, in Treatments 3 and 4, consumers’ evaluations of the first $( m e a n = 4 . 9 7 0 ; S D = 1 . 3 2 9 )$ and second (mean = 4.260; SD = 1.223) questions were significantly different $( t = 3 . 0 4 8 ; \mathfrak { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ ), which implies that the two types of reviews presented in the first and second pages were achieved. Similarly, in Treatments 5 and 6, consumer evaluations of the first (mean = 3.920; SD = 1.359) and second (mean = 5.310; SD = 0.985) questions were also significantly different $( t = - 6 . 5 0 9 ; \mathfrak { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Our results suggest that the different display orders work in our experiment. In the next stage, we run the data and test the hypotheses.

## 6 Discussions

Table 5 summarizes the findings of our study. The experimental data indicate that the attributebased and experience-based reviews presented in an ordered manner is more effective than reviews presented in a random manner. Consistent with what we hypothesized, combining reviews that describe the same type of information (e.g., attribute-based) leads consumers to perceive the reviews more favorably. This result validates our proposition that presenting the same type of review information in a

#

chunk will facilitate consumers to evaluate with a more fluent processing and positive attitude. In addition, we determined that product type significantly moderates the relationship between the review display order and perceived ease of comprehension of the reviews, whereas its moderating effect on the relationship between review display order and perceived review helpfulness is not significant. That is, the reviews presented in a typical manner (either attribute-based followed by experience-based or experience-based followed by attribute-based) are perceived as easier to comprehend than the reviews presented in a random mixture for search products than for experience products. However, the effect of ordered display in relation to random mixture on review helpfulness is not significantly different for search and experience products. The findings enable us to understand that, although presenting the review with a special order (i.e., attribute-based followed by experience-based reviews or experience-based followed by attributebased reviews) is important for search and experience products, the effect for an experience product is more significant. As such, consumers are able to comprehend the reviews much more easily.

With regard to the two types of ordered review display (i.e., attribute-based followed by experiencebased or experience-based followed by attribute-based), our findings suggest that, when attribute-based reviews are presented before experience-based reviews, the reviews are perceived as more helpful in describing search products. In addition, when experience-based reviews are presented before attributebased reviews, the reviews are perceived as more helpful in describing experience products. However, no significant difference was observed in the evaluation of the ease of comprehension of reviews when a match occurs between the product type and the review display order. A possible explanation for the insignificant result is that both presentation manners require consumers to switch from one type of review to another, which would affect information process fluency and may require consumers to expend a similar amount of effort. As such, consumers may not sense a match of product type and review display order would ease their comprehension of the reviews. This finding is consistent with those of Hong, et al. ## 6.1 Implications for Research

Our research makes three main theoretical contributions. First, this study offers a new lens to examine review helpfulness. Compared with most prior studies which focus on exploring a series of review characteristics influencing review helpfulness [20; 45; 49], this study, as one of the first attempts, theorizes and tests the review order effect by taking product type into serious consideration. Although a recent study by Huang, et al. [28] proposed that the review and product congruity proposition (i.e., examining the fit relationship between product type and review type), their study did not proceed further to examine how consumer responses to mixtures of attribute-based and experience-based reviews. To this end, the pragmatic approach of this study to propose and validate how product reviews could be explicitly ordered for display to positively influence consumer evaluation is an important incremental step toward providing a facilitating information environment for consumers to process the reviews. In this perspective, this study serves as a step forward from the work of Huang, et al. [28] in specific and product review research in general [20; 45; 49].

Second, by integrating information presentation literature, schema theory, and theoretical “fit” notion, this study further conceptualized how a consumer evaluates an information environment that is purposely organized (i.e., ordered in a certain sequence) for specific product characteristics. Although information presentation studies have been tested and examined in various fronts (e.g., text versus image versus animation; descriptive text versus tabulated text) in several disciplines including psychology, marketing, and human–computer interaction areas (e.g.,[3; 7; 23]), the explicit schematic consideration of top-down and bottom-up information display is absent. By anchoring on the three theoretical perspectives and bridging across them to formulate a set of tested hypotheses, we also advocate an important message that information presentation studies should theorize the consequential impact of information display through cognitive schematic rationales (thus, schema theory) and its interaction with the context in which the display occurs (thus, “fit” notion).

#

Third, as reviewed earlier, the top-down approach is characterized by processing from the unity to the part, whereas the bottom-up approach is reflected by processing from the part to the unity (i.e., the reverse order) [24; 52]. Inconclusive predictions on perceptual outcomes from adopting the top-down versus bottom-up form of processing exist [6; 50]. We reconcile the literature by leveraging on the “fit” notion to suggest, in the context of product review display, that a fit between a consumer’s information processing strategy and the information display manner, namely, the order of attribute-based followed by experience-based reviews or experience-based followed by attribute-based reviews, leads to favorable evaluations. From this theoretical conjecture, we also extend the understanding and application of “fit” concept in the information comprehension arena.

## 6.2 Implications for Practice

This research also provides two practical contributions. First, we showed that random display of the product review information may not yield favorable consumer evaluation. Hence, an ordered sequence of the review display may be effective. Therefore, review website operators and shopping website operators where product review system is also incorporated would need to ponder on the issue of whether the traditional approach of listing the product reviews solely based on chronological order would be effective (a kind of random display that disregards the type of product content). As shown in this study, ordered display of the product reviews based on the product type may be favorable to consumer evaluation.

Second, based on the theoretical integration of information processing theories, schema theory, and “fit” notion, this study suggests that designing a review website in specific and a commercial website in general would need to consider the contingency nature of the information that is provided and the fundamental product/task that is focused on. Specifically, this study provides a prescriptive guideline on how to display ordered product review information depending on the type of product that the reviews are describing. For a search product, the display order of attribute-based reviews before experience-based reviews is more helpful. In contrast, for an experience product, the reverse order of presenting reviews, that is, experience-based reviews before attribute-based reviews, is more likely to help consumers make a decision. Thus, existing review information display algorithms could be revised to cater to this contingency nature of review information.

## 6.3 Limitations and Future Research

As with all studies, this research also has its limitations. First, although we focused on presenting a series of attribute-based and experience-based reviews to obtain interesting findings, the combination of attribute-based and experience-based characteristics in one review is not examined. How such reviews are processed should be explored in future studies. Second, we used half of the total reviews to represent attribute-based and experience-based reviews and did not examine the combination of these two types of reviews. Future research can further examine the best combination of these two types of reviews to achieve the highest review effectiveness. Third, cultural differences may be an important factor in influencing consumer comprehension and evaluation behavior. However, to the best of our knowledge, no research has showed any difference between the Chinese samples and broader samples. Based on prior studies [63], future research should consider exploring whether and how cultural differences have an important role in influencing consumer behavior. Fourth, in our experimental design, we randomly placed negative reviews in the review set. Although choosing the random display of the negative review will not provide a biased result, we conjecture that it would be much more interesting to explore whether placing the negative reviews in different positions influence consumers’ evaluations of the product. Prior studies have argued that the effect of negative reviews is more significant than positive reviews [53]. Meanwhile, information process theories also suggest that consumers faced a switch when they are presented with different types of information, such as positive and negative [71]. Last but not the least, although we used the student samples to explore review comprehension behavior, the investigation in broader samples would be more interesting, which would provide generalized results. These explorations represent interesting future research directions.

## 7 Conclusion

This research examines the different display orders of product reviews on consumer evaluations. Via a controlled experiment, we determined that the reviews presented in a certain order were deemed to be more helpful and easier to comprehend than the randomly presented reviews. In addition, the different product reviews described also moderate this effect. Through this study, we did not only integrate three theoretical perspectives, namely, information processing theories, schema theory, and “fit” notion, but we also encourage future information systems research to examine the design of website features that support the display of product review information.
