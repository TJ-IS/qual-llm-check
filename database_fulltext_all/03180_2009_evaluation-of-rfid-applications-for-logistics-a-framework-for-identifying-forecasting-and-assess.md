## Abstract

As with all information technologies, there is a necessity to determine the profitability of investments in Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) ex ante. A particularly important aspect is the challenging task of evaluating the multifaceted benefits of RFID deployments. While a large body of research on RFID benefits exists, our literature review indicates the absence of a comprehensive approach. We introduce a framework that combines the benefit evaluation steps of identification, forecasting and assessment. Based on insights gained in a 3-year research project with case studies in logistics, we refine a processbased IT-benefits classification and subsequently derive six types of RFID benefits that support the systematic identification of benefits, as well as the selection of forecast and assessment methods. We discuss how our framework can facilitate and enhance RFID investment decisions and guide future research activities.

## Introduction

Setting realistic expectations and defining clear objectives are significant success factors for information technology (IT) projects (Standish Group, 1995; Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998). This presupposes the ability to define the intended applications and to evaluate desired outcomes. This task becomes difficult, however, when an innovative technology like Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) is introduced to a new application domain – in this case logistics – and when the scope of possible applications and benefits is not yet fully understood.

RFID is a technology that has attracted considerable attention in the realm of Information Systems during recent years. Therefore, it is not surprising that considerable effort has been expended on the development of dedicated concepts for the economic evaluation of RFID investments. Existing RFID-specific approaches for ex ante profitability analysis of RFID tend to focus exclusively on individual aspects of the evaluation problem. This can lead to incorrect and misleading investment decisions.

To date, no comprehensive evaluation approach has been available. The work presented in this paper aims to address this gap by developing an evaluation framework that can serve as a guideline for application-oriented researchers and for practitioners. This framework focuses on the benefit rather than the cost side of the evaluation in order to address the particular challenges inherent to benefit evaluation. In general, the costs of IT

Subsequent to reviewing and discussing IT business value literature, in the next section we lay the methodological groundwork for our research. First, we use a process-based automation–information–transformation classification to identify the benefits of RFID. Since the quality of a forecast or monetary assessment of benefits is heavily dependant on the soundness of the underlying identification of benefits, we have extended the classification process. Interpretative case study research was used to derive further criteria for differentiating RFIDbased process impacts. We then define six types of RFID benefits as a comprehensive guide for benefit identification. This enables the selection of methods for forecasting benefits in quantitative terms. Finally, the results of the quantification are used as input to the assessment step, which completes the overall evaluation framework.

## RFID evaluation – available studies and approaches

We have identified two major methodological approaches for the ex ante quantification of RFID benefits. The first suggests following a path similar to that applied to most other technological innovations (Boer, 1999). This approach proposes the adjustment and application of well-established methods from the fields of capital budgeting and performance measurement. Examples encompass the application of dynamic capital budgeting methods such as Net Present Value (Laubacher et al., 2006), Activity-Based Costing (ABC) (Subirana et al., 2003) and non-financial scoring methods (Schuster et al., 2007). More highly evolved financial approaches that deal with uncertainty, for example the Real Options Analysis (Trigeorgis, 1996; Copeland & Antikarov, 2001), have not yet been applied to RFID, but to related infrastructural IT investments, for example in the realm of telecommunication networks (dos Santos, 1991).

Most of the methods referred to above address the problem of how to achieve overall quantification with the aim of establishing a basis for decisions regarding the initiation or postponement of an RFID investment (or, for that matter, decisions in respect of any investment accompanied by cost–benefit consequences that are insufficiently understood). The main criticism of these approaches is that they fail to satisfactorily operationalise underlying indicators, that is, cash flows, scores, etc. This criticism is directed in particular at capital budgeting methods that are widely used. Due to the fact that these methods rely on assumptions regarding cash flows, they fail to take the issue of specifying the relevant causal relationships in the underlying processes into account (Wen et al., 1998). As a result, they neglect the problems of how to predict consequences of RFID regarding the efficiency and quality of business processes (forecast) and of how to transform these consequences into monetary figures, such as cash flows (assessment). Gille & Stru¨ker (2008) provide an overview and discussion of these methods.

The second methodological approach is manifest in the extensive and expanding body of work aimed at developing specific methods for evaluating RFID investments (see Table 1). By following the chronology of a typical investment evaluation it is possible to distinguish four categories. The first category encompasses classification frameworks that facilitate the identification of potential benefits of RFID and raise awareness for specific problems related to RFID projects. It should be emphasised that, by design, these classification schemes are not geared to forecasting and valuation.

The second category, forecast models, provides more extensive information, as it deals with the prediction of process improvements, that is, the extent of RFID-induced changes in number of processes and activities and/or resource consumption per activity. This category includes two subsets: analytical models and simulation studies. These methods focus on the impact of the improved visibility enabled by RFID, for example, for buffer stocks, availability levels, etc. in specific process settings.

In contrast, the third category, assessment models, makes simple assumptions about the expected process improvements. Moreover, researchers in this category extend their view towards multiple processes and application scenarios, in order to come to a comprehensive financial evaluation of RFID investments. Many of these assessment models have been implemented as RFID calculation tools (mostly by the use of spreadsheets). They are designed to provide simple and practitioner-friendly support for the ex ante assessment of RFID investments. A downside of these tools is that they often prompt users to estimate future RFID-induced process improvements (e.g. the extent of achievable shrinkage reduction) without offering guidance on how to arrive at these figures.

The fourth and final category is represented by a number of field studies and live tests focusing on RFID benefits. These provide potential reference values for similar investment decisions.

In summary, it can be stated that while some contributions only indirectly address ex ante benefit evaluation (namely field studies and live tests that require an actual RFID implementation), the remainder focus on only one or two of the three main aspects of benefit evaluation: classification frameworks, such as Curtin et al. (2009), help in identifying potential benefits of RFID applications without addressing quantification issues. Forecast models, such as the one presented by Lee & O<sup>¨</sup> zer (2007), address the forecast problem of RFID investments, that is, on predicting the extent to which the number of processes and activities and/or resource consumption change, while largely neglecting the financial side. Finally, assessment models, such as Laubacher et al. (2006), focus on the assessment problem, that is, on attaching a monetary value to multiple process improvements, while using ‘expert estimations’ to bypass the forecast problem.

## Methodology

As mentioned above, to date there has been no holistic concept for RFID benefit evaluation that covers identification, forecast and assessment. In this paper we propose an evaluation framework that links these three steps and acts as a guide for both application-oriented researchers and practitioners. The key element of this framework is a classification schema for the identification of benefit types.

The value of any forecasting and assessment approach is dependent on the underlying identification of benefits. Hence, it is expedient to start by delineating the ways in which RFID contributes to improved performance in relation to the broader context of IT value generation. In IS literature, significant attention has been given to addressing the problem of how to ascertain the value of IT investments. Dedrick et al.’s 2003 review examines more than 50 empirical studies between 1985 and 2002. Overall, the literature on IT productivity and performance includes a substantial number of studies measuring the value by output results at country, industry or company level (Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998). As Mooney et al. (1996) point out, these studies provide an important, but limited understanding of the value generated. A singular focus on company level output or end-product-based measures of value does not tell us how value is created using IT (Tzeng et al., 2008). Moreover, the productivity factor does not provide a complete measure of the benefits of IT (Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998) because many of these are highly intangible (Brown, 1994). Accordingly, studies measuring the value of IT by output results alone are inadequate for making decisions about whether to invest in a specific IT project.

There is growing consensus that a better understanding of IT impacts requires a shift from output-focused to process-oriented research (Mooney et al., 1996; Ward et al., 1996; Dutta & Roy, 2004; Tellkamp, 2006; Tzeng et al., 2008). Melville et al. (2004) have proposed an integrative model to explain IT business value generation. While the core of their approach is also the business process, the primary foundation of their theory is the resource-based view of the company. By taking into account organisational performance, country and industry characteristics, as well as trading partner resources, they provide a comprehensive framework for viewing the sources of competitive advantages within companies. However, although they describe some factors affecting the business value generation process, they do not say how to identify – not to mention how to quantify and assess – the resultant benefits.

Based on a business process approach (Mooney et al., 1996; Dedrick et al., 2003), and using the terminology of Zuboff (1985, 1988), our proposal is that IT usage can affect performance characteristics of business processes via three bottom-up and non-exclusive effects: automation, information and transformation. Following Tellkamp (2006), Gille & Stru¨ker (2008) and Faupel et al. (2008), these can be adapted for the evaluation of RFID:

\- Automation effects: Manual activities of data acquisition and transmission can be automated by deploying RFID technology. For example, installing mobile barcode scanners in a company’s goods receipt area can eliminate the need for employees to capture data from incoming pallets manually. Such measures produce benefits at activity level (reduced resource consumption per activity – most notably labour costs). The extent of savings is dependent on the frequency with which the data capture activity is deployed (Subirana et al., 2003; Laubacher et al., 2006).

\- Information effects: If one takes the results of RFID data collection into consideration (Tellkamp, 2006), RFID increases the quality of the information used for business decisions. These information effects do not require modified structures and processes. They might, however, include additional data gathering, which is not economically feasible without RFID. Quantifiable benefits appear in terms of changes in the number of processes and activities, such as out-of-stock records, handling errors, etc.

\- Transformation effects: In addition, an improved information base enables existing business processes to be re-engineered which, in turn, supports further performance improvements. In many cases, process adjustments, that is, modified workflows, responsibilities and job specifications are indispensable. In order to reap substantial benefits, process changes to complement the technology deployment are required (Tellkamp, 2006). Initial studies support this assumption (Dighero et al., 2005; Stru¨ker et al., 2008). For example, conventional picking processes in retail distribution centres can be partially replaced by the provision and reallocation of pre-picked, store-level pallets at the loading bay (cross-docking). The highest degree of variation is effected by transformation effects at company level, which encompass creating new products, delivering a new set of services to customers, addressing new markets, etc. As company level effects transcend the process perspective and are accompanied by very specific, marketing issues, they are not the subject of the research under discussion.

While these three effects are helpful for identifying and categorising RFID benefits (Stru¨ker et al., 2008), they offer inadequate guidance for selecting and applying concrete instruments for an ex-ante benefit quantification of an RFID application. Only automation effects can be quantified in a straightforward fashion, due to the narrow definition of potential consequences on process performance (largely a reduction in handling time). On the other hand, information and transformation effects bundle multiple causalities for the generation of process performance and financial benefits. These causalities need to be addressed in more detail. For this reason we derived further criteria that help in identifying the RFIDrelated business potential in a case-specific setting and that are linked to specific quantification, that is, forecast and assessment instruments.

The research design for achieving this task implemented an interpretive, case study approach (Walsham, 1995). The results from a series of case studies were used to analyse and illustrate the particularities of the different levels of process changes for the purpose of identifying relevant benefit categories. Each of the studies followed an explorative, multi-method case study research design (Eisenhardt, 1989; Lee, 1989; Yin, 2003) and addressed the overarching research question ‘What applications are enabled by RFID and what benefits do they have at the process level?’ – taking into consideration propositions regarding the organisational and system environment. Table 2 provides an overview of the individual studies and their characteristics. The research was non-profit and partly supported by the ‘Stiftung Industrieforschung’, a private German foundation that finances research projects of scientific institutions. Most of the participants requested anonymity.

The methods applied included interviews with semistructured guidelines, interactive workshops on the benefits and the relevance of the applications, analysis of documents (company, process, project and technical descriptions), as well as on-site observations. The studies can be characterised as follows:

\- The food manufacturing study focused on the logistics processes of six medium-sized enterprises (see also Baars et al., 2008b): two confectionery manufacturers, one manufacturer of baking products, one manufacturer of pickled goods and two wineries. Each of the companies runs one or two production facilities and manufactures convenience food products, which are distributed via classical retail channels. There were two phases to the study. First, a series of interviews with representatives from five of the companies in August and November 2007 and additional follow-up interviews for results validation in April 2008. Interviewees were three logistics managers, one IT manager and one plant manager. Second, an in-depth case study with interviews, and a process and document analysis in one of Europe’s largest wineries between May 2007 and June 2008. Interviewees were two logistics managers, a member of the board of management and one IT manager. Most of the food manufacturing RFID projects were aimed at automation level effects.

\- The interviews and workshops in the second case were conducted between January and March 2008 at a large automotive OEM. Participants included experts responsible for logistics planning, departmental reporting and analysis systems, process development, and supply chain planning. The processes discussed were related to the production of engine parts and their transport and assembly, as well as the handling of reusable packaging. The enterprise is distributed across two continents and three production sites, and engages in engine component production, engine assembly and integration into the vehicle. These projects emphasised information effects. To date, no greater reorganisation is planned.

\- The third case involved the logistics initiative of a European retailing group (Baars et al., 2008a). The main parts of this study were completed between March and April 2007 (the project under investigation was still ongoing as of March 2009). Interviews and workshops included representatives from the retail group (Project Manager Strategy and Architecture, Project Manager RFID, Leader Warehouse) and an IT service provider to the group (Project Manager EMEA, Senior Solution Architect Worldwide). The retail group includes Chinese manufacturers, a Goods Distribution Centre (GDC) in Germany (that handles the goods transfer to individual retailers), as well as retail outlets across Europe. The applications discussed in the study presuppose transformational reorganisation spanning the complete chain.

The studies gathered data on the results of the respective RFID initiatives, the layout of the supported sites, RFID sensor locations, the relevant IT landscape, the processes concerned and their deficiencies, the nature of the conceived applications, as well as their overall relevance and the concrete benefits (attributed to indicators).

## Case study results: six RFID benefit types

The findings in respect of the process context, the deficiencies addressed and the benefits enabled further analysis of the value of RFID. This led to insights regarding the three types of process impact and unveiled two further categories (direct–indirect and operational– managerial) that are relevant for distinguishing benefits from an evaluation perspective.

In the context of the proposed identification schema, direct benefits of RFID are understood to be effects that are immediately positive results of introducing the technology, while indirect benefits result from changes in decisions or systems that are enabled by a purposeful distribution and utilisation of RFID data. This is in line with a similar distinction made by Laubacher et al. (2006), who differentiate between ‘localised’ and ‘distant’ benefits of RFID. Due to the inherent characteristics of logistics, indirect benefits become particularly relevant when the locations of data capture and data usage are separated and need to be bridged in near time, and with a high degree of precision.

It should be emphasised that the chosen distinction between automation and information scenarios logically excludes indirect automation effects, as the automation perspective always focuses on changing inputs that become directly effective. In other words, according to our RFID-specific definition, automation inevitably implies direct effects as it is geared at data capture. Where data usage is concerned we assign these events to the class of information effects. Although comparable effects are well known in IS research (e.g. cf. Hamilton & Chervany (1981), who discussed this issue in the context of Management Information Systems), they play a particular role in RFID-based logistics applications. Indirect effects are not only delayed in time, but also reach across unit and system borders – and usually cannot be grasped at the local facility that introduces the tags. While direct automation and direct information effects frequently show up together, we consider the distinction to be highly useful for raising awareness, analysing the effects and assigning appropriate quantification and assessment methods.

The separation of managerial and operational benefits adds another category for the identification of benefit types. In this paper, managerial benefits denote effects stemming from enhanced management support with integrated, aggregated, RFID data which, in most cases, has been further refined. The terminology operational and managerial benefits is in line with similar distinctions in IS literature (Shang & Seddon, 2002) motivated by the respective classification of managerial levels that was introduced by Anthony (1965). Managerial benefits are always indirect since they are based on data usage.

The total number of theoretically possible combinations of process impacts is 12 (cp. Figure 1). As we illustrate, only six of these are relevant for the data gathering technology RFID. In the following, they are referred to as benefit types. A benefit type groups positive process impacts based on similarity regarding the pertinent evaluation approaches. The schema we have derived is visualised as a tree structure in Figure 1. Having introduced the supporting categories we now derive the six relevant benefit types for RFID.

## Automation scenarios: Type 1

Most of the applications addressed in the food manufacturing study fall into the category of ‘automation’. They are confined to the replacement of existing data capture procedures and the unit of interest is restricted to the individual object identification activity. Examples include RFID-based identification of incoming packaging material and packaged provisions at goods receipt, automated inventory procedures, paperless documentation of goods flows and RFID-based order-picking. It needs to be emphasised that in the food manufacturing examples there is simply no need for further reaching changes: the processes under discussion are characterised by a high degree of automation, effective quality management and a low degree of complexity (Baars et al., 2008b). It should be highlighted that automation benefits can, indeed, be substantial. One interviewee even claimed that in her company tracking of material flows ‘makes up 10% of the value generation process’. And as a manager from the retail case put it: ‘Automation precedes all further applications’. The well-defined, cost-driven nature of these applications leads to a homogenous type of benefits (Type 1).

Further examples of Type 1 benefits and their relevance can, for example, be found in Alexander et al. (2002), who uncover a significant cost reduction potential in labourintensive receiving, picking and shipping activities within a distribution centre environment. Boushka et al. (2002) discuss the benefits of reducing informationgathering activities in yard control and present the example of a freight terminal that realised substantial labour reductions by supporting the localisation of idle trailers or free docks.

## Information scenarios: Types 2–4

Some examples from the food manufacturing case studies are also accompanied by an additional information effect: in goods receipt and picking, RFID is particularly effective in reducing errors from manual data input and avoiding mistakes during goods handling. These types of benefits address data usage, rather than data collection (Type 2). Although the respective benefits are slightly more challenging, due to their qualitative nature, they are, nevertheless, structurally similar from a forecasting perspective. Yet again, the changes introduced are virtually hard-wired to the automated data capture activities. As a consequence, the results can be elicited with a reasonable degree of certainty and, for the purposes of forecasting, can be treated as the current status of processes. Additional examples of Type 2 effects discussed in the literature pertain, for example, to automated sorting processes (Boushka et al., 2002), or the avoidance of correction activities in picking (Alexander et al., 2002) and in goods receipt (Boeck et al., 2008).

The benefit evaluation becomes a lot more challenging in information settings that depend on the potential of RFID to capture and distribute data in near time and with a higher temporal and spatial resolution (Type 3). Several examples can be found in the automotive case, for example a monitoring of goods flows with high precision and timeliness or in the tracking of reusable packaging (Kemper et al., 2008). The packaging applications were deemed particularly relevant and aimed at the provision of precise data on units used, their locations and the duration of usage in order to increase the efficiency of unit handling and to reduce losses (Koch & Baars, 2009). As one expert puts it, the introduction of RFID is expected to ‘shed light on the Bermuda Triangle of packaging’.

All of these applications go beyond changing the quality of the existing data: the complete underlying information structure for operation, monitoring, steering and optimisation decisions is altered. For a valid evaluation, it becomes necessary to consider the decisions affected or even newly introduced decisions – which might accompany the need for complementary data management and decision support technology (Baars & Sun, 2009). The business case, therefore, is based on system changes that go far beyond isolated modifications on the data capture side. The indirect nature of the benefits needs to be taken into account.

Owing to the inherent characteristics of logistics, indirect benefits become particularly relevant when the locations of data capture and data usage are separated and need to be bridged in near time and with a high degree of precision. In the context of the proposed identification schema, the distinction between direct and indirect effects forms a relevant benefit category. In this respect, direct benefits of RFID are understood to be effects that are immediately positive results of introducing the technology, while indirect benefits result from changes in decisions or systems that are enabled by a purposeful distribution and utilisation of RFID data. This is in line with a similar distinction made by Laubacher et al. (2006), who differentiate between ‘localised’ and ‘distant’ benefits of RFID. It should be emphasised that the distinction between automation and information scenarios logically excludes ‘indirect automation effects’, as the automation perspective is always focused on changing input and produces an immediate effect. Although comparable effects are documented in IS research (e.g. cf. Hamilton & Chervany (1981), who discuss this issue in the context of Management Information Systems), they play a particular role in RFID-based logistics applications. Indirect effects are not only delayed in time, but also extend beyond unit and system borders and, usually, cannot be grasped at the local facility that introduces the tags.

Understanding RFID purely as an ‘efficient replacement of barcodes’ easily leads to a project definition that restricts the respective business value. It is no surprise that the automotive and retail case studies provided insights into these effects: the automotive applications were primarily conceived by dedicated process and SCM departments, while the retail chain project was driven by a supply-chain-spanning task force (Baars et al., 2008a). Both initiatives were therefore located in cross-functional units that had a bird’s eye view of the logistics processes under discussion and already had a high awareness of data quality, data availability, and data precision-related issues.

Many of the larger-scale projects presented in the literature can be assigned to Type 3, for example Moran et al. (2003), who discuss how RFID-enabled transparency enhances rework processes in shop floor environments. The conclusions drawn in the paper are that the changes to systems and processes for enabling indirect effects do not always require a great deal of effort and that applications can be designed to be expandable, for example by adding more RFID readers or reader types later. Loebbecke (2007) describes applications for tracking fashion products in a warehouse and in a department store. Among other issues, she also discusses the consequences of adding more RFID readers to create an enhanced information base on store formats and equipment arrangement. Boeck et al. (2008) point out the relevance of enhanced data quality in goods receipt for improving management decisions. This publication is also among those that emphasise the issue of how the value from indirect benefits is distributed among the partners involved (in this case in a supply chain) – a crucial subject linked to the matter of indirect RFID benefits.

The retail and the automotive case studies uncovered a sub-type of indirect benefits that is in line with particular forecasting issues: in several applications in the automotive and the retail chain cases, the focus of interest was to collect and prepare RFID data to support managers confronted with unstructured situations (Type 4). Examples include pinpointing shrinkage in the retail case or dealing with unplanned exceptions in the production process. In spite of the fact that it is not possible to predict whether concrete issues will arise and, if so, under what conditions, it can be expected that the use of RFID (in combination with additional enriching data) will help to deal with these types of unstructured decisions.

The more turbulent the environment becomes, the greater the demand is for such management support applications, which facilitate the analysis of possible causes of unexpected results. The ex post nature of this sort of managerial benefit is much harder to grasp than changes in operational support, which can be analysed on the basis of defined processes, known issues and preconceived solutions. Managerial benefits require a data pool that is integrated, aggregated, and that stores historical data.

The separation of managerial and operational benefits adds another category for the identification of benefit types. In this paper, managerial benefits denote effects stemming from enhanced management support with integrated, aggregated RFID data, which in most cases has been further refined. The terms ‘operational’ and ‘managerial’ benefits are in line with similar distinctions in IS literature (Shang & Seddon, 2002), which have been motivated by the respective classification of managerial levels introduced by Anthony (1965). Managerial benefits are always indirect since they are based on data usage.

## Transformation scenarios: Types 5 and 6

Transformation scenarios can be illustrated by the retail chain case: the mid-term to long-term project trajectory of the retailing group includes significant changes in responsibilities and reporting channels. This encompasses the introduction of a ‘Vendor Managed Inventory’ approach, a move towards a demand-driven ordering process and the centralisation of decision rights at the GDC (Baars et al., 2008a). Many of the applications pursued on the basis of those changes are at first glance similar to Types 3 and 4 (information-indirect) and address analogous problems. For example, both the automotive and the retail case dealt with the routing of goods. In fact, their evaluation can, in principle, be dealt with on an equal basis. However, unlike ‘pure’ information scenarios, reorganisation itself with all its risks and uncertainties becomes an integral part of the equation, which is why additional types of benefits are introduced for transformation settings. These scenarios are especially challenging to deal with in the forecasting step.

Since RFID is a passive data gathering technology, benefits in transformation scenarios are always indirect – transformations encompass system changes. This does not necessarily apply to other technologies, for example actuators or sensors, which might directly lead to a decentralisation of decisions.

However, benefits from RFID-based transformations can come both in operational (Type 5) and managerial (Type 6) forms. An example of a transformation-managerial setting is the analysis of root causes for spoilage and shrinkage (as planned in the retail case) with the aim of deriving adequate measures. With regard to the reorganisation of an operational process (Type 5), the new structure that has been conceived provides at least a starting point for the extrapolation models. The contingent changes in managerial scenarios, however, are much more elusive. It is sometimes not even clear, whether a managerial support scenario is of transformational nature at all. One application of the retail chain case dealt with uncovering the root causes of spoilage. The decisions derived from respective results might be far reaching indeed (e.g. changing a supplier or introducing a new distribution strategy), or they might remain within the limits of information settings (e.g. when the issues can be adequately tackled by a different approach to communication with logistics partners).

The trisection into automation, information and transformation, together with two benefit categories introduced, that is, direct–indirect and operational—managerial, help to identify the six types as visualised in Figure 1. As has been pointed out, some branches of the tree are not valid for RFID: while ‘automation-indirect’ and ‘direct-managerial’ are, by definition, contradictory, ‘transformation-direct’ is not plausible, given the passive and data capture nature of RFID. In fact, the schema that has been derived embodies the specific characteristics of applying RFID in logistics with its combination of relevant impacts on efficiency, data quality and quantity, its reach across organisational borders and its potential relevance for management support.

## A framework for evaluating benefits of RFID applications for logistics

The identification of six relevant benefit types lays the groundwork for the selection of methods to forecast benefits in quantitative terms. Before specific methods are assigned to the six benefit types, supporting quantitative parameters need to be determined. The results of the quantification then form the input to the final assessment step, which concludes the overall evaluation process.

## Benefit forecast

Type 1 (Automation): The well-defined nature of this scenario makes it accessible for the more straightforward assessment models and calculation tools. Viewed from a more general perspective, the established way to deal with respective changes is by applying ABC (Cooper & Kaplan, 1988) and Activity-Based Performance Measurement (Laubacher et al., 2006) – which also encompass the assessment step. As each application brings individual environmental and system specific characteristics, results from the literature often need to be adjusted on the basis of additional field trials and pilot studies. For example, for the selection of applications in the winery case, parameters like the time required for data capture had to be determined for the status quo and for the deployment of RFID, which included a special wearable reader device. Type 2 (Information/Direct): is similar to Type 1 and, in general, can be assessed with suitable assessment models. In contrast to the automation scenarios, these benefits not only have an impact on the costs per activity, but also on the number of activities, for example, due to a reduction of error correction activities.

Type 3 (Information/Indirect/Operational): When it comes to indirect benefits, the RFID support and the benefit realisation diverge. In the case of logistics, these benefits are primarily reflected in enhanced inventory and material flow visibility for other departments of a corporation or even for other tiers in the supply chain (Toraskar & Joglekar, 1993). They are manifest in terms of enhanced availability, traceability or reduced buffer stocks. Thus, indirect benefits require instruments that focus on inventory and the flow of materials. The extrapolation of the effects can be accomplished, for instance, by process analysis and respective analytical and simulation-based forecast models. Field trials and pilot projects, however, offer only limited support, due to the delayed and overarching nature of these settings.

Type 4 (Information/Indirect/Managerial): The introduction of managerial effects leads to another crossroad in the selection of forecast methods: managerial benefits cannot be compared to well-defined business objectives and target figures, but rather represent an optional improvement of future decisions. The enhanced agility gained with the data collection comes at the price of creating significant difficulties in extrapolating specific effects on process indicators. In fact, it is only possible to make vague ex ante projections regarding the purpose of decision support data. The theoretical approach suitable for these types of problems is a real options approach. However, this method needs extensive input to specify potential future scenarios and their probabilities. Only a careful derivation of such scenarios – ideally supported by prototype-based visualisations and decision tree analysis – can narrow the range of potential outcomes.

Type 5 (Transformation/Indirect/Operational): Although Type 5 is similar to Type 3, it is important to note that existing process models and historical data cannot necessarily be forecast as easily, due to dynamic effects and complex interactive patterns in the newly ordered structures. Given this, it is not surprising that many of the simulation approaches are designed for these types of scenarios. A simulation can uncover relevant parameters and help find the range of likely outcomes, for example when switching from a push-based to a pull-based replenishing strategy or giving the GDC the option of relocating goods among retailers.

Type 6 (Transformation/Managerial): This scenario is affected by the same difficulties as Type 4 and, as pointed out above, the two managerial settings merge.

## Benefit assessment

In many of the simpler methods, forecasting and assessment merge and they can, in fact, be implemented in one step if the valuation is straightforward. This applies in particular to reductions of input quantities, that is resource consumption per activity or frequency of process executions – the predominant form of benefit in direct benefit scenarios. In these cases, there are often existing valuation rates that allow for an immediate translation into monetary terms: for example wage rates, cost driver rates (see, e.g., Ivantysynova et al., 2007).

Many of the applications discussed, however, aim at altering the outcome of the data capture and, therefore, raise issues of designing or deriving adequate valuation rates for outcome-related indicators. Some approaches can be found in the existing literature, for example the concept of quality and failure costs (Juran, 1979) that encompasses potential customer reactions can be applied for assessing the consequences of RFID-based reduction of external errors.

## Discussion and conclusions

The findings presented in this paper support the identification of relevant applications and structures and guide the evaluation process for RFID projects. Concretely, we provide a holistic framework that takes into account the entire evaluation process, that is the three steps of identification, forecast and assessment. This includes a systematic and comprehensive schema for the identification of RFID benefits in logistics that also serves as a basis for the assignment of forecasting and assessment methods. In the following section, implications for theory and practice are discussed.

With regard to its practical application, our framework supports business professionals in both discovering and evaluating possible applications in a systematic way, which extends beyond juxtaposing concrete application examples. It incorporates a perspective that offers more than a mere piecemeal evaluation of singular benefits. Its application is designed to foster a more holistic and longterm view that includes indirect and managerial process effects. The framework supports practitioners in developing plans for RFID implementations: Which RFID application promises which kind of benefit? Which benefit requires which quantification indicators? What are appropriate methods for forecasting the benefits identified in quantitative terms? How can one assess the result of the quantification? As exemplified in Table 3, the framework shows how to find answers to all of these questions. The framework helps managers to pre-classify their intended RFID applications, to determine the scope of the project and to plan resource allocations accordingly. Finally, the framework can certainly be of help in scrutinising the success of ongoing RFID projects and deriving future development trajectories.

With regard to research contributions to RFID benefit evaluation, we provide a comprehensive literature review and a three-step evaluation path that is a useful means for classifying and determining the potential and limitations of evaluation approaches. Furthermore, the refined automation–information–transformation approach to the RFID domain enables systematic and comprehensive identification of the benefits of innovative RFID solutions. However, with regard to the forecast and the assessment, our framework is only as good as the methods selected. We neither provide the ultimate answer on how to quantify qualitative benefits, nor do we come up with a means to bypass the complexity of methods to grasp managerial benefits. In particular, simulation and model-based approaches for the indirect and transformation scenarios are often highly specific and need labour-intensive adjustments. Ideally, a comprehensive simulation tool would cover the complete spectrum of typical RFID to support this task. At the same time, the structure of the framework derived can also reveal gaps in the methodological support and, therefore, catalyse method evolution.

To date, most organisations have not moved beyond small-scale applications and pilot studies. As a result of this and ongoing technological developments, the potential of RFID is in constant flux. Therefore, it is not possible to obtain robust data on the appropriateness and reliability of the methods discussed. An ex post validation of evaluation results or even the collection of large-scale empirical evidence is currently beyond reach. With growing use of RFID, empirical evaluation will become both a necessary next step and a challenging research opportunity.

The aspect of distribution provides the essential criterion for the distinction between direct and indirect benefits. Finally, without a clear process impact, the chosen automation-informationtransformation framework is hardly applicable and needs to be replaced by a different perspective.
