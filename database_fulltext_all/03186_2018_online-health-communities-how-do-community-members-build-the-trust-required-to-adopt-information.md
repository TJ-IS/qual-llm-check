# Online health communities: how do community members build the trust required to adopt information and form close relationships?

Hanmei Fan and Reeva Lederman

School of Computing and Information Systems, University of Melbourne, Victoria, Australia

## ABSTRACT

Online Health Communities (OHCs) have become a popular avenue for people to share common concerns about a health topic. However, because users of these forums tend not to have formal medical training, they often cannot verify objectively the soundness of the advice provided. Thus, there is a need to understand better the mechanism through which people trust and utilise information found on these forums. In testing a theory-based trust model, we find that forum users accept advice more readily as they develop cognitive trust (trust based on a cognitive assessment) and/or afective trust (trust based on an emotional connection). Afective trust is also closely related to formation of relational closeness. This indicates that, while both forms of trust are important, the development of emotional connections provides for long-term OHCs interactions. Findings here, indicate that prior exchanges are not needed to access and utilise  OHC community resources.  However, trust (developed through prior exchanges) is required for a recipient to leverage the contributor’s social capital for information adoption and relational closeness. These findings allow the distribution of social capital to be viewed in terms of an asymmetrical model where it is possible to accept capital, in the form of advice, without first demonstrating trustworthiness. In the OHC setting the onus is on the contributor of advice to establish trustworthiness, whereas traditionally social capital theory requires receivers of capital to establish trust. These findings are important for site designers and provide insight to managers and developers of OHCs regarding the behaviours and motivations of forum users.

## 1. Introduction

Shauna, a 35 year old mother, was concerned. This second pregnancy was not going as well as the last. She was feeling nauseous most of the time, and really wanted to speak to someone who understood what she was going through. Her close friends didn’t seem to understand her problems, so she posted some requests for help on a baby forum. Shauna was pleasantly surprised to receive some replies. Even better, one of the people was in the same state. “I actually got a really nice email that helped me cope with my nausea. It was private, so no-one else can see it, and that actually meant a lot to me: I felt that person really cares about me. After a while I got to know some of the contributors. I value the judgement of some women who post a lot, but I don’t believe what everyone says without checking.”

John is a patient sufering from chronic fatigue syndrome. His experience: “ I’ll put up a post saying I have this problem. What do other people do about it? That’s quite interesting because they come back saying ‘they never have that’ or ‘this is what I do.’ So there is a sharing of experience which is very important because we are a very small community [of people with this condition] in Australia, and the outside world doesn’t recognise this illness. But in the community people know what you are talking about and it’s extremely useful.”

Patients and caregivers are increasingly turning to Internet-based resources, such as online news groups, websites, blogs, as a key resource for the on-going management of health conditions (Fox & Jones, 2009). Some of this online interaction is replacing the traditional doctor-patient communication, but often the interaction provides information and support that is not otherwise available. In particular, patients and caregivers are seeking user-generated health content, including the personal experiences of like-minded others, in online communities (Sillence, Hardy, & Briggs, 2013). An OHC is a collection of small internet-based virtual discussion groups in which people with a common concern about a health topic share information, experiences, and feelings and provide support and encouragement to fellow members (Wright, 2002) in a text-based forum. Those accessing these forums are typically searching for “justin-time someone-like-me” information, such as a commentary or someone else’s experience about a health or medical issue.

Information seeking and sharing outside of the doctor-patient relationship is not a new phenomenon. People have always discussed medical issues (to varying extents) with family and other members of their local community because family and friends tend to understand a patient’s needs, and the patient will understand the confidant’s attitudes and expertise (Burt, 2001), and therefore know who to consult and the likely response to the problem.

This traditional mode of interaction, however, is less applicable to isolated individuals and those with a rare or socially stigmatised condition. These people have historically been unable to access relevant information or been excluded from emotional support networks (Van Brakel, 2006). Online health forums can provide these excluded groups with emotional support as evidence suggests that users have some level of intrinsic motivation for social acceptance that can drive interaction in online forums (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). This can involve the desire to help others (Wasko & Faraj, 2005) and mirror close relationship-type behaviour despite users being personally unknown to one another. Such forums also provide information about how to manage their condition, and solutions to problems via reciprocal or oneway interactions with fellow suferers regardless of their location, and may allow users to be selective about the forums in which they provide and seek social support (Maier, Laumer, Eckhardt, & Weitzel, 2015). Individuals can even protect their identity using an alias, while still having access to information and support. A downside to this type of interaction, however, is that identity detachment and a reduced ability to assess the credentials of community members can lead to exploitation and other misbehaviour. For example, someone could pretend to be a cancer patient and develop online relationships with actual patients to obtain personal gain, such as selling a product or service (Greene, Choudhry, Kilabuk, & Shrank, 2010).

In this paper, we examine two relationship formation mechanisms in the OHC context, cognitive and afective trust. We examine how perceptions of an information contributor such as similarity with self influences these mechanisms, and how both these mechanisms influence subsequent information adoption by recipients and formation of close relationships online. Both types of mechanism operate in a community whenever advice is contributed and sought, and received and possibly actioned by recipients. A contributor is a person who writes postings to provide informational and emotional support to others on OHCs. A recipient, receives this advice and may choose to act on it or not, and may also contribute. The established view of this process is that individuals seeking access to a resource, such as advice, are only granted that access where they have accumulated goodwill by forming ties with others and internalising the shared values of the group (Adler & Kwon, 2002; Field, 2003). This is the traditional view of social networks, described by Granovetter (1973), Bourdieu (1986), and others. It is a contributor-centric model in which the person requesting help has accumulated a bank of goodwill, and the contributor-as-gatekeeper regulates access to that assistance.

We propose that, within an OHC, perceived attributes of a contributor, and how that contributor’s actions are seen by others, are the foundations for trust, and that trust in a contributor is the precursor to adopting advice and formation of relational closeness.

The basis for these predictions is that, within interpersonal networks, people most readily trust, and form relationships with those with whom they share common values (Jones & George, 1998; Putnam, 1995). However, although what you are is an important foundation, how you demonstrate trustworthiness through actions allows a relationship (social ties) to develop. The formation of social ties, in turn, is associated with knowledge sharing to meet specific needs (Hatzakis, Lycett, Macredie, & Martin, 2005; Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005).

We test our hypotheses by conducting an online survey of patients who use OHCs, asking about their interactions within an OHC. Although caregivers are valid users of these sites, for this study we focused only on patients with a physical ailment, and so excluded all other types of illness and all other involved people from our sample. Of the 473 responses received, 320 usable responses were analysed.

Results indicate that afective trust is influenced most strongly by familiarity with how a contributor has interacted with others and a perception that the contributor has a deep understanding of the receiver’s situation. Cognitive trust is also influenced by the contributor’s interactions with others and the credibility of facts or advice posted. Having an emotional connection (afective trust) is the foundation for a close relationship, while afective trust and cognitive trust are equally important in deciding whether to adopt information provided.

A major implication of our findings is that, in the OHC context, the traditional social capital model is turned on its head. That is, advice is readily available to OHC readers; however, advice is accepted where the contributor has been able to form ties with others and shares values with both the receiver and other members of the group. This is a receiver-centric model in which those seeking assistance determine who is worthy of acting as a contributor: an inversion of the traditional social capital resource allocation model. In addition, our results indicate that using an OHC is not dependent on reciprocity, as might be suggested in social capital theory, but that use is instead dependent on trust, which can be a one-way relationship (not necessarily reciprocal).

## 2. Literature review

In this section, we provide a brief overview of research into trust and of the importance of trust in social capital formation. Trust appears to be required for interactions to take place in the type of social networks we analyse, OHCs. Social capital formation and use in social networks is also reliant on trust and our results potentially inform research into social capital because trust plays a pivotal role in the operation of social networks. To support that aspect of this work, we provide a brief overview of social capital research following our discussion of trust.

## 3. Conceptualisation of trust

Research into trust has tended to conceptualise trust as either (1) a uni-dimensional cognitive construct that indicates an individual’s beliefs about a person or process or (2) a multi-dimensional construct that has both cognitive and afective dimensions.

## 3.1. Uni-dimensional perspective – cognitive trust

The cognitive/rational perspective of trust has originated from theories in sociology, economics, and politics, and is characterised by a view of trust as the result of a calculative process in which a person considers available evidence about potential benefits and risks to decide whether to rely on another party for a specific activity (Williamson, 1993). Under the cognitive/rational perspective, trust is an expectation that others can be relied upon to act predictably and fairly in a matter rather than opportunistically (Gulati, 1995; Gulati & Sytch, 2008; Zaheer, McEvily, & Perrone, 1998). Many studies have incorporated these elements into their conceptualisation of trust. For example, Sako (1992) proposes that trust is a type of contract based on goodwill and a belief that a party is competent to perform a particular activity. Leimeister, Ebner, and Krcmar (2005) similarly proposes that the relation is between someone with a particular attitude towards trust (dispositional trust) and another who is expected to perform an action in a given context, and that if a system or process is involved, for example, the hardware and software enabling an online community, there will be reliance on that system to perform as expected (system trust).

This stream of work believes that trust can be explained by character-based theories (Dirks & Ferrin, 2002), and results from a deliberate assessment of a trustee’s characteristics achieved by weighing the benefits of trusting over risks (Lewicki & Bunker, 1995). Mayer’s extensively cited model (Mayer, Davis, & Schoorman, 1995) is an example of this type of approach and has influenced many studies in the IS field (e.g., Gefen, Karahanna, & Straub, 2003; Kim, 2008; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004). It proposes that one’s propensity to trust and the assessment of a trustee’s trustworthiness subsequently leads to risk taking in a relationship moderated by the level of perceived risk in the context. A major contribution of Mayer’s work is the insight that trust involves a willingness to take a risk and be vulnerable in a relationship, and so is quite diferent to predictability, which is solely concerned with risk reduction (Mayer et al., 1995).

Doney, Cannon, and Mullen (1998) propose another significant trust development model. Their model has five cognitive trust-building processes: a calculative process (cost vs. rewards of a trustee cheating or cooperating), a prediction process (a trustee’s past actions provide a reasonable basis for predicting future behaviour), a transference process (transfer trust from a known entity to an unknown one), an intentionality process (form trust from interpreting the trustee’s words and behaviour), and a capability process (an assessment of a trustee’s ability to meet obligations). These processes have been incorporated, tested, and further extended in a number of studies (Gefen et al., 2003; Gefen & Heart, 2006; Jarvenpaa, Tractinsky, & Vitale, 2000; Kim, 2008; Lowry, Vance, Moody, Beckman, & Read, 2008).

## 3.2. Multiple dimensions: cognitive and afective trust

The uni-dimensional cognitive approach to trust has not been accepted by all researchers. Some previous research into online communities suggests that the behavioural or afective dimension to trust is “redundant” as it is a consequence of cognitive trust (Casaló, Flavián, & Guinalíu, 2011). Many researchers consider cognitive trust to be multidimensional because some dimensions appear to form at diferent times and via diferent processes. For example, some trusting beliefs (Gefen, 2002), such as ability and integrity, form quickly, whereas emotion-based beliefs are stronger determinants of trust (Ba & Pavlou, 2002) and take longer to form (Dimoka, 2010).

An alternative view is that trust has a cognitive component that uses rational assessment (cognitive trust), and an emotion-based component (afective trust) based on a close relationship (Ha, Park, & Cho, 2011) and other interpersonal emotional bonds (Lewis & Weigert, 1985; McAllister, 1995). Jones and George’s (1998) conditional trust (drawn from knowledge and positive expectation of others and unconditional trust (induced by positive afect and mutual identification) and the character-based and relationship-based perspective of Dirks and Ferrin (2002) echo this duality.

The collective attribute approach suggests that trust is truly viewed as multidimensional by incorporating both cognitive and afective components (Cook & Wall, 1980; Johnson & Grayson, 2005; Webber, 2008; Webber & Klimoski, 2004). The multi-dimensional view acknowledges two fundamentally diferent components of trust that may have diferent antecedents and outcomes (McAllister, 1995). Cognitive trust is rooted in a cognitive assessment of others’ competence, reliability and dependability, while afective trust is grounded in emotional bonds, caring and reciprocity (Cummings & Bromiley, 1996).

## 3.3. Comparisons between cognitive and afective trust

The multi-dimensional view of trust is further supported by the nature and the development of cognitive trust and afective trust. Several studies have found that afect induces deeper levels of trust (Lewicki & Bunker, 1996; McAllister, 1995; Rempel, Holmes, & Zanna, 1985). For example, faith, as the deepest level of trust, is built upon afective attachment and the emotional investment of caring (Rempel et al., 1985). Strong trust is built on internalised values, principles and standards of behaviour in the face of high vulnerability or low legal or contractual protection (Barney & Hansen, 1994). With unconditional trust, short-term behavioural lapses are tolerated because shared values direct the orientation of the relationship (Jones & George, 1998). In the face of trust violation or absence of solid evidence, afective trust is more enduring and stable (Lewicki & Bunker, 1996; McAllister, 1995; Morrison & Robinson, 1997) because emotional attachment can override cognitive assessment and drive people to take risks (Weber, Malhotra, & Murnighan, 2006).

The stability and influence of afective trust can be explained by the way it emerges. Longitudinal work (Webber, 2008) indicates that cognitive trust emerges at an early stage in a relationship, while afective trust emerges later as the relationship becomes established. This finding is consistent with results from experimental studies showing that, in the absence of any prior relationship, trust is initially based on non-afective factors (Dirks, 1999; Dunn, Ruedy, & Schweitzer, 2012).

In summary, the fundamental diferences between cognitive trust and afective trust support the notion that trust is a multi-dimensional construct that is developed over time. By encompassing both long and short-term members, OHCs are a suitable environment to capture these relationships.

## 3.4. Antecedents of trust

The two dimensions of trust also difer in their anteced ent relationships with other variables. Cognitive trust results from a conscious assessment of a trustee’s characteristics, including weighing the benefits of trusting against the risk of opportunistic behaviour (Lewicki & Bunker, 1995). Many studies have documented antecedents of this type of trust, of which knowledge-based and capability-based factors are particularly relevant to this study. Knowledge-based factors, such as familiarity, similarity (McAllister, 1995; Zucker, 1986), knowledge level (Gefen, 2000; Gefen et al., 2003; Gefen & Heart, 2006)

provide a basis for trusting that someone understands a task and the trustor’s values. Capability-based factors, which indicate a person’s ability to honour or fulfil expectations, include information credibility (Pavlou & Dimoka 2006), information quality (Zahedi & Song, 2008), and expertise (Johnson & Grayson, 2005) Other antecedents including calculative factors, such as transaction costs (Gefen, 2000; Zahedi & Song, 2008), stereotypical attributes of a role (Li, Valacich, & Hess, 2004), structural issues, structural normality and privacy protection (McKnight, Cummings, & Chervany, 1998; Pavlou, 2002; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004), referrals or recommendations (Hu, Wu, Wu, & Zhang, 2010; Kim, 2008) are not relevant to the context of this study and so are not discussed in detail.

Derived from sociology, psychology, and organisational theory, afective trust is developed through caring behaviours and emotional responses (Lewis & Weigert, 1985; Rempel et al., 1985). In other words, expressing care and concern for the welfare of someone, and expressing emotions in interactions will develop this form of trust. Other antecedents include group membership (Kramer, 1999; Williams, 2001), friendship ties (Chua, Ingram, & Morris, 2008), interdependence (Poppo, Zhou, & Ryu, 2008), similarity (Johnson & Grayson, 2005), and interaction frequency (McAllister, 1995).

## 4. Social capital

The operation of relationships across groups of people, including families, clubs, townships, and so on, is explored in theories of social capital. The term social capital was first mentioned in research into how communities of people operate, and was used to describe how people survive by utilising their personal relationships in their network or neighbourhood (Jacobs, 1965).

Researchers observed that people who did better seemed to be better connected (for example, knowing someone in a company led to employment), and that communities with more connected people were better of. Later researchers made the crucial insight that social capital is a relational construct: valuable resources are embedded in social relationships, and individuals and communities with more relationships have more of these resources (Bourdieu, 1986; Granovetter, 1973).

## 4.1. Social capital and online health communities

Social capital theory is a broad theory reflecting a basic feature of social life – that people form ties that can be used to deliver a wide variety of benefits, including providing moral and material support (Adler & Kwon, 2002). Social capital consists of three dimensions: the structural, the relational, and the cognitive. The structural dimension refers to the connections between actors in a social network, describing who you reach and how you reach them (Burt, 1992). The key facets of this dimension are the presence or absence of network ties between actors (Scott, 1991) and the density, connectivity, and hierarchy of a linkage pattern (Tichy, Tushman, & Fombrun, 1979). The relational dimension describes interpersonal connections (Moran, 2005) or relationships that have been developed through repeated interactions between actors (Granovetter, 1992). The most important aspects of this dimension include trust (Putnam, 1995), norms (Coleman, 1990) and obligations (Burt, 1992). Moran suggests that in uncertain or risky situations, one aspect of the relational dimension, friendship-type ties, discussed in the social capital literature under the umbrella of relational closeness (Jung, Gray, Lampe, & Ellison, 2013; Ledbetter, Mazer, DeGroot, Meyer, & Yuping Mao, 2011), are important to the sharing of social capital (Moran, 2005).

Finally, the cognitive dimension refers to the shared values, representations, interpretations, and systems of meaning that serve as a common ground for exchanging and combining social capital (Cicourel, 1973). In this study, we focus on the relational and cognitive dimensions of social capital rather than structure, which is beyond the scope of our model.

Adler and Kwon refine discussion about the relational dimension by dividing it into social relations, market relations and hierarchical relations (Adler & Kwon, 2002). Social relations can involve the exchange of favours or gifts and are also generally symmetrical (possibly over time). These types of relations are typically made up of the type of social structure that underpins social capital. Market relations involve the exchange of goods or services and the exchange is generally symmetrical (equally reciprocated) and not necessarily based on social ties. In hierarchical relations material or spiritual security is exchanged for an acceptance of authority and these relations are generally asymmetrical and not regarded as an exchange of social capital, as relations between parties are not primarily social (e.g., between an employer and employee). However, observers of OHC describe a type of relationship which is both asymmetrical and social. That is, it appears to be based on social ties in that the contributors have no oficial authority status and are not exchanging material goods yet, in these relationships there is no reciprocation or symmetry. Studies report the high presence of lurkers in OHC (Maloney-Krichmar & Preece, 2005), who merely observe the posting of others. This suggests some relations may involve an exchange of social capital yet be asymmetrical in that many members of OHC do not reciprocate or contribute themselves yet get benefit from other contributor’s postings when they see these oth ers as having some sort of authority (akin to a position in a hierarchy) which they trust (Nonnecke & Preece, 2000). For example, 50% of members in one study did not post over a 130 day period (Cummings, Butler, & Kraut, 2002), yet consistently logged on and presumably benefitted from information absorbed onsite.

There is a large body of work that discusses trust facilitating social exchanges from the contributor’s perspective (Hau & Kim, 2011; Putnam, 1995; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). In these exchanges, the contributor tends to trust that the request is genuine, not opportunistic or dishonest, and assistance is provided in the absence of immediate or certain returns, rationalised via a belief in generalised reciprocity such as “I’ll do this for you now, knowing that somewhere down the track you or others who I helped directly or indirectly will do something for me” (Kankanhalli, Tan, & Wei, 2005; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). The belief that assistance rendered will be reciprocated is, of course, not necessarily true, as we see in the case of lurkers who do not contribute, but social exchanges nevertheless occur, so we can assume that, in these instances at least, the potential for harm is outweighed by mutual trust.

In the Social Capital literature, trustworthiness is established by developing social capital. That is, developing a network of social connections by sharing, supporting, and otherwise contributing to the community builds individual social capital (Adler & Kwon, 2002). Those with social capital are, by definition, better known, which allows others to assess that individual via informal word-of-mouth communications, and ultimately develop a trusting relationship (Awad & Ragowsky, 2008) This trust can lead to greater self-disclosure in a social network where there is a perception that members of a network are providing positive assistance (Chen & Sharma, 2015). In terms of our previous trust discussion, this self-disclosure assists cognitive trust formation and the likelihood that a relationship will continue, and continuing interactions help to develop afective trust.

## 5. Conceptual model

The model in Figure 1 shows the hypothesised relationships of both cognitive and afective trust with their antecedents and consequences in the context of OHCs. We looked at both antecedents that are perceptions of contributor supplied information and perceived attributes of contributor. Antecedents of cognitive trust relevant to the online medical forum context are perceived information credibility, as the perception of contributor supplied information, perceived similarity between the contributor’s and the recipient’s medical conditions and familiarity which are perceived attributes of contributor. Antecedents of afective trust are all perceived attributes of contributor, including familiarity, perceived empathy, and perceived similarity in values. The reason we have specified these particular antecedents of trust is because in an OHC the viewer can see information provided and can infer information about the contributor. Thus, those are the two types of variables that we use in our measurement model, drawn from Coombs (1966) and Byrne (1997). The presence of both cognitive and afective trust is predicted to make information adoption (i.e., accept the credibility of advice and use of the information posted) more likely, while the formation of closeness (investing emotion and establishing close relationship) relies only on the presence of afective trust.

## 6. Hypotheses

## 6.1. Perceived information credibility and cognitive trust

Perceived information credibility is the extent to which information is perceived as believable, true or factual (Mcknight & Kacmar, 2006). In face-to-face interactions, trust is initially based on situational cues and known attributes of the other person, such as occupation or fame, but is then influenced by what is said and how it is said (Lewicki & Bunker, 1995). Physical and identity-based cues are absent in an online forum, so trust assessment here must be based on a person’s postings.

The credibility of an online posting is assessed in a deliberative process based on, among other things, the appropriateness, relevancy, suficiency, and authoritativeness of postings (Wathen & Burkell, 2002). Contributors who are seen to conform to these qualities will be regarded as more trustworthy (a cognitive assessment), whereas those who post inappropriate, incorrect, or irrelevant responses will lose credibility and ultimately be regarded as untrustworthy. We describe the OHCs we study as “text-based relationship oriented OHC” as contributors communicate by text in an environment. The environment is predicated on on-going interaction and is thus relationship-oriented in the sense used in marketing (Keillor, Stephen Parker, & Pettijohn, 2000), rather than being based on a discrete transactional approach.

As a result, we propose the following relationship between the perceived credibility of information posted and its contributor:

Hypothesis 1 (H1) The perceived information credibility will positively contribute to the formation of cognitive trust in a text-based relationship-orientated OHC.

## 6.2. Perceived similarity in medical condition and trust

People feel more comfortable when they think others are similar to them (Infante, Rancer, & Womack, 1997), and they are more likely to be persuaded in communication (Walther, Pingree, Hawkins, & Buller, 2005). In an OHC, perceptions of similarity are based on a person’s medical status, including medical-related characteristics, and that person’s values, beliefs, and attitudes (Mechanic & Meyer, 2000). These referents correspond respectively to status homophily, meaning perceived similarity based on ascribed status, and value homophily, meaning similarity in values, attitudes, and beliefs (Lazarsfeld & Merton, 1954).

Analysis of OHC contributions indicates that interactions often involve experience-sharing as a way to connect with group members and demonstrate empathy (Maloney-Krichmar & Preece, 2005; Welbourne, Blanchard, & Wadsworth, 2013). Perceiving a shared experience implies that the contributor is seen to possess special insight and is therefore potentially trustworthy in matters concerning those experiences. This assessment is largely a deliberative process as it involves an assessment of the plausibility of the account and the similarity of experiences. Because the assessment is deliberative, the specific form of trust developed is cognitive trust (Slater, Buller, Waters, Archibeque, & LeBlanc, 2003). As a result, similar experience leads to a higher likelihood of adopting any advice ofered (Wang, Walther, Pingree, & Hawkins, 2008).

In previously reported research (Fan, Smith, Lederman, & Chang, 2010; Lederman, Fan, Smith, & Chang, 2014), we found evidence to support this argument:

I tend to be drawn or believe those people who are similar because I know [whether] what they say is true I suppose because I’m experiencing what they’re experiencing. (A9)

You talk to people with similar symptoms. I’ll trust those people more because it’s all their experience. It doesn’t mean I’ll try [what they say] straight away myself but I’ll store that knowledge and thinking; later on I may need it. (A13)

Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 2 (H2) Perceived similarity in medical status will contribute to cognitive trust in a text-based relationship-orientated OHC.

## 6.3. Perceived similarity in values and trust

Relationships thrive among people who have similar personalities and common values (Grabner-Kräuter & Kaluscha, 2003; Lee et al., 2009; Sullivan & Transue, 1999). The value theory of homophily and homogamy (Coombs, 1966) and the attraction-similarity paradigm (Byrne, 1997) both propose that interpersonal attraction is founded on similarities in deeply held attitudes and opinions. This attraction reinforces the value of those beliefs and increases the value of complementary attributes (Byrne, 1997; Coombs, 1966). The more similarities perceived, the more positively the user will perceive the other party and create stronger trusting personal relationships (Carmon, Miller, Raile, & Roers, 2010). As trust is essential to interpersonal relationships, and perceived similarity and trust are highly correlated in empirical studies (Ziegler & Lausen, 2004), we propose:

Hypothesis 3 (H3) Perceived similarity in values will positively contribute to afective trust in a text-based relationship-orientated OHC.

## 6.4. Familiarity, afective trust and cognitive trust

Familiarity is an understanding, often originating from previous interactions, of what, why, where and when others do what they do (Gefen, 2000). In OHCs this understanding is gained through reading others’ postings. The more frequent the contact, the greater the amount (extensiveness) and depth (intensiveness) of self-disclosure, and the greater the familiarity with a contributor’s strengths, weaknesses, intentions, values, personality and background (Berscheid, 1998).

Familiarity influences the development of both cognitive and afective trust. Cognitively, trust is partly formed using past behaviour to infer future behaviour (Gulati, 1995). There are a number of studies that empirically support the positive relationship between familiarity and cognitive trust in other contexts (Gefen & Heart, 2006; Gulati & Sytch, 2008). Regarding the development of afective trust, familiarity is a necessary foundation for establishing and maintaining an emotional bond with another person (Prior & Glaser, 2006). Like cognitive trust, interactions over time will increase familiarity and can lead to a deeper relationship. Unlike the cognitive trust process, however, afective trust develops through emotional connections that occur in those interactions. For example, consistent supportive postings will develop cognitive trust via the pattern of behaviour, but the emotional content of those interactions determines afective trust. Over time, emotional bonds strengthen leading to trust that is stronger and more resilient than trust formed purely via cognitive processes (Dirks & Ferrin, 2002). Afective responses are therefore qualitatively diferent to cognitive responses. This is true even at the most basic level of analysis: neuro-imaging studies show that viewing personally familiar faces creates a decreased state of vigilance or wariness compared with viewing the faces of strangers (Ida Gobbini, Leibenluft, Santiago, & Haxby, 2004), suggesting that emotionally bonded people are less guarded, more relaxed, and feel safer with people to whom they are close. We therefore propose:

Hypothesis 4 (H4) Familiarity will influence afective trust towards a person in a text-based relationship-orientated OHC.

Hypothesis 5 (H5) Familiarity will influence cognitive trust towards a person in a text-based relationshiporientated OHC.

## 6.5. Perceived empathy and afective trust

A relationship can begin from a position of cognitive trust but, through what are perceived as empathic interactions, can be transformed into afective trust (Erdem & Ozen, 2003). Afective trust indicates an emotional bond between individuals (Claro & Claro, 2008). Similarly, empathy is described as an emotional response to another persons’ emotions (“feeling another persons feeling”) (Trusty, Ng, & Watts, 2005). Thus perceived empathy is the extent to which an individual perceives this emotional bond, that his/her feelings are understood and accepted by another (Kim, Kaplowitz, and Johnston, 2004). This understanding and acceptance are derived from observation, memory, knowledge and reasoning (Ickes, 1997) and is strongest among people who share experiences or see themselves as similar to others (Ickes, 1993). Many authors connect trust-related behaviours to empathy (Williams, 2012), including in the online environment (Feng, Lazar, & Preece, 2004). Where Feng et al. identify a connection between empathy and trust in online interactions they do not distinguish between types of trust. However, they do find that factors such as “liking” a person have an impact on trust online and describe a type of trust formed from emotional factors akin to afective trust (Feng et al., 2004). Based on this previous work we propose that initially, a person seeking support (a seeker) perceives that a contributor empathises with him based on their responses to the seekers postings. They may come to the view that the contributor feels empathy with them and is vicariously experiencing his/her feelings and dislikes his/her sufering; Finally, the seeker may believe that the contributor demonstrates genuine caring and an intention to improve his/her welfare. Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 6 (H6) Perceived empathy will positively contribute to afective trust in a text-based relationship-orientated OHC.

## 6.6. Information adoption and trust-related behaviours

A commonly raised concern about interactions in online medical forums is that some people adopt useless or even dangerous treatment regimens because they lack the knowledge and expertise necessary to assess medical information (Benigeri & Pluye, 2003). This type of situation is unusual, however; people do not adopt advice from forums unthinkingly, particularly not treatments (Nettleton, Burrows, & O’Malley, 2005), and instead tend to use forums as a social support tool rather than as a substitute doctor (Fan, Lederman, Smith, & Chang, 2014; Welbourne et al., 2013). Where the advice can be validated, trust is not an issue. It is an issue, however, when the recipient cannot readily assess its quality in the absence of direct experience, and sometimes cannot evaluate quality even with the benefit of experience. Trust is not required to enable adoption of advice where quality can be verified beforehand, but is crucial where the assessment has to be made based on partial information. In this situation, the advice is an experience or “credence good” (because it is dificult to assess), and so adoption is contingent on the strength of cognitive and afective trust in the contributor (Lewis & Weigert, 1985; McAllister, 1995).

If complete information about the contributor were available, there would be no need for trust formation because opportunistic behaviour could be predicted beforehand (McAllister, 1995). However, complete information about a contributor’s competence, empathy with others, and the reliability and usefulness of past advice, is never available, so trust involves more than simply cold-blooded rational analysis of a contributor’s past interactions, and afective trust must be used to supplement cognitive trust because it is never really possible to decide whether to trust a person based solely on rational information processing (Lewis & Weigert, 1985, p. 970) if nothing else is known about the contributor. Nevertheless, the cognitive dimension is likely to dominate when the contributor and recipient have little or no history of interaction because only observable attributes and responses to other people are obtainable. Afective trust becomes more important when a relationship has developed and deepened over time because it is only through multiple interactions that an afective (and personal) relationship can be established (Komiak & Benbasat, 2006; Rempel et al., 1985). In other words, both cognitive and afective dimensions are important, but are activated via diferent mechanisms. On this basis we propose:

Hypothesis 7 (H7) Cognitive trust will contribute to information adoption in a text-based relationship-orientated OHC.

Hypothesis 8 (H8) Afective trust will contribute to information adoption in a text-based relationship-orientated OHC.

## 6.7. Formation of relational closeness

Closeness in relationship is most commonly understood to involve self-disclosure, support and shared interests (Parks & Floyd, 1996). As discussed earlier the term relational closeness has been used previously in the Social Capital literature to denote the formation of the type of relationships that embrace friendship-type behaviours that have these qualities. In line with this approach we will use the term closeness to refer to the extent to which one invests emotion and establishes connectedness with a contributor. Intimacy and closeness are often seen as equivalent terms (Parks & Floyd, 1996) and are the essential ingredient to close relationship and any other relationship (Sternberg, 1986). The degree of closeness between individuals depends on the level of afective trust and manifests as the depth of self-disclosure in OHCs (Reis & Shaver, 1988). Knapp’s relational development model (1978) and Altman and Taylor’s social penetration theory (1973) assert that the development of a relationship is accompanied by deepening self-disclosure and increasing intimacy. Each self-disclosure is a test of the contributor’s trustworthiness and a chance to probe the potential of a relationship. The deeper the self-disclosure, the more emotionally vulnerable a trustor is, and the more trust is required. Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 9 (H9) Afective trust will contribute to the formation of relational closeness in a text-based relationship-orientated OHC.

## 7. Methodology

The hypotheses are tested on survey data because we want to verify our framework from the perspective of patients who use OHCs to manage their diseases. Survey is one of the best methods to collect original data on attitudes and orientations in a large population (Babbie, 2007). It is suitable for answering research questions on self-reported beliefs and behaviours (Newman, 2000). Specifically, an online survey was chosen considering its anonymity, coverage, and convenience. Without the prerequisite of any personal information (e.g., phone number, postal address, email), it is expected that respondents will be less concerned about privacy (e.g., linking their identities to the medical conditions) and more willing to respond.

To ensure the mathematical tests conducted will test the theoretical models exactly as proposed, all the measures have to properly reflect these concepts and precisely capture their meanings. We used q-sort method to increase content validity and address the threat to reliability and validity due to subjectivity (MacKenzie, Podsakof, & Podsakof, 2011; Schriesheim, Powers, Scandura, Gardiner, & Lankau, 1993; Straub, Boudreau, & Gefen, 2004). This method is helpful when performing the first three steps of Churchill’s multi-item scale development (1979) and MacKenzie et al.’s scale development procedure (2011): conceptualise constructs, generate items, and assess content validity.

## 7.1. Construct operationalisation and scale development

All constructs are measured using multi-item seven-point Likert scales. New scales are developed for the formative construct perceived similarity in medical status with three independent sub-dimensions, such as medical condition, medical background, age (Fan et al., 2014) Reflective measures were refined and adapted from existing scales and the literature. Following Churchill (1979) and Mackenzie et al.’s (2011) scale development procedure, the development and refinement of scales started from a preliminary item pool of 158 items, either selected from past studies according to their match with the construct definitions used in this study, or written to reflect the relevant definition. This initial pool generation process ensured a large number of items so as to maximise the coverage of the conceptual domain of each construct. All items were adapted, where necessary, to fit the context of this study, and then presented to an expert panel for review (four Ph.D. students who are familiar with online health forums and questionnaire validation procedures) The panel screened for items that were leading questions, not neutral, double-barrelled, ambiguous, unfamiliar, or dificult to comprehend, and problematic items were rewritten or deleted.

A q-sort procedure was then conducted to evaluate the content validity of the remaining candidate items. Participants who have experience with online health forums were recruited via the Amazon Mechanical Turk system and asked to complete two tasks:

(1) group statements based on their similarity (the number of groups was not pre-determined and an “I don’t know” option was provided to avoid forced choices); and

(2) assign a score from 0 to 5 to each statement based on its similarity to a given definition (5 means very similar and 0 means not similar at all).

To avoid participant fatigue but still obtain at least 20 assessments of each item (Brown, 1986; Kiser, Medof, Black, Nurse, & Fiese, 2010; McKeown & Thomas, 1988; Nikolaus, Bode, Taal, & van de Laar, 2010), the 158 statements were divided among 10 work tasks so that each task involved assessing around 15 statements, representing one or two constructs. For example, one task involved assessing an amalgamation of items for perceived similarity in values and similarity in medical status so that participants could assess whether they were one, two, or more separate constructs. Each participant was able to complete as many as 10 tasks. Restricting the number of items to be assessed helped to ensure accuracy, while the limit on the number of tasks helped to ensure diversity in the assessors. This is essential in q-sort method and more important than sample size (Nikolaus et al., 2010). By the end of the assessment period, each construct had been evaluated 27–35 times. A correlation matrix was then constructed for all items and all evaluators, with each item shown in the header line for the rows and columns. Within each matrix (one per evaluator) a cell was given a value of 1 if the evaluator had grouped the item named for that column with the item for the row, and zero for non-matches. The average of these correlation matrices was then calculated. Fortyfour items with average correlations of more than 0.5 were retained for a pre-test.

This pre-test examined the reliability and validity of the tentative instrument. Again using Amazon’s Mechanical Turk, 108 participants with prior experience with online health forums were sought to complete a pilot survey. Using that data, explanatory factor analysis (EFA) was performed on reflective constructs as EFA is a covariance-based approach and is meaningless for validity analysis of a formative construct. Although the sample size is small, it can be acceptable if communalities are high and factors are clearly determined (MacCallum, Widaman, Zhang, & Hong, 1999). To improve the results, using the principle component extraction method and Varimax rotation, all variables with skewed distribution were transformed using log10 (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2007). Validity analysis shows an overall Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) statistic of 0.875, indicating that the data should to factor well, a significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity, and communality measures above 0.60, also indicating that the items related to others enough to perform a meaningful EFA. Loadings exceeding 0.5 were highlighted (see Appendix A).

The EFA shows a clear loading pattern with some minor issues and the items of trust formation constructs were tweaked for the oficial survey. Items with problematic loadings were analysed. First of all, AFT13, AFT4 (Afective Trust items), COT14 (Cognitive Trust item), RCF17 (Relational Closeness Forming item), SIM1 (Perceived Similarity in Values item) were dropped (highlighted in yellow in Appendix A) due to their poor loadings on the focal factor and high cross-loadings, and SIM3 (Perceived Similarity in Values item) was dropped because it was found to be ambiguous. Secondly, INA1 (Information Adoption item) showed moderate loading but the feedback from participants expressed that INA1 is not generally relevant, as in some occasions there was no action suggested in a posting. INA1 was therefore dropped and two replacement items were drawn from the pool. Thirdly, the items of perceived empathy had borderline loadings and were replaced. A new set of items were generated based on the conceptual domain of the construct, and the results of the q-sort procedure. EMP36 (Perceived Empathy item), 39, 40, 41, and 42 were dropped. EMP33 (the person understands my emotions, feelings and concerns) and 44 (this person helps me work through situations and problems/concerns) were added to the remaining EMP measure (EMP35, 37, and 38).

The formative construct perceived similarity in medical status was assessed based on face validity. Items of this construct were derived from the interview of a previous study (Fan et al., 2014). Medical condition, medical background, medical treatment and age were the most frequently mentioned criteria for assessing medical similarity. The statistical analysis of measurement relations will be administrated in the next section. The final scales used in the study are detailed in Appendix B.

## 7.2. Data collection

Following development and verification of the measures, an online survey was conducted over a four-week period to test the model. Respondents were asked to answer questions about their most recent visit to an OHC in the past 7 days (see Appendix B).

## 7.2.1. Targeted population

The theoretical population of this study encompasses people who seek and provide support regarding a specific medical condition from the perspective of patients and care givers in computer-mediated OHCs. Specifically, the sampling population excludes care givers and only focuses on patients who use OHCs. Moreover, a medical condition refers to the general condition of a person’s body, caused by physical illness, disease, disorder, or injury, and excludes mental disorders and spiritual health. Additionally, focusing on trust towards non-authoritative figures, we excluded those OHCs that are explicitly involved with and moderated by professionals who have trained knowledge, credentials and skills in the medical field, such as general medical practitioners, specialists, and registered nurses. Finally, we included communities based in countries with a similar national culture, such as Australia (AUS), the United States (USA) and the United Kingdom (UK) (Geert Hofstede, 1994).

Due to the sensitive nature of our research, participants were self-selected. To minimise non-response bias we used a number of recommended techniques (Atif, Richards, & Biglin, 2012; Rogelberg & Stanton, 2007):

(a) Providing incentives: A chance to win one of four \$50 gift cards provided additional incentive to participate.

(b) Sending multiple reminders to attract responders with varying levels of enthusiasm: Two reminder messages were posted in the same thread at the beginning of the second and the third week.

(c) Providing good communication with potential respondents: Questions, feedback and concerns raised by potential participants in the communities were addressed promptly.

(d) Recruiting through multiple channels: The online survey recruited respondents were recruited through two channels: advertisements posted on 41 OHCs, and advertisements posted around two Australian universities. The 41 OHCs are those who granted approval and supported the study from 294 OHCs we contacted. They are mainly from USA, Australia, and Canada based on the forum name (e.g., Bowel Cancer Australia) and the contact address. This mixture of online and ofline advertising was designed to increase the chance of recruiting both regular and ad hoc users of OHCs and part of a recommended distribution plan (Atif et al., 2012) intended to reduce non-response.

(e) Managing survey length (and participant fatigue) The survey had 33 questions which could all be addressed by a simple tick box or indicator on a Leichardt scale.

(f) Provide questionnaire reach to a large volume of possible respondents and across multiple locations: The survey was distributed across 41 diverse sites in multiple countries.

Several further procedures were implemented to increase response rate to overcome the sensitive nature of the study. A recruiting invitation was only posted on communities that granted approval of and supported the study. Whenever possible, a newsletter enclosing the invitation was, also, sent out to registered users of the site.

Skowronek and Duerr (2009) further suggest that one of the ways to minimise the problems of self-selection is to ensure that there is nothing in the way the survey has been presented to respondents that will reduce the diversity of the sample. In this case the survey was available for 4 weeks and because of the online nature of the survey, it could be undertaken at any time of the day. This reduced the probability that it would be undertaken by any particular group (e.g., people who were not working, people without family responsibilities or other time constraints) that might be the case in a survey that was time or location restricted.

## 7.3. Characteristics of respondents

In the end, the online survey was viewed 2355 times including both unique and repeated views. In total, 473 users visited the survey, indicating that roughly 20.1% of users who viewed the invitation attempted the survey, and 408 of the visitors (86.26%) answered at least one question. Thus, the estimated response rate was 17.32%. The response rate is similar to other research using non-student subjects (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Gruen, Osmonbekov, & Czaplewski, 2006). After deleting unusable cases and outliers and estimating missing values using expectation maximisation (EM) from LISREL, 320 responses were eligible for analysis.

Two-thirds of respondents are females and more than 60% of respondents have a tertiary education. The average age was 42, with a standard deviation of 15.74 and a range from 18 to 78, similar to previous studies (Fox, 2011; Powell & Clarkem, 2006; Weaver et al., 2010). More than two-thirds of respondents have visited OHFs more than once per week and over half of respondents completed the survey on the same day as a visit to an OHF. The country of residence is quite varied: 55% reside in the United States, 24% in Australia, 13% in the United Kingdom, 4% in New Zealand, 2% in Canada, and the remaining 2% were from other countries. Furthermore, in total, there were 113 medical conditions reported by respondents, classified into 18 groups using the World Health Organisation’s International Classification of Diseases (ICD) version 2010 (WHO, 2010).

Although cancer and diseases of the nervous and digestive systems account for 46.90% of cases, a wide range of medical conditions were covered in this study: from chronic (irritable bowel syndrome) to acute (renal failure), from genetic (autoimmune diseases) to environmental (allergies), from symptomatic (back pain) to non-symptomatic (high blood pressure), from high impact (stroke) to low impact (eczema), and from incurable (Parkinson’s disease) to curable/treatable (dermatitis) There are also 4.4% of respondents who visited an

OHC due to experiencing symptoms such as bloating, dizziness, fatigue, headache, rather than sufering a diagnosed medical condition.

While the methods discussed above for reducing the impact of self-selection and non-response bias were taken, the final sample is nevertheless not completely balanced. Males and females appear to show diferent behaviour patterns in single-gender OHCs (Seale, Ziebland, & Charteris-Black, 2006), but behave similarly in mixed-gender OHCs (Mo, Malik, & Coulson, 2009). Of course, this may simply be due to the diferent types of problems dealt with in single and mixed-gender OHCs. To help to eliminate this efect, the majority of respondents in this study are from mixed-gender OHCs. Less than 17 respondents are females with a female-specific medical condition, such as pregnancy, menopause, or ovarian cancer, and no more than 38 males have a male-specific illness, such as prostate cancer. It is not always possible to use statistical methods to estimate non-response bias (Atif et al., 2012) as is the case here where we do not have access to information about the total pool of potential users of OHC. ## 7.4. Assessing the threat of common method variance

Self-reported measures of psychological states and perceptions can cause bias from common method variance (CMV), posing a serious threat to the validity of a study. To address this threat, we used a variety of techniques to minimise bias, including adopting mature measures, performing q-sort procedure to validate new measures, pre-testing on a diferent data-set, using multiple scale formats, creating speed bumps and temporary separation, and protecting participants’ anonymity.

To assess the threat of common method variance, we identified several statistical tests and remedies in the literature: Lindell and Whitney’s Marker variable test (Lindell & Whitney, 2001), Harman’s post hoc single factor test (Harman, 1967), the unmeasured latent method construct (ULMC) test (Liang, Saraf, Hu, & Xue, 2007), the multitrait-multimethod (MMTM) (Shadish, Cook, & Campbell, 2002), and the method-method pair technique (Sharma, Yetton, & Crawford, 2009). However, most of these methods were problematic in some way. The use of redundant methods or measures, as required by the MMTM and method-method pair techniques, was ethically problematic because having a longer data gathering process would potentially over-burden the patients participating in the study (the target population) and thereby reduce the number of usable cases. In addition, the ULMC test is technically problematic. Monte Carlo simulations conducted by Chin, Thatcher, and Wright (2012) indicate that the ULMC test is an unreliable indicator of common method bias.

We therefore used Harman’s single factor test and Lindell and Whitney’s (2001) marker variable tech nique. The first test uses unrotated EFA and constrains the number of factors extracted to be one. If the majority of the variance (i.e., >50% variance) can be explained by a single factor, CMV is considered an issue. The result indicated that no one general factor accounted for the majority of variance with the first factor contributing to 42% of total variance. That second test involves using a marker variable that has no theoretical relationship with other variables (and therefore should be uncorrelated) to partial out the shared variance on the assumption that any high correlation between the marker variable and a construct is due to common method bias. Age was chosen as the marker variable based on consistent evidence that age has no systematic influence on trust after early adulthood (Sutter & Kocher, 2007), and post hoc analysis indicates that age has no significant relationship with any other construct.

All constructs were included in the marker variable test (see Appendix C). The significant unadjusted correlations between age and afective trust, and age and cognitive trust become non-significant after controlling for CMV contamination using the marker variable technique, while all other significant correlations remain significant after the adjustment. This result suggests that the relationships found reflect the true underlying relationships between variables rather than any methodological artefact, and we therefore conclude that CMV has no substantive impact on the results reported.

## 7.5. Results and analysis

PLS analysis (using SmartPLS 3 consistent PLS algorithm and Boostrap) (Ringle, Wende, & Becker, 2015) was chosen to assess the measurement model and test hypotheses due to the complexity of the model and the small sample size (Gefen, Straub, & Boudreau, 2000). We chose SmartPLS 3 because it contains algorithms that rectify the inconsistent, potentially inflated PLS path coeficient estimation in SmartPLS 2 (Dijkstra & Henseler, 2015).

## 7.6. Reflective measurement model assessment

Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability. Cronbach’s alpha scores fall between 0.87 and 0.93 (see Tables 1 and 2), satisfying Nunnally’s (1978) recommendation of 0.8 or 0.9 for studies in later phases, and the composite reliability score exceeds the recommended 0.7 cut-of (Hair, Anderson, Tatham, & Black, 1998). In addition, all indicators except SIM1 have loadings well above the widely accepted threshold of 0.7 (Doll, Raghunathan, Lim, & Gupta, 1995), demonstrating a technically high level of indicator reliability. Because SIM1 has a low loading score, it was not used in the analysis.

To analyse factorial validity, each item must load on its latent construct with a significant t-value. Convergent validity is supported if the average variance extracted (AVE) of a construct exceeds 0.5 (Chin et al., 2012) AVEs of all factors meet this requirement. Discriminant validity is firstly demonstrated with low cross-loadings and diferences of at least 0.2 between each factor loading and its corresponding cross-loading. Further, the Fornell–Larcker criterion is also met where the square root of the AVE is higher than the correlations between the latent variable and all other latent variables. The diagonals in Table 2 are the square root of AVE, which is higher than any values in its row and column. Based on the above tests, the reflective measurement model is reliable and valid.

Because indicators of a formative construct should co-vary and correlate as little as possible, a multi-collinearity analysis was conducted by calculating how much an indicator’s variance is explained by the other indicators in the construct. The commonly accepted cut-of value for the variance inflation factor (VIF) is smaller than 5 (Hair, Ringle, & Sarstedt, 2011). Table 3 lists VIF values from each round of regression and the low VIF values indicate that multi-collinearity is not an issue.

## 8. Discussion

In this paper, we report on our investigation into the development of trust in OHCs, particularly (1) how perceived attributes of the contributor and past behaviour (postings made by the contributor) influence cognitive and afective trust and (2) how both forms of trust influence subsequent information adoption and close relationship formation behaviours by the recipient.

The methodological approach used here highlights the benefits of testing for common methods variance. Similar to Hu, Kettinger, and Poston (2015), we recognised the potential threat to validity from measurements based on common methods and incorporated a number of tests for bias. Had bias been detected we would have been able to correct it using statistical methods. However, our analysis indicates that common method bias is not a serious concern for this study, thereby providing some confidence that the results are not simply the result of a methodological artefact.

These results are consistent with our hypotheses that familiarity with a contributor, perceptions of that contributor, and perceptions of the contributor’s past actions, influence trust, and that trust is the foundation for the formation of closeness and information adoption. Specifically, afective trust in a contributor is influenced strongly by familiarity with the contributor via postings, and a perception that the contributor empathises with the recipient of the contributor’s online postings. Afective trust is influenced to a small extent (still statistically significant) by a perception that the contributor and recipient share similar values (beliefs, morals, hobbies) Cognitive trust in a contributor is influenced strongly by the credibility of past postings (i.e., past actions) and familiarity with the contributor, but also, to a lesser extent, by a recipient’s perception that the contributor and recipient have the same medical condition or are undergoing the same treatment. Additionally, we conducted a post-analysis to test the significant of the relationship between cognitive trust and the formation of closeness. As expected, the relationship is insignificant (β = 0.078, T-value = 1.892) We find that only the formation of closeness depends on the presence of afective trust, while the adoption of the information posted is driven by both afective and cognitive trust.

Our results suggest that concerns expressed in the popular press about the dangers of accessing health information online (e.g., D’arcy, 2015) have some validity: recipients of OHC postings develop an emotional connection (afective trust) with an information contributor based on perceptions of similarity with the contributor and a perception that the contributor expresses empathy for the recipient’s plight. That connection can lead to formation of close relationship and the use of advice that is objectively of dubious value. On the other hand, as pointed out by Fan et al. (2014), OHC interactions tend to involve emotional support or requests for anecdotes about what to expect in the future, while postings that provide treatment advice are rare. In other words, forming an empathetic emotional connection online is likely to be beneficial to participants and is potentially the reason why people join and continue membership of a forum. It is also important to note that emotional response is only one influence on information use. Our model indicates that recipients make a rational assessment of both the contributor and the credibility of a contributor’s prior postings, which may protect against accepting harmful advice.

This process of assessing the contributor may also be viewed through the lens of social capital theory. Adler, in discussing this theory, suggested that hierarchical relationships exist in the real world where the provider of capital needs to demonstrate their authority, with the recipient merely accepting this authority (Adler & Kwon, 2002). Adler and Kwon did not regard such exchanges as generally underpinned by social structure and so the capital exchanged was not obviously represented as social, such as in relations between employer and employee. However, it is interesting to consider how a hierarchical relationship, as described by Adler and Kwon, can fit within the social capital model in the OHC context. As discussed earlier in the paper, in some face-to-face interactions, Adler and Kwon described hierarchical exchanges taking place where individuals accept authority in exchange for material and spiritual security. In these exchanges there is no social capital exchanged by either recipient or contributor and they are considered asymmetrical as a hierarchy is regarded by Adler as a form of domination. Similarly, the typical ofline social capital formation process is also a model in which the contributor determines who is worthy to receive assistance. It is a model that involves an exchange in which the assistance seeker must demonstrate trustworthiness, and possibly acceptance of group values, before assistance is provided.

In the online setting, however, assistance is readily available to be exploited by both passive (anonymous) recipients and active contributors who ascribe social capital to the contributor based on previous opportunities to build trust. In this setting, social capital is therefore required before assistance is accepted and will often be distributed asymmetrically, based on the perceived authority of the contributor. This is in contrast to Adler and Kwon’s view that exchanges of social capital are regarded as symmetrical, yet in hierarchical relations (that do not involve social capital) are asymmetrical. In OHC we see exchanges of social capital, where it is clear that assistance is only accepted once the social capital of the contributor is determined, yet in many cases favours are never returned by passive site users. Consequently, the expectations of an symmetrical exchange, suggested in much of SCT, are not as clear in the OHC environment. A practical implication of this finding is that an understanding of this diferent use of social capital in OHC is important to how users present their credentials on line or are encouraged to do so by site designers.

## 9. Conclusion

While many OHCs have evolved from simple websites rather than been actively designed, their growth and influence as sources of information demand that we have a deeper understanding of how they are used and of why users may decide to continue or abandon use. This research develops the OHC literature by adding to our theoretical understanding of how OHC operate by showing what factors contribute to the formation of both cognitive and efective trust by users of OHCs.

In this paper we assess the role of two forms of trust, cognitive and afective, on information adoption and the formation of closeness, both representative of relationship forming behaviours in an online community. Our study contributes to research by demonstrating that cognitive trust and afective trust are distinct constructs that share some common antecedents and contribute to some similar outcomes (but do so via diferent mechanisms) The antecedents of cognitive trust are familiarity with the person, perceived information credibility, and perceived similarity in medical status, whereas the antecedents of afective trust are familiarity with the person, perceived empathy, and perceived similarity in values. This is in contrast to research that treats trust as a uni-dimensional first-order construct (Bansal, Zahedi, & Gefen, 2015; Matook, Brown, & Rolf, 2015).

One important contribution we make in this research to the body of work on OHC is to show that cognitive trust is formed in the online setting using a deliberative assessment of past behaviour and whether the other person can ofer meaningful advice, whereas afective trust is based on emotional bonds. As a result of these diferences, cognitive trust significantly influences information adoption behaviours only (a weak form of relationship), whereas afective trust leads to both information adoption and closeness forming behaviours.

Our research also contrasts with studies discussed earlier that discount the impact of afective trust and treat information use within an online community as a type of technology acceptance problem (e.g., Casaló et al., 2011). That is, our study contributes to OHC theory by indicating that information adoption is an aspect of the relationship building process and therefore part of a much broader set of community interactions. That is, information provision and adoption are important relational activities, but focusing only on that exchange is arguably inadequate in the online community context because information exchange is only one of the activities that sustain a community. Moreover, the acceptance or rejection of that information is not based entirely on rational criteria, but on a mixture of rational analysis and emotional attachment. Modelling information acceptance in this way is quite diferent to the typical technology acceptance based approach, in which the adoption decision is assumed to be based on rational criteria, such as anticipated benefits and ease of implementation (Venkatesh, Thong, Chan, Hu, & Brown, 2011).

Our study contributes further to practice by showing that because familiarity and perceived similarity are foundations of trust, OHC designers might consider allowing familiarity and similarity-based searches within a forum. Supporting a familiarity-based search might be as simple as allowing a search for a particular contributor. Similarity-based searches are potentially more complex. Searching for postings from people with a similar medical condition (or about that condition) may be relatively easy, although some intelligence will be needed to allow the search to recognise related conditions (e.g., dementia and Alzheimer’s disease) Searching for people with similar values is much harder, particularly when contributors are anonymous or take steps to conceal their identity. Given this constraint, identity-based searches are not feasible, so a similarity-based search might instead use interaction patterns to suggest connections. For example, if B has interacted with A and C, then it is possible that A and C share common values. These connections are typically not hidden within a forum but are nevertheless suficiently dificult to analyse manually that even the basic form for matching described could add value by helping users to identify and connect with new sources. Attachments can be further enhanced by allowing private interactions (Fortin & Dholakia, 2005) so that sensitive issues can be discussed outside of public view.

This study informs practice in additional ways: Given the large impact of empathy on afective trust, designers may wish to incorporate mechanisms that encourage empathetic postings as a social norm. For example, postings that are seen as insensitive or overly critical could be “red flagged” by recipients. Website scripts could then de-emphasise those postings by making only the heading visible, or perhaps contributors who accumulate these flags could be highlighted. Site-wide approaches that encourage empathetic postings are likely to encourage stronger participation.

## 10. Limitations and future research

One limitation of our work is that prior experience with an OHC is not explicitly modelled here, but could conceivably influence information adoption and relational closeness formation. Future work will need to ensure that experience is modelled explicitly. However, modelling experience adequately is not simply a matter of measuring frequency of use. Experience is a multi-dimensional variable that needs to be defined carefully. The efect is unlikely to have influenced results greatly in this case because health forums are a relatively new phenomenon, but its absence is nevertheless a limitation.

A further limitation of our work is that our results are based on a sample of respondents from Western nations only (e.g., the United States, Australia, the United Kingdom) Although national culture is unlikely to change the underlying trust mechanism fundamentally, it is possible that culture may influence trust formation at the individual level. For example, studies in the online shopping context, where the nature of the transaction means that users rely more on cognitive assessment than emotional bonding, show that customers from a collectivist culture (e.g., Korea or China) tend to trust less than those from an individualistic culture. Collectivist cultures are family/clan-centred and are unlikely to trust or rely on strangers in OHCs (Gefen & Heart, 2006; Park, Gunn, & Han, 2012). On the other hand, individualists can be more concerned about their privacy and anonymity (Kim, 2008). People in an individualistic culture (e.g., American or Australian) may limit self-disclosure on OHCs because they tend to have a private space where even close family members are not welcomed without permission. Collectivism is associated with uncertainty avoidance (Hofstede & Bond, 1998) and, in a strong uncertainty avoidance culture, the relationship between the perceived adherence to subjective norms and cognitive trust of consumers is significantly strengthened. While uncertainty avoidance has previously been found to have no significant impact on the relationship between subjective norms and afective trust (Hwang & Lee, 2012), the missing moderating impact on afective trust may be due to the minimal afective trust invoked in the online shopping context. Thus it would be worthwhile for future research to investigate the generalisability of the trust formation framework to other national cultures, particularly the cultural moderation on afective trust.

Future research might also be able to recruit a larger sample, particularly of men, in targeted forums, as in this study more women self-selected to participate. Additionally, recent work by Söllner, Hofmann, and Leimeister (2016) shows that a future study examining other elements of an OHC relating to the IS, the provider or the Internet may provide further insights about trust and user uptake (Söllner et al., 2016).

Finally, previous experimental studies show that cognitive trust precedes afective trust (Webber, 2008). However, in this study, we observed that cognitive and afective trust operate as two parallel routes and cognitive trust does not show significant predictive power towards afective trust as proposed in previous studies. One explanation lies in the context of the OHC, where emotion exchange is prevalent and users can establish emotional bonds directly without cognitive assessment. Another explanation is that a users’ emotion overrides rationality due to the diseases they are experiencing. Individuals who perceive their medical condition as more severe tend to experience a large degree of negative emotion, and this will ultimately afect behaviour (Higgins, 1987). Future research could investigate these alternative explanations to determine the role of the OHC environment and the medical condition on trust formation and information adoption.
