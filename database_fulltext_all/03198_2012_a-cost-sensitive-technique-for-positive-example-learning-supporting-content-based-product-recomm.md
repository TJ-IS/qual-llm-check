# A cost-sensitive technique for positive-example learning supporting content-based product recommendations in B-to-C e-commerce

## a b s t r a c t

Existing supervised learning techniques are able to support product recommendations in business-toconsumer e-commerce but become ineffective in scenarios characterized by single-class learning, such as a training sample that consists of some examples pertaining to only one outcome class (positive or negative). To address such challenges, we develop a COst-sensitive Learning-based Positive Example Learning (COLPEL) technique, which constructs an automated classi<sup>fi</sup>er from a training sample comprised of positive examples and a much larger number of unlabeled examples. The proposed technique incorporates cost-proportionate rejection sampling to derive, from unlabeled examples, a subset that is likely to feature negative examples in the training sample. Our technique follows a committee machine approach and thereby constructs a set of classi<sup>fi</sup>ers that make joint product recommendations while mitigating the potential biases common to the use of a single classi<sup>fi</sup>er. We evaluate the proposed method with customers' book ratings collected from Amazon.com and include two prevalent techniques for benchmark purposes; namely, positive naïve Bayes and positive example-based learning. According to our results, the proposed COLPEL technique outperforms both benchmarks. as measured by accuracy and positive and negative F1 scores.

## 1. Introduction

Product recommendations are crucial in business-to-consumer (Bto-C) e-commerce, in which many vendors compete by offering vast product choices and diverse, extensive information through their Web sites with far fewer capacity constraints than those that limit traditional, physical store outlets. The fast-expanding range of product offerings, in combination with rich and in-depth product and service information, can create substantial information overloads that overwhelm customers' product selections and make their purchase decisions dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, thus affecting their shopping experiences negatively [21,23,32]. To alleviate the substantial cognitive processing commonly required of customers and enhance their values and experiences, many vendors now implement recommender systems that analyze important product attributes and customer characteristics (e.g., demographics, behaviors, preferences) to identify products that may appeal to focal customers [41,55,62,71,73,74,76,77]. A good case in point is Amazon.com, which offers instant recommendation services by analyzing customers characteristics and preferences (e.g., purchased and rated items) across various products [37]. Effective product recommendations can improve customer satisfaction and experiences, while contributing to the vendor's bottom line.

Collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering and content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering are commonly used to develop prediction systems for product recommendations [7]. Collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering exploits important relationships among the product ratings (assessments) by customers. In its most popular incarnation, it identi<sup>fi</sup>es the other people most similar to a focal customer (i.e., neighbors) and produces a weighted vote across these neighbors to predict their ratings of different products, in relation to the focal customer [20,55,63]. Typically, the use of collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering for product recommendations requires a large number of users and suf<sup>fi</sup>cient product ratings [20,74] that are not available in many B-to-C e-commerce scenarios. For example, a vendor usually has many customers but relatively few product ratings by its customers; most customers rate few products, and most products receive ratings from only a handful of customers [37]. Furthermore, collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering may have limited scalability because its use usually involves examinations of all customers and their respective product ratings, which necessitate substantial computational processing to generate product recommendations. Such sparsity and scalability can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly constrain the feasibility and practical value of collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering in B-to-C e-commerce, in which adequate, agile product recommendations are critical [37,58].

Content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering generates product recommendations by considering speci<sup>fi</sup>c, important product characteristics, such as content descriptors; it has been applied in various recommendation scenarios that involve books, Web sites, and news media [12]. In general, content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering assumes important associations among products that can be analyzed, measured, and compared, on the basis of their respective content attribute values, to make appropriate product recommendations [3,36]. The underlying rationale is that customers consider a product interesting if it has attribute values similar to their previously stated or observed preferences [5,22,74]. Therefore, instead of referring to “similar” customers (i.e., neighbors) and their product ratings, content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering emphasizes the focal customer's own preferences, regardless of the number of ratings a product has received from other customers [45]. Compared with collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering, content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering offers more pragmatic utilities for product recommendations in B-to-C e-commerce in which sparse product ratings are often the norm rather than an exception.

The use of content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering for product recommendations also can be considered a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem: Does the focal customer show interest in a product or not? Existing classi<sup>fi</sup>cation techniques can support content-based product recommendations by constructing, from a sample of products (characterized with important product attributes) and customers (described by demographics, behaviors, preferences, or combinations), an automated classi<sup>fi</sup>er capable of predicting whether a product will be of interest to the focal customer. Results from previous research suggest that such a classi<sup>fi</sup>er induced from a training sample with appropriate, supervised, learning-based classi<sup>fi</sup>cation techniques seems effective in recommendation scenarios that involve structured data, such as music or movies [4], as well as unstructured data, such as textual documents, Web site URLs, and news [29,31,51,53,60].

Although traditional, supervised, learning-based classi<sup>fi</sup>cation techniques can build classi<sup>fi</sup>ers by discovering important patterns from a training sample of historical examples (instances) that pertain to different outcome classes, they often become ineffective if the training sample only consists of examples pertaining to one outcome class and many unlabeled ones, which constitutes the single-class learning challenge [13,25,66,80]. Single-class learning prevails in Bto-C e-commerce. For example, a vendor may have some knowledge about products that might appeal to focal customers because it has analyzed the products they browsed, compared, purchased, or reviewed previously; however, the vendor usually has dif<sup>fi</sup>culty gauging customers' interest in the products they have not browsed, purchased, or reviewed before.

Several techniques have been developed to address the single-class learning challenge. Tax and Duin [66] propose a support-vector data description technique that extends the support vector machine (SVM) but requires substantial processing time that in turn reduces its applicability and practicality in scenarios that demand time-ef<sup>fi</sup>cient, agile recommendations. Yu et al. [80] propose a positive example-based learning (PEBL) technique, which follows an iterative strategy to build a prediction model with both known positive examples (e.g., identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, preferred products of a focal customer) and probable negative examples inferred from the unlabeled examples (e.g., other products not rated by the focal customer) in the training sample. By adapting the naïve Bayes induction algorithm, Denis et al. [13] develop a positive naïve Bayes (PNB) technique by using both positive and unlabeled examples to estimate the a priori probability of each outcome class and then construct a prediction model accordingly.

Although the initial evaluations of PNB and PEBL seem promising [13,80], both techniques suffer several fundamental limitations that impede their effectiveness for product recommendations in B-to-C e-commerce. For example, the PNB technique assumes some knowledge about the accurate a priori probabilities of the different outcome classes, and it is likely to produce inaccurate estimates, because it generally cannot estimate, with reasonable accuracy, probabilities from a training sample that contains a handful of positive examples (e.g., focal customer's preferred products) and many more unlabeled examples. The PEBL technique extends an existing learning-based classi<sup>fi</sup>cation technique and is relatively more complex to implement than PNB, and its effectiveness still depends on the representativeness of the initial array of strongly negative examples [72]. That is, the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, probable negative examples tend to be highly associated with strongly negative examples. The use of strongly negative examples that are less representative can initiate a downward cycle that continually diminishes the predictive power of the classi<sup>fi</sup>er constructed in each iteration. Furthermore, the iterative processing of PEBL demands signi<sup>fi</sup>cant time and computational processing that impede its applicability and practical value for product recommendations in B-to-C e-commerce.

To address these limitations, we develop a COst-sensitive Learningbased Positive Example Learning (COLPEL) technique as a solution to the single-class learning challenge. Our technique includes a novel cost measure to assess the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost associated with each unlabeled example and employs cost-proportionate rejection sampling [6,70,81] to derive a subset of likely negative examples, according to their respective misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost estimates. To reduce potential biases common to the use of a single classi<sup>fi</sup>er, we adopt a committee machine approach that builds a set of classi<sup>fi</sup>ers to make product recommendations jointly. The committee machine approach also enhances the predictive power of COLPEL. The proposed technique can avoid the downward cycle learning problem triggered by an inadequate seeding of less representative but strongly negative examples. We empirically evaluate the COLPEL, in terms of predictive effectiveness, with book ratings collected from Amazon.com, using both PNB and PEBL techniques as performance benchmarks.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, we provide an overview of collaborative and content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering for product recommendations and highlight our motivation. In Section 3, we describe the proposed COLPEL technique, followed by the details of our evaluation design and important parameter tuning experiments in Section 4. We report the key evaluation results in Section 5 and conclude with a summary, discussions of our contributions and future research directions in Section 6.

## 2. Literature review and motivation

Collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering and content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering represent two salient approaches for product recommendations. In this section, we provide an overview of each, analyze their respective limitations for product recommendations, and then review previous single-class learning research to identify the gaps that motivate our research.

## 2.1. Collaborative and content-based filtering for product recommendation

A recommender system makes product recommendations by sifting through a vast collection of products to identify those that appear relevant or interesting to focal customers. To succeed in the increasingly competitive B-to-C e-commerce sector, many vendors strive to provide personalized services with effective product recommendations [14,41,47]. The ease with which an online vendor can gather, integrate, and analyze voluminous data about customers and products has fostered greater interest in and actual implementation of personalized services delivered by intelligent recommender systems [1,3,37,60,74]. Not surprisingly, a rapidly growing group of vendors (e.g., Amazon, Genius, StyleFeeder, Net<sup>fl</sup>ix, MovieLens, CDNOW, Barnes and Noble, IMDb, Baynote, iTunes, Musicmatch, TiVo, LibraryThing, Hollywood Video) deploys various recommender systems to better serve customers.

Both collaborative and content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering can be employed to build intelligent recommender systems [7]. Collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering identi<sup>fi</sup>es customers who share similar tastes or preferences with the focal customer and recommends products that these referent others like [5,22,55], with a fundamental assumption that people's tastes and preferences will not change drastically in short periods of time. Using collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering, a vendor can analyze customers evaluations, usually in the form of product reviews or ratings, and exploit the relationships among product ratings across a group of customers. That is, collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering identi<sup>fi</sup>es a focal customer's neighbors and develops weighted predictions about the focal customer's interest levels in different products [20,63]. Whereas the number of customers may be enormous in B-to-C e-commerce, their product ratings are often limited [28,37,58], which substantially constrains the applicability or practical value of collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering for product recommendations [37]. Many vendors receive only a few product ratings and cannot analyze products not rated by customers previously for product recommendations—that is, the <sup>fi</sup>rst-rater problem. Such sparsity issues greatly constrain the use of collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering to identify neighbors for product recommendations. Furthermore, collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering tends to favor “popular” products because it often places less weight on new or obscure products that could be recommended to customers [49]. Finally, collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering demands substantial computational resources that further limit its applicability in B-to-C e-commerce, which demands agile, nearly realtime recommendations [37].

In contrast, content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering emphasizes a focal customer's preferences and can recommend products that have received few or even no ratings from other customers [45,50,60]. Content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering builds a preference pro<sup>fi</sup>le for each customer, uses it to search prospective products with attributes or characteristics highly relevant and similar to those speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by the focal customer's pro<sup>fi</sup>le, and makes product recommendations accordingly [5,22,51,53,60]. Content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering can be supported by traditional, inductive learning techniques that construct automated classi<sup>fi</sup>ers on the basis of the important patterns in customers' preference pro<sup>fi</sup>les. Empirical evidence from previous research indicates that content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering is generally effective for product recommendations [26,29,31,51,60,61,82]; compared with collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering, it offers greater capability to cope with the sparsity constraint because it builds a predictive classi<sup>fi</sup>er in advance and thus is more appropriate for recommendations in B-to-C e-commerce.

The overall process of content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering includes several phases: feature extraction and selection, representation, customer pro<sup>fi</sup>le learning, and recommendation generation [1,50,74]. In the feature extraction and selection phase, a set of features are extracted through an analysis of all products under examination [17,48], from which key features then can be selected to represent each product [54]. Feature selection can improve learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly [17,27,44,65]. Typically, a feature selection measurement score is assigned to each feature, and the top k features with the highest scores are chosen to represent the products. Existing evaluation functions for feature selection include within-document term frequency (TF), within-document term frequency×inverse document frequency (TF×IDF), correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient, mutual information, and the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ metric [17,30,34,48].

In the representation phase, the selected feature set is used to represent products; that is, each product is described by an N-dimensional vector that consists of the N features selected from the training sample. Common feature representation schemes include binary, TF, IDF, and TF×IDF [78]. In the customer pro<sup>fi</sup>le learning phase, a preference model is constructed for each customer by analyzing the relationship between the customer's preference (i.e., dependent variable) and the attribute values of different products (i.e., independent variables). Customer pro<sup>fi</sup>le learning can be supported by statistical methods and computational techniques built on the basis of supervised inductive learning, back-propagation neural networks, or Bayesian probability [3,45,56,82]. In the <sup>fi</sup>nal recommendation generation phase, appropriate products are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by assessing the relationship between the focal customer's preference model and the respective products' attribute values.

The use of content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering for product recommendations requires a training sample that, in principle, should comprise examples (instances) of different outcome classes, such as positive and negative examples that represent products of interest and products that do not appeal to customers, respectively. However, the availability of such a balanced training sample is not always guaranteed. In B-to-C ecommerce, positive examples can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed through customers' browsing, purchasing, or reviewing records; however, obtaining negative examples can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, because they often re<sup>fl</sup>ect products that customers have not browsed, purchased, or reviewed. This dif<sup>fi</sup>culty underscores the single-class learning challenge for effective product recommendations in B-to-C e-commerce.

## 2.2. Previous single-class learning research

Constructing a classi<sup>fi</sup>er for effective product recommendations requires a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient number of properly labeled training examples, in addition to an adequate classi<sup>fi</sup>cation technique. Conventional classi<sup>fi</sup>cation techniques emphasize the discovery of important patterns or rules that distinguish instances in a training sample. For example, existing binary classi<sup>fi</sup>er learning techniques are not effective for single-class learning, because the training sample usually consists of just a few positive examples and many more unlabeled examples, such that it lacks negative examples. Previous research has attempted to address the single-class learning challenge. One approach performs density estimation by <sup>fi</sup>tting the positive examples to a statistical distribution [52] and extending an existing multi-class classi<sup>fi</sup>cation technique by identifying the boundaries of positive examples [46,59,66], without making use of the unlabeled examples. Examples that have a lower density value or fall outside of the boundary are then considered representative of the negative class. Another approach uses both positive and unlabeled examples to develop techniques that estimate the parameters necessary for building a probabilistic prediction model [8–10,13,33]. This approach requires prior knowledge of the probability of positive examples, which is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to obtain pragmatically, so it often assigns unlabeled examples to a negative class when making estimations. Yet another two-step approach attempts to identify probable negative examples from the unlabeled examples in the training sample, then use the estimated negative examples, in combination with the known positive examples, as input to a conventional classi<sup>fi</sup>cation technique to make predictions [19 80 35 39 40] The approach that relies on density or boundary estimation does not consider unlabeled examples and is highly sensitive to the representation and dimensionality of input examples [42]. Therefore, the resulting techniques become less effective than those built with a probabilistic prediction model or a two-step approach that takes advantage of the unlabeled examples [39]. Positive naïve Bayes (PNB) and positive example-based learning (PEBL) are two representative techniques that follow a probabilistic prediction model and a two-step approach, respectively. In the following, we analyze these two techniques and identify their limitations, which motivate our research.

In principle, the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model induced from a training sample should effectively delineate examples pertaining to different outcome classes, such as positive or negative. As we show in Fig. 1, the PEBL technique follows a two-stage process to learn from positive and unlabeled examples. In the mapping stage, PEBL uses initial approximations of “strongly negative” examples to construct a “rough” classi<sup>fi</sup>er; that is, it <sup>fi</sup>rst identi<sup>fi</sup>es strong positive features by analyzing the important features most frequently observed in the positive examples but rarely appearing in the unlabeled examples. A feature is considered strong and positive if it occurs in more than $\alpha _ { P T } \%$ of the positive examples but less than $\alpha _ { U T } \%$ of the unlabeled examples. Then, these strong positive features can identify, from unlabeled examples (UD), examples that do not contain any strong, positive features, which thus produces the strongly negative examples. The examples that remain in the UD set are plausible, positive examples.

In the convergence stage, PEBL constructs an initial, “rough” classi<sup>fi</sup>er from the positive examples and the strongly negative examples identi-<sup>fi</sup>ed in the mapping stage. Using a support vector machine (SVM), this technique iteratively searches for additional probable negative examples from the remaining unlabeled examples (i.e., plausible, positive examples) and includes them in the training sample as “negative examples.” During each iteration, PEBL uses the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model constructed from the prior iteration to classify each instance in the current set of examples as positive or negative. In this way, PEBL expands the set of negative examples by including the additional examples identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the current iteration, then uses SVM to reconstruct a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model. In each iteration, the set of examples as provide positive instances represents the plausible, positive examples in the next iteration. By repeating this process of identifying and selecting negative examples from the remaining unlabeled examples, the PEBL technique builds a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model continually, until no additional negative examples remain in the unlabeled examples. At the completion of the convergence stage, the learning examples that pertain to different outcome classes converge, creating the boundaries that delineate the positive-class examples in the feature space.

## 2.3. Motivation

Although both the PNB and PEBL techniques can address single-class learning, they suffer from several fundamental limitations that hinder their applicability and value for product recommendations in B-to-C e-commerce. For example, the estimates (e.g., ${ \hat { P } } r ( C _ { n } )$ and $\hat { P } r ( w _ { i } | C _ { n } ) )$ <sup>ð Þ</sup>by PNB are derived from an estimated a priori probability $\hat { P } r \big ( C _ { p } \big )$ <sup>Þ</sup>of the class $C _ { p } ,$ because of the lack of negative examples in the training sample. The accuracy of $\hat { P } r \big ( C _ { p } \big )$ greatly affects the PNB's effectiveness, which is troublesome when considering the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of obtaining an accurate estimate of $\hat { P } r ( C _ { p } ) \ [ 5 7 , 7 2 ]$ . In scenarios in which the class distribution may change considerably over time, dynamic adjustments of the $\hat { P } r \big ( C _ { p } \big )$ estimate, in congruence with the true class distribution of the current state $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , situation), become necessary but challenging.

The implementation of the PEBL technique requires no estimation of the a priori probability of each outcome class; rather, it identi<sup>fi</sup>es probable negative examples from unlabeled examples to construct a more re<sup>fi</sup>ned classi<sup>fi</sup>er in an iterative manner. Yet the effectiveness of PEBL depends on the representativeness of the initial array of strongly negative examples; that is, these examples should contain no key features of positive examples. If it starts with strongly negative examples that are less representative, PEBL is likely to be trapped in a degrading cycle that decreases the predictive power of the constructed classi<sup>fi</sup>er progressively throughout the course of its iterations. However, the training sample usually consists of a few positive examples and a large number of unlabeled examples. In many cases, a large array of unlabeled examples constitutes strongly negative examples that contain no strong positive features, which can result in a skewed distribution of the positive and strongly negative examples in the training sample. Consequently, the classi<sup>fi</sup>er tends to predict examples of the negative outcome class. Furthermore, the iterative nature of PEBL requires substantial computational processing that also reduces its practical value for recommendations in B-to-C e-commerce.

Motivated by the importance of product recommendations for Bto-C e-commerce and the associated single-class learning challenge, we address the fundamental limitations of salient techniques by developing our COst-sensitive Learning-based Positive Example Learning (COLPEL) technique, which builds effective classi<sup>fi</sup>ers from a single-class training sample and offers more predictive utilities than PNB or PEBL, because it does not require accurate estimations of the a priori probability of each outcome class in the training sample or depend too much on strongly negative examples. Rather, COLPEL incorporates cost-sensitive learning to construct a classi<sup>fi</sup>er from positive examples, in conjunction with a subset of negative examples derived from the unlabeled examples, in proportion to the distribution of their misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost estimates. We propose a measure to assess the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost associated with each unlabeled example. To mitigate the potential prediction biases common to the use of a single classi<sup>fi</sup>er, COLPEL follows a committee machine approach and constructs a set of classi<sup>fi</sup>ers that jointly identify the appropriate products to be recommended to focal customers. In the following section, we describe COLPEL's design and implementation.

## 3. COst-sensitive learning-based positive example learning

As we show in Fig. 2, the overall process of the COLPEL technique includes three phases: strong positive feature selection, misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost estimation, and cost-sensitive learning. In the strong positive feature selection phase, the features most representative of the positive examples are selected (i.e., products appealing to focal customers). In the following misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost estimation phase, COLPEL estimates the cost of misclassifying an unlabeled example with the selected strong positive features. It then performs cost-sensitive learning by using cost estimations as important criteria for sampling negative examples. To sample the unlabeled examples, COLPEL employs cost-proportionate rejection sampling and the respective misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs to identify probable negative examples from the training sample, then uses them, in combination with positive examples, to build a set of classi<sup>fi</sup>ers (i.e., committee machine) that jointly make product recommendations. We next detail essential design considerations in each COLPEL phase.

## 3.1. Strong positive feature selection

The COLPEL technique <sup>fi</sup>rst identi<sup>fi</sup>es the features most representative of the positive examples (i.e., known examples of interest to the focal customer) in a training sample. It employs the same method used by PEBL to select a strong positive feature set, $F _ { p } .$ . That is, a feature is included in the strong positive feature set if it appears in more than β % of the positive examples and less than β % of the unlabeled examples.

## 3.2. Misclassification cost estimation

The COLPEL technique attempts to identify from the unlabeled examples likely negative examples, together with the positive examples for classi<sup>fi</sup>er construction. It assesses the likelihood that each unlabeled example will be a negative example by estimating its misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost, that is, the cost of misclassifying an unlabeled example as a positive example. Intuitively, the higher the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost of an unlabeled example, the more likely it is to be a negative example. To estimate the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost of an unlabeled example, COLPEL considers the difference between the example's features and the strong positive feature set, while taking into account the length of the feature list of the unlabeled example. An example may be less relevant or similar to other examples if it shares fewer features with them. According to this reasoning, examples that have more features are more likely to overlap with those of other examples, so an example with a longer list of features is more likely to be a negative example (i.e., higher misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost) than one with a shorter list, if those examples have identical or highly similar overlap with the strong positive feature set. An unlabeled example then has a relatively higher tendency of being classi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the negative outcome class if it has more features but smaller overlap between its features and the strong positive feature set. We address this tendency by de<sup>fi</sup>ning a misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost estimation function as follows: $M C ( e _ { i } ) = ( M a x \left( \left| F _ { p } \cap F _ { e _ { i } } \right| \right) - \left| F _ { p } \cap F _ { e _ { i } } \right| ) ^ { 2 } \times \log ( \left| F _ { e _ { i } } \right| )$ , where $F _ { p }$ <sup>¼</sup>represents the strong positive feature set, $F _ { e i }$ is the set of features in an unlabeled example $e _ { i } ,$ and $M _ { i = 1 } ^ { \mathrm { n } } ( \left| F _ { p } \cap F _ { e _ { i } } \right| )$ calculates the maximum number of strong positive features in all unlabeled examples-that is, the maximum overlap between the strong positive feature set and the features of each unlabeled example. By using maximum overlap to suppress the overlap of an unlabeled example, we ensure a minimal misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost of 0. An example $e _ { i }$ may have a higher misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost if the overlap between its features and the strong positive features set is small. Conceivably, the difference between the maximum overlap and the overlap of an example should be considered more important, because it can produce a larger effect on the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost than the length of the example's own feature set, even though the value of the former is usually less than or equal to that of the latter. We therefore represent the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of feature overlap by using the squared difference between the maximum overlap and the overlap of the respective examples, then adjust the estimated misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost of example $e _ { i }$ by multiplying the logarithm of the length of its own feature set.

To illustrate the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost function, assume that a strong positive feature set $F _ { p }$ has <sup>fi</sup>ve features {A, B, C, D, E} and that examples $e _ { 1 }$ and $e _ { 2 }$ have the sets of features $F _ { e _ { 1 } } = \{ \mathsf { A } , \mathsf { F } ,$ H, I} and $F _ { e _ { 2 } } = \{ \mathsf { A } , \mathsf { B } \}$ , respectively. In this case, the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost for $e _ { 1 }$ is $( 2 - 1 ) ^ { 2 } \times \log ( 4 ) = 2 ,$ , and the cost for $e _ { 2 } { \mathrm { ~ i s ~ } } ( 2 - 2 ) ^ { 2 } { \times } \log ( 2 ) = 0 ,$ . As shown, e has a higher misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost than does e . Because it exhibits a higher likelihood of being a negative example, $e _ { 1 }$ should have a greater probability of being classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a negative example. Furthermore, misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs are relative; examples with higher misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs suggest a greater probability, though not necessity, of being negative examples in the cost-proportionate rejection sampling. In contrast, examples with a lower misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost have a smaller probability of being sampled, suggesting that they are less likely to be sampled than are those with higher misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs.

## 3.3. Cost-sensitive learning

The use of unlabeled examples with different misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs (i.e., importance) to construct a classi<sup>fi</sup>er resembles cost-sensitive learning that builds a classi<sup>fi</sup>er by comparing examples with nonuniform misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs [6,81]. Existing cost-sensitive learning techniques can be broadly classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two categories [38]: using a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation learning algorithm that considers the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost or importance of each example when inducing a classi<sup>fi</sup>er from the training sample [16,18], or using a wrapper method to transform an existing cost-insensitive classi<sup>fi</sup>er into a cost-sensitive equivalence, without modifying the underlying learning algorithm. In general, a wrapper technique incurs ex post processing costs for producing output from a cost-insensitive classi<sup>fi</sup>cation technique or must preprocess the training sample, thus necessitating a choice of between thresholding and sampling [38]. A thresholding technique determines a threshold for classifying training examples as positive or negative; thus, it requires a cost-insensitive classi<sup>fi</sup>er to produce the a posteriori probability estimation for each example under consideration [11,15,43,64,75]. In contrast, a sampling technique preprocesses the training samples through proportional sampling and adjusts the outcome class distribution of the sampled examples (instances), then directly applies a cost-insensitive learning algorithm to the sampled examples or classi<sup>fi</sup>er induction [81]. Extending or re<sup>fi</sup>ning cost-insensitive learning techniques to costsensitive learning is neither ef<sup>fi</sup>cient nor <sup>fl</sup>exible in practice. Furthermore, a thresholding technique is restricted and requires a costinsensitive classi<sup>fi</sup>er that can produce accurate a posteriori probability estimations of the training examples. With sampling, examples are selected from the original training sample, according to their misclassi-<sup>fi</sup>cation costs, and then used to construct a classi<sup>fi</sup>er with an adequate classi<sup>fi</sup>cation learning technique, which results in improved effectiveness and computational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency [67,69,81]. We therefore approach cost-sensitive learning from the perspective of cost-insensitive learning and adopt a sampling method.

In the cost-sensitive learning phase, COLPEL performs two tasks: negative example sampling and classi<sup>fi</sup>er learning. For negative example sampling, cost-proportionate rejection sampling [70] is used to identify, among the costly unlabeled examples, those that pertain to the negative outcome class. When determining whether to include an example, cost-proportionate rejection sampling <sup>fi</sup>rst generates, according to the uniform distribution, a random number $r _ { i } ,$ ranging from 0 to 1, and compares it with the acceptance probability $c _ { i } / Z$ of the example under evaluation, where $c _ { i }$ represents the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost of an example and Z is a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned constant. An example $e _ { i }$ appears in the sample if r does not exceed its acceptance probability; it is not included otherwise. The acceptance probability measure can be re<sup>fi</sup>ned to $c _ { i } / ( \gamma { \times } Z )$ where γ represents the weight of Z. As suggested by Zadrozny et al. [81], the constant Z usually is assigned as the maximal misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost among all examples. We introduce γ to control for sample size and thus can increase the sample by setting γb1 or reduce it by setting γ>1.

The use of a sample set created by cost-proportionate rejection sampling guarantees a cost-minimizing classi<sup>fi</sup>er, assuming the underlying learning algorithm can achieve an approximate minimization of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation errors [81]. Toward that end, COLPEL follows a committee machine approach to generate an ensemble of estimators (i.e., classi<sup>fi</sup>ers) with a learning process, which predicts an example using the combination of the predictions made by individual committee members. The use of a committee machine could, to some extent, balance the prediction errors of individual classi<sup>fi</sup>ers [68], and computational time requirements can be reduced with a divide-and-conquer strategy, especially for a learning algorithm that is based on regression, neural networks, or support vector machines, whose training time requirements can increase drastically with the size of the training sample [68]. Instead of building a classi<sup>fi</sup>er with all training examples, a committee machine partitions the training sample into several subsamples and uses them to construct individual classi<sup>fi</sup>ers in a parallel fashion. Thus the computational complexity of a committee machine increases linearly with the size of the training sample. This reduction of computational complexity is crucial for recommender systems in B-to-C e-commerce, which often involve enormous, fast-growing data and demand agile, nearly real-time recommendations.

In this way, COLPEL randomly generates k sets of negative examples to build individual classi<sup>fi</sup>ers that jointly form a committee to reduce the probable classi<sup>fi</sup>cation biases common to the use of a single classi<sup>fi</sup>er. For classi<sup>fi</sup>er learning, each set of randomly sampled negative examples, in combination with the positive examples, induces a classi<sup>fi</sup>er with a cost-insensitive learning technique. Similar to PEBL, COLPEL employs SVM as a baseline, cost-insensitive learning technique, primarily because of its effectiveness and robustness across a wide range of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation applications [24,79]. As a result, k classi<sup>fi</sup>ers form a committee that makes product recommendations to a focal customer if more than half the members favor such recommendations. Regarding the proposed COLPEL, we theoretically analyze its time requirements versus that of SVM. Although COLPEL adopts SVM as its associated classi<sup>fi</sup>er learning algorithm, in principle it should be more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient than the SVM-based algorithm. Conceivably, the computational time required to construct a single classi<sup>fi</sup>er of COLPEL (hereafter, $\mathrm { C O L P E L } _ { \mathrm { s i n g l e } } )$ is less than that of SVM when the training data set is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large, because $_ { \mathrm { C O L P E L } _ { \mathrm { s i n g l e } } }$ is trained by the partial set of unlabeled examples, whereas SVM uses the complete set of unlabeled examples. Because each member classi<sup>fi</sup>er of COLPEL (committee machine) can be constructed concurrently, its time requirement should be comparable to that of $\mathrm { C O L P E L } _ { \mathrm { { s i n g l e } } } .$ Even in the worst-case scenario, when the member classi<sup>fi</sup>ers of COLPEL are constructed sequentially, the time requirement only grows linearly with the number of member classi<sup>fi</sup>ers in the committee machine. That is, COPEL is more time-ef<sup>fi</sup>cient than SVM, whose time requirement increases exponentially with the full size of the training data set.

## 4. Data collection, evaluation design, and parameter tuning

We empirically evaluated COLPEL with book ratings gathered from Amazon.com. Our evaluation also included the PNB and PEBL techniques as performance benchmarks. In this section, we detail our data collection, study design, performance measures, and parameter tuning for each investigated technique

## 4.1. Data collection

Our evaluation targeted content-based product recommendations. We targeted the top 40 reviewers from Amazon's top reviewer $\mathrm { l i s t ^ { 1 } }$ (in April 2008) and gathered their book ratings, as well as key content features: statistically improbable phrase (SIPs) and capitalized phrase (CAPs) of each book rated. Amazon's book rating system indicates a reviewer's assessment of a book, using a <sup>fi</sup>ve-point scale. In our evaluation, we considered books with a rating of 4 or higher as positive examples (i.e., potentially interesting to customers) and those with lower ratings as negative examples. To ensure a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient number of negative examples by each reviewer, we removed reviewers who had fewer than <sup>fi</sup>ve negative examples, which reduced the data set to reviews by 36 top reviewers. Amazon also provides customers with a quick glimpse of each book's contents by offering SIPs and CAPs that together describe its content. With content-browsing software, Amazon identi<sup>fi</sup>es the most representative and distinctive SIPs of a book—that is, the phrases least likely to appear in any other

Table 1

books available on Amazon. The CAPs include phrases about people, places, events, or topics that frequently appear in the book. Jointly, the SIPs and CAPs convey key content features of each book (product) in our evaluation.

We collected 23,162 book ratings, associated with 18,469 books, by the 36 top reviewers. On average, each book received 1.25 ratings from the reviewers, which exempli<sup>fi</sup>es the sparsity problem that prevails in B-to-C e-commerce. Among the reviewers, the maximum number of book ratings generated was 2681, and the minimum was 65. The maximum, minimum, and average numbers of content (product) features (i.e., SIPs and CAPs combined) were 155, 2, and 57, respectively. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our data set consisted of 20,962 positive examples and 2200 negative examples.

## 4.2. Evaluation design

For each reviewer, we created a testing data set by randomly selecting 40% of the negative examples and an equal number of positive examples, which enabled us to mitigate the potential noise resulting from the prediction biases associated with a recommender system, such as a tendency of predicting an example to be positive or negative. We then randomly sampled 40% of the remaining positive examples and considered the remaining positive and negative examples as unlabeled examples. For example, assume an original data set of 1000 positive examples and 200 negative examples. We would create a balanced testing data set by sampling 40% of negative examples (i.e., 80 negative examples) and 80 positive examples. We therefore sampled 40% of 920 remaining positive examples to serve as the positive examples (i.e., 368) and considered all remaining examples—552 positive and 120 negative—unlabeled. With this sampling, the maximum, minimum, and average numbers of positive examples remaining in the training sample among reviewers were 823, 19, and 223, respectively.

To reduce the potential biases of random sampling and obtain accurate, robust performance estimates, we repeated the evaluation process 10 times for each reviewer. Our measures of the effectiveness of each technique re<sup>fl</sup>ected average performance over 10 trials. Because the volume of the testing data signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly varied across reviewers, we examined the overall effectiveness of each technique using weighted average performance across the 36 reviewers, considering the number of testing examples that each reviewer created as a proportion of the total number of testing examples.

## 4.3. Performance measures

Our evaluation targets prediction accuracy: How accurately does each investigated technique recommend products of interest to focal customers? That is, we examined how well each technique performs with respect to the different outcome classes. In addition to accuracy, the F1 performance measure, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as <sup>2</sup> <sup>×recall precision</sup>, offers a single-<sup>-</sup>recall precision <sup>ð Þþ</sup>value summary that considers both recall and precision rates, as well as their trade-off. The use of the F1 measure also allows us to evaluate the respective techniques with respect to each outcome class (i.e., positive or negative); thus, we considered the separate F1 scores for the positive class and for the negative class.

The use of COLPEL also involves two additional parameters: the weight of constant Z used in cost-proportionate rejection sampling (γ) and the number of classi<sup>fi</sup>ers to be constructed (k).

To avoid excessively tuning parameters that could lead to over<sup>fi</sup>tting in the experimental data set, we randomly sampled three reviewers and used their preference records to <sup>fi</sup>ne-tune the respective parameters. ## 5.3. Discussion

The proposed COLPEL technique, which incorporates cost-sensitive learning and a committee machine approach, can address the positive example learning challenge in product recommendations and seems more effective than existing techniques, such as PNB and PEBL. In particular, the use of cost-proportionate rejection sampling can identify, from a set of unlabeled examples, the most likely negative examples for improving predictions of both positive and negative examples and thereby enhance prediction accuracy. The COLPEL technique undertakes sampling on the basis of the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs of unlabeled examples and therefore is not sensitive to the class distribution of a training sample. In contrast, the lack of negative examples in B-to-C ecommerce (e.g., products not interesting to focal customers) makes accurate estimations of the a priori probabilities for predictive classes dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, which dampens the performance of the PNB technique. The predictions by PNB seem biased toward the negative class, partly because this technique assumes all unlabeled examples are negative. Instead of sampling repeatedly and extensively, PEBL starts with an array of strongly negative examples and identi<sup>fi</sup>es, in an iterative fashion, probable negative examples from the remaining unlabeled examples. Its effectiveness may be constrained by the representativeness of the strongly negative examples; its predictive performance can be restricted if the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed negative examples are limited.

The sampling quality of probable negative examples thus has important effects on the performance of COLPEL. Using a quali<sup>fi</sup>ed set of probable, negative examples allows for the discovery of more distinct patterns for the positive class and thus improves the performance of the positive example learning classi-<sup>fi</sup>er for product recommendations. Our analysis results also suggest the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of cost-proportionate rejection sampling on the sampling quality of probable, negative examples, which in turn improves the predictive power of COLPEL.

COLPEL<sub>single</sub> has a tendency to predict that unlabelled examples are members of the negative class, which creates a trade-off between positive and negative recalls and favors the use of a committee machine to mitigate potential prediction biases by a single classi<sup>fi</sup>er, such as $\mathrm { C O L P E L } _ { \mathrm { s i n g l e } }$ . The negative recall attained by COLPEL decreases as its positive recall increases; in comparison, $\mathrm { C O L P E L } _ { \mathrm { s i n g l e } }$ can achieve a negative recall comparable to that of PNB. The prediction bias associated with COLPEL could be explained partially by the large number of different negative examples identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from the unlabeled examples, which can improve the prediction precision of COLPEL and result in high scores for both positive and negative precision. To construct a committee of k classi<sup>fi</sup>ers, COLPEL generates k sets of negative examples from unlabeled examples; as a result, the increased number of training examples enhances the prediction precisions of COLPEL.

Compared with the PNB and PEBL, COLPEL is more effective for building recommender systems; it exhibits relatively high prediction precision for both positive and negative examples and thereby offers more utility in the form of deliberative product recommendations to focal customers, even as it <sup>fi</sup>lters out products not particularly interesting or relevant to those customers. The COLPEL technique is selective in its product recommendations, as suggested by a relatively high positive precision score and acceptable positive recall score. Such precision and selectivity are crucial because frequent, highly inappropriate recommendations can diminish the vendor's reputation and erode customer trust.

Our evaluation results offer several insights regarding the development of effective content-based recommender systems in B-to-C ecommerce. First, cost-sensitive learning that incorporates an appropriate sampling method seems more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and is subject to fewer training sample constraints, especially in scenarios where customers' purchasing and browsing behaviors change frequently. Second, the $\mathrm { C O L P E L } _ { \mathrm { s i n g l e } }$ technique, a single-classi<sup>fi</sup>er version of COLPEL, outperforms both PNB and PEBL, which suggests the possibility of improving a classi<sup>fi</sup>er's effectiveness and robustness through extensive sampling of the unlabeled examples to form a representative training sample. Third, the use of a committee machine approach to construct classi<sup>fi</sup>ers by sampling from the unlabeled examples repeatedly may produce different but representative examples from a training sample, which is indispensable for effective product recommendations. Although the use of a large training sample can improve the resulting classi<sup>fi</sup>er's performance, a balanced distribution of examples that pertain to different outcome classes (e.g., positive or negative) could help avoid prediction biases by the classi<sup>fi</sup>er induced from a training sample. In addition, the committee machine approach, unlike classi<sup>fi</sup>er learning in sequence (e.g., PEBL), can construct classi<sup>fi</sup>ers in parallel and therefore improve the agility of product recommendations.

## 6. Conclusion and future research directions

Content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering offers a viable approach for product recommendations because it analyzes important product features and focal customers' demographic and behavioral characteristics, without making reference to other customers (e.g., neighbors). Existing supervised learning techniques can support content-based recommendations by inducing from a training sample a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model for predictions. Effective content-based product recommendations require a training sample that consists of suf<sup>fi</sup>cient examples of different outcome classes, which is not available in many B-to-C e-commerce scenarios.

To address the single-class learning challenge, we develop a costsensitive, learning-based, positive example learning technique that employs cost-sensitive learning and a committee machine approach. Our technique incorporates cost-proportionate rejection sampling to construct individual classi<sup>fi</sup>ers that jointly form a committee machine for product recommendations. Cost-proportionate rejection sampling can identify highly probable negative examples from unlabeled examples; the committee machine approach can reduce potential biases common to the use of a single classi<sup>fi</sup>er. We examine the effectiveness of COLPEL empirically with book ratings by 36 top reviewers' preference records, obtained from Amazon.com. Our evaluation includes two salient techniques as benchmarks, PNB and PEBL. Overall, our results show that COLPEL outperforms both benchmark techniques, as measured by their accuracy and average F1 score.

This study contributes to research into product recommendations in B-to-C e-commerce in general and single-class learning in particular. First, the proposed COLPEL technique takes a content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering approach and is not constrained by the limitations common to collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering; it can make appropriate product recommendations in the presence of the sparsity, <sup>fi</sup>rst-rater, and scalability challenges that prevail in B-to-C e-commerce. Our technique adopts cost-sensitive learning to address the constraints of the prevalent content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering techniques and cope with single-class learning, characterized by a training sample with a few positive examples and a much larger number of unlabeled examples. Second, COLPEL, supported by a model-based classi<sup>fi</sup>cation method and a committee machine approach, can construct predictive models for respective customers in advance and make product recommendations in parallel; therefore, it is appropriate for B-to-C e-commerce that requires nearly real-time, agile product recommendations. Overall, COLPEL shows desirable precision and selectivity in recommendations and thus might enhance vendors' reputations and customer trust. Third, to address cost-sensitive learning, we incorporate cost-proportionate rejection sampling to select, from the unlabeled examples, examples that are likely to pertain to the negative class, according to their respective misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost (i.e., importance), which conveys different product features that mark unlabeled examples rather than a strong positive feature set derived from positive examples. In particular, we propose a misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost estimation function and incorporate it into COLPEL to evaluate different products characterized by a set of key features. Our cost function can be extended for different classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (prediction) scenarios with similar characteristics, such as e-mail spam <sup>fi</sup>ltering or clinical diagnosis decision support. The cost-proportionate rejection sampling method can select properly, from unlabeled examples, a set of probable negative examples to build an effective classi<sup>fi</sup>er. Furthermore, our proposed technique adopts a committee machine and is subject to fewer limitations than salient single-class learning techniques, including PNB and PEBL; it also can generate more effective product recommendations.

This study has several limitations that in turn suggest important future research directions. First, COLPEL uses misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs to identify negative examples from the unlabeled examples in a training sample. Assigning an adequate misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost to each unlabeled example is important for cost-sensitive learning. Our proposed technique estimates misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs by considering the overlapping features between an unlabeled example and the positive examples only. Promising extensions could increase the sophistication of misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost estimations by taking into consideration other important factors, including the weight (importance) of the different features of the positive examples. Second, COLPEL adopts SVM as its baseline classi<sup>fi</sup>er induction technique; other classi<sup>fi</sup>cation techniques, such as inductive decision trees or neural networks, should be explored and evaluated to extend the proposed COLPEL technique with alternative classi<sup>fi</sup>cation algorithms and compare their performance empirically. Third, for this study, we assume customer preferences are time invariant, but a customer's preference might change subtly over time. Accordingly, COLPEL needs suf<sup>fi</sup>cient adaptive capability if it is to provide sustainable performance. The evolving nature of customers' preferences may favor an evolutionary approach, with surveys of customers' preferences over time and assessments based on longitudinal behavioral data. Fourth, this study considers monolingual content-based recommendations; extensions to support multilingual recommendations, such as for a vendor's Web site available in different languages, are desirable. The book recommendations we used record reviewers' ratings at a single Web site; continued research should evaluate COLPEL with customers' preferences recorded in multiple recommendation contexts and related data from different vendors.
