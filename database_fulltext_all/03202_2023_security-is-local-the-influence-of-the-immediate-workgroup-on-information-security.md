# Security is Local: The Influence of the Immediate Workgroup on Information Security

Dawei (David) Wang,<sup>1</sup> Alexandra Durcikova,<sup>2</sup> Alan R. Dennis<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Missouri University of Science and Technology, USA, dwang@mst.edu <sup>2</sup>University of Oklahoma, USA, alex@ou.edu <sup>3</sup>Indiana University, USA, ardennis@indiana.edu

## Abstract

Information security is a multilevel phenomenon with employee security decisions being influenced by macrolevel factors (e.g., organizational policies), mesolevel factors (e.g., one’s immediate workgroup—IW), and microlevel factors (e.g., individual personalities). We argue that an employee’s local IW (i.e., immediate supervisor and coworkers) has a strong effect on security. This paper focuses on the effects of these mesolevel factors in the presence of macro- and microlevel factors. Drawing on the social structure and social learning framework as well as workgroup research, we hypothesize that the security behavior of an employee’s IW supervisor and coworkers moderated by the nature of these relationships influences information security decisions. Our research, based on a sample of 217 full-time employees, reveals that the IW significantly affects security decisions, over and above the micro- and macrolevel factors. These effects are moderated by the nature of the relationship between employees and their IW supervisor (leader-member exchange) and coworkers (team-member exchange). A post hoc analysis shows that the mesolevel factors alone had the same explanatory power as the micro- and macrolevels combined. Our research suggests that future theory and research should include the IW and that organizations should share security responsibilities with line managers and help them understand their substantial impact on information security. Security training programs should ask employees about the behaviors of their IW supervisor and coworkers and, where needed, deliver anti-neutralization training to mitigate the effects of the IW’s noncompliance behaviors.

Keywords: Immediate Workgroup, Information Security Policies (ISPs), Social Learning and Social Structure, Team-Member Exchange, Leader-Member Exchange, Supervisor, Coworker, Multilevel

## 1 Introduction

Workgroups are ubiquitous in organizations today. Their impact on individuals is vast and goes beyond that of leaders (Bollmann & Krings, 2016, p. 185)

On average, a security breach costs \$3.86 million (Sloan & Warner, 2019). Since most security breaches can be traced back to employees not following good security practices (EY, 2020; Nead, 2021; PwC, 2020), employees have often been labeled the weakest link of information security (Esteves et al., 2017). Therefore, many organizations have implemented policies and programs to encourage employees to follow good security practices and comply with formal information security policies (ISPs). These include punishments and security education, training, and awareness (SETA) programs (Barlow et al., 2018; Kirova &

Baumöl, 2018). Despite these measures, employees’ ISP violations are not declining (PwC, 2021).<sup>1</sup>

Much IS security research has examined the antecedents of employees’ intentions to violate an ISP (Cram et al., 2019). One research stream takes a macro stance and incorporates organizational-level factors influencing employees’ security behavior (e.g., SETA programs and sanctions). For example, organizational SETA knowledge is a significant predictor of security behavior (Kirova & Baumöl, 2018), and organizational sanctions effectively reduce misuse (D’Arcy et al., 2009). Another research stream takes a micro stance and explores various individual-level factors affecting security behavior. For example, self-control has been identified as an important factor that drives security decision-making (Burns et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2015; Luo et al., 2020). Similarly, Big Five personality traits have been shown to influence security behavior (Gratian et al., 2018; Shropshire et al., 2015). A recent meta-analysis concluded that individual differences at the microlevel (e.g., personal ethics, attitudes, and beliefs) explain about 25% of the variance explained across studies. In contrast, organizational efforts at the macrolevel (e.g., security training, policies, and sanctions) account for about 15% (Cram et al., 2019).

In this paper, we argue that while these studies contribute considerably to our understanding of employees’ ISP violation behavior, we, as a research area, have tended to overlook the mesolevel effects of the employee’s workgroup despite its importance (Bollmann & Krings, 2016). There were insufficient mesolevel studies for the Cram et al. (2019) meta-analysis to assess the effects of the workgroup (IW). Thus, although we know that the IW exerts a strong influence on a wide array of employee behaviors (Bollmann & Krings, 2016), and therefore also likely plays a central role in information security, we have little understanding of the IW’s effects on information security.

Understanding the effects of the mesolevel is becoming increasingly critical because collaboration is more prominent in today’s organizations (Cross et al., 2016); employees spend about 50% more time collaborating with their coworkers than they did a few years ago (Boskamp, 2021; Dimovski, 2021). Given the increasing collaboration of employees, security behavior may be multilevel in nature, with mesolevel factors playing as important a role as macro- and microlevel factors (Hsu et al., 2015; Tsohou et al., 2015). The multilevel nature of security behavior is vital because “analyzing a single level in isolation would result in a distorted view of the situation and/or less informative action planning” (Tsohou et al., 2015, p. 52).

We draw upon the social structure and social learning (SSSL) model (Akers, 2017), which argues that individual deviant behaviors are a function of social environments and immediate situations conducive to deviance (a theoretically different effect than subjective norms (Johnston et al., 2019; Yoo et al., 2020); for additional discussion, see Appendix A). We focus on the immediate workgroup—i.e., the relatively stable small group of coworkers and the supervisor with whom an employee spends much of their time. Three distinctions separate the effects of subjective norms, defined as what important others think one should do (e.g., Herath & Rao, 2009; Yazdanmehr et al., 2020) from our focus on the IW. First, the reference group shifts to the employee’s immediate coworkers and supervisor (hereafter, supervisor/coworker) rather than “important others” who can be anybody in the organization (Bamberger & Biron, 2007; Fugas et al., 2011). Second, our focus is on observed behavior, not what others espouse (i.e., “what one should do”). Third, we use LMX theory and TMX theory to consider how the relationships with the IW supervisor and coworkers affect the relative strengths of this relationship.

Security is a multilevel issue (Tsohou et al., 2015); thus, we examine the influence of the IW after controlling for important macro- and microlevel factors, subjective norms, and other workgroup predictors from past research (collective security efficacy, security knowledge coordination). Including these factors ensures the robustness of our research findings and permits us to assess the relative importance of each. As a contextual understanding of how the behavior of the IW influences the security decisions of the focal employee is poorly understood, we ask the following research questions:

RQ1: To what extent do the security behaviors of an employee’s immediate supervisor and immediate coworkers influence the employee’s security decisions?

RQ2: To what extent do an employee’s relationships with the immediate supervisor and immediate coworkers influence the strength of these effects?

We used two waves of data collection from CloudResearch with a sample of 217 full-time working professionals in the United States who work in workgroups, use computers in their daily work, and have workplace ISPs. We tested our model using hierarchical linear modeling, examining five frequently occurring security situations (failing to log out, sharing passwords, writing down passwords, taking data offsite, and disabling security settings). We found that microand macrolevel factors together predicted security decisions with a large effect size. The addition of the mesolevel IW behaviors increased the model’s explanatory power by between a medium and large effect size. The IW supervisor’s security behaviors influenced the employee’s security decisions, and the strength of LMX increased this effect. The employee’s security violation intention was also influenced by IW coworkers’ security behaviors. This effect was increased by the strength of TMX receipts (the extent to which the employee received support from coworkers). Interestingly, TMX receipts reduced the supervisor’s influence, but the supervisor’s influence did not reduce coworkers’ influence.

The results show that over and above micro- and macrolevel factors, employee security decisions are directly influenced by mesolevel IW behaviors. These effects are strengthened as the relationships with the supervisor and coworkers become stronger. A post hoc analysis shows that the mesolevel factors alone had the same explanatory power as the micro- and macrolevels combined. To paraphrase O’Neill & Hymel (1994), it may be that all security is local—or at least much of it.

This paper makes five contributions. First, it contributes to the emerging IS security research on workgroups by showing that the IW supervisor’s and IW coworkers’ security behaviors have a medium to large effect on an employee’s intention to violate an ISP, an effect that is as strong as microlevel factors (e.g., personality traits) and macrolevel factors (e.g., deterrence policies) combined. Second, it shows that the effects of coworkers’ and supervisors’ behavior are different from subjective norms. This is an important contribution because the effect of social norms is well established in the ISP literature. Still, we show that the influence of IW members’ behavior has a significant influence beyond social influence. Third, it introduces LMX and TMX as important concepts for IS security, providing fresh insights into understanding the mechanisms by which IW relationships influence security. Fourth, it provides insights for managers by suggesting that information security could be improved by making it partly a line management responsibility that is included in the annual performance evaluations of workgroup supervisors. Holding supervisors accountable for the security behavior of their subordinates offers a new tool in the information security arsenal. Finally, it provides insights for security software providers (e.g., KnowBe4 and Proofpoint) by suggesting that they should tailor their training modules to identify and reverse the effects of poor IW security practices. For example, such training could ask questions about IW coworkers’ and supervisors’ security compliance. If an IW has poor security compliance behaviors, anti-neutralization techniques (Barlow et al., 2018) could be used to mitigate the effects of the IW’s noncompliance.

## 2 Prior Theory and Research

Prior IS security research has most often focused on two categories of antecedents that might play a role in predicting employee security outcomes: macrolevel factors (i.e., those the organization can directly influence) and microlevel factors (i.e., those about the individual) (Cram et al., 2019). Prior research has also considered mesolevel factors associated with the employee’s IW (Yoo et al., 2020), but this is still an underexplored topic.

Organizations often have a distinct information security culture in the same way they have an organizational culture (van Niekerk & von Solms, 2010). Organizational security culture includes artifacts (e.g., an ISP), espoused values (e.g., the reasons for the ISP), and shared tacit assumptions (e.g., values and beliefs about what matters) (van Niekerk & von Solms, 2010). Such a security culture may be uniform across an organization, or different parts of the organization may have very different assumptions and values that lead to various security cultures (da Veiga & Eloff, 2010). Likewise, individual employees may adopt the values and assumptions that are central to the organizational security culture or may eschew them and develop their own security values and assumptions (da Veiga & Eloff, 2010). Thus, information security is a multilevel phenomenon that can be influenced by macro-, meso-, and microlevel factors.

We focus on the less-studied mesolevel factors associated with the employee’s IW. Researchers have begun conceptualizing ISP violation as a group phenomenon. Guo et al. (2011) surveyed 335 office workers about nonmalicious security violations and found that workgroup norms (the expected approval or disapproval of coworkers) significantly influenced the extent to which participants intended to engage in nonmalicious security violations. Tsohou et al. (2015) conducted an action research study that held nine meetings to bring together all managers of a public sector organization in Greece to design a security awareness program (e.g., emails, leaflets, posters, website, newsletter). But the senior manager who launched the project refused to approve it for four months. A deeper analysis showed that organizational norms had evolved so that security violations were accepted as normal, and there were no compliance monitoring mechanisms or penalties for violating the ISP.

Hsu et al. (2015) created a formative construct of social control (comprised of attachment to one’s workgroup members, commitment to the organization, involvement with the organization, and the belief that behavior leads to outcomes). They studied 78 workgroups in various companies in Taiwan and found that greater social control led to more in-role security behaviors (complying with an ISP) and extra-role behaviors (altruistic behaviors not part of the ISP), even after controlling for formal controls (i.e., the macrolevel of the ISP and its rewards).

Johnston et al. (2019) used a single-group case study to show that collective security efficacy, a group’s collective understanding of its ability to recognize and react to information security incidents that align with ISPs, is essential for understanding information security. Yoo et al. (2020) extended this work, finding that security knowledge coordination within the workgroup (i.e., knowing who knows what, knowledge sharing, discussion about knowledge) improved its collective security efficacy and, together, security knowledge coordination and collective security efficacy led to better security effectiveness. Further, Yazdanmehr and Wang (2023) theorized peer monitoring in groups and demonstrated that monitoring of group members can inhibit one’s intention to violate ISPs.

In sum, these studies suggest that the IW plays a role in employees’ security decisions. They also indicate that attitudes and behaviors of the IW surrounding the employee can reinforce the organizational ISP (Hsu et al., 2015; Johnston et al., 2019; Yoo et al., 2020) or undermine it (Guo et al., 2011; Tsohou et al., 2015).

This study examines all three levels together (micro, meso, and macro). We focus on the mesolevel, specifically an individual’s IW supervisor and coworkers, while including microlevel and macrolevel factors as controls.

We utilized the social structure and social learning (SSSL) model of crime and deviance (Akers, 2017) to understand how IW supervisor and coworkers influence individuals’ security behaviors. SSSL, an extension of social learning theory (Akers, 1977), is a general theory of deviance that focuses on one’s intermediate social context and how deviant behaviors learned from it affect behavior (Akers, 2017). SSSL posits that individuals acquire, repeat, and maintain deviant behaviors by observing others in their typical social environment (Akers, 2017). Akers (2017) stresses that the individual’s workgroup is the primary source of deviant behaviors in organizational contexts because workgroups provide an intermediate social context in which social learning mechanisms operate.

Lowry et al. (2016) were the first to use SSSL to explain deviant cyberbehaviors. In a study of cyberbullying and cyberharassment, they found that key elements of SSSL influenced cyberbullying frequency (differential association, differential reinforcement [negative but not positive], and both positive and negative definitions); we explain these terms below. We theorize that SSSL can also be applied to other deviant cyberbehaviors, such as the deliberate decision to violate an ISP.

SSSL highlights four dimensions in the social learning process: (1) differential association (i.e., direct association and interaction with others who perform certain policy-breaking behaviors); (2) definitions (i.e., one’s interpretation of performing certain policy-breaking behaviors as acceptable, appropriate, or desirable), (3) differential reinforcement (i.e., the balance of rewards and punishments from performing certain policy-breaking behaviors); and (4) imitation (i.e., committing certain policy-breaking behaviors after observing coworkers’ similar actions) (Akers, 1977). We examine these four dimensions in the context of information security.

Differential associations occur first. Observed behaviors that occur regularly and involve close interactions with others will have a stronger impact on individuals who are more likely to create associations (Akers, 2017). In other words, the more frequently an employee is exposed to policy-breaking behaviors, the more likely it is that the employee will create an association between the situation and the behavior. Repeated exposure to policy-breaking behaviors shifts individuals’ definition of appropriate behavior (Akers, 2017). For example, if the acceptable use policy is to log out of your computer when leaving your desk but you observe that none of your coworkers do this, you begin to associate leaving your desk with not logging out.

Second, some of the associations that employees make based on their observations are strong enough to become definitions (Akers, 2017). For example, your association of leaving your desk with not logging out gradually shifts the definition of acceptable use to match what you observe your coworkers doing. Employees’ definitions make certain policy-breaking behaviors more permissible in a given situation. For example, an employee may define “do not share your password” as a rule used only for “outsiders,” while sharing a password with a supervisor or coworker within their workgroup is considered acceptable behavior.

Third, differential reinforcement relies on a balance of rewards and punishments for performing policybreaking behaviors (Akers, 2017). Individuals observe their supervisor and coworkers balancing the rewards and punishments associated with engaging in deviant behaviors. Behaviors that are performed frequently and rewarded frequently become salient even if the rewards are small. For example, leaving your desk without logging out brings a small reward in effort saved by not having to log in each time you return. If the pattern of rewards outweighs the punishments, policy-breaking behaviors become viewed as appropriate.

These three factors (differential association, definitions, and differential reinforcement) lead to imitation behavior. Imitation is partly vicarious reinforcement (Akers, 2017); individuals copy behaviors for which they see others being rewarded.

Individuals are likely to continue the behavior to the extent that it is reinforced (a balance of rewards over punishment) with both direct rewards (i.e., the individual gains a benefit) and social rewards within the workgroup (e.g., social status, becoming a member of the in-group). Both types of rewards are effective, but social rewards are often stronger (Akers, 2017).

We draw upon SSSL to examine how the intermediate social environment—an IW consisting of one’s supervisor and coworkers—exerts influence on one’s security behaviors. SSSL highlights the role of interaction in the intermediate social context and why the workgroup effects become stronger or weaker. It proposes that structural conditions “can affect one’s exposure to criminal associations, models, definitions, and reinforcement to induce or retard criminal actions in individuals” (Akers et al., 2017, p. 94). As such, SSSL is more inclusive than the social learning theory and better fits our research question.

## 3 Hypothesis Development

Our study examines the extent to which mesolevel factors influence employees’ security decisions in the presence of macro- and microlevel factors. Put simply, does the inclusion of mesolevel factors help us better understand and explain security decisions over and above micro- and macrolevel factors? Figure 1 presents our research model.

Our focus is on the effect of the IW supervisor’s and coworkers’ behaviors on employees’ security decisions, as moderated by the social structure of the IW (LMX and TMX). We include mesolevel factors that have been found to be important in prior research, but these are controls for our study: subjective norms, workgroup collective efficacy, and security knowledge coordination (Guo et al., 2011; Hsu et al., 2015; Tsohou et al., 2015; Yoo et al., 2020). We begin with a brief overview of the well-understood microlevel (Big Five personality factors—self-control, security self-efficacy, and demographics) and macrolevel factors (SETA, sanctions, and industry type). We only briefly review the research here because these effects are well understood, and they serve only as controls in our study.

## 3.1 Macrolevel Predictors of ISP Compliance/Noncompliance

One approach that organizations use to manage cyberrisks is establishing, enacting, and enforcing ISPs and providing employees with the needed knowledge to comply with ISPs and sanctions to promote compliance (Chen et al., 2012). To accomplish this, organizations often design SETA programs to increase employees’ awareness of and ability to comply with

ISPs by improving their general knowledge of desirable security practices and their specific knowledge of their responsibilities as prescribed by the organization (Bulgurcu et al., 2010). These efforts often increase employees’ compliance with ISPs (Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Xu et al., 2021).

Sanctions for noncompliance may include mechanisms such as reprimands, fines, dismissals, and incarcerations. IS security research shows that sanctions effectively promote employees’ intentions to comply with ISPs (Chatterjee et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2022; D’Arcy et al., 2009). However, the effectiveness of sanctions depends on the combined effects of punishment severity and detection certainty (Gibbs, 1975). If either is low, then increasing the other will have limited effects because either the penalties are too low to be meaningful or the chances of being caught make the penalties less salient (Chen et al., 2012).

In addition, an institution-wide work climate on ethical dimensions may play a role in ISP compliance/ noncompliance. For example, Gwebu et al. (2020) introduced the ethical work climate to ISP literature. This marcrolevel factor captures “the prevailing perception of typical organizational policies, practices, and procedures that have ethical content” (Barnett & Schubert, 2002, p. 280). An organization’s ethical work climate may follow or deviate from ISPs. When an organization’s ethical work climate aligns with ISPs, the ethical work climate can add an extra layer of security to employees’ security decisions. Gwebu et al. (2020) found that an ethical work climate predicts ISP noncompliance.

## 3.2 Microlevel Predictors of ISP Compliance/Noncompliance

Another approach to ensuring ISP compliance is to employ the right people (Cram et al., 2019). In IS security, this line of research is rooted in the belief that personality traits predict “what a person will do in a given situation” (p. 2) and that deviant behaviors are a symptom of an underlying personality problem (Cattell, 1950).

Thus far, IS security research drawing on personality traits has identified the Big Five traits, self-control, and self-efficacy as salient factors influencing ISP compliance (see Appendix B). The Big Five traits are agreeableness, conscientiousness, extraversion, neuroticism/emotional stability, and openness to experience (Barrick & Mount, 1991). Agreeableness reflects one’s interpersonal orientation, and agreeable people are often described as likable, friendly, and cooperative (Barrick et al., 2001). Conscientiousness reflects one’s job task-completion orientation (Barrick et al., 2001), and conscientious people are often described as responsible, diligent, and persistent.

Extraversion reflects the nature of interpersonal interaction (Bateman & Crant, 1993); extroverts are often described as sociable, proactive, and energetic. Neuroticism reflects how emotionally unstable a person is, and neurotic people are often described as nervous, anxious, and emotional (McCrae & Costa, 1987). Openness reflects one’s tendency to be open to new experiences, and high-openness people are often described as curious, creative, and adventurous (Costa Jr. & McCrae, 1992).

In IS security research, both conscientiousness and agreeableness have been linked to better security behaviors. For example, Shropshire et al. (2015) found that conscientiousness and agreeableness enhance the link between adopting and using information security software. Likewise, Johnston et al. (2016) observed that employees with high stability (high consciousness, high agreeableness, and low neuroticism) or low plasticity (low extraversion and low openness) are less likely to commit ISP violations. The other three Big Five traits (extraversion, neuroticism, and openness) showed inconsistent effects. Pattinson et al. (2015) and Shropshire and Gowan (2017) found extraversion to have no impact on security decisions. In contrast, Kajzer et al. (2014) and Gratian et al. (2018) found extraversion to influence security behavior positively in only 20-25% of situations. Alohali et al. (2018) found neuroticism to have a negative effect, whereas Gratian et al. (2018) and Pattinson et al. (2015) found neuroticism to have no effect. Kajzer et al. (2014) and Shropshire and Gowan (2017) linked openness to worse security decisions, whereas Pattinson et al. (2015) linked it to better security decisions and Gratian et al. (2018) found it to have no effect.

In addition to the Big Five traits, IS security research has examined the role of self-control and self-efficacy. Selfcontrol is one’s ability to limit the tendency to engage in deviant behaviors (Gottfredson & Hirschi, 1990). Like the Big Five traits, self-control is formed at an early age and remains relatively constant throughout life (Gottfredson & Hirschi, 1990). Low self-control has been linked to deviant behaviors (Vazsonyi et al., 2001). In IS security research, Moon et al. (2010) found strong support for the positive relationship between low selfcontrol and computer crime (e.g., downloading illegal software). Hu et al. (2015) found that people with low self-control make faster and riskier security-related decisions than those with high self-control. Burns et al. (2022) showed that self-control has a direct and moderating impact on insider computer abuse.

Self-efficacy is the self-perception that one can perform security behaviors (Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2021). While self-efficacy and self-control capture one’s responses to challenges (Mukhopadhyay & Johar, 2005), they are theoretically different. Selfcontrol is often linked to unwanted thought suppression and diversion (Baumeister, 2002), whereas self-efficacy is often linked to goal achievement (Mukhopadhyay & Johar, 2005). In the IS security context, self-control permits an active control process that reduces deviant security behavior. In contrast, self-efficacy increases one’s expenditure and persistence toward goals (Locke & Latham, 2002), which helps enhance ISP compliance. For example, Bulgurcu et al. (2010) and Xu et al. (2021) observed that employees with high self-efficacy tend to have a higher intention to comply with ISP. Abraham and Chengalur-Smith (2019) found that self-efficacy is positively related to employees’ intentions to comply with security best practices. Chen et al. (2021) showed that high ISP compliance efficacy contributes to increasing ISP compliance intentions and decreasing ISP noncompliance intentions.

## 3.3 Mesolevel Predictors of ISP Compliance/Noncompliance

Mesolevel factors are those between the micro- and macrolevels and are often considered as either coming from professional groups such as those for physicians, nurses, and hospital staff (Sarkar et al., 2020), or from individuals’ workgroups within the larger organizational unit (Luciano et al., 2015; Mathieu & Chen, 2011). Past security research theorized professional groups as a mesolevel factor. Sarkar et al. (2020) showed that ISP noncompliance varies widely among professional groups because of the disparity in power, prestige and multitasking. This suggests that one’s profession needs to be considered in dealing with ISP noncompliance. However, this study does not show the relative impact among mesolevel factors and among meso-, micro-, and macrolevel factors. In addition to theorizing mesolevel factors by profession, past security research on mesolevel factors has focused on workgroup collective efficacy and security knowledge coordination (Johnston et al., 2019; Yoo et al., 2020). Another important mesolevel factor that has been studied in past research is subjective norms (Guo et al., 2011; Herath & Rao, 2009). Subjective norms are what employees think should be done based on their perceptions of what others across the organization think security compliance ought to be.

In contrast with prior research focusing on the effects of what others think (i.e., subjective norms) and the IW’s capabilities (i.e., workgroup collective efficacy) and coordination (i.e., security knowledge coordination), we theorize on how the observed behaviors of an individual’s immediate supervisor and coworkers in their IW influence the individual’s security decisions. Therefore, we include these three constructs (subjective norms, workgroup collective efficacy, and security knowledge coordination) as control variables.

Coworkers’ security behavior: The immediate environment regarding information security within an individual’s IW may be aligned with the information security policies promulgated by the larger organization (i.e., the macrolevel). The individual’s supervisor and coworkers may have internalized the behaviors espoused by the organization’s policies and may routinely enact them (Cialdini & Goldstein, 2004). Conversely, the IW may have developed a set of norms that subverts the organization’s espoused norms, meaning that the behaviors enacted by the workgroup do not align with the espoused norms of the organization (Abrams et al., 2000). Many examples exist of workgroups diverging from organization norms: political deviance, illegal corporate behavior, lying, noncompliant behavior, or creative disobedience (Warren, 2003).

To understand the role an IW plays in employees’ ISP violation intentions, we turn to SSSL (Akers, 2017; Akers et al., 2017). We focus on the IW (supervisor and coworkers) as it is the primary source of social learning in organizations (Akers, 2017). Applying SSSL to the information security context, differential association (supervisor and coworkers’ security violation behaviors) serves as the source of learning policyviolation behaviors. Definitions and differential reinforcement (interactions with coworkers and supervisor) serve as the cognitive and behavioral source for either enhancing or inhibiting policy-violation behaviors. Imitation (violation intention) serves as the outcome of the social learning process. Having witnessed that ISP violations of coworkers are rarely punished, one would feel these violations are morally acceptable within one’s IW and thus would be more likely to imitate coworkers’ ISP violation behaviors. We use SSSL as our theoretical foundation, but unlike Lowry et al. (2016), we do not test the relative effects of its individual components (e.g., differential association vs. differential reinforcement). We theorize that the security behaviors of the coworkers in an employee’s IW will directly influence the employee’s security decisions. Policy-following behaviors engender ISP compliance; policy-breaking behaviors engender ISP violations. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1a: The observed security violation behavior of an employee’s IW coworkers has a direct positive effect on the employee’s intention to violate the ISP.

Deviant behaviors are often learned by interacting with other people in the process of communication (Akers et al., 2017); thus, the nature of the relationship between employees and their immediate coworkers may also influence the extent to which behaviors have an effect. Prior research has studied this relationship among coworkers in the same workgroup and defined TMX. TMX refers to an employee’s perceived quality of social exchange with coworkers (Seers, 1989). It reflects an employee’s “willingness to assist other members, to share ideas and feedback and, in turn, how readily information, help, and recognition are received from other members” (Seers, 1989, p. 119). Higher TMX indicates a higher perceived quality of bilateral resource exchange between the focal employee and coworkers in the form of interpersonal helping and information sharing, thereby signifying “a heightened sense of mutual commitment, obligation, and trust” (Farh et al., 2017, p. 1117).

TMX varies not only in strength but in direction as an employee receives support from coworkers (TMX receipts) and contributes support to coworkers (TMX contributions) (Ford & Seers, 2006; Seers et al., 2006). TMX receipts might be more important than TMX contributions in influencing behavior (Ford & Seers, 2006) because when an employee receives interpersonal support and help from coworkers, the employee feels more connected, committed, and attached to coworkers (Farh et al., 2017; Pollack, 2009). Also, receiving IW support creates a strong sense of group ownership, whereas giving IW support may create the perception that some IW members are free riders (Ford & Seers, 2006). In other words, those who experience TMX receipts act differently from those who experience TMX contributions (Ford & Seers, 2006), as the former would be more influenced by IW behavior. We focus on TMX receipts, but for completeness, we include TMX contributions in our research model.

Akers (2013) notes that the social learning of policybreaking behaviors is a function of “social exchange in which the words, responses, presence, and behavior of other persons directly reinforce behavior, providing for reinforcement, or serve as the conduit through which other social rewards and punishers are delivered or made available” (p. 67). Extending this to the security context, high TMX creates a positive atmosphere where employees support each other’s decisions and actions (Akers, 2013) and are more likely to adopt similar ISP violation behaviors of coworkers in the IW (Akers, 2017). In contrast, when TMX is low (e.g., distant or troubled relationships with coworkers), coworkers’ ISP violation behavior may not sufficiently influence employees’ behavior because they are either less connected and attached to the IW or have fewer opportunities to observe coworkers’ behavior (Akers, 2017).

This suggests that the stronger the social exchange among individuals in the IW is, the stronger the effects of social learning will be and thus the greater influence their ISP violation behavior will have. Employees who routinely receive TMX support are more likely to be influenced by others in the group because their help and support are important to the employee (Farh et al.,

2017; Pollack, 2009). In other words, TMX receipts will moderate the extent to which employees will copy the ISP violation behaviors of coworkers in the IW. The extent of TMX contributions is likely to have little moderating influence because those who contribute are no more likely to be influenced by those to whom they provide support. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1b: TMX receipts moderate the relationship in H1a, such that as TMX receipts increase, the effect of observed IW coworker security violation behavior has a stronger influence on the intention to violate the ISP.

Supervisor’s security behavior: The direct supervisor of an employee is a special member of the IW. Power and status make lower-level employees pay more attention to the higher-level supervisor’s behavior (Magee & Galinsky, 2008). In the workplace, a supervisor serves as a role model for employees (Weiss, 1977). Witnessing a role model engage in deviant behavior lessens the observer’s inhibitions to behave similarly (O’Leary-Kelly et al., 1996). An ISPviolating supervisor conveys a message that violating the ISP is not a problem in the IW, creating local security norms that are permissive of employees engaging in similar ISP violations. Further, employees who directly resist imitating a supervisor’s ISP violation behaviors (e.g., password sharing) may worry about being punished by their supervisor. To be perceived as a good follower, one might explicitly violate the ISP as a way to get along well with the supervisor (Schyns & Schilling, 2013). Thus:

H2a: The observed security violation behavior of the IW supervisor has a direct positive effect on employees’ intention to violate the ISP.

According to SSSL (Akers et al., 2017), the relationship between employees and their IW supervisor may also influence how behaviors influence the intention to violate the ISP. The nature and quality of the employeesupervisor relationship are captured through the leadermember exchange (LMX) (Graen & Uhl-Bien, 1995). The central premise of the LMX is that leaders establish an active, transactional relationship with their subordinates rather than subordinates being only passive recipients of leadership (Anand et al., 2018; Dansereau et al., 1975; Hollander, 1980). LMX is one of the most influential theories that explain how the leadersubordinate relationship influences outcomes such as attitude, interpersonal behavior, and job performance (Yu et al., 2018).

LMX posits that employees develop relationships of different qualities with their supervisors. With highquality LMX, employees enjoy high-level support and rewards from their supervisors and perceive a sense of obligation toward them; with low-quality LMX, individuals receive less support and encouragement from their supervisors (Graen & Uhl-Bien, 1995). LMX research suggests that when LMX is high, individuals feel obliged to follow their supervisor’s behavior because doing so is perceived as helpful and supportive (Graen & Uhl-Bien, 1995). Further, high-quality LMX is considered to be a resource gain (i.e., a gain of social support and safety), whereas low-quality LMX is viewed as a resource loss (Griep et al., 2016).

In the context of information security, LMX can buffer the feeling of unease when witnessing a supervisor’s ISP violation behavior. Indeed, one may feel safer violating the ISP with the supervisor’s social support than not violating it and potentially jeopardizing this support. We argue that LMX serves as a moderator (a high-quality LMX relationship acts as an intensifier), such that LMX moderates the impact of the IW supervisor’s ISP violation on the employee’s intention to violate the ISP. In particular, the positive relationship between the IW supervisor’s security violation behavior and the employee’s intention to violate it is strengthened when LMX is high. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H2b: LMX moderates the relationship in H2a, such that as LMX increases, the observed IW supervisor security violation behavior has a stronger influence on the intention to violate the ISP.

## 4 Methodology

## 4.1 Scenarios

Scenarios are commonly used in ISP research (Cram et al., 2019) because the sensitive nature of information security (and ISP violations in particular) makes it challenging to collect accurate answers to direct questions about deviant behavior (Barlow et al., 2018; Crossler et al., 2013; Siponen & Vance, 2014). Scenarios can help disassociate the participant from socially undesirable behavior (Aguinis & Bradley, 2014; Applegate et al., 2000) because they allow participants to respond about how a character in the scenario behaved, thus reducing personal incrimination (Crossler et al., 2013) and social desirability bias (Krumpal, 2013). The analysis by Cram et al. (2019) shows little difference between intentions to comply and self-reported compliance, other than that the effects of the response cost are weaker for intentions (see Section 2 in Table 8, p. 541), a pattern that has been widely observed for the use of scenarios in other contexts (Aguinis & Bradley, 2014; Shaw et al., 2003). Thus, we opted for a scenario-based approach.

We used five short scenarios, each designed to examine a different type of common information security violation behavior studied in prior research: workstation logout, password sharing, writing down passwords, disabling security configuration, and taking data home on a USB. See Appendix C4 for the scenarios. To ensure that the security violations in the scenarios were still common and relevant, we asked participants to rank bad information security practices at the beginning of the survey. The five violations were ranked as the top five violations, providing evidence that the behaviors were relevant for our participants (see Appendix C, Table C2).

Each scenario presented a main character who engaged in a specific information security violation. The characters were given gender-neutral names (e.g., Taylor). The specific violations the characters performed were: taking sensitive data home on an unencrypted USB drive, routinely failing to log out when leaving their work computer, writing down passwords, sharing organizational passwords with coworkers, and disabling the browser’s enhanced security.

## 4.2 Data Collection, Sample, and Procedures

To address common method bias (CMB), we employed a two-part data collection strategy with a temporal separation of the measurement of explanatory and dependent variables (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Data were collected in two waves at 10-day intervals because prior research suggests that a one-week separation is usually sufficient to alleviate CMB (Castille et al., 2017; Klotz et al., 2018; Yazdanmehr & Wang, 2023). All data were collected pre-COVID-19. Independent variables (e.g., IW supervisor’s and coworkers’ ISP violation behavior) were collected at Time 1, and study outcomes (e.g., ISP violation intentions) were collected at Time 2.

We recruited participants using CloudResearch, <sup>2</sup> a platform designed to collect longitudinal data from working professionals with diverse backgrounds. We enlisted full-time US employees who were 22 to 65 years old and who had completed at least 100 tasks on CloudResearch, maintaining a ≥ 90% approval rating. Respondents were paid at the end of each wave—\$2.50 for Wave 1 and \$1.50 for Wave 2. Each wave was available to respondents for seven days, and respondents completed all survey items online.

Qualified respondents also met the following three ISPrelated criteria: (1) used a computer daily for work, (2) worked with a group or team of colleagues, and (3) their organization had a formal required ISP. After selecting the initial participants, we followed the approach of Bulgurcu et al. (2010) by excluding respondents who were unaware of the ISP in their organizations. Lastly, we removed respondents whose organizations did not have ISPs matching the scenarios used in the study as well as respondents who did not consider the five practices in the scenarios to be bad (this was assessed via screening questions at the start of the study). A total of 304 respondents met our criteria and participated in the initial wave of the survey. We removed 56 (18%) participants who failed one or both of the two attention check questions to ensure data quality. Studies of attention checks show that typical failure rates range from 14%-19.5%, depending on the type of attention check (Abbey & Meloy, 2017). Directed query attention checks like those we used (e.g., please choose strongly agree) had a failure rate of 14% per question in Abbey and Meloy’s studies. So, our failure rate of 18% over two questions is not unusual (Abbey & Meloy, 2017). This resulted in 248 usable participants, who were invited to complete Wave 2. While 244 respondents (98%) participated in Wave 2, only 217 (89%) completed the study. The detailed demographic characteristics of our respondents who completed the study are in Appendix D.

In Wave 1, participants assessed the frequency with which their IW supervisor and coworkers engaged in the ISP violation behaviors in our scenarios over the prior three months (workstation logout, password sharing, writing down passwords, disabling security configuration, and taking data home on a USB) and also answered questions about the macrolevel factors and personality traits (see Appendix E for the items). In Wave 2, participants received each of the scenarios in random order. They were instructed to read each scenario carefully and consider it in their organization’s context. Following each scenario, participants answered questions assessing the realism of the scenario and then reported how likely they would be to act in the same way as the characters if they were in the same situations (see Appendix C4).

## 4.3 Dependent Variable

The dependent variable was ISP violation intention. Following each scenario, participants were asked questions about their intention to engage in the same security violation undertaken by the character in the scenario. The ISP violation intention for each scenario was measured using two items adapted from prior research (e.g., D’Arcy et al., 2014; Siponen & Vance, 2010), with high scores indicating high-security violation intentions.

## 4.4 Mesolevel Independent and Control Variables

We asked participants to assess the frequency with which they had observed supervisor(s) and coworkers in their IW engaging in each of these security violation behaviors over the past three months using a 5-point scale ranging from $\mathrm { \bf ~ \cdots ~ } 1 = \mathrm { N e v e r } ^ { 3 }$ to “5 = Always.” Sample items included, “Please estimate the frequency that your coworkers in your workgroup were not logging off their work computers when they left their desk in the past three months.” As one may have worked for more than one IW in the past three months, the number of workgroups was a single-item question. We measured LMX with seven items from Graen and Uhl-Bien (1995) and Huang et al. (2017) and TMX contributions with six items, and TMX receipts with six items from Seers (1989; 2001).<sup>3</sup> We measured our mesolevel controls of workgroup collective efficacy with three items from Yoo et al. (2020), security knowledge coordination with five items from Yoo et al. (2020), and subjective norms using five items from Herath and Rao (2009).

## 4.5 Macrolevel Control Variables

Many prior studies examining deterrence take a policyas-perception approach, which theorizes that organizational policies are filtered by individual cognitive processes and are manifested in the eye of the individual (e.g., D’Arcy et al., 2009; Foth, 2016). Although policies exist at an organizational level, they are understood differently by different individuals. If employees are unaware of a policy, the policy does not influence their behavior. Therefore, macrolevel concepts, such as sanctions, are best understood by measuring individual perceptions (e.g., D’Arcy et al., 2009; Foth, 2016). The extent of sanctions is the joint effect of the severity and certainty of sanctions (Chen et al., 2012). Sanction severity (three items) and sanction certainty (two items) were adapted from Herath and Rao (2009). The sanction measure was formed by multiplying sanction severity and sanction certainty. We used six information security awareness items from Bulgurcu et al. (2010) to measure SETA knowledge. We included one item to measure whether one’s organization is from a regulated industry, adapted from Johnston et al. (2016) and Al-Ubaydli and McLaughlin (2017).

## 4.6 Microlevel Control Variables

We measured the Big Five traits following Johnston et al. (2016). The scale consists of five personality traits: extraversion (5 items), agreeableness (5 items), conscientiousness (5 items), neuroticism (5 items), and openness (8 items). Self-control was measured with 20 items adapted from Hu et al. (2015). The scale consists of five aspects of self-control: impulsivity, risk-taking, selfcenteredness, simple task, and temper. We reverse-coded the scales so that a high score represented high selfcontrol. We used six items from Yoo et al. (2020) to measure information security self-efficacy. We included gender, age, whether the individual had an IT job, and length of professional tenure, as measured by D’Arcy et al. (2014) and Yeh and Chang (2007).

## 4.7 Pilot Tests

We used previously validated scales and created a few new scales. We conducted two pilot studies to assess the relevance, realism, and content validity of the scenarios and the validity and reliability of the constructs. Both the pilot and main studies confirmed the realism of our scenarios (average realism score = 6.95 out of 10, SD = 2.28).<sup>4</sup> Based on the pilot study results and qualitative feedback from pilot test respondents, we refined the wording of the scenarios. We performed factor analysis using principal component extraction with varimax rotation on the data from both pilot studies. Items with loadings of less than 0.6 were reworded or replaced. The final items, as well as their correlations, loadings, and cross-loadings, are presented in Appendix F.

## 4.8 Convergent and Discriminant Validity

Scale reliability was assessed using composite reliability and Cronbach’s alpha scores, and all of them were greater than the recommended 0.70 (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994). All average variance extracted (AVE) values were greater than 0.50, and the square roots of AVE values (on the diagonal of the table in Appendix G that reports descriptive statistics and the correlations between constructs) were greater than the off-diagonal correlations, indicating adequate convergent and discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). We also performed heterotraitmonotrait ratio analysis of correlations (HTMT) (Henseler et al., 2015; Voorhees et al., 2016) to further evaluate discriminant validity. All HTMT values were well below the threshold of 0.85, indicating that sufficient discriminant validity was established among constructs (Henseler et al., 2015; see Table H1 in Appendix H). Thus, all the measures displayed appropriate reliability and validity. Before testing our hypotheses, we ensured that our operationalization of LMX and TMX was appropriate (see Appendix I).

## 5 Results

We analyzed the data using hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) because it had a hierarchical structure, with Level 1 being the security scenarios and Level 2 being the respondent (see Table 1). HLM is commonly used when there are multiple observations per subject (Dennis et al., 2020; Judd et al., 2017; Moravec et al., 2020; Moravec et al., 2019; Seymour et al., 2021). As is typical with HLM, we used centered variables with robust standard errors and built the model in stages to better understand the effects of different sets of constructs (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). See Appendix J for the formal model specification.

Model 1 includes the microlevel control factors. Selfcontrol significantly reduced violation intentions, as did conscientiousness. Self-efficacy was also significant, but its significance disappeared in later models when more constructs were added. It is possible to calculate R<sup>2</sup> at both Level 1 (the scenario) and Level 2 (the individual) (Snijders & Bosker, 2011). Our focus is on R<sup>2</sup> at Level 2 because we are more interested in the factors that explain differences in behavior between individuals than we are in factors that explain differences between scenarios. Level 2 R<sup>2</sup> was 32.7%, which Cohen (1988, p. 414) calls a large effect size.

Model 2 added the macrolevel control factors. Sanctions significantly reduced violation intentions, as did SETA knowledge. Level 2 R<sup>2</sup> significantly increased to 36.8%, a large effect size. The change in R<sup>2</sup> from Model 1, measured as f<sup>2</sup>, was 0.06, which Cohen (1988) calls small.

Model 3 added mesolevel factors from prior research. Subjective norms significantly reduced violation intentions, but workgroup collective efficacy and security knowledge coordination were not significant. Level 2 R<sup>2</sup> significantly increased to 39.9%, a large effect size. The change in R<sup>2</sup> from Model 2, measured as f <sup>2</sup>, was 0.05, which Cohen (1988) calls small. As a robustness check, we ran a separate model adding only workgroup collective efficacy and security knowledge coordination to Model 2; neither was significant, and the R<sup>2</sup> was the same.

Model 4 is the full model, adding the IW behavior constructs that are the focus of this study. Immediate coworkers’ behaviors had a significant direct effect on violation intentions, which was moderated by TMX receipts (receiving support from coworkers increased the effect of their behaviors).

## 5.1 Tests for Bias

Social desirability bias, where participants alter their responses to present themselves in a more positive light, is always a possibility in information security research (McCormac et al., 2017). We used a 13-item measure of social desirability adapted from (Reynolds, 1982) to see whether social desirability might have affected our results. We partialled out the effect of social desirability from the independent and dependent variables. The differences in the partial correlation between the explanatory and dependent variables were revealed to be insignificant.

We used both a priori and post hoc statistical remedies to alleviate CMB (Cram et al., 2019). First, we used a two-part data collection strategy to ensure a temporal separation between the explanatory and dependent measures, with an average of a 10-day time lag between each data collection. Measures collected at two different points in time allowed us to test our hypotheses from a single source in a way that minimized CMB threats (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Second, we ensured anonymity and reduced evaluation apprehension (e.g., told respondents in instructions that there were no right or wrong answers and to choose the option that best represented their immediate thoughts) (Podsakoff et al., 2012).

We used three post hoc statistical techniques to assess CMB risks. First, Harmon’s single-factor test (Podsakoff & Organ, 1986) revealed eight factors with no single factor having significant (p < 0.01) loading for all items or explaining large variance (20%, 8%, 6%, 5%, 4%, 3%, 3%, and 3%). Second, following Cenfetelli and Bassellier (2009), we checked all factor-level VIFs, which were less than the threshold of 3.3, and used SmartPLS to assess inner and outer VIFs, all of which were less than 10 (Hair et al., 1995) (see Tables H2 and H3). Third, we performed marker variable tests (Lindell & Whitney, 2001) using perceived ease of use (PEOU) (Venkatesh, 2000) as the marker variable. The marker variable test revealed a 2% maximum shared variance with the other variables in the model. All significant correlations remained significant after the inclusion of these marker variables. Taken together, we conclude that CMB was minimal in our study.

## 6 Discussion

This study examined two research questions that questioned the extent to which the behavior of an employee’s IW (i.e., immediate supervisor and coworkers) and the employee’s relationships with the members of the IW influence security decisions over and above the well-known microlevel (e.g., personal characteristics) and macrolevel factors (e.g., sanctions and SETA). The results showed that mesolevel IW behaviors and relationships had a significant, meaningful influence on employee ISP violation decisions over and above micro- and macrolevel factors. The total effect size was large, R<sup>2</sup> = 50.7%, and the effect size of adding the mesolevel factors in addition to the micro- and macrolevel factors was between medium and large. These mesolevel factors have a significant, meaningful influence on security decision-making in addition to the more well-studied micro- and macrolevel factors, thus answering our research questions that the mesolevel factors contribute a medium to a large extent to security decisions. The mesolevel factors explained as much variance as the microlevel and macrolevel factors combined, suggesting that the security behavior of an employee’s local IW is the most important influence on his or her security compliance. In other words, information security is largely a local issue.

We believe this paper makes two contributions to information security. First and foremost, it shows that the observed behaviors of supervisor and coworkers in an employee’s IW play a major role when assessing individual ISP violation decisions above and beyond what others think (i.e., subjective norms) and the IW’s capabilities and coordination. Employees bring individual traits (e.g., the Big Five traits, self-control, and self-efficacy) and are influenced by organizational ISPs and actions (e.g., SETA programs and sanctions), but they learn the enacted local security norms—what behaviors are acceptable and unacceptable—by observing the behavior of their IW. This learning occurs as individuals associate with their IW, revise their definitions of appropriate behavior, see reinforcement for behaviors, and then begin to imitate others in their workgroup (Akers, 2017). If an IW reinforces the organization’s ISP, then its members are more likely to comply. However, members are substantially less likely to comply if the IW refutes the ISP.

Second, we contribute to IS security research by applying the theories of LMX and TMX to understand how relationships within the IW weaken and strengthen IW effects. Regarding LMX, we found that employees’ relationship with their IW supervisor (LMX) and their supervisor’s behavior had a strong influence on security violations. Figure 2 shows that the behaviors of both the IW supervisor and coworkers influence security decisions. The general pattern suggests that the influence of the IW supervisor is somewhat stronger than the influence of IW coworkers. This is likely because the IW supervisor is the official and legitimate organizational agent and acts as an “ideal candidate for communicating and enforcing organizational goals to employees” (Chan et al., 2005, p. 24). Supervisors are often more concrete in role modeling, having more vivid, salient images than coworkers (Katz & Kahn, 1978). The IW supervisor has more power and status than coworkers, so the probability of formal or informal sanctions is higher when an employee does not follow the IW supervisor (Schyns & Schilling, 2013), especially when an employee is less attached to IW coworkers. Thus, we conclude that the employee-supervisor dyad is important and needs to become more central in security research and practice.

Similarly, we found that employees’ relationships with their immediate coworkers (TMX) and their behavior had a strong influence on security violations. Employees learn from their peers, especially about deviant behaviors (Akers, 1977). The behavior of immediate coworkers has even stronger effects when employees receive help and support from them, reducing the influence of the employee’s immediate supervisor. Strong workgroups build strong norms for both good and bad behavior. It may be that some of the idiosyncratic security behavior that Cram et al. (2019) attribute to individual differences may be due to different security norms that have been enacted by coworkers in the IW. Thus, we also conclude that IW coworkers’ effects are important in influencing security compliance and need to become more central in information security research and practice.

Overall, individual characteristics and organizational policies may influence security behaviors, but their IW supervisor and coworkers as strongly influence employees as they are by organizational policies or their individual differences. The relative impact of their IW supervisor and coworker behavior depends on LMX and TMX receipts, but often the behavior of the IW supervisor has a stronger effect. Thus, we advocate that researchers and managers should focus more attention on workgroups (supervisors and coworkers), both as a way to understand security violations and as a remedy to reduce violations.

## 6.1 Limitations

Before examining the implications for future research, we need to understand the limitations of this study. First, our sample was collected in the United States; therefore, the relationships found in this study may or may not transfer to other cultures. Individuals from different cultures may observe the same behaviors but draw different conclusions about what happened. Second, we measured self-reported intention rather than actual behavior. Still, the meta-analysis by Cram et al. (2019) found little material difference between intentions and self-reported behavior regarding information security outcomes. Third, individuals often belong to more than one workgroup, so we asked participants to select their primary workgroup and respond to the survey with that in mind. Thus, our study does not consider the effects of different workgroups with different security behaviors. Finally, we did not theorize or test that different security situations (e.g., scenarios) would change the relative effects of IW supervisor and coworker influence. It may be that some ISP violations are more or less visible to others (Moody et al., 2018), so the strength of IW influence may depend on the specific security situation.

## 6.2 Implications for Future Research

We believe that this study provides three implications for future research. First, our research shows that the IW (a mesolevel factor) matters to employee security behavior. Prior research has focused primarily on the impacts of micro- and macrolevel factors the metaanalysis by Cram et al. (2019) only includes these factors due to a paucity of research at the mesolevel. Our research indicates the importance of considering mesolevel factors in addition to the micro- and macrolevel factors. A disparity in ISP compliance/noncompliance has been observed by profession (e.g., Sarkar et al., 2020) and by workgroup membership (e.g., Johnston et al., 2019; Yoo et al., 2020). Future IS security research should continue to identify other mesolevel factors.

Second, our research suggests that information security is as strongly influenced by IW supervisors and coworkers as by organizational policies and individual characteristics. We need more research on these effects and a deeper understanding of the mechanisms by which they influence security behaviors. One important question for future research is the influence of remote work. About 45% of small businesses and 90% of large firms adopted a remote or hybrid work model in response to the COVID-19 pandemic (Bartik et al., 2020). Gartner (2021) forecasts that half of all knowledge workers will continue to work remotely post-pandemic. The massive shift in the work model (Faulds & Raju, 2021) is a factor that future research should consider because employees in hybrid or remote work models are less likely to observe deviant behavior unless it is deliberately displayed, which is less likely than positive behavior. However, SSSL explains that deviant behavior is redefined to be appropriate, so deviant behavior may be deliberately displayed. Thus, in a remote work era, the social learning that may occur from the IW may or may not be similar to social learning in face-to-face settings. This is an issue for future research.

Third, we also measured subjective norms—what others think you should do (e.g., Gwebu et al., 2020; Herath & Rao, 2009)—and consistent with D’Arcy and Lowry (2019), we found them to have a separate, distinct effect from the observed behaviors of IW supervisors and coworkers. Interestingly, the correlations between subjective norms and two IW behaviors were about 0.2, indicating they are neither totally unrelated nor genuinely related. The enacted local security norms, what others in the IW do, was different than espoused subjective norms, what important others think should be done. This raises three implications for future research. First, subjective norms include “important others” from beyond the IW. We need more research on the extent to which the locus of the norms matter, be it in the IW, elsewhere in the organization, or outside the organization. Second, the distinction between espoused and enacted security norms is central. We need more research separating these two and identifying the relative importance of each. Finally, subjective norms are different from the enacted norms in the IW, so research wanting to include control variables to capture the effects of others’ norms and behaviors need to look beyond subjective norms and include factors that include the behaviors of the IW.

We found that neither workgroup collective efficacy nor security knowledge coordination was significant, both of which were found to be significant in a prior study (Yoo et al., 2020). The respondents in Yoo et al. (2020) were drawn from South Korea, a country known for a more collectivist culture, whereas our sample was from the United States, a country known for a more individualistic culture (Dinev et al., 2009; Hofstede & Bond, 1988). Likewise, the Yoo et al. (2020) respondents were from a law enforcement agency, which also tends to have a more collectivistic culture (Filstad & Gottschalk, 2010). In contrast, our respondents were from a variety of organizations in different industries (see Table D1 in Appendix D). Thus, workgroup collective efficacy and security knowledge coordination may have more influence in countries and organizations with collectivist cultures than in those with individualistic cultures. This could be explored in future research.

## 6.3 Implications for Practice

Our research’s most important implication for practice is that the IW matters. Employees are influenced by the security behaviors they observe in their IW, over and above the influence of organization training, policies, sanctions, and the individual differences they bring. Policies, training, and sanctions are necessary, but it is also important to teach a good security social context at the local workgroup level because what the IW does influences information security far more than the organization’s ISP. Thus, information security is likely to differ from workgroup to workgroup.

A second implication is that sharing responsibility for information security with line managers should improve security. Information security should become part of the supervisor’s job responsibilities, acting as information security mentors to their subordinates in the same way as they act as mentors in their functional discipline (Feng et al., 2019). Security staff should develop special security training for supervisors, and annual merit reviews should include information security. Security incidents by subordinates should be part of supervisors’ annual reviews.

Third, security training programs could be tailored to local workgroups. Our study shows that poor compliance behavior is not uniformly or randomly distributed across an organization. Lack of compliance is often clustered within workgroups. An organization can have clusters of high-compliant workgroups and low-complying workgroups. Organizations should identify the pockets of noncompliance and target these workgroups with additional training rather than target individual employees. Enterprise security awareness training programs (e.g., KnowBe4, Proofpoint) are typically offered online, and employees receive the same standard set of training videos, quiz questions, or games. Instead of following this approach, security software providers (e.g., KnowBe4 and Proofpoint) should diversify their training module portfolio by tailoring training modules to identify and reverse effects the effects of poor IW security practices. For example, a training module could add questions to identify employees whose IW displays poor compliance and then use anti-neutralization techniques (Barlow et al., 2018) to mitigate the effects of the IW’s noncompliance behaviors.

## 7 Conclusion

This study extends our understanding of individuals’ ISP violations by examining the mesolevel and showing that the behaviors of the supervisor and coworkers in an employee’s immediate workgroup have significant, meaningful effects on security decisions above and beyond the micro- and macrolevel factors. In short, information security is local. Thus, our research suggests that security managers should consider the local information security norms within individual workgroups and design management interventions that target the local level.

## Appendix A: Distinguishing Between Immediate Workgroup Behavior and Subjective Norms in IS Security Research

Prior IS research has attempted to capture the effects of subjective norms on security behaviors (e.g., D’Arcy & Lowry, 2019; Herath & Rao, 2009). This research has traditionally been rooted in the well-established theory of planned behavior (Ajzen, 1991) and measures that focus jointly on what others think the focal individual should do. This is useful and has shown that subjective norms may or may not influence security behaviors (D’Arcy & Lowry, 2019; Herath & Rao, 2009).

Our focus differs in five theoretical ways from prior IS research. First, prior IS security research on subjective norms has focused theoretically and empirically on what behavior should be done. When it comes to behavior that has socially desirable implications, such as information security policy compliance/noncompliance, this focus on theorizing and asking about what others say the individual “should” do has the potential to distort. Our focus is on what immediate workgroup supervisor and coworkers “actually” do (i.e., the security behaviors they actually engage in) rather than what subjective norms say behavior should be.

Second, prior IS security research using subjective norms has not specified the boundary of others/colleagues/coworkers/peers and has thus typically comingled the beliefs of supervisors, top management, security professionals, etc. with the beliefs of coworkers (considering them all as important others) (e.g., see Herath and Rao, 2009). This makes theoretical and empirical sense when the beliefs of these different groups are similar. However, executives, senior managers, and close coworkers from other units can hold different beliefs than employees for behaviors that are socially desirable or mandated by organizational policies (Bamberger & Biron, 2007; Fugas et al., 2011). In this study, we focus exclusively on the immediate workgroup (an organizational subunit comprising a relatively permanent small group of coworkers with whom the focal individual employee spends the most time and space), including the immediate supervisor and immediate coworkers. By explicitly specifying the boundary of social context, we focus clearly on the enacted local security climate surrounding the employee. Meanwhile, by separating the two immediate sources of local influences, we were able to compare their relative influence on employees’ ISP violation intentions.

Third, Herath and Rao (2009) focused on ISP compliance where espoused beliefs are likely shared across people, whereas we focus on deviant behavior where beliefs may differ; research shows that factors affecting compliance are different than those affecting deviant behavior (c.f., Bulgurcu et al. 2010; D’Arcy et al. 2014).

Fourth, prior IS security research using subjective norms that asked about peers have not typically differentiated between close peers and distant peers. Herath and Rao (2009), for example, observed that descriptive norms (the perceived compliance of “other employees” with the ISP) were significant predictors of an employee’s intent to comply with an ISP. Individuals routinely interact with peers within their organization, both inside and outside their workgroup. When a climate is shared across different parts of an organization, this may be less important. However, the information security climate may differ in different parts of the organization as some may be more or less sensitive to attacks (e.g., research and development lab vs. procurement department). Therefore, we focus only on the immediate workgroup that is most likely to influence the employee.

Finally, our focus is on the enacted recent local climate, not general norms; thus, we examine specific security behaviors observed recently instead of general beliefs about security and compliance. In other words, we explicitly recognize that employees within an immediate workgroup may comply with some security policies described in an organization’s ISP while eschewing other demanded security policies. For example, they may regularly change their passwords but fail to log out when leaving their desk for coffee. Thus, in examining the enacted local security climate, it is important to focus on specific observed behaviors, not beliefs of security compliance/noncompliance in general terms. Likewise, since climate changes over time, it is important to focus on recent behavior rather than events from the distant past.

Collectively, we believe our conceptualization and operationalization of security behavior of the immediate workgroup offers a more conceptually clear and parsimonious approach to understanding one’s local security social context and thereby provides additional novel insights.

## Workstation Logout (adapted from Siponen and Vance 2010)

Taylor was recently hired as a mid-level manager in a medium-sized company. Taylor’s department uses an inventory procurement software application program to allow only authorized employees to make inventory purchases. The company has a firm policy that employees must log out of or lock their computer workstation when not using it. Taylor expects that keeping his user account logged-in could save him and co-workers time in ordering inventory.

## Sharing Passwords (adapted from Siponen and Vance 2010)

Taylor is a low-level manager in a small company where he was recently hired. His company has a strong policy that each computer workstation must be password protected and that passwords are not to be shared. However, Taylor is on a business trip and one of his co-workers needs a file on his computer. Taylor expects that sharing his password could save his co-worker a lot of time and effort.

## Writing down Passwords (D’Arcy et al., 2014)

Taylor is a low-level manager in a small company where she was recently hired. Her company recently installed a computer system for managing employee personal information (e.g., employee emergency contacts, retirement benefits, salary information). Each employee has been given a username and password for the system. Her company has a strong policy that employees must keep their passwords to themselves and not let other people know or use them. However, finding it difficult to remember the password, Taylor expects that writing down her username and password on a sticker and attached it to the computer she usually uses could save her time and energy.

## Disable Security Configuration (self-developed scenario and ranked the fifth most frequently occurring ISP violation behavior in our survey)

Taylor is a mid-level manager in a medium-sized company, has worked for several years. Her company has a strong policy that employees must keep the Internet Explorer Enhanced Security Configuration feature enabled to prevent cyber-attacks on company servers. With this feature enabled, some websites are blocked. To freely browse the websites without encountering this restriction and to save her time in searching suppliers, Taylor disabled the Internet Explorer Enhanced Security Configuration option on her work computer.

## Taking USB Drive Home (D’Arcy et al., 2009)

Taylor, a mid-level manager in a medium-sized business, has worked for several years. Taylor is currently working on a sales report that requires the analysis of the company’s customer database, which contains sensitive financial and purchase history information. Because of the sensitive nature of the corporate data, the company has a strict policy prohibiting the copy of corporate data to unencrypted media, such as USB drives. However, Taylor will travel for several days and would like to analyze the corporate database on the road. Taylor expects that copying the data to the USB drive and taking it on the road could save the company a lot of time and money.

## Appendix I: An Alternative Operationalization of LMX and TMX

Prior to running our main analysis, we considered an alternate operationalization of LMX and TMX. For example, considering that the construct of LMX is multidimensional in nature, we ran a factor analysis to see if these dimensions (trust, respect, and mutual obligation) were sufficient to qualify as a separate measure. As shown in Table I1, the first factor showed an eigenvalue of 4.778, indicating 47.78% of the variance in the seven items is explained by the first factor. The second factor showed an eigenvalue of 0.701, below the cutoff value of 1, as did the rest of the factors. This is consistent with the theorization and operationalization of seminal work by Graen and Uhl-Bien (1995) (p. 237) and more recent work by Huang et al. (2017). Further, Table I2 shows that Factor 1’s loadings are substantial, with all well above 0.79. This range is remarkable, as opposed to the conventional minimum required loading of 0.4. As such, Factor 1 gave us confidence that all seven items pointed to a single dimension. We opted to run the HLM using a single measure for LMX instead of three separate measures for each dimension.

## Appendix J: Formal Specification of HLM Model

The formal specification of our final model (Model 4 in Table 1) is given below. HLM is an iterative ordinary least squares technique (i.e., linear regression) that fits multiple simultaneous equations to estimate the coefficients $\gamma _ { \mathrm { i j } }$ There was one equation at Level 1 (scenario) and three at Level 2 (participant). The first equation estimates $\beta _ { 0 \mathrm { j } } ,$ which is the intercept for that participant (based on the micro-, macro- and meso-controls). The other two equations estimate $\beta _ { \mathrm { 1 j } }$ (the slope on coworker behavior for that participant) and $\beta _ { 2 \mathrm { j } }$ (the slope on supervisor behavior for that participant) because the slopes may be stronger or weaker for each participant depending on their relationship with their immediate coworkers (TMR and TMC) and supervisor (LMX). We used the random intercept and fixed slopes model, which means that each participant can have a different intercept $\gamma _ { _ { 0 0 } } ,$ but all other coefficients $\gamma _ { \mathrm { i j } }$ in the model are the same for all participants. Likewise, β<sub>3</sub>, $\beta _ { 4 , } \beta _ { 5 , }$ <sub>,</sub> and $\beta _ { 6 }$ are the same for all decisions. Because there are separate equations for $\beta _ { \mathrm { 1 j } }$ and $\beta _ { 2 \mathrm { j } , }$ these two terms are likely to be different for each participant depending upon the values of TMR, TMC and LMX for that individual, even though the $\gamma$ coefficients on TMR, TMC, and LMX will be the same for all participants.

As a robustness check, we also tested a random intercept and random slopes model in which $\gamma _ { 1 0 }$ and $\boldsymbol { \gamma } _ { 2 0 }$ were allowed to have different values for each participant. All the statistical conclusions were the same, and the model fit was no different.
