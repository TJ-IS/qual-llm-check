## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online auctions Feedback systems Online trust Empirical studies

## A B S T R A C T

Data from ebay.com online auctions were used to examine the impact of buyer feedback and other trustevoking variables on the final auction prices realized by sellers. Direct counts of positive and negative feedback were found to influence ending auction price significantly. However, when difference measures between positive and negative feedback were used as buyer feedback measures, no significant price effects were found. Since much more positive, than negative, feedback is obtained by sellers, the marginal impact of negative feedback is much greater than that of positive feedback. Therefore, subtracting negative feedback counts from positive feedback counts may not be appropriate. Support was also found for other factors, such as product warranties, impacting final auction prices.

## 1. Introduction

Online auction markets have created new channels through which buyers and sellers can transact goods and services. However, since buyers and sellers generally have no personal interaction in these markets, and since they often do not have any ongoing relationships, trust is a critical factor [2]. Since traditional trustbuilding mechanisms, such as state-enforced contractual guarantees, may be less effective in an online environment [4,11], it is necessary to understand how trust may be established in these markets. This was our primary research purpose.

One way to enhance trust in online markets is through the establishment of feedback systems that allows market participants to provide information on their online experience. Feedback systems are available in many online marketplaces, such as ebay.com and Yahoo Auctions. The effectiveness of these feedback systems has attracted a significant amount of research interest [e.g., 2,5,8], however, the conclusions have been mixed. Effective systems could be expected to result in higher ending auction prices for reputable sellers and lower ones for disreputable ones. However, ending auction prices may also be influenced by other factors, such as product quality, the availability of accessories, and the type of shipping. Also, the type of feedback system employed may be a determinant of its effectiveness. Here, we strictly controlled for product- and auction-specific factors that influence the ending auction prices in an effort to examine the effectiveness of a variety of feedback scores. Our results indicated that the effectiveness of the feedback system was strongly influenced by the type of feedback score employed, and that many of these product- and auction-specific factors had significant influence on final auction price.

Using the trust-building theories that have been widely adopted in IS, operations management, and marketing literatures, we categorized online factors into two trust-building processes: calculative and a capability based. The effects of both jointly established online trust. Then, using data from ebay.com, a hedonic price function [20] was estimated to assess the impact of the identified factors on ending auction prices.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses development

The buyer–seller relationship is fundamental to all commerce and much research has been dedicated to its study. However, unless a buyer can trust the seller to deliver the desired product or service, and unless a seller can trust the buyer to pay for it, establishing a successful marketplace will be difficult [22,23].

Researchers have distinguished between two general types of trust – credibility (the belief that the other party in a transaction is honest, reliable, and competent) and benevolence [11,16] (how interested a party is in its partner’s welfare, and how strong is the party’s intention to benefit its partner [1]). Five processes have been proposed to help develop one or both of these types of trust [6]: prediction, intentionality, transference, calculative, and capability. Two of these, the calculative and the capability processes, are particularly relevant to building trust in online markets. These were used as a basis in formulating our hypotheses.

The calculative process involves an estimation of the other party’s intentions by analyzing potential returns and costs from the relationship. If one party (usually the seller) has more information about a product than the other party (usually the buyer), then the buyer needs to ‘‘calculate’’ the likelihood that the seller will act in good faith; for example, by delivering a product of the agreed-upon quality. In a market with asymmetric information (such as the online market) [21], the buyer has less information about the seller’s capabilities, intentions, and products than the seller and may be unable to distinguish between reputable sellers of high quality products and those selling low quality products. As a result, the buyer will tend to underbid forhigh qualityproducts, and overbid for low quality products due to the inability to determine, a priori, product quality [12]. If buyers are unable to determine the intentions of sellers to deliver quality products, then there will be little or no differences in the prices paid to reputable or less reputable sellers [10]. However, if effective feedback systems are implemented in online markets, then buyers will be able to estimate the probability of satisfactory outcomes, and reputable sellers should realize higher prices then disreputable sellers.

The capability process refers to the ability of a party, to meet its obligations. With online markets, buyers and sellers generally are not personally acquainted. In many cases, they are not collocated, and the seller represents a small firm with no national reputation. However, information provided on online auctions, such as prior feedback, may be used by buyers to assess the capabilities of sellers to deliver products according to expectations. Again, with effective feedback systems, the predicted capabilities of a seller to deliver products should be reflected in ending auction prices in online markets.

The effectiveness of feedback systems in online marketplaces has attracted many researchers in fields, such as marketing, IS, and economics (e.g. [3,14,15,18]). Much of the research has examined whether sellers with good reputations are able to command higher prices for their products. However, the results are not consistent. For example, Lucking-Reiley [13] regressed online auction price on feedback reputation and found that the feedback system was not fully effective at predicting price. On the other hand, Houser and Wooders [7] tested the impact of feedback systems on the end price of auctions of Intel Pentium III 500 chips, with their results indicating that the coefficients for positive feedback and negative or neutral feedback were as predicted and significant. Livingston [8] tested the feedback effect on golf iron prices using data collected from ebay.com, and found only negative feedback to be significant in influencing final prices. Finally, Melnik and Alm [15] found that reputation was positively correlated with price, although the impact was relatively small.

A number of factors may contribute to these inconsistent results.

\- Most studies used data from simple, standardized, products, such as coins or dolls, where feedback systems may not be important due to low product value, little difference in quality, or other well-developed means of assessing quality [9].

\- Data were generally only collected for new products, where product quality is standardized.

\- Product-specific information, such as bundling the product with accessories, has not been noted in empirical estimates.

• Factors that are useful in evaluating the capability of sellers (such as acceptable payment methods, warranty information, or length of time the seller has been in business) may have been left out of the estimation

\- Feedback has not been measured in a consistent manner. It has been captured in at least four ways: direct count of positive and negative feedback; percentage of feedback that is positive; feedback score calculated by the auction market site; and, the difference between positive and negative feedback. This lack of consistency makes the results difficult to compare.

In our study, we attempted to account for all of these considerations. Specifically, our data were collected for auctions of digital cameras, which are a relatively high-value, complex product; the data were collected for both new and used product auctions, as well as for added (or subtracted) accessories. In addition, factors, such as ID age, payment options and warranty provisions, that might influence seller capability, were included in our estimating equations. Finally, our model was tested using four measures of feedback in order to assess the differential influences of these measures on ending auction price.

An effective feedback system should generate higher final auction prices for reputable sellers of good quality products. Without an effective feedback system, buyers will not be able to distinguish good quality from poor quality products, and may withdraw from the market. Similarly, sellers of good quality products may be reluctant to participate in a market without an effective feedback system since they may not be rewarded for providing a better product. Therefore, assuming that a feedback system would be effective, our first hypothesis was:

Hypothesis 1. The better a seller’s feedback profile, the higher the ending price of an auction.

However, since there are (at least) four ways that feedback can be measured, Hypothesis 1 was split into four subcomponents:

Hypothesis 1a. The higher a seller’s positive feedback count, the higher the ending price of an auction; The higher a seller’s negative feedback count, the lower the ending price of an auction.

Hypothesis 1b. The higher the percentage of positive feedback in a seller’s profile, the higher the ending price of an auction.

Hypothesis 1c. The higher the seller’s feedback score, the higher the ending price of an auction.

Hypothesis 1d. The larger the difference (positive counts minus negative counts) between a seller’s positive and negative feedback counts, the higher the ending price of an auction.

Since, in general, the sellers in online auction markets have much more positive then negative feedback (sellers with more negative then positive feedback may be forced to withdraw from the market), then it may be argued that an additional incident of negative feedback provides more information to a potential buyer than an additional positive feedback. Therefore, our second hypothesis was:

Hypothesis 2. The mean impact of negative feedback on the ending price of an auction will be higher than the mean impact of positive feedback.

The capability process suggests that other information may be useful in assessing the capability of sellers to deliver products as requested. For example, ID age may be a good proxy for experience, while sellers that accept credit card payments may be deemed more capable then sellers without this capacity. Finally, the provision of a warranty with a product might be an indication of the seller’s willingness to stand behind the product since better quality products are more likely to be accompanied by longer and more comprehensive warranties. Pinker et al. [17] noted that sites and sellers offered warranties to demonstrate confidence in their products. Therefore, we hypothesized:

Hypothesis 3. Inclusion of a warranty with the product purchase is associated with a higher ending price of an auction.

Hypothesis 4. The ID age of a firm is positively associated with a higher ending auction price.

Hypothesis 5. Acceptance of credit cards by the seller is positively associated with a higher ending price of an auction.

In summary, an effective feedback system implies that sellers with a good reputation should be rewarded for their reputation with higher ending auction prices. Also, other factors that indicate seller capability should be associated with higher ending auction prices.

## 3. Data collection and research methodology

Data were collected directly from auctions of digital cameras from ebay.com during the first 2 months of 2004. Two models were targeted: the Sony Cyber Shot DSC-F717 and the Nikon Coolpix 5700, resulting in a total of 1797 auctions, of which 1025 had at least one bid. The auctions that garnered at least one bid were split fairly evenly between the two products, 525 for the Sony camera and 500 for the Nikon.

A hedonic model [20] was formulated to estimate the effect of seller reputation, as measured by feedback, on the end price of an ebay.com auction. It was as follows:

End price ¼ f ðFeedback; ProductCondition; Accessories;

Warranty; CreditCard; IDage; Shi p pingFree; ProductDummyÞ

Variables in the model above were defined as follows. Endprice was the price the winner paid at the end of the auction; Feedback was a variable used to measure the reputation of the seller. Four measures were used to assess feedback: the feedback score as assigned by ebay, and three measures based on feedback left in the previous 12 months (direct counts of positive and negative feedback numbers, the percentage of positive feedback to the total feedback count, and the difference between the positive and negative feedback counts); Product Condition was a control variable used to assess the value of the product being auctioned. It was assessed using two dummy variables. The first, mint, was given the value, 1, if the auction description claimed the product was in mint condition, and 0 otherwise. The second, used product, was given the value, 1, if the auction description included the word used, and 0 otherwise. The use of either the term mint or the term used indicated that the product was not new. The coefficients for both of these variables should be negative (i.e., prices should be lower for these cameras than for new cameras, the base case); Accessories were generally included with the camera purchases. They could be divided into two classifications: those originally included by the manufacturer with the new cameras, and those sold as extra accessories by the vendor. Two variables were used to assess the impact of accessories on the ending price of the auction: Value of Miss Acc measured the value of accessories included by the manufacturer but missing from the product auction. Extra Accessories measured the value of accessories included in an auction (e.g., an extra lens) but not normally originally sold as a part of the camera sale. We assumed that the coefficient for the first variable would be negative and the coefficient for the second positive; Warranty information was assessed using two dummy variables. A full warranty was 12 months of coverage and a partial warranty was for a shorter period. If there was a full or partial warranty, the respective variable was coded 1, and otherwise 0. Since the comparison cameras were those without warranties, it was expected that both of these coefficients would be positive; Credit Card was a dummy variable used to assess the seller’s acceptance of either a credit card, or ebay.com’s alternate payment method, Paypal, as a means of payment. Acceptance of one or both of these methods resulted in this variable being coded 1. We assumed that the coefficient for this variable would be positive; ID Age was the number of days that a seller’s ID had been registered with ebay.com; that is, the time between the creation date of the ID and the ending date of the auction. A positive coefficient was predicted; Shipping Free was coded 1 if the shipping cost was listed as zero or paid by the seller, and 0 otherwise. Including the shipping cost in the price of the auction should result in a higher ending price. Therefore, a positive coefficient for this variable was expected; Product Dummy was 1 if the auction was for a Nikon camera and 0 for a Sony camera. See Table 1 for a summary of variables.

The coefficient for positive feedback was significant at the 1% level, and the marginal effect from positive feedback was shown to be \$0.003. Although the marginal effect may seem trivial, the effect of positive feedback at the mean feedback level (5313 counts of positive feedback) was about \$15.94, with a standard deviation of about \$18.89. The coefficient for negative feedback was also significant at the 1% level. Adding an additional negative feedback incident to a seller reduced the ending price by around \$0.36, which translated to a \$26.29 reduction in the selling price of a camera at the mean negative feedback count of 73.

Similar results were found for the log-linear model (column 2). Coefficients obtained from this estimation can be interpreted as elasticities. The coefficient for positive feedback implied that a 1% increase in positive feedback increased the ending price of an auction by 0.03%. At the mean ending auction price for the Sony camera, \$578.71, a 1% increase in positive feedback would raise the price by \$17.36. The coefficient for negative feedback implied that a 1% increase in the negative feedback decreased the ending price by 0.05%, or \$28.94 based on the mean ending auction price for the Sony camera.

In column 3, the coefficient for percent of positive feedback was significant at the 1% level. The coefficient value of 78.82 indicated that a one percentage point increase in positive feedback resulted in a \$78.82 increase in the average ending auction price. It should be noted that most of the sellers had a very high value for percent of positive feedback (mean = 0.97; standard deviation = 0.12), and that the range between the very best and the very worst sellers was not particularly large.

The two other feedback measures did not turn out to be significant predictors of the ending auction price. Both of these variables were difference measures. It may have been inappropriate to subtract negative feedback incidents from positive feedback incidents, given that the impact of negative feedback on price was much greater than the impact of positive feedback on price. For example, a seller with 50 positive feedback incidences and 30 negative incidences (difference of +20) may not be deemed as trustworthy as a seller with twenty positive incidences and no negative feedback. Although the two sellers have the same difference scores, they will not be perceived as having similar reputations.

In general, the results support Hypothesis 1 (that the ending price of the auction would be affected by the seller’s feedback profile). However, in line with previous research, some forms of feedback may be more effective in influencing the ending price. In particular, buyers may want to weigh the number or percent of positive feedback incidences against the number or percent of negative feedback incidences when choosing to bid at an auction. This is possible when both positive and negative counts have been provided, or when the percent positive feedback was provided. The provision of a difference score, on the other hand, may potentially hide the effect of negative feedback counts, and thus would be less effective for determining the ending price of an auction.

The results could also be used to assess Hypothesis 2. A test between the magnitudes of the coefficients for positive and negative feedback revealed that negative feedback had a significantly greater effect on the ending price than did positive feedback. An F-test for the difference between the positive and negative count coefficients (Model 1) gave a value of 135.32, which was significant at a = 0.001. This result may have occurred because of the much greater incidence of positive than negative feedback.

A number of other indicators of the trustworthiness of a firm and its products have been proposed. The effect of these on the final price at an auction was also tested in our five regression runs. Hypothesis 3 proposed that the provision of product warranties should be positively associated with the final price. The coefficient for Full Warranty was significant at the 0.01 level in all five regression runs, whilethe coefficientfor Partial Warranty was only significant in the first run (at the 10% level). These results lent support to Hypothesis 3 for full warranties, although inclusion of partial warranties does not seem to result in significantly higher final prices.

Hypothesis 4 posited that the ending price should be higher for firms that have transacted longer on ebay.com (measured by ID Age), but the coefficient for this variable was only significant at the 1% level in the first regression run, lending only partial support to the hypothesis.

Finally, Hypothesis 5 suggested that the use of credit cards should positively affect the ending price. The coefficient for this variable was significant, at least at the 10% level, in all five regression runs. Based on the result of the first estimation (column 1), the coefficient suggested that those firms that accepted credit cards (or Paypal) garnered \$37.95 more per camera sale than those firms that did not, all else held constant.

Other control variables generally had the expected coefficient sign. The coefficients for Used Product were negative and significant in all regressions, indicating that used cameras sold at a lower price than new ones. The coefficients for Mint Product were negative and significant in the first two regressions, suggesting that products labeled as ‘‘mint’’ commanded a lower price than new ones. The coefficients for Shipping Free were not significant, indicating that consumers did not account for the inclusion of free shipping in auction prices. The coefficients for Value of Missing Accessories were negative and significant in most regressions, indicating that buyers discounted camera prices when factory-included accessories are missing. The coefficients for Extra Accessories were positive and significant in all of the regression runs, reflecting that buyers were willing to pay an extra price for accessories not generally provided free by the manufacturer. Finally, the coefficients for Product Dummy were positive and significant in four of the five regression runs, reflecting the higher value buyers placed on the Nikon compared to the Sony camera.

## 5. Conclusions, contributions and limitations

Data were collected from ebay.com online auction markets to examine the impact of buyer feedback and other trust-evoking variables on the final auction prices realized by sellers. Consistent with previous research (e.g. [4]) and in support of the first hypothesis, buyer feedback was found to affect auction price, although this was dependent on the computation of buyer feedback: when direct counts of positive and negative feedback were used to assess the buyer feedback profile, or when the percentage positive feedback was used, significant effects on the final price was obtained. Feedback profiles with higher counts of positive feedback led to higher ending prices. Higher negative feedback counts significantly reduced the ending price. Although the marginal effects of positive and negative feedback were both small, the accumulation of feedback may bring substantial benefits or penalties to the seller.

Some representations of buyer feedback were not found to have a significant impact on the ending auction price [19]; that is, when the difference between positive and negative feedback counts, or the when the ebay.com feedback score was used as the buyer feedback measure. These results may be due to the different effects from positive and negative feedback on the ending price, since much more positive than negative feedback is obtained by sellers. Thus, the marginal impact of negative feedback is much greater than that of positive feedback.

Support was also found for other hypotheses: Hypothesis 2 was supported; that is, the marginal impact of negative feedback was found to be significantly greater than the marginal impact of positive feedback. Hypothesis 3 was partially supported, as the inclusion of full warranties had a positive impact on auction prices while the inclusion of partial warranties were not found to affect ending auction prices significantly. Hypothesis 5 was supported in that sellers that accepted credit card payments generated, on average, higher auction prices. On the other hand, Hypothesis 4, that ID age would be associated with higher auction prices, was only supported in one of the regression runs.

The results from this paper contribute to the existing body of knowledge in the following ways:

\- By analyzing the effectiveness of a variety of feedback measures and comparing the results, it was found that those measures that use differences between positive and negative feedback counts are not effective at predicting the ending price of an auction. This is notable because the ebay.com feedback score for sellers is a difference measure and was ineffective at predicting the ending auction price.

\- By including a number of potentially trust-enhancing factors (warranties, ID age, acceptance of credit cards), the results presented helped to identify factors, other than feedback, that can contribute to higher auction prices. These factors complement the feedback system and help to attenuate the information asymmetry found between buyers and sellers. The factors offered evidence for both calculative and capability processes as trust-builders in the online environment.

\- By controlling for factors that could potentially influence the ending auction price (such as the value of accessories), the models were able to isolate the impact of a seller’s reputation on the ending price of an auction.

\- By testing the effect of feedback systems on non-standardized products, this paper was able to provide a good reading of the effectiveness of feedback systems.

There are, however, a number of potential limitations to this analysis: First, since data were collected on only two camera models, results may not be generalizable. Second, the dataset was collected in 2004; more current data are desired. Third, using ID age can be at the best a proxy for seller experience. Fourth, the relationship between reputation and auction prices may be more complex than that modeled in the paper with interactions between variables. For example, the provision of full warranties by sellers may act as a replacement for a good feedback profile.
