# Toward an Integrated Model of Group Development: Disruption of Routines by Technology-Induced Change

Moni ca J. Ga rfie ld and Alan R. Denis

She holds an A.B. from Vassar College, an MBA and an M.S. from Boston University, and a Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Georgia. Abstract: Current research argues that the most prominent models of group development (the linear stage model and the punctuated equilibrium model) are simply different lenses for studying the same phenomenon. We argue that the two models are distinct (groups do not simultaneously follow both models) and that the key to understanding their use lies in routines. We studied six newly formed groups whose members came from the same organization that worked on similar projects over a seven-week period. Three groups worked nonmediated and three groups used a collaboration technology that was new to them. The three nonmediated groups followed the punctuated equilibrium model and the three collaboration technology groups followed the stage model. We argue that groups that enact the shared routines common in their organizations will experience a different group development path than those groups whose shared routines are disrupted and which must adapt to a new technology. When group members enact shared routines (which they may share due to having a common organizational culture), they can quickly begin work, and group development follows the punctuated equilibrium model. When groups cannot enact shared routines, they must first negotiate how they will work before work can begin, so group development follows the stage model. Thus, the introduction of new collaboration technology (or any new technology or work process) influences how group development occurs.

Key words and phrases: case study, collaboration technology, field experiment, group development models, mixed methods, routines.

Group developm ent research has proposed various m odels of how new groups form, work together, and disband [3, 49]. Chang et al. argue that the two major types of models (the linear stage model, often exemplified by Tuckman [78], and the punctuated equilibrium model of Gersick [31]) occur concurrently in all newly formed groups “albeit on different dimensions” [15, p. 106]. They argue that groups simultaneously follow both models, so researchers can use either model to explain group behavior depending on the unit of analysis and the dimension of observation the researcher employs, that is, all groups exhibit behaviors matching both models. Other researchers argue that there are fundamental differences between the two models that make them quite distinct and that groups follow one model or the other but not both [3, 74].

We believe that there are fundamental differences between the models and that the key to understanding the different developmental paths taken by groups lies in the routines that participants enact while working in groups (e.g., [68]). We believe that groups that enact the routines common in their organizations will experience a different group development path than those groups whose routines are disrupted and must adapt to a new technology. This paper proposes an integrated model of group development that subsumes both the linear stage model and the punctuated equilibrium model.

Our model is based on the study of six naturally occurring groups formed to work on a specific project. Three groups were given access to a collaboration technology and three were not. Collaboration technology alters the way individuals work together in groups, and we believe that it affects group development not just through the communication features it offers but also because those features are unfamiliar. Unfamiliar technologies have the potential to disrupt routines [4, 23, 70, 81].

## Prior Theory and Research

## Group Development

Group developm ent m eans different thing s to different researchers [16, 73]. We define group development as the changes over time in group behavior as a newly formed group moves through its life (e.g., [16]). Most group development models fall into one of two categories [15, 36, 40]: a linear stage model or the punctuated equilibrium model. Groups either progress linearly through fairly distinct stages (e.g., forming, storming, norming, and performing [78]) or they jump immediately into working on the task at hand, until a midpoint transition when they reevaluate their work and often decide to take a new direction [31].

The seminal linear stage model was proposed by Tuckman [78] as a synthesis of the literature on group formation (see Table 1 for a summary);<sup>1</sup> the Tuckman model is the most widely used model of group development [11]. Tuckman proposed that groups go through four stages: forming, storming, norming, and performing (later updated [79] to add adjourning).

In the forming stage, the group members are in the process of getting to know one another and familiarizing themselves with the task at hand. Group members explore the goals and the boundaries of the task. They often look to existing standards, routines, or leaders to guide their interactions and discuss the work processes to be used in performing the task. This stage is characterized by the questioning of authority within the group, and members may be defensive.

In the storming stage, the group members sort out the work processes that will be used by the group. This is seen in an increase in the level of interpersonal conflict between the group members, as individuals question the controls that are in place to guide group work. There are often emotional, sometimes even hostile, reactions to requests as members seek out their roles and assert their independence.

After this stage, the group has begun to create a shared understanding and can move into the norming stage, when the group settles into a set of mutually accepted work processes and norms for the project. Conflict typically subsides as the group has come to agreement on the nature of the task and the way group members will work together. At this stage of development, the group typically begins to show a concern for the development of interpersonal relationships and a desire to build trust and group cohesiveness.

Finally, in the performing stage, the group has settled into an established set of behavioral routines and has come to consensus on how to work together. They set about performing the task they have been assigned, showing an intense focus on attaining their goals, and the group members tend to be less hostile toward one another. There is increased task activity and the group focuses both on the task and individuals’ roles within the group.

In the punctuated equilibrium model [31, 32] (see Table 1 for a summary), groups form rapidly, determining the work processes and task goals within the first few moments (sometimes seconds) of their first meeting. The task goal and work processes remain essentially unchanged until reaching the temporal midpoint of the project, but this initial work is often unfocused and unproductive. While Chang et al. [15] conclude that the first three stages of the linear stage model roughly fit into this phase of the punctuated equilibrium model, the current study challenges that assertion.

At the project’s temporal midpoint, groups undergo a series of radical changes called a “midpoint transition.” Group members become acutely aware that time is passing, and this triggers them to rethink the project. They question the task goals and the work processes, which typically leads to a shift in goals and work processes. This second phase is characterized by an increased focus on the task and increased task performance. Research has shown that although groups evaluate their progress

It is this midpoint evaluation, not a transition, that is the marker of the punctuated equilibrium model [58, 59]. Work after the transition matches the fourth stage (performing) of the linear stage model.

Research by Chang et al. [15] has attempted to unify these two models. They argue that the two types of models focus on different levels of analysis. The linear stage model focus on micro elements such as the structures and processes of task and socioemotional activities, whereas the punctuated equilibrium model focuses on more macro issues of task activities, time awareness, and pacing [11]. Chang et al. [15] contend that all the groups go through the four (or five) stages of the linear model but that research using the punctuated equilibrium model has missed this because such research has not focused on and coded behaviors matching stage model behaviors. Likewise, Chang et al. contend that all the groups quickly jump into working on the task and have the midpoint transition of the punctuated equilibrium model, but that research using the stage model has missed this because such research has not used a coding scheme that would recognize these behaviors.

Chang et al. [15] studied 25 groups in a 40-minute laboratory experiment and coded the extent to which their behavior matched the punctuated equilibrium model and a linear stage model. Using the coding scheme for the punctuated equilibrium model, Chang et al. found “that 9 groups underwent midpoint transitions. The other 16 groups fitted the description of linear progression more closely than that of a progression marked by a midpoint transition” [15, p. 112]. Using the coding scheme for the linear stage model, they found no significant differences in behavior in the second and third stages, suggesting that the linear stage model did not fit well. Based on these results, Chang et al. concluded that “both punctuated equilibrium and linear progression were observed simultaneously in simulated project teams, with the dimension of observation and the unit of analysis accounting for the type of development that was observed” [15, p. 115]. In other words, the two models are just different lenses for examining group development and that the level of analysis and focus of the coding schema employed will determine the behavior that the researcher sees. “Depending upon the temporal dimension they are interested in, researchers and practitioners working with organizational project teams can choose either the integrative [linear stage] model or the punctuated equilibrium model” [15, p. 116].

Although we agree with Chang et al. [15] that there are similarities between these two conceptions of group development, we believe that there are some striking differences that may become more apparent in groups working on projects of longer duration (e.g., [3, 74]). Subsequent work has used Chang et al.’s [15] model as an enhancement to Gersick’s punctuated equilibrium model [31] as support for activities that occur in the stage model [91], or have largely ignored the stages model, or have used both models without acknowledging what we see as fundamental differences between the two [76].

While both models argue that groups perform most of the task-oriented work in the latter part of the project, they differ sharply in how group members behave in the first part of the group’s life. With the punctuated equilibrium model, the first part of a group’s life is characterized by the quick adoption of a goal and work processes, with the group immediately beginning work on the task. With linear stage models, the first part of a group’s life is devoted to forming, storming, and norming over the goals and work processes. We do not believe that for groups working on longer tasks (i.e., hours or days), the differences can be fully explained by the temporal granularity of analysis or a difference in focus. We believe that these two models predict fundamentally different behavior in the early part of a group’s life (i.e., prior to the project midpoint) and that the key to group development lies in the routines that group members bring to the group.

## Routines

“Routines can be defined as repetitive, recognizable patterns of interdependent actions carried out by multiple actors” [29, p. 95]. Repetition is the essential characteristic of routines [6]. Routines may be thought of as multiperson recurrent behavior patterns, akin to habits at an individual level [5, 6]. Routines can be formal procedures or the informal, subjective understandings of those who perform the routines [29]. Routines can be characterized as abstract patterns used to guide specific performances of a routine (their ostensive aspect) and they can also be characterized as actual performances by specific people, at specific times, in specific places [65]. Thus, routines exhibit a duality of structure and action in that there is a memory of a pattern (structure) and the action that enacts it (agency) [29, 34]. Routines enable and constrain how individuals act by defining ”acceptable” and “unacceptable” behavior [33]. Routines generally do not fully specify all actions, but provide general guidance; the actor interprets the meaning of a routine in each context in which it is enacted [29].

Group and organizational routines are not just individual routines performed in a group context, they are the coordinated interdependent actions of multiple actors in the group or organization [29, 65, 70]. Like dancers, group members must adjust to each other’s actions [29]. There is an improvisatory nature of performing organizational routines, and the degree of divergence from the routine can involve taking into consideration the actions of others and the details of the situation [65]. Routines create expectations for how group members will work together [45] and provide a means for interpreting the behavior of others [33].

Group and organizational routines are collective phenomena stored in distributed procedural memory that enable a group of people who share the same organization to invoke common routines [7, 19]. As individuals are repeatedly exposed to similar situations, they develop shared routines that enable them to work together in an interlocking fashion observed in most organizations, even if they have never met before [68]. Even though individual group members may never have met before, their shared organization culture and common experiences in that culture may produce a shared set of routines. Consider, for example, the first day of class; although the instructors and students may have never met, it is likely that they share some common routines.

Routines are enacted in response to a stimulus [5, 67]. They may be invoked automatically without conscious thought and without considering alternative ways to behave [33, 44, 52]. When presented with a situation that matches a commonly encountered situation, groups simply enact their routine for the situation with little conscious cognitive planning [77]. The more a routine is used, the less conscious it becomes, and the more likely it is to be invoked automatically when presented with the same stimuli [33]. The routine becomes so ingrained that it is no longer part of the group members’ consciousness; it is not explicitly invoked but is instead the default way to interact [14, 33, 72, 86]. For example, in hospital settings, operating room groups have very strong routines that are enacted regardless of organizational features and group membership; the routines are so ingrained that groups invoke them automatically with little verbal interaction [26].

The duality of routines means that they exist through action, as group members rely on routines to inform their behavior [29]. No routine fully specifies all needed behaviors, so behavior is an improvisation on the routine based on the needs of the specific situation in which the routine is enacted [29, 66]. Behavior can reinforce and maintain existing routines, change routines, or form new routines [25, 29, 33, 70]. Thus, in every enactment of a routine, there is the potential for change [29, 34].

Group members’ behavior is constrained by routines [29]. Individuals cannot easily act alone and remain in the group; they must coordinate their behavior with other group members [29, 66]. Routines create expectations for behavior and help group members interpret and understand the behavior of other group members [6, 33, 34]. Changes from (and to) group routines therefore require an accounting and explanation [29] and an agreement among group members. Thus, group and organizational routines are not as malleable as individual habits.

Changes in routines often are sparked by a failure or exposure to a novel situation [33]. Because routines are the default path in many commonly encountered situations [33, 86], it takes a discrepancy—a mismatch between the routine and the situation—to inhibit the use of a routine [33, 44]. In general, discrepancies between the routine and the situation encountered need to be significant in order to trigger a departure from the habitual routine [44], although for some group members, a minor discrepancy is sufficient to trigger cognition that challenges the use of a routine, whereas others may persist in using the habitual routine even in the face of clear evidence that it no longer applies [5, 33, 44].

Changes to routines can take place either explicitly through group discussion or implicitly through subtle actions that evolve the routines into different interaction patterns [5, 23]. When groups are uncertain about what routine to enact or there is a certain level of ambiguity about the action required, they may engage in “sensemaking” to understand a new situation [78, 88]. Burke et al. [14] argue that when groups encounter a new situation for which they lack a routine, they move iteratively through four phases. The first phase is awareness, in which group members individually see something in the situation that causes them to question the applicability of their existing shared routines. These situation elements may be perceived similarly or differently by different group members but they are discussed such that the group becomes aware that existing routines are not a good fit for the situation. The second stage is plan formulation, during which group members develop a shared plan for action, even though all members may not share the same mental models or understanding of the situation. The third phase is plan execution, during which the group performs the plan. The fourth phase is group learning, in which the group discovers the consequences of its actions (both intended and unintended), which is then fed back into the previous phases and the cycle begins again.

It is sometimes assumed that established group routines are effective because they have been well honed by prior use and therefore have evolved into their present state because they lead to the best organizational performance. Existing routines may have evolved because they lead to the best task performance, but group members often have other goals in addition to maximizing task performance, such as group relationships and individual member growth [42], so routines may have evolved because they enhance these outcomes in addition to or instead of task performance. Likewise, group routines may have evolved to maximize performance on the usual tasks that groups perform and may not be effective for nontraditional tasks [76, 77].

Group and organizational routines are closely intertwined with technology, as routines often rely on technology to support them [7, 30, 70, 83]. Past research has shown that a new technology can trigger a change in routines, although it does not always result in such a change [4, 81, 83]; technologies can reinforce existing routines or break them [23, 26]. The key point is that it is a new technology, a technology that is unfamiliar to those who will use it, that may trigger changes in routines. Once a technology, even a radical technology, is no longer new, groups will develop routines for its use [23] and further use is unlikely to trigger changes to those technology-based routines (unless, of course, new discrepancies occur). It is the familiarity with the technology and the extent to which a new technology enables or challenges existing routines that influences whether use of that technology can trigger a change in routines.

## Collaboration Technology

Most research on group development has focused on groups working without technology, yet many groups today are digitally enabled [10, 71]. Digitally enabled groups are those groups that have access to collaboration technologies that enable them to communicate from different places at the same time (e.g., instant messaging, audioconferencing, videoconferencing) or from different places and times (e.g., e-mail, discussion boards, Google Docs). Collaboration technology also enables groups to communicate electronically in traditional face-to-face meetings.

Collaboration technology is a class of technology that is often explicitly designed to change group routines [22, 23, 27, 53], so it is an excellent vehicle for research on routines. Collaboration technologies can have particularly powerful effects on routines because they are designed to influence the way in which group members communicate, process, and remember information [66]. A collaboration technology is a package of components that can be combined in different ways [53, 62, 66]. When a group adopts a new collaboration technology, group members must learn its different components and develop new collaborative routines to employ them [23, 26, 66]. These changes in interaction patterns can change the routines that guide how group members work together [4, 54, 60]. When groups have very strong routines, it may be hard for a technology to modify those routines, although it still can modify the routines in some cases [26].

A collaboration technology has both spirit and structural features [23]. The spirit of the collaboration technology is the general intent of its structural features and is broadly defined to include the system design, its features, user interface, and training materials [23]. The spirit of many collaboration technologies (including the one used in this study) is to promote a meeting process that is fair and participative [1, 84]. The spirit encourages what Habermas terms ideal speech: “to ensure that (a) all voices in any way relevant can get a hearing, and that (b) the best arguments we have in our present state of knowledge are brought to bear, and that (c) disagreement or agreement on the part of the participants follows only from the force of the better argument and no other force” [35, p. 104].

Structural features are the specific components of the technology, their capabilities, and the “specific types of rules and resources, or capabilities, offered by the system” [23, p. 126]. Most collaboration technologies enable text communication. Field studies and case studies of collaboration technologies have found that communication is improved when a group utilizes collaboration technology [30].

Text communication can provide four structural features that may improve communication: parallelism, anonymity, meeting memory, and process structure [53, 94]. Parallelism is the ability for members to exchange information simultaneously. Everyone can type at the same time, so no one needs to wait for others before contributing as they do verbally. Parallelism reduces production blocking (caused by an individual needing to wait their turn and thus being blocked from putting forward their own ideas) [20, 24, 37, 41], but it can also increase the amount of information a group member is exposed to and reduce a group member’s ability to pay attention to important pieces of information [20, 50].

A second structural feature, anonymity, is the ability for group members to make contributions without attaching their names, which is not possible when contributions are made verbally. Anonymity may reduce the reluctance to challenge the views of those in power, but may also increase free riding [53]. SIDE (social identity model of deindividuation effects) postulates that anonymity can increase social influence because it obscures the recognition of interpersonal differences among group members [69, 75]. Group members may place more importance on the role identified with a group member, thus maximizing the role’s stereotypical differences [69, 75]. Although anonymity was present in our study, it was likely less powerful since the groups were relatively small, the group members were familiar with one another, and verbal communication often augmented the electronic discussion, so members might have identified who put forth which comment because of familiarity or accompanying verbal comments (e.g., [38]).

The third feature, shared meeting memory, means that all typed comments are stored, which may increase the equality of participation because every comment that is entered is saved. The group as a whole owns its memory instead of having one person take notes and be in charge of their collection, documentation, and distribution.

A collaboration technology also enables the group to explicitly structure their work processes [30, 53, 94]. Field studies and case studies of collaborative technologies have found that process structure is improved when a group utilizes collaboration technology [30]. Process structure helps a group organize the way in which they plan to work via the development of a task agenda that the group can follow to perform the assigned task [94]. Process structure can be delivered using training that shows a group how to follow a planned agenda or via facilitation (often provided by a person external to the group) that helps group members follow the agenda [90]. None of the groups in this study chose to use a facilitator.

The adoption and use of collaboration technology can be challenging. Much research has shown that the use of collaboration technology can improve group performance [13, 19, 30], yet groups and organizations are often slow to adopt it [13]. Part of the reason for this may be an inherent resistance to changing established routines [13]; part may be because it can be challenging to learn new collaboration technology–based routines [12].

## Group Development with Routines and Collaboration Technology

Prior research has exam ined g roup developm ent in digitally enabled groups (e.g., [17, 18, 51, 54, 55]). Most of this work has examined the development of group cohesiveness and other characteristics. However, there are three articles that shed light on how digitally enabled groups form. Yoon and Johnson [93] examined development in seven virtual learning groups enrolled in an online course. Two groups followed a linear stage model of development. The other five groups began in a linear progression but experienced negative forces that required them to revisit a previous stage before they could continue. None of the groups reported midpoint transitions. Sarker and Sahay [73] studied 12 American–Canadian student groups. Using an inductive approach, Sarker and Sahay found that the groups iteratively and sometimes nonlinearly move through four phases in their group development. They did not observe a midpoint transition. Munkvold and Zigurs [51] studied five American–Norwegian student groups who had little experience with collaboration technology and virtual work. Most teams began by discussing project goals and deliverables, team member roles and responsibilities, and setting milestones, which is closer to a linear stage model than the punctuated equilibrium model; there was no mention of midpoint transitions.

Chang et al. [15], argue that group development models are merely different lenses for examining behavior and that all groups progress through the development paths of both models; researchers will find behaviors that match both models in all groups so the choice of model is up to the researcher. We argue that the models are fundamentally different [3, 74], and that the key to understanding group development lies in routines. We believe that groups that enact the routines shared in their organizations will experience a very different group development path (likely one closer to punctuated equilibrium models) than those groups whose routines are disrupted (who are more likely to follow the stages model of group development) and which must adapt to a new technology.

When a group is first formed, and individuals come together for the first time, the routines that exist in the organization will influence how they behave [73]. In “collocated teams, there may be a preexisting common ground in understanding because members share institutional as well as location-related assumptions (including a collective sense of norms, meanings, and power distributions). Having such a shared frame among a team’s members introduces different dynamics in its development, and in many cases, makes the state of collaboration easier to achieve” [73, p. 30]. In many organizations, as with the hospital operating room groups in the example above, group routines are ubiquitous and deeply ingrained. If the situation matches typical situations and there are no discrepancies to challenge the use of the routines, group members will enact those shared routines; the shared routines will guide behavior and group members will not need to think about how to behave. They will act in ways similar to well-established groups, even though the individual group members have never worked together before. In short, the forming, norming, and storming stages are unnecessary and will be bypassed because the group has already figured out how to work using its shared routines. Thus, this type of group will follow the punctuated equilibrium model of group development and immediately begin working on the task (akin to jumping right to the performing stage).

In contrast, if a novel event occurs, such as the introduction of an unfamiliar technology, group members may not be willing or able to enact their default routines because those routines may not fit the situation. New collaboration technology, for example, provides a spirit and structural features that can be adopted to create new ways of working—ways that do not fit the current routine [84]. Members are likely to behave quite differently from what would have occurred without the introduction of the novel technology, and thus the pattern of group development will be quite different from that seen without the technology. Groups will engage in sensemaking about the new technology and the routines it enables [77]. They are likely to develop an awareness that their existing routines are not a good fit. They will formulate a plan for the use of the technology, execute the plan, and learn from their experiences [14]. They will discuss options, perhaps debate and argue over them, and ultimately settle on a new and new set of work processes; that is, they will be unable to immediately start work on the task but instead will form, storm, and norm before performing the task. Thus, these groups will follow the stages model of group development. This leads to two hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1: Newly formed groups whose members enact a common set of shared routines for group work will follow the punctuated equilibrium model of group development.

Hypothesis 2: Newly formed groups that encounter a novel technology and do not enact a common set of shared routines for group work will follow the stages model of group development.

## Method

## Research Design

We used a m ultiple case study desig n with the unit of analysis being the group. We used a collaboration technology with a spirit and structural features designed to promote participative group work processes to disrupt the routines of groups in a hospital setting—a setting often known for a hierarchical leader-centric culture [2, 39]. We followed six newly formed groups as they worked on similar projects over a seven-week time period. Three groups (randomly assigned) were given access to a collaboration technology that was new to them. The other three groups (randomly assigned) received no intervention. In our analysis below, we term these groups “collaboration technology groups” and “nonmediated groups,” respectively.

## Participants

Members of the six groups were nurses from the Department of Nursing at the American Medical Center (AMC) (all names are pseudonyms) and nurses from the American Physicians Corporation (APC), its sister organization of physicians working in the hospital. The Department of Nursing was divided into five principal directorates (e.g., pediatrics) and three supporting directorates (e.g., research) that reported to the vice president of nursing (the VP). Six groups were formed from the directors and managers in these directorates, plus their counterparts from APC. The six groups were organized around the five principal directorates with the exception of the critical care/ trauma directorate, which, because of its size, was divided into two groups: one for critical care and one for trauma. All of the groups were newly formed; none existed prior to the study. Of course, the participants were from the same two organizations, so many participants had worked with many of their group members before (as well as a members of other groups), but each group was formed specifically for this project and existed only for the life of the project.

The groups ranged from six to eight members (mean 6.8) and were predominantly female, with an average of one male per group. Each group was comprised of one AMC director and members of the next management level below director and their counterparts from APC. All the participants knew all the other members of their group. They had worked together on other projects, although no individual had previously worked with the exact set of individuals in his or her group and members of APC did not know members of AMC well and vice versa. The most senior-ranking person on each team was from AMC.

## The Project

The project (and our involvement) was proposed by the VP. It was a naturally occurring project, into which we were able to intercede with a treatment and a control condition. The VP had been planning the project prior to our involvement; we were fortunate to join the project in the final stages of planning prior to the first group meeting. The groups’ objective, as defined by the VP, was to write a proposal to improve the satisfaction of four customer groups: patients, families, physicians, and nurses. The project began with a kickoff meeting in which the VP introduced the project and its goals, and explained the role of the researchers and the collaboration technology. To help organize, analyze, and report the project plans, the groups were given the 15-page Baxter Planning Worksheet, which had been used by AMC for many years to develop organizational change proposals. This worksheet covered items such as project overview, project definition, key success factors, required investment, experience with similar projects, project competitors, other alternatives considered, and potential effects. The groups were asked to draft a project plan using the worksheets and to give a short presentation seven weeks later at the final meeting. The amount of time each team devoted to this project ranged from 10.9 to 31.9 hours (see Table 2 for group specifics). In the kickoff meeting, the groups were given 45 minutes to organize their plans; no collaboration technology support was provided. All the groups were introduced to the collaboration technology and told whether or not they had been randomly assigned to use it.

## The Collaboration Technology

Although most of the participants had prior experience with some collaboration technologies (e.g., e-mail, discussion forums), no one had used the form of collaboration technology used in this study before. Thus, the technology was new to these groups, and the participants did not have established routines for its use in group settings. In this study, group members worked together in a specially designed meeting room that provided each person with a computer and special purpose software that enabled them to conduct anonymous electronic discussions, electronic outlining, group writing, and voting. Although the collaboration technology could be used from different places at different times, these groups chose to use it only to supplement face-to-face meetings in the purpose-built meeting room located in a building adjacent to AMC’s main building. This room provided 16 networked microcomputers with a large-screen video projection system. The software was developed by GroupSystems (see [53, 82]). We offered to provide a facilitator, but none of the groups in this study chose to use one. Instead, they created their group agendas on the fly during their meetings or at the beginning of each meeting (this was true in the nontechnology groups as well—in each case the group leader ran the meetings). However, the technology groups did use a research group member to assist in the technical operation of the technology and to act as a scribe when asked, but most of the time the research group member sat in a chair observing the group.

## Data Sources and Analyses

Data were collected from multiple sources (observation, transcripts collected from the electronic discussions, and interviews). Because routines often occur outside a person’s awareness [64], our primary form of data was observational data. Our observational data focused on individual and group behaviors rather than on linguistic or dialogue artifacts. Observational data were collected to build a chain of evidence to describe each group’s work processes [42, 92]. First, all the meetings except one were observed by the first author with about one-third of meetings also being observed by a doctoral student (the doctoral student observed the one meeting missed by the first author). Detailed notes were taken and case reports completed within 24 hours of each meeting; minor differences in notes were resolved between the observers. We wanted to audio tape meetings, but this was not permitted. Second, the electronic comments made during technology meetings were collected. Third, each group leader was interviewed between each meeting.

We began our coding effort using open coding, trying to make sense of how these groups evolved. Through this coding technique we identified leadership and group interaction patterns, signs of project efficiency and group cohesion as well as group formation patterns. We then did theoretical coding based on the two group formation models discussed above. We coded all the groups using both coding schema’s (one based on the linear stages model and one based on the punctuated equilibrium model). Because Chang et al. [15] had concluded that groups follow both group development models simultaneously and that the coding scheme used will determine the conclusions reached, we began our analyses with a within-group analysis to determine whether each model of group development fit each group and, if so, to what extent. The second analysis was a between-groups analysis to determine whether there were patterns in group development that could be linked to the introduction of the technology.

The data were analyzed using a coding schema based on the descriptions of the stages in Tuckman [78] and the activities in the punctuated equilibrium model [31, 32]. We included task-oriented categories as well as process categories to enable us to uncover various levels of temporal granularity. The purpose of the coding scheme was to identify actions that would fall within the various stages in the stage model or represented actions from the punctuated equilibrium model. Initial coding schemes for the two models were developed based on the theoretical constructs defined for each model. While we coded the case notes, we considered not only the content of the behaviors we observed but also the sequence and time they occurred during the group life span.

One author and a graduate student independently examined the data from one group and coded them using the initial coding scheme. The two then discussed the codings and revised the coding scheme by consolidating two categories based on insights from this analysis (“quick adoption of work processes” and “quick adoption of work roles” were merged into one category). Next, the other author and the graduate student used the revised coding scheme to independently code the remaining groups. All three met again and discussed the codings. There were few disagreements. Four categories were merged; “examination of goals” and “defining boundaries of task” were merged into one category, and “questioning of authority” and “emergence of a leader” into another. These categories were merged because there was a very high overlap between them both conceptually (i.e., goals and task boundaries are related and questioning of authority often is centered on establishing who is the leader) and empirically (i.e., the same data were coded as fitting in both categories).

The final coding scheme is presented in Table 1. For the stage model, we identified 12 behaviors linked to the four time-based stages. For example, in the first or second meeting, the group should examine goals and task boundaries. For the punctuated equilibrium model, we identified eight behaviors linked to the two time-based phases (before and after the midpoint, which occurred in the fourth week of this seven-week project). For example, during the first meeting, the group should quickly adopt a project goal. Behaviors were coded only if they occurred in the theorized stage or phase period. For example, for hostility to be coded as “storming” under the stage model, it had to occur in the first two meetings; if a group had experienced hostility later in the project when it was not predicted by the stage model, it would not have been coded as “storming.” In some cases, it was not clear if a behavior was observed; there was some evidence that the behavior occurred but the evidence was not beyond a reasonable doubt. In these cases, we coded the behavior as a “maybe” because we did not believe it was appropriate to definitively code the behavior as occurring or not occurring.

Once we had coded behaviors within each group, we produced a summary table of the data showing how many of the coded behaviors were observed in each group and then converted this to a percentage for the relative fit of each model. For example, if we observed 9 of the 12 behaviors of the stage model, the fit would be recorded as 75 percent. “Maybes” were scored as 0.5. As we noted above, the later time periods of development are somewhat similar between the two models, so some of the coded behaviors are similar between the two models. For example, the stage model predicts increased task activity in the final “performing” stage, whereas the punctuated equilibrium model predicts increased task activity after the midpoint transition. Thus, if increased task activity were observed in the later time periods, then the behavior would be coded as matching both models and the resulting percentage fits would show both models fitting the data to some extent.

## Analysis

We beg an our analysis within the two types of g roups and then compared across the two types. In the Discussion section, we interpret these patterns to develop our integrated model of group development.

## “Nonmediated” Groups

There were three groups that we term “nonmediated” groups since they did not use a collaboration technology (N1, N2, and N3). The groups experienced no intervention and were simply observed. The participants told us in interviews that the behavior we observed was their typical routines. We originally felt that the presence of the 15-page Baxter Planning Worksheet might significantly influence the groups’ interactions, but the groups told us that the use of such a structure was not uncommon when they worked on similar projects. Table 2 provides a summary of the groups.

In group N1, the leader began the kickoff meeting by proposing to build a surgery consultation room and using old pagers to notify families when a surgery was complete, which was immediately accepted by the group. The leader led the discussion and recorded the notes from the discussion. In the next meeting (week 2), the leader again led the discussion and took notes; group members were positive and excited about the project. During the third meeting in week 5, the leader again led the meeting and took notes. This meeting focused on completing the planning worksheet, and during this process three very significant problems emerged. The group members thought the problems required major changes, but the leader would not diverge from the original plan and continued on as though they did not exist. During the final meeting in week 7, members again raised concerns about the project, but the leader ignored them.

The kickoff meeting of group N2 was led by the leader, who immediately focused the group on doctor satisfaction. The leader began working through the planning worksheet and recording the group’s comments on it. The next two meetings (week 2 and week 4) were led by the leader who again worked the group through the planning worksheet, asking questions and recording responses. The group developed ideas for many small steps to improve doctor satisfaction. The leader opened the final meeting in week 6 by abruptly changing the focus of the project from what had been previously discussed to being only a survey of doctors (the survey was a small part of the plan). Group members were openly troubled by this change, but there were no challenges to the leader.

The kickoff meeting for group N3 was led by the leader, who immediately proposed a survey of the doctors, an idea she had previously proposed to the VP but had been told to wait. The group quickly agreed, and the leader led them through the worksheet. During the next meeting in week 4, the leader was absent so her deputy led the meeting and continued filling in the worksheet. Group members raised challenges to the survey but the deputy ignored them. During the final meeting in week 5, the leader led the meeting by reviewing the completed worksheet, listening to comments on it from the group but failing to record them, and discussing items to be included on the survey.

It never settled into a good pattern of interaction and failed to produce a report because of it.

## Collaboration Technology Groups

There were three groups that received the external intervention of a collaboration technology (CT1, CT2, and CT3). See Table 2 for a summary of the groups experiences.

In the kickoff meeting, members of CT1 turned to the leader for direction, but she deferred and assigned another member to be the “coordinator.” By the end of the meeting, the group had reached consensus that the two major issues to focus on were the lack of support for family members and the flow of patients through the ICU. During the next meeting in week 2, the group used the technology to electronically brainstorm solutions to these problems, followed by a combination of electronic and verbal communication to organize their ideas into some candidate solutions. The group wanted to vote on the solutions, but the leader said it was premature and that they needed to better understand the solutions. In the next meeting in week 3, the group wrestled with the technology, used it to discuss the solutions, and collectively chose to integrate the top solutions into one proposal. During the meetings in week 4 and week 5, the group again wrestled with the technology and used it to elaborate on the solution using the items from the planning worksheet. In the final meeting in week 6, the group reviewed and polished the proposal.

The kickoff meeting for group CT2 was raucous with many discussions occurring simultaneously. The leader proposed implementing a nurse-run clinic, an idea of hers that had been rejected by the VP several months earlier. The idea was openly challenged. In the next meeting in week 1, the group electronically and verbally discussed the leader’s idea in a very open and direct manner; the leader was openly hostile to the use of the technology. Nonetheless, by the end of the meeting there was consensus that the group should pursue the clinic project. In next meeting in week 4, the group reviewed the notes and began developing the plan using the planning worksheet. The plan development continued in the next meeting in week 5. The final meeting in week 7 was used to review the plan.

During the kickoff meeting of CT3, the discussion was vibrant and ended with a consensus to pursue employee empowerment as a key step to improving satisfaction. Less than half the group attended the next meeting in week 1; the leader was not there. Without the leader, the group argued and debated issues but made no progress. All the members attended the next meeting in week 7. They began by brainstorming ideas and settled on a computer bulletin board. The group began working through the worksheet but left in disarray and failed to complete the project.

We conclude that the introduction of the collaboration technology disrupted the groups’ routines. Rather than quickly enacting existing hierarchical leader-centric routines, as happened in the nonmediated groups, these groups attempted to adapt to the spirit and features offered by the new technology. This adaptation process was more like “ongoing improvisation enacted by organizational actors trying to make sense of and act coherently in the world” [61, p. 65] than the somewhat rational process suggested by our reading of Burke et al. [14]. The experiences of group CT1 illustrate this improvisation (much of the same pattern occurred in CT2, and less so in CT3). In the first meeting, members of CT1 used a simultaneous mix of electronic and verbal discussion. In the second meeting, one group member argued for using just verbal discussion, but the group decided to start with just electronic discussion; after 35 minutes they switched to just verbal discussion, using the technology to take notes. In the next meeting, they began with just verbal discussion, with one scribe taking notes, but gradually switched to just electronic discussion after 20 minutes. In subsequent meetings, they worked in two-peron subgroups, using verbal discussion to coordinate the use of the technology.

We see individuals in this group (CT1) struggling to make sense out of how to effectively use the features of the technology such as parallel communication, anonymity, and shared meeting memory. These features offered new ways of working that did not occur in the routines enacted by the nonmediated groups. For example, in the nonmediated groups, the leader dominated the discussions (in contrast to parallel communication enabled by the technology), all the comments were verbal and therefore not anonymous (in contrast to anonymous text communication), and the leader maintained the meeting memory (in contrast to the shared meeting memory provided by the technology). Members of CT1 tried using these new features in one way, only to switch to a new way, and switch again and again before settling on one work process. The same is true of roles; members of CT1 invented several roles for different group members (e.g., coordinator, scribe) before abandoning them and rotating activities among group members.

This disruption had an effect on the process of group development. Table 4a shows the codings reflecting the punctuated equilibrium model while Table 4b codings reflect the stage model. Italicized items in the tables indicate those cells for which the codings match the model.

We see a fairly consistent pattern in Table 4a. In none of these three groups was there a quick adoption of work processes or a project goal, although the leader of one group (CT3) was quickly accepted. There was no long period of interaction matching the original pattern, as groups experimented with the technology [14]. The work prior to the midpoint was unfocused for one group (CT3), which never did write a project plan as directed by the VP. The initial work in CT2 was unfocused until the leadership issues were resolved in the second meeting. No group experienced a midpoint transition, although task performance increased as the groups worked on the projects.

We also see a fairly consistent pattern in Table 4b. All three groups performed activities associated with the forming stage in the first meeting or two: examination of goals and task boundaries, questioning of methods, and questioning of leadership. All three also experienced the problems of control, emotions, and hostility associated with the storming stage in the first or second meetings. For group CT3, the problems and hostility continued into their third and final meeting, so one might conclude they never made it past the storming stage. In the other two groups, the storming was fol lowed by the development of norms that were used for the remainder of the project. The intensity of task focus and performance increased in later meetings.

Once again, the only group that failed to fit the expected pattern of interaction also failed to create their deliverables. As with N3, group CT3 did not settle into a pattern of interacting and did not produce their deliverables on time. Neither N3 nor CT3, the two groups that failed to complete their deliverables, appear to fit the expected patterns of group development and did not appear to have become functioning groups over the course of the project.

## Discussion

Why would the g roups that used a collaboration technolog y be more likely to follow the stage model of group development while groups that did not use the technology follow the punctuated equilibrium model? Clearly, one plausible explanation lies in the nature of the technology. The new work processes enabled by the technology’s features might have induced different patterns of group development or have changed the ways a group adapted. As mentioned above, these collaborative technologies are designed to influence the process by which groups interact. However, we do not believe that the changes we observed are purely due to the nature of the technology per se.

Instead, we believe that the unfamiliar technology hindered group members in enacting their typical group routines. While all the teams were able to perform teamwork (the ostensive aspect of the routines they were invoking), they were unable to replicate the performance portion of their typical routines. Lacking the ability to perform their routines, they could not replicate them and thus needed to find new ways to perform their routines. It was this hindrance (not the technology itself) that induced the changes in behavior: any disruption that hinders the enactment of routines—technology or otherwise—is likely to induce these same changes in group development. In the sections below, we begin by developing our integrated model of group development, then apply it to these groups, and later return to the issue of technology-induced change.

One question we often get when we present this research is why didn’t you study the nonmediated work processes of the collaboration technology groups? The answer is that it is quite literally impossible. Each of these groups was formed for this project, worked on the project, and then disbanded. We studied the entire life of the groups; there were no nonmediated work processes that could have been observed for the collaboration technology groups. Many of the participants in these groups worked together in other groups prior to the project and after the project, but the specific individuals in each group only functioned as a group for the duration of the project. Thus, as we argue in theory, the participants arrived at the first meeting of each group with expectations about what nonmediated work processes would be. We used interviews to attempt to understand whether the work processes we observed for this project were similar to the work routines these individuals had used in other groups and were unanimously told that the work processes in the nonmediated groups were their typical work processes and that the work processes in the collaboration technology groups were “not typical.” An alternative research design would have been to introduce the collaboration technology into some groups midway through their work processes (to understand a before and after view) but then we would not have been able to study the effect of technology on the entire process of group development, which is the process from initial formation through disbandment.

## Toward an Integrated Model of Group Development

When individuals first meet as a newly formed group (as did all of the groups in this study), they search for ways to organize and make sense of the task they are to perform and the work processes they will use to interact with other group members. This sensemaking often entails drawing on past experiences and importing knowledge from past work into the current situation [9, 31, 87]. One important component of past work is the routines that group members can draw on to help them establish new work patterns [6, 29]. Routines for group work provide knowledge templates that describe appropriate roles, responsibilities, procedures, and communication patterns for how individuals should interact [8, 9, 47].

The extent to which the group members share common routines is likely to differ depending on the familiarity that the individual group members have with each other and with the task the group has been asked to perform [48]. The routines associated with group work have both an abstract component (ostensive aspect) to them and a performance aspect to them [65]. Established groups with long histories of working together on well-understood tasks are most likely to share a common set of routines and shared performances of these routines for group work processes that have been well honed through prior interactions [28, 43, 47, 57]. Little discussion will be needed about how to perform the task or the role each group member will play because these routines will have evolved into habitual routines for the group; the group will be able to quickly begin work on a task [48].

## Group Development with Shared Routines

When a set of individuals in a newly formed group share common group routines, they can quickly enact these routines and begin performing activities associated with the group task. Having shared common performances in the routines that fit together enables the group to quickly adopt these routines to guide its interaction. Group members simply enact these routines without debate [33, 63]. These groups will not need to form, storm, and norm about group work activities because there is no need for discussion; group members know how to work together. Instead, they will quickly start to perform task activities—just like operating room groups [22]. Thus, these groups will follow the initial behavior theorized by the punctuated equilibrium model—the rapid adoption of shared work processes and a very quick movement into performing the task assigned to the group.

However, these groups may find that they jumped into the task-related work too quickly, without enough attention to the task goals and their work processes. After an initial period of performing task activities, the members may feel conflict over their quickly adopted task goal and work processes and they may consider alternatives to their initial task activities (i.e., a midpoint transition). It is unclear if this reconsideration of the group’s goals and processes is always at the exact midpoint but in groups that meet for a rather short period of time (e.g., days or weeks); it appears as though the midpoint triggers the group’s desire to reevaluate their goals and processes, as the punctuated equilibrium model predicts. Future work needs to focus on the timing of this transition in groups that are working on more complex problems, over a longer period of time, such as months or years. Our teams had a fixed deadline for completing the project so it was easy for them to identify the midpoint. For teams without a fixed time line, it may be more difficult for teams to identify a midpoint.

## Group Development Without Shared Routines

When individuals in a group do not share ways to perform a common routine for group work, even if they share the ostensive aspect of the routine, they cannot quickly come to consensus on how to perform the routine. For example, when a group encounters a new situation, its members are unable to quickly perform a routine because the situation does not fully match any prior situation [26, 57]. Likewise, when individuals from different organizational cultures meet as a group, it may be difficult to quickly enact routines because they are less likely to share common routines (both ostensive and performance aspects) that match. Other circumstances may also make it difficult for a group to enact common routines. For instance, when a new technology is introduced, or an unfamiliar task is assigned, group members are much less likely to have shared common ways to perform the routines that match the situation.

Therefore, the group must first work together to establish a set of norms for work processes and group member roles (i.e., routines in the making) to guide their behavior [57, 68]. Until group members develop and agree on work processes and roles, they will be unable to move forward and work on the task [9, 47]. Group members must discuss and negotiate the work processes and roles before they can begin working on the task. If a new technology is involved, they will try the new features it offers, learn from their experiences, and adapt their new work process to use the new features (if any) that help them [14]. Groups will need to get to know each other (forming), experience conflict over work process norms and task goals (storming), and come to agreement on them before they can perform the task [56]. Thus, their behavior will match that of the stage model of development.

By taking this additional time to form, storm, and norm, these groups may create greater group member buy-in to the work processes, roles, and task goals as compared to those groups that begin the project with shared common routines. Furthermore, group members will establish both a shared ostensive understanding of a routine and shared performances of the routine with one another. Therefore, it may be less likely that these groups will undergo additional conflict in their task processes, roles, or goals at the midpoint because of these discussions.

## An Integrated Model of Group Development

Figure 1 summarizes our integrated model. We argue that when a group comes together, the members enact routines that they believe fit the situation. If group members share common routines and they are able to perform them, the group will quickly enact those routines and begin performing task activities. However, if group members lack the ability to perform shared common routines that fit the situation (e.g., using a new technology) even if they share ostensive aspects of a routine, or if there is disagreement over the routines to enact, members must work together to form (examine task goals, work processes, and roles), storm (debate these), and norm (establish accepted goals, processes, and roles) before they can perform task activities.

As the group members perform task activities, they periodically reflect on their progress. This reflection may be triggered by endogenous factors (e.g., the completion of an activity, an unexpected event, or a disagreement) or exogenous factors (e.g., new directives, reassignments of group members, or the passage of time—especially the midpoint). If this reflection identifies a discrepancy or triggers conflict over work processes, roles, or task goals, then the group is likely to reexamine them [48] by going through another cycle of forming, storming, and norming until they are able to resume task performance. If this was triggered by the recognition of time passing, then it could be seen as a midpoint transition or at least a midpoint evaluation [31, 58, 59]. We believe that groups that immediately enact shared common routines with no forming, storming, and norming are more prone to having this reflection and to having this reflection trigger forming, storming, and norming because they did not go through these processes initially and explicitly discuss group members’ assumptions.

## Interpreting the Group Development Patterns in Our Groups

## Nonmediated Groups

The three groups that did not use a collaboration technology quickly adopted and performed existing routines and began to work on the task. There was no initial discussion of group work processes. Group members simply enacted their shared common routines. All three groups independently enacted virtually the same leader-centered routine and quickly adopted the project goal proposed by the leader—as might be expected in a hierarchical culture often found in hospitals [2, 39].

We conclude that even though group members came from two different organizations and had not worked together before as a group, the organizations had a long history of working together and shared a common culture for group work processes that engendered the development of common group work routines (both ostensive and performance aspects of the routines). These common shared routines enabled these newly formed groups to quickly come to consensus on work processes and roles so they could begin performing task activities. These common shared routines were very leader-centric, so when the leader proposed a task goal, group members followed the routine and simply accepted it.

All three groups reflected on their work at the approximate midpoint of the project (since the groups were given a definitive deadline for the project, they were well aware of the midpoint) and felt some dissatisfaction with the progress. All three groups experienced conflict before the group leader, following a leader-centric routine, stepped in and quashed the group’s concerns. Two groups proceeded with no changes while the third followed the leader’s change in direction.

## Collaboration Technology Groups

In contrast to the nonmediated groups, because the technology was unfamiliar, the technology groups lacked shared common ways to perform their shared routines for group work (due to the injection of the collaboration technology). The technology presented a novel situation for which their existing routines could not be enacted. The technology offered new features, such as parallel communication, anonymity, and a shared meeting memory, that group members had to learn about. These groups had to experiment with the new features and revise their group work process and roles and form norms (routines to be). The technology we used was particularly disruptive to the way these groups typically interacted because its spirit and structural features were participative, whereas the existing routines (as observed in the nonmediated groups) were hierarchical and leader-centric.

As the descriptions of the initial meetings of these three groups show, the development of new work processes and roles was not easy. All three technology groups began with forming behaviors. The first two meetings of all three groups involved experimenting with incorporating the technology features into their work processes. The groups tried different processes, rejected them, and then tried new ones during the course of their first two meetings. Lacking routines that fit the situation, the groups experimented and attempted to develop new norms (cf. [14]). They formed and stormed over work processes, roles, and the project goal. Even with the 15-page Baxter Planning Worksheet, the groups did not quickly settle on a structured set of group work processes by which to use it. Once the routines were disrupted, the usual leader-centric routine was not automatically adopted. Instead, the participative spirit and features of the technology presented an alternative set of work processes and roles that groups explored (much to the chagrin of one group leader).

Two groups (CT1 and CT2) moved past the forming and storming stages in later meetings (establishing norms, building relationships and developing cohesiveness, and then performing the task). Group CT3 was not as conscientious about the project as the other two groups and ultimately failed to complete the project. Thus, it is not surprising that their behavior does not fit either the stage model or punctuated equilibrium model very well. Our proposed group development model predicts this outcome in groups that never find norms from which to work and thus are unable to move into the performance stage of the group interaction.

## The Role of Technology

A potential rival hypothesis to our conclusions is that the collaboration technology itself could have induced the changes in group development. In other words, it is not the hindrance of enacting routines, but rather the technology itself, that induced the nature of group development. For example, perhaps the increased “rationality” in the technology affected the groups’ processes, causing them to first consider how they should work together before plunging into the task. Therefore, these groups better matched the stage model. The technology affected the way in which interactions occurred in the groups, but it did not set the agenda for the activities that were undertaken during each meeting. While many groups that use collaborative technologies begin by developing an agenda, this was not a step that the groups in our study had to take. The group interaction processes evolved like those in other small groups (e.g., [45]) rather than the “rational” highly structured processes in larger groups’ uses of collaboration technology (e.g., [80]). Thus, we do not see “increased rationality” as a plausible explanation for our results.

We believe that this explanation had less of an impact than the disruption of the performance of routines. From a theoretical point of view, which factor is likely to have a greater impact on group development over the long term—hindrance of routines or increased “rationality”? If an organization reached the point at which the use of a collaboration technology was part of its common culture, the members of newly formed groups would have routines for technology use (but, of course, this would take a long time to occur [85]). In this situation, members of newly formed groups would not need to form, storm, and norm over work processes or roles because it would be well understood how to perform their routines. Instead, members of newly formed groups would enact their routines and immediately jump into performing the task at hand with little or no discussion of work processes or roles. Even though group members might never have met before, the routines, derived from a shared culture (both the ostensive and the performative aspects of a routine), would start the group in an equilibrium state. This would be true regardless of the spirit of the technology.

Therefore, we conclude that the primary driving factor in the nature of group development is not technology per se, but rather the extent to which technology disrupts shared common routines. If the technology disrupts routines, group development is likely to unfold in a very different way than if technology does not disrupt routines. Novel technology offers new features that can disrupt existing routines, both intentionally and unintentionally [84]. If group members become interested in using the new technology features, then they must discuss how to integrate those new features into their work processes before they can begin to work on the task [10]. Typically, this will also involve trial and feedback, leading to adaptations and additional cycles of trial and adaptation until the process stabilizes into new routines [10, 84].

## Implications for Research and Practice

This study suffers from the usual limitations of field research. We studied a limited number of groups drawn from two health care organizations, so it is possible that factors idiosyncratic to those organizations or to the health care environment had a significant influence on our observations. Nonetheless, we believe that these results have several implications for future research. First, to address the limitations, more research is needed over a larger number of groups in a variety of different settings to replicate and extend these results and to investigate the boundary conditions to the applicability of our model. We need to study groups that have routines for collaboration technology use to ensure that it is not the technology itself that induced this pattern of group development.

Second, we believe that our conclusions have implications for group development when new collaboration technologies are introduced in work groups or virtual teams. Because it is unlikely that all members would have performance routines for the use of an unfamiliar technology, we would expect group development to begin with forming, storming, and norming. This would suggest the need for time to be set aside for the development of shared norms (routines to be) for group work, time that might need to include the use of synchronous media (e.g., telephone, face-to-face discussion) for the forming, storming, and norming stages [21]. Understanding and expecting the need for initial procedural discussions and disagreements could facilitate the transition from old routines to new routines. Likewise, including training on how to adapt traditional group work routines into the new technology-supported environment could also ease the transition.

This conclusion applies only to collaboration technologies new to a group. The extent to which a technology is familiar to group members (and the extent to which they share routines for its use) will influence the need to form, storm, and norm before they begin working on task activities. For virtual teams where the culture is shared and the technologies used are familiar to the members—even for teams that have never met before—we can expect little need for initial forming, storming, and norming; a highly synchronous first meeting is likely unnecessary.

Third, our results suggest that the group development process will change over time. We expect that the initial use of collaboration technologies in organizations would invoke the need for initial forming, norming, and storming; the results found in early research, however, would likely not apply to ongoing use once group work routines have been developed and become assimilated into the organizational culture. At this point, groups would likely bypass forming, norming, and storming and move quickly into performing.

Fourth, we need more research on newly formed groups in organizations that have a well-established culture and widely shared routines for virtual team work. Much research on virtual teams has examined the “start-up” phase in which the technology is novel and there is a lack of shared routines for its use. Our research from the start-up phase may not apply to later phases once groups have developed routines for using the technologies.

Fifth, these conclusions have implications for laboratory research, which is commonly done on newly formed groups using unfamiliar technology; these groups would more likely need initial forming, storming, and norming than would groups in organizations after the widespread deployment of collaboration technologies. To the extent that the need to form, storm, and norm before performing the task would affect the constructs of interest in this study, we should be extremely cautious in generalizing the conclusions to organizational groups. For example, the need to form, storm, and norm increases the time needed to perform the task and changes the media best suited to the task, so we should be particularly cautious in generalizing conclusions about the inefficiency of using technology or media choices in laboratory settings from groups lacking routines to the ongoing use of technology in the field in organizations with well-established routines for technology use. The same may be true for performance. The first-time use of any technology is likely to lead to lower performance than subsequent uses of the same technology, as groups learn more about how to best use the technology to improve performance.

Sixth, there are many novel situations that would hinder the use of habitual routines [9, 33]. The introduction of new collaboration technologies is just one such hindrance, because new technologies often impede the use of existing routines [3, 4, 9, 26, 45, 70, 81]. Forming groups whose members are from different cultures with different routines for group work would likely also result in the need for initial forming, storming, and norming over work processes (e.g., cross-cultural groups, global virtual teams, interorganizational groups).

Finally, we conclude that the forming, storming, and norming stages provide a useful model of the processes by which groups adapt routines into mutually accepted interaction patterns. It would be of interest to understand the cyclicality of the group formation phases in understanding how change affects the formation and reformation of routines (see [59]). Future work needs to be done to determine how the management of the task and the management of the group affects group formation as well as how different tasks and different social context influence the way in which a group evolves. Furthermore, future work that utilizes various perspectives of time and the multitasking of groups would aid in our understanding of group processes beyond a single project (see [46]).

## Conclusion

Group developm ent has been a long -standing research issue in the study of both computer-mediated and non-computer-mediated groups. Past research suggests that there are two different processes for group development: the stage model and the punctuated equilibrium model. It has been argued that these models are complementary and are followed simultaneously by groups, their differences lying in the temporal dimensions and group functions that are focused on by the researcher. We argue that these models are distinctly different models and are invoked in groups under different circumstances. Our integrated model of group development focuses on the enactment or disruption of shared group routines and reconciles the differences between these two models to present one overarching model that combines both group development models into one new model. We believe that our model opens a new direction in the study of group development.
