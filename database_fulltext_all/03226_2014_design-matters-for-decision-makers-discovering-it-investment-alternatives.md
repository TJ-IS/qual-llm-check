# Design matters for decision makers: Discovering IT investment alternatives

Jane Elisabeth Frisk<sup>1</sup>, Rikard Lindgren<sup>2</sup> and Lars Mathiassen<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Business Administration, School of Business, Economics and Law at University of Gothenburg, Sweden; <sup>2</sup>Department of Applied Information Technology, University of Gothenburg, Sweden and School of Business and IT, University of Borås Sweden; <sup>3</sup>Department of Computer Information Systems, Georgia State University, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Jane Elisabeth Frisk, School of Business, Economics and Law at University of Gothenburg, Vasagatan 1, SE 405 30 Gothenburg, Sweden. E-mail: elisabeth.frisk@handels.gu.se

## Abstract

The literature has traditionally focused on how decision makers choose among alternatives based on rational behaviors, organizational politics, and organized anarchies. However, there is little research on decision making as a creative process where managers discover and evaluate alternatives. We address this gap by proposing a design approach to transform decisions in organizations. The approach views decision making as a creative and adaptive process in which managers recursively collect and interpret heterogeneous evidence, explore and test different ideas, and discover and evaluate alternatives. While the approach is rooted in the know-how of the involved stakeholders, its underpinning design attitude invites managers to better understand possible alternatives based on analyses of structured as well as unstructured data. To assess its utility, we applied the approach to IT investment evaluations through a Collaborative Practice Research project involving Swedish public organizations. Building on the project findings, we discuss the proposed approach and spell out its implications for transforming decision making in organizations. Keywords: decision making; design thinking; IT investment; action research

## Introduction

Strategic decisions made by managers are crucial because they commit resources, set precedents, and direct actions (Mitchell et al, 2011). Ultimately, these decisions shape an organization’s general direction (Dean & Sharfman, 1996). Eisenhardt & Zbaracki (1992) suggest that received decision-making theory primarily has focused on the major choice perspectives rooted in rational behaviors, organizational politics, and organized anarchies. Given the incomplete assumptions of these perspectives (Boland et al, 2008), there have been calls for studies that produce more realistic views of decision making (Eisenhardt & Zbaracki, 1992). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it has been argued that integrative models that blend different perspectives (Hitt & Tyler, 1991; Elbanna & Child, 2007) can help provide a better understanding of organizational decision making (Shrivastava & Grant, 1985; Papadakis et al, 1998; Sutcliffe & McNamara, 2001). Such knowledge promises to support approaches that allow more creative and adaptive decision making (Eisenhardt & Zbaracki, 1992).

While organizations engage in different types of strategic decisions, those related to IT investments have become increasingly important and they illustrate the involved challenges well (Boonstra, 2003). The IT productivity paradox was coined some 20 years ago to highlight that the relationship between IT and productivity was poorly understood (Solow, 1987), and that managers rarely justi<sup>fi</sup>ed their investment decisions (Brynjolfsson, 1993).

For every claim of IT-enabled value delivery, there was an equal number of claims of poor returns. Over the years, however, researchers have suggested several ways out of this paradox. One of the more critical focuses on current limitations and possible improvements in how IT investment alternatives are discovery- and evaluated (Willcocks & Lester, 1996). Rooted in early IT applications that replaced manual work, discovery, and evaluation of IT investment alternatives have traditionally focused on <sup>fi</sup>nancial issues and have been supported by readily available instruments that measure tangible impacts (Avgerou, 2000). However, developing a comprehensive understanding of IT investment alternatives has since turned into a much more complex task for public and private organizations alike (Bannister, 2001, 2005).

The fact that business trajectories and IT choices have become virtually intertwined has triggered the need for new IT evaluation approaches that recognize tangible as well as intangible paths to value creation (Jones & Hughes, 2001; Stockdale & Standing, 2006; Kohli & Grover, 2008). Still, although the IT productivity paradox is increasingly seen as a thing of the past (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996; Mithas et al, 2012), the most frequently used evaluation approach remains the <sup>fi</sup>nancial business case building on calculation of the return on investment (Ward & Daniel, 2006). Moreover, managers tend to disregard other approaches due to the inherent complexities involved in more comprehensive IT evaluation (Weil & Ross, 2004).

There is therefore a need for new approaches to discover and evaluate alternatives that can help transform IT investment decision making. Extant research has criticized the <sup>fi</sup>nancial perspective for being too mechanistic, observing that it pays marginal attention to the organizational context, sees IT as technical systems rather than social systems, and neglects human dimensions of sense making (Jones & Hughes, 2001; Stockdale & Standing, 2006). In contrast, with an interpretive perspective, discovery and evaluation of alternatives become dynamic political processes in which stakeholders on multiple levels interact to negotiate and learn about the socio-technical consequences of IT investment alternatives (Walsham, 1993, 1999; Jones & Hughes, 2001; Stockdale & Standing, 2006). The interpretive perspective has so far received marginal attention from decision makers (Stockdale & Standing, 2006), and few attempts, if any, have been made to improve IT investment decisions by combining the two perspectives into an integrated, comprehensive approach.

Against this backdrop, we propose a new approach to organizational decision making in the particular context of IT investments. It is based on design thinking (Boland and Collopy, 2004; Boland et al, 2008), evaluation theory (Guba & Lincoln, 1989; Remenyi & Sherwood-Smith, 1999), and IS evaluation theory (Jones & Hughes, 2001; Stockdale & Standing, 2006). In contrast to the choicefocused perspectives, it sees decision making as a creative and adaptive process in which managers recursively collect and interpret heterogeneous evidence, explore and test different ideas, and discover and evaluate alternatives.

While it is rooted in the know-how of the involved stakeholders, its underpinning design attitude (Boland and Collopy, 2004; Boland et al, 2008) invites managers to discover and evaluate IT alternatives by measuring the tangible and by sensing the intangible. Despite its particular focus on IT investment decisions, the approach can be adapted to most decision situations where managers seek to discover and evaluate alternatives based on analyses of combinations of structured and unstructured data.

Accordingly, we investigate the following research question: How can managers discover and evaluate IT alternatives by measuring the tangible and sensing the intangible? Given our objective to contribute to the understanding and transformation of decision processes in organizations, we applied the approach to IT investment decision making based on a Collaborative Practice Research project (Mathiassen, 2002) involving Swedish public organizations. Relying on opportunistic sampling, our multiyear collaboration was executed during a period in which the involved organizations faced many and complex IT investment decisions. Inspired by Leidner & Elam (1995), we wanted to understand how our approach could help them combine data from multiple sources to evaluate alternative investment decisions and discover new ones, and we also wanted to explore the relationship between advanced IT support and improved organizational intelligence in their decision making.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. First, we review the related literature to develop the proposed design approach to decision making. Next, we describe the research design and context, and present <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our Collaborative Practice Research project. Finally, we explicate our contributions to theory, discuss the utility of the approach, and articulate its implications for decision processes and business intelligence systems adoption in organizations.

## Theoretical background

## The choice paradigm

Decision processes are crucial because they shape the course of an organization (Dean & Sharfman, 1996; Mitchell et al, 2011). In fact, capabilities to make important decisions represent enduring sources of competitive advantage (Eisenhardt & Zbaracki, 1992). While the available literature on organizational decision making includes a plethora of ideas by many authors (Langley et al, 1995), three major perspectives have dominated this mature research <sup>fi</sup>eld (Eisenhardt & Zbaracki, 1992) and they all focus on how managers make choice among the alternatives that are presented to them (Boland et al, 2008).

First, the rational model of choice (e.g., Anderson, 1983) suggests that actors enter decision situations with known objectives, which determine the consequential value of a decision. On the basis of appropriate information sources (structured), actors develop a set of alternative actions from which they then select the optimal alternative. In reaction to the ‘straw man’ of purely rational choice, researchers have acknowledged that processes are often bounded rational. That is, in environments characterized by high uncertainty and external control, decision makers can at best strive for rationality by using more information, creating diverse viewpoints, and making systematic comparisons between alternatives (Dean and Sharfman, 1996). Accordingly, decision makers usually move along the continuum between rationality and bounded rationality (Schweiger et al, 1986).

Second, the perspective of politics and power was put forward as a reaction to the prevailing economic assumptions that organizations are driven by a single and superordinate goal. Because people are not collectively rational (March, 1962), this perspective de<sup>fi</sup>nes decision making as a process by which con<sup>fl</sup>icts are resolved among individuals with competing interests (e.g., different bets on the shape of the future, biases induced by position, and clashes in personal ambitions). Simply put, this research argues that the most powerful people and their desires and subsequent choices govern decisions (Salancik & Pfeffer, 1974).

Third, Cohen et al (1972) <sup>fi</sup>rst articulated the garbage can model as a response to the rational and political models of choice. In contrast to these, this model addresses decision making in complex, unstable, and ambiguous settings called organized anarchies. Its basic premise is that decision makers do not necessarily understand their goals before choice, but rather through the actions they undertake. While decision making is seen as a stochastic meeting of choices looking for problems and vice versa, the model recognizes the importance of chance. Hence, it argues that the fuzzy character of decisions can be traced to a random con<sup>fl</sup>uence of people, problems, solutions, and choice opportunities.

Despite the fact that research on decision processes has progressed, it has been argued that there is still too much emphasis on the incomplete assumptions of the dominating perspectives (Boland et al, 2008). So, instead of continuing the debates about single organizational goals and perfect rationality, it is time to produce more realistic views of decision making (Eisenhardt & Zbaracki, 1992). In particular, there is a need to integrate different perspectives (Hitt & Tyler, 1991; Elbanna & Child, 2007) to provide a better understanding of decision processes in organizations (Shrivastava & Grant, 1985; Papadakis et al, 1998; Sutcliffe & McNamara, 2001). Such integrative studies can help organizations transform their decision making to become more creative and adaptable (Eisenhardt & Zbaracki, 1992). This requires a break away from the traditional choice perspectives toward broader views of decision making. Accordingly, we draw on design thinking to understand decision making as a multifaceted process of reality creation and discovery (Laroche, 1995; Boland et al, 2008).

## A design attitude

More than 40 years ago, Simon (1969) argued that design is central to management. While management scholars largely ignored his arguments for long, there has recently been a renewed interest in design science in the wider <sup>fi</sup>eld of organization research (Romme, 2003). A design approach runs counter to the traditional choice paradigm and invites more multidimensional approaches. Boland and Collopy (2004, p. 4) argue that managers can improve their actions by taking a step from a choice attitude to a design attitude, which can be traced back to Simon (1969).

The difference between the two perspectives is that the choice attitude ‘assumes it is easy to come up with alternatives to consider, but dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to choose among them. The design attitude toward decision making, in contrast, assumes that it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to design a good alternative, but once you have developed truly a great one, the decision about which alternative to select becomes trivial’ (Boland and Collopy, 2004, p. 4). Adopting a design attitude means that instead of being given, alternatives must be discovered. Boland and Collopy argue that such an attitude provides an opportunity to integrate decision making and sense making as proposed by previous research on organizational decision making (Elbanna & Child, 2007).

Accordingly, Boland et al (2008) view design as a becoming and unfolding recursive process conditioned by available resources, skills, and time. Relying on heterogeneous inputs, multiple ideas, and novel alternatives, such processes involve envisioning a new situation, creating artifacts that represent ideas, and inspiring others to follow (Yoo et al, 2006). Acting as designers, managers understand the importance of ‘commitment to and enthusiasm for a constructive engagement with the actors who will use the artifacts and realize the events shaped by their designs over time’ (Orlikowski, 2004, p. 92). While designs are representations of possible realities, they are incomplete until integrated into everyday human action (Orlikowski, 2004). Thus, given the importance of language in design, it is necessary to form a vocabulary to achieve appropriate functionality and enable dialogue across professions (Boland et al, 2008).

Simon (1969) argued that the role of managers is to act responsibly in their design efforts to transform existing situations into more preferable situations with processes, products, and services that leverage sustainable societal values. According to van Aken (2007, p. 68), ‘a <sup>fi</sup>eld problem is a problem regarding the realization of a “better” reality’. On the basis of this notion, designing implies an integral approach to achieve real improvements in organizational performance on one or more criteria (Boland et al, 2008). While business performance is in the interest of all potential stakeholders, these criteria may include everything from <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance to quality of working life (van Aken, 2007). A <sup>fi</sup>eld problem should therefore be approached with an array of in<sup>fl</sup>uences that are both pro<sup>fi</sup>table and socially acceptable.

Design activities are endemic in many professions. Experienced professionals in engineering, law, and medicine have internalized the design science into their disciplines to support discovery of alternative solutions for various types of <sup>fi</sup>eld problems. Hevner et al (2004) argue that most of the work performed by IS practitioners (including managers) deals with design, that is, ‘the purposeful organization of resources to accomplish a goal’ (p. 78). Hence, we assume that design in general will play an increasingly important role in decision processes, and that managers in particular will become more actively engaged. At the same time, a design perspective has recently been proposed as an effective, working alternative to the dysfunctions of the current approach to public sector management (Wastell, 2011). IS researchers are therefore well positioned to help establish management as designing in a wide variety of decision situations, simply because of their mature knowledge of design (Wastell, 2010).

## IT investment decisions

To develop a design approach to decision making, we turn to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c area of IT investments. With a focus on how organizations can leverage IT to generate business value (Boonstra, 2003), we de<sup>fi</sup>ne an IT investment decision as a decision to invest or not invest in new IT. Recent research has explored this concept to better understand its ambiguous meaning (Remenyi et al, 2007). While most researchers have dealt with the relationship between IT inputs and tangible outcomes (Kohli & Grover, 2008), such outcomes are usually understood in <sup>fi</sup>nancial terms as those that directly affect an organization’s pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. Chau et al (2007) note that current research provides rich documentation that IT investment can have positive, tangible outcomes (e.g., additional sales, business productivity, cost savings, and market share). In addition, IT investment evaluation has dealt with tangible outcomes in technical terms such as performance, reliability, robustness, and security of new IT applications (Klecun & Cornford, 2005).

Despite pressures to use prevailing <sup>fi</sup>nancial and technical measures, there has been a growing recognition of the complementary and important role intangible indicators play in IT investment decisions (see, e.g., Belcher & Watson, 1993). For example, standard business measures of productivity are largely irrelevant to public organizations and their IT investments (Bannister, 2001). Accordingly, Kohli & Grover (2008) propose that business value must be expanded to include indirect and intangible indicators such as business alignment, customer service, process agility, user satisfaction, and workplace <sup>fl</sup>exibility. However, intangibles are more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to estimate and measure, and Murphy & Simon (2002) suggest that an activity resulting in direct cost reduction is more tangible than one that enables investments in customer service.

Managers appear to rely primarily on tangible values, simply because they are unable to capture intangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Given this situation, researchers have drawn on other reference disciplines to further theorize IT investment evaluation. In social science, evaluation is generally understood as a multifaceted human activity (Alking, 2004) with different types of evaluation for different purposes, that is, methods relying on measurement, description of intended usage, and valuing based on judgment. Evaluations may be either summative or formative (Remenyi & Sherwood-Smith, 1999). The former focuses on <sup>fi</sup>nancial issues and seeks to assess tangible impacts and outcomes in terms of merit, worth, and signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. The latter builds on an open-ended design and aims at integrating qualitative information from diverse sources to stimulate continuous re<sup>fl</sup>ection and learning among multiple stakeholders (Guba & Lincoln, 1989). Interestingly, there is an abundance of approaches, methods, and models available in the IT investment literature (Irani & Love, 2001), which creates a fragmented discourse with a wide-ranging scope (Berghout & Remenyi, 2005) involving multiple concerns (Cronk & Fitzgerald, 1999).

Drawing on design thinking and including different perspectives, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne IT investment evaluations as those activities that are designed to assess the expected tangible and intangible outcomes of an IT investment, to discover viable alternatives, and eventually to help make decisions within a strategic framework (cf., Huerta & Sánchez, 1999; Boonstra, 2003). Moreover, as the characteristics of a project and its organizational environment shape the way an IT investment decision is handled, we believe there is no such thing as a best method for all IT investment situations (Walsham, 1999).

## The prevailing agenda

As further background, it is appropriate to reiterate the <sup>fi</sup>nancial paradigm that still dominates IT investment decision making (Symons, 1991; Walsham, 1999; Wilson & Howcroft, 2005). This paradigm is based on formal, overt, and quantitative methods that attempt to de<sup>fi</sup>ne and measure IT investment costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to support decision making. There are many such methods readily available, including cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis, discounted cash <sup>fl</sup>ow and payback, and return on investment, which all share a speci<sup>fi</sup>c emphasis on shareholder interests. Building on a calculation of the latter, the <sup>fi</sup>nancial business case is the most frequently observed method (Ward & Daniel, 2006). Next to the dominance of these <sup>fi</sup>nancial methods, Hirschheim & Smithson (1999) assert that decisions taken by professionals often rely on technical evaluation indicators.

Hence, formal IT evaluations rest on documented, mechanistic processes to monitor and measure broad costs, technical indicators, and perceived accrued bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Symons, 1991). Although it would appear that such evaluation activities have considerable legitimacy (Hirschheim & Smithson, 1999), Introna (1997) argues that they represent accountable, objective, and rational management ideals, rather than approaches to assist IT investments. From this point of view, these IT evaluations are ritualistic rather than substantive, and the usefulness of formal approaches has therefore been and continues to be the cause of much debate (Walsham, 1999).

First, while it is not clear which <sup>fi</sup>nancial metrics and technical indicators to include, there are inherent dif<sup>fi</sup>culties associated with quantifying estimates and subsequent analyses (Walsham, 1999). Second, the prevailing paradigm has been criticized for its inadequacy to assess intangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of IT investments, simply because not all business values are easily quanti<sup>fi</sup>able in monetary terms (Symons, 1991). Third, treating IT evaluation as a technical problem overlooks cultural, political, and social dimensions and leads to restricted conclusions (Hirschheim & Smithson, 1999). Fourth, because these approaches tend to ignore the timing of IT investment decisions, little attention is paid to understanding the context of affected stakeholders (Klecun & Cornford, 2005).

Attempts have been made to develop and adopt more appropriate <sup>fi</sup>nancial and technical IT evaluation methods. The IT Balanced Scorecard (Willcocks et al, 1998), based on Kaplan and Norton’s Balanced Scorecard model, is an illustrative example. As Hirschheim & Smithson (1999) note, however, practitioners and stakeholders have viewed these alternatives with much skepticism. Re<sup>fl</sup>ective of the premise that stakeholders often view the success of an IT investment decision differently, Seddon et al (1999) proposed the Organizational IT Effectiveness Model in response. This model depicts business value from both a management perspective (based on economic measures) and a user perspective (based on value perceptions). By doing so, it complements DeLone & McLean’s (2003) IS success model by recognizing that stakeholders may draw different conclusions about the outcome of a particular IT investment.

Multi-criteria models have also been developed to move away from simple <sup>fi</sup>nancial measures of business value (Robson, 1997). Further, progressive approaches such as the Bene<sup>fi</sup>ts Management Approach and the Active Bene<sup>fi</sup>ts Realization Approach have outlined comprehensive evaluation frameworks covering both quantitative and qualitative indicators (e.g., Ward, 1990). Walsham (1999) notes, however, that these approaches have only been scarcely adopted and with limited success. This is because of the complexity that stems from their large scope, following the multiplicity of involved evaluation indicators and stakeholders. Overall, adapting the general principles of formal IT evaluation approaches to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c conditions of individual organizations has proved to be a challenge.

## An interpretive perspective

Considering that IT investment decisions represent a complex task for managers and given the lack of appropriate means to assess the business impact of their decision (e.g., Huerta & Sánchez, 1999), IS researchers have begun to explore perceptual measures as complements to objective measures of IT investments (Chau et al, 2007). Still, there has been a paucity of interpretive evaluation approaches (Wilson & Howcroft, 2005). Two notable exceptions emphasize that the pluralistic nature of ITenabled business value requires a sense-making attitude that appreciates diverse stakeholder involvement. Jones & Hughes’ (2001) approach, based on Heidegger’s hermeneutics and Suchman’s situated action, supports analysis of organizational reality by focusing on multiple stakeholder experiences, interests, and perspectives. Stockdale & Standing’s (2006) approach draws on Symons (1991) and Pettigrew (1985) to determine what is being measured, by whom, and for what purpose.

Context, content, and process (Pettigrew, 1985) constitute the essential cornerstones in these interpretive evaluation approaches. Context deals with the motives for the IT investment and explains who should take part in the evaluation. Content concerns what to evaluate and is dependent on the involved stakeholder perceptions. Process refers to the formative structure of the evaluation activity and prescribes how and when to pursue it. Focusing on participation, re<sup>fl</sup>ection, and learning, managers can improve IT investment decisions by creating an understanding of why the evaluation is being done, who is conducting it, what needs to be measured, and for what audience (Wilson & Howcroft, 2005). These approaches re<sup>fl</sup>ect Walsham’s (1993) idea that discovery and evaluation of alternatives must be understood and appreciated as a longitudinal socio-political process through the life cycle of a system within multilevel contexts.

Stockdale & Standing (2006), however, note that an integrated approach to IT investment decisions is required to understand how cultural, political, and social dimensions in<sup>fl</sup>uence <sup>fi</sup>nancial bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Accordingly, the interpretive and <sup>fi</sup>nancial evaluation perspectives need to be enacted in tandem to allow for a comprehensive, yet detailed picture of the tangible and intangible business values that ultimately determine decision outcomes. Such an approach can help public and private organizations transform how they make decisions (Bannister, 2001, 2005).

## Our design approach

Our proposed approach to decision making combines design thinking (Boland & Collopy, 2004; Boland et al, 2008), evaluation theory (Guba & Lincoln, 1989; Remenyi & Sherwood-Smith, 1999), and IS evaluation theory (Jones & Hughes, 2001; Stockdale & Standing, 2006). It views decision making as a creative and adaptive process in which managers recursively collect and interpret heterogeneous evidence, explore and test different ideas, and discover and evaluate alternatives. While the approach is rooted in the know-how of the involved stakeholders (who occupy different roles across organizational levels), its underpinning design attitude invites managers to discover and evaluate alternatives by measuring the tangible and by sensing the intangible, that is, by combining rational evaluations that result in actual or approximate value statements (typically presented as structured data) with interpretive evaluations that offer multiple, possibly con<sup>fl</sup>icting views (typically involving unstructured data). Similar to Simon’s (1977) three-stage model of intelligence–design–choice and traditional stage models of decision making, we propose a <sup>fi</sup>ve-staged design approach to decision making that is ideally executed iteratively so that organizations gradually improve their capability to make decisions about possible alternatives (cf., Boland & Collopy, 2004; Boland et al, 2008) (see Figure 1).

Representing concerns the initial activity where managers diagnose and articulate the problem situation related to IT investment decisions. On the basis of interviews with people at multiple levels serving different roles, managers generate a detailed understanding of current investment decisions and how evaluations of alternatives could be designed to cover both tangible and intangible indicators. The representation of the problem situation is questioned, adapted, and modi<sup>fi</sup>ed vis-à-vis different stakeholders improvement suggestions and future expectations. It is important that managers identify and expose central decision assumptions previously unknown to the organization as a whole.

Knowing deals with managers’ efforts to create a knowledge base that can help discover and evaluate alternatives.

Careful assessment of prior knowledge in the form of research evidence, practitioner literature, and project documentation is essential. Drawing on this vocabulary of decision alternatives, managers then draft evaluations that address tangible and intangible indicators identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by stakeholders. These evaluations are intended to help stakeholders go beyond simplistic reasoning, make sense of proposed alternatives, and discover new ones.

Generating is the stage where different stakeholders interact and discuss expectations of the IT investment decision. The idea is to explicate and leverage their perceptions of how the problem situation could be changed. By proposing different forms of tangible and intangible analyses, managers stimulate stakeholders to think radically or ‘out of the box’ to identify potential novel values of the investment. The goal is to create a value case that re<sup>fl</sup>ects a pluralistic understanding of business value in the considered context and that can supports stakeholders’ discovery and evaluation of speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT investment alternatives. Such a value case aligns con<sup>fl</sup>icting ideas about what the IT investment decision should deliver in terms of <sup>fi</sup>nancial and social betterment. To help achieve the sought-for business value, managers plan a discovery and evaluation process with speci<sup>fi</sup>c activities and stakeholder participation based on the developed evaluation vocabulary.

Applying is then the stage in which stakeholders discuss, revise, and further develop the agreed-on value case by following the espoused discovery and evaluation process. Given its preliminary nature, the value case must be carefully adapted to the speci<sup>fi</sup>cs of the implementing organization. To this end, managers and other stakeholders jointly engage in an iterative design activity. The nature of this activity is emergent because it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to anticipate its complete set of features. The participants carefully scrutinize the value case and a decision can be made about its full-scale implementation when their feedback no longer challenges the case.

Reflecting is the <sup>fi</sup>nal stage where managers consider the decision process and its outcome by engaging in follow-up discussions with the involved stakeholders. The goals are to facilitate individual learning from the process among the participants and to help the organization improve its ability to make IT investment decisions. That is, the process is viewed as an opportunity to train organizational members to better deal with the tangible and intangible indicators. As the decision can be more or less complex, the level and extent of re<sup>fl</sup>ections vary with the IT investment at hand. The success of the process depends on the capability of the participants to articulate and negotiate different views of the investment decision. In case the process does not converge, the lessons learned provide the impetus for kick-starting another <sup>fi</sup>ve-stage cycle.

## Research Method

## Action research

Given our objective to develop a new approach to organizational decision making, we selected action research as our mode of inquiry. Action research combines theory generation with researcher intervention to solve immediate organizational problems (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998). That is, the researcher investigates a research question about the phenomenon under study by implementing and assessing change in a real-world setting (Susman & Evered, 1978). On the basis of discrepancies between anticipated and actual changes in the client-system infrastructure (Susman, 1983), both theoretical and practical knowledge can be generated (Susman & Evered, 1978).

The centrality of collaboration and change puts action researchers in a helping role with practitioners (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996). Action research therefore goes beyond merely studying a real-world problem to contribute to its resolution based on existing and new knowledge (Mathiassen, 2002). While such pragmatist philosophy suggests that practical outcome embodies truth (Baskerville & Myers, 2004), action researchers deliberately seek to link theory and practice as well as thinking and doing (Susman, 1983).

IS researchers have used different action research approaches such as Canonical Action Research (Lindgren et al, 2004), Dialogical Action Research (Mårtensson & Lee, 2004), Networks of Action (Braa et al, 2004), and

Participatory Action Research (Street & Meister, 2004). Our study resembles Collaborative Practice Research (Mathiassen, 2002) as presented by Iversen et al (2004). This approach recognizes the dual goals of action research (McKay & Marshall, 2001), and invites researchers to pursue various focused research initiatives based on different methods as the problem solving evolves (Mathiassen, 2002; Chiasson et al, 2009). Projects that belong to this particular genre of action research are often design-oriented in that they not only produce valuable knowledge, but also tangible artifacts (e.g., frameworks or methods). For example, Iversen et al (2004) developed an approach for risk management in software process improvement.

## Project setting

Our research project was initiated in early 2005 as a Collaborative Practice Research study between the Swedish Fire Rescue Agency (SFRA) and the Viktoria Institute. SFRA is a government authority at the national level, pursuing emergency operations such as <sup>fi</sup>re protection, disaster prevention and response, and accident and incident management. Viktoria Institute is a non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t research institute specializing in applied IT research for business value creation. The project was managed by the Viktoria Institute and funded by the SFRA research program. A researcher–client agreement specifying the responsibilities of the participating organizations was signed to avoid any potential con<sup>fl</sup>icts over each party’s contribution to and role in the project (Rapoport, 1970). It addressed the allocation of resources such as <sup>fi</sup>nancial support, labor, and equipment, as well as rights and responsibilities for the research results. The project had a reference group involving experienced practitioners from SFRA and senior researchers from Viktoria Institute. They met twice a year to discuss the project’s progress and its immediate outcomes.

The project sought to transform IT investment decision making in three <sup>fi</sup>re rescue services (FRSs), namely Gothenburg, Lund, and Stockholm. The selection of cases re<sup>fl</sup>ected SFRA’s desire to include organizations of different sizes. Composed of several <sup>fi</sup>re stations within its boundaries, a FRS is a public organization that provides rescue services (primarily <sup>fi</sup>re protection) for one or more municipalities. The Gothenburg FRS contained nine <sup>fi</sup>re stations with approximately 1000 employees, serving a total of 5 municipalities. With responsibility for one municipality, the Stockholm FRS employed 650 people who were distributed over 9 <sup>fi</sup>re stations. The Lund FRS also served a single municipality, and was comprised of 3 <sup>fi</sup>re stations employing 100 people.

The study included managers who served in different roles at the operational, functional, and strategic levels. With regard to the Gothenburg FRS, two operational managers, <sup>fi</sup>ve functional managers, and two strategic managers took part. From the Stockholm FRS, three operational managers, two functional managers, and two strategic managers were included. As for the Lund FRS, two operational managers, two functional managers, and one strategic manager were involved. In addition, in view of the size of the Gothenburg FRS, 22 people serving at the operational level were also taking part. Our project was thus comprised of 43 FRS employees in total. Table 1 offers an overview of the roles that people occupied at various levels.

Consistent with the classic genesis of action research (Avison et al, 2001), our Collaborative Practice Research project was established when SFRA sought assistance from knowledgeable researchers at the Viktoria Institute. The problem at hand concerned that the three partner organizations (Gothenburg, Stockholm, and Lund) shared a history of repeated IS failures and malfunctioning enterprise systems. Intended to remedy the information fragmentation of function-oriented legacy systems (e.g., Shang & Seddon, 2002), the enterprise systems were designed to provide a total, integrated support to emergency operations. They included modules such as event registration, resource allocation, risk assessment, accident and incident management, performance evaluation, and <sup>fi</sup>nance calculation. Despite their sophisticated design, however, the systems did not achieve the anticipated goals and the underlying investment decisions were surprisingly not questioned.

The principal reason for failure was traced to inadequate IT investment decision making. The prevailing rational mindset in all three organizations meant that their discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives had a technical orientation and focused on calculation of costs for hardware and software. Decisions had therefore been based on insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient understanding of alternatives and their cultural, political, and social consequences. Lack of requisite knowledge in IT investment decision making, especially among managers at the strategic level, was a contributing factor to this unfortunate situation. The organizations were therefore determined to transform such decision making and improve their capability to discover and evaluate IT alternatives. Consistent with our approach, the involved managers wanted to learn how a design attitude could help them leverage such capability by measuring the tangible and by sensing the intangible.

## Research process

Our Collaborative Practice Research study was therefore organized based on the stages of the proposed design approach to organizational decision making (see Figure 1). In our quest to help SFRA and its member organizations, the representing stage started with an investigation into the problems that effectively hampered adequate discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives. Our investigation included a series of interviews with managers occupying different roles at multiple levels in the three organizations. In addition, we did two observational studies at the Gothenburg FRS. The goal with these diagnostic activities was to detect, illuminate, and represent the problems that the study participants felt had a detrimental effect on IT investment decision making. To verify the accuracy of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed problems, we discussed them with the client organizations. The net effect was a shared understanding of the problems and a desire to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to overcoming them. The outcomes of this stage guided subsequent activities.

The next stage, knowing, was executed to support problem solving by reviewing the extant literature on IT investment decision making. The analysis was performed by the action researchers and resulted in development of a multi-indicator model for discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives. The model synthesized prominent business value indicators, and was supposed to guide actions that could resolve or ameliorate the problem situation. Following our design approach to decision making, the generating stage sought to translate the theoretical understanding gained into concrete instantiations. In collaboration with the practitioners, we embarked on a development effort aimed at yielding a value case and an associated decision process for each of the three organizations.

In the applying stage, the goal was to empirically test the instantiations materialized during the previous stage by practicing them in each client organization. While we opted for an empirical test that would be consistent across all organizations, practical pressures forced us to accept and pursue an ‘as good as it gets’ approach (see the section ‘Research practice’ for details). At Stockholm FRS, a combination of political decisions and budget cuts meant that its collaboration with us could not continue. At the Gothenburg FRS, the value case guided the execution of two IT investment decision projects, but there was no explicit effort to fully embrace the supporting process. As for the Lund FRS, however, the value case and the decision process were implemented as originally planned.

The subsequent re<sup>fl</sup>ecting stage involved a joint assessment (researchers and practitioners), which showed that the interventions rendered both anticipated and

unanticipated consequences. To assess whether the positive effects of our efforts would persist and become sustainable after we left, we therefore followed the evolution of IT investment decision making at the Gothenburg FRS by pursuing complementary follow-up activities over a 2-year period. Our original plan was to carry out similar follow-up activities at the Lund FRS. A strategic decision to integrate it into a larger organizational structure capable of serving several municipalities, however, led to a turbulent situation that ultimately hampered our chances to get the necessary access. ## Research practice

## Representing

Given our intention to identify what hampered successful implementation of enterprise systems, we started our action research cycle by investigating existing approaches to discovery and evaluation of IT investment alternatives within the three organizations. Actors at multiple levels participated and we revealed two key problems. Several actors who served in a variety of roles across different organizational levels expressed the <sup>fi</sup>rst problem: the dominating business value concept dealt almost exclusively with the <sup>fi</sup>nancial side of IT investments. The chief manager of the strategic level at the Stockholm FRS conveyed this tangible value orientation:

Even if you aim for rationalization through investing in IT it’s likely that some additional costs appear … and you do not get the value expected.

This problem was further exacerbated by most IT investment evaluations being restricted to the technology itself, while surrounding cultural, political, and social dimensions were largely ignored. The IT manager serving at the strategic level at the Stockholm FRS explained the consequence of this mindset:

The current practice of paying attention only to hardware and software has contributed to a tendency to invest in new IT applications … instead of upgrading existing systems already being embedded into everyday routines and structures.

Most managers across the three sites argued that the narrow cost and technology focus undermined efforts to make strategic IT investment decisions with appropriate and sustainable effects. Discussing these discovery and evaluation practices, the IT manager at the functional level of the Gothenburg FRS echoed what the other managers asserted:

The internal discussions mostly concern technology, technical platforms, and systems, while questions ought to be raised about what actual needs should be fulfilled … what the goals we should seek to achieve in the public sector are, and how we should essentially proceed.

During the interviews, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a second problem in current discovery and evaluation practices: the organization lacked a structured process, which ultimately led to waste of resources and ineffective decision making. The IT manager serving at the functional level at the Gothenburg FRS said:

Unfortunately, we often miss the total picture because we tend to focus way too much on individual desires. For example, when it comes to digital calendars [as an add-on feature to the enterprise system] we’ve failed to agree on a single brand for the entire organization. This has led to a costly ad-hoc approach to IT investments, and therefore we must improve coordination within the organization.

Virtually all of the interviewed managers con<sup>fi</sup>rmed this problem. Taking it one step further, the business manager at the strategic level at the Lund FRS called for a radical change of the organization’s routines to discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives:

We need a more systematic IT evaluation process … not just to catch the bad guys and prevent their inappropriate or problem behaviors, but also to create beneficial conditions for reflection and learning.

On the basis of our investigation of the three organizations, we concluded that the problems in existing IT investment decision making could be traced back to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant knowledge gap. We assumed that an underlying cause for this gap was lack of IT investment approaches that view business value as pluralistic and multifaceted and engage different actors on multiple organizational levels. With the ambition to transform the organizations’ decision making, we anticipated that our design approach could help managers to discover and evaluate IT alternatives by measuring the tangible and sensing the intangible. However, this new approach to organizational decision making needed to be integrated into the social system of everyday work within the three organizations. We therefore agreed with the participating managers that it was important to leverage their experiences to improve the problem situation.

## Knowing

Guided by our change agenda, we set out to develop a multi-indicator model that would stimulate the three organizations to transform their current approaches to discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives. We developed this model, largely in isolation from the participating managers, relying on the insights we gained in the representing stage and available knowledge in the general evaluation and the IS evaluation literature. The model was created through repeated iterations and comprised eight essential indicators:

● Strategic match concerns the alignment between IT investments and business, organizational as well as technological strategies (e.g., Ballentine et al, 1998).

● External pressure concerns how authorities, collaborators, competitors, and suppliers affect the outcome of IT investments (e.g., Li & Ye, 1999).

● Organizational impact concerns consequences of IT investments for culture, politics, processes, and routines (e.g., Kannellis et al, 1999; Klecun & Cornford, 2005).

● Benefit and cost awareness deals with bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and drawbacks as well as direct and indirect costs of IT investments (e.g., Berghout & Remenyi, 2005; Love et al, 2005).

● Risk sensitivity concerns organizational and technological uncertainties with potentially disruptive effects (e.g., Ropponen & Lyytinen, 1997).

● IT capacity deals with accuracy, <sup>fl</sup>exibility, quality, reliability, and usability of IT investments (e.g., Choenni et al, 2003).

● Stakeholder understanding deals with expectations to IT investments originating from customers, management, suppliers, and users (e.g., Grif<sup>fi</sup>th & Remenyi, 2003).

● Project competence deals with the ability to successfully manage implementation of IT investments (e.g., Clay et al, 2003).

We reasoned that these indicators would be effective in transforming IT investment decision making, thereby providing managers with a comprehensive foundation for understanding business value during discovery and evaluation of IT investment alternatives. The multi-indicator model was subsequently applied in the design of a value case and an associated decision process for each of the three organizations.

## Generating

Embarking on the generating stage, we relied on a process that came as close as possible to an authentic situation. We organized workshops in each of the three organizations, involving experienced managers with <sup>fi</sup>rst-hand knowledge of discovery and evaluation of IT investment alternatives. The activities focused on development of value cases and associated decisions processes following the proposed approach (Figure 1), but we also sought to maximize learning outcomes by sharing knowledge across the three organizations about achievements and steps taken.

The vocabulary of the multi-indicator model was intended to serve as a catalyst to spur re<sup>fl</sup>ections about potential business values of IT investments. While the managers generally found the model to be helpful and relevant, there were variations in how it was perceived. IT managers in Gothenburg and Stockholm were enthusiastic, while business managers from all three organizations appeared more reserved. We traced the reservations to the fact that the adopted model essentially promoted discovery and evaluation practices that required them to move beyond their existing <sup>fi</sup>nancial and technical skills.

Still, it was decided that the multi-indicator model should guide subsequent development efforts. Recognizing the efforts by the action researchers during the previous stage, several participants saw the thorough literature review as a strong argument for further deployment of the model. Accordingly, the involved managers drew on their contextual understanding to develop value cases. That is, they analyzed the model’s indicators in light of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c conditions of their organizations (e.g., business plans, existing technology, project routines, and union demands). Table 3 presents a summary of the value cases developed by the three organizations.

Most managers argued that the development of the value cases was a necessary <sup>fi</sup>rst step to transform IT investment decision making, and they gradually started to develop a better understanding of what it takes to measure the tangible and sense the intangible. As an illustration, the IT manager at the strategic level of the Stockholm FRS said:

This effort has helped shed light on the complex nature of evaluation of IT investments. The workshop discussions clarified not only why organizations must go beyond cost analyses but also how they can do it by paying attention to benefits as well as other complementary value indicators. In particular, I took active part in the development of the value case based on the multi-indicator model and felt that the design activity itself was a truly rewarding experience for everyone involved including myself.

The positive feedback encouraged us to continue our engagement with the three organizations. We were committed to help them transform IT investment decision making by embedding the developed value cases into the organizations and by establishing supportive processes. We therefore exposed managers to methods, models, and tools in the IT investment literature that could help them design and establish such processes. The goal was that each organization would tailor a decision process based on its speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs and requirements. Managers in all three organizations agreed on the need for a decision process that would support re<sup>fl</sup>ection and learning based on a multifaceted business value concept and drawing on a mix of perspectives from multiple levels of the organization. Hence, each organization designed a decision process in which the value case would be applied through a life-cycle perspective.

In each of the three organizations, actions were taken to facilitate the adoption of the new decision process. An IT council was proposed as a supporting organizational structure in both Gothenburg and Stockholm. While these councils ideally would involve managers from different departments and at multiple levels, they were supposed to draw on the value cases to follow-up on IT investments over their entire life cycle. Accordingly, a key task for them was to inform executive groups as well as management boards about the effects of IT investment decisions, and thereby help the organizations transform IT investment decision making. At the Lund FRS, the change effort involved the formation of a forum with close collaboration between the IT manager and the department manager. This forum was intended to serve as the nexus for IT investment decisions, while the generated value case was made available to the organization as a critical resource.

## Applying

We sought to empirically test the utility of the developed value cases and decision processes by implementing them as complements to the three organizations’ existing practices. While all three organizations recognized the value of these instantiations, they implemented them to varying degrees. Only one of the organizations, namely Lund, implemented both the value case and the decision process. Its comparatively small size and less bureaucratic routines allowed this organization to make the necessary decisions.

The value case successfully guided the execution of two IT investment decision projects, and eventually the IT department started to use it as an enabling tool in conversations with business managers about IT investment alternatives. However, the scale and complexity of this FRS combined with a generally immature IT investment capability ultimately meant the organization decided not to apply its developed decision process. It was simply not feasible to deal with both content and process simultaneously within the realm of the action research project. In addition, there were other incidents that almost stalled our actions to help the organization. For example, the ambulance department temporarily recruited the head of the IT department to manage an IT investment decision project unrelated to the research effort.

While practical pressures only partly jeopardized the interventions in Gothenburg, such pressures brought the interventions at Stockholm FRS to a complete halt. The collaboration with this organization was terminated because of political decisions and budget cuts that led to a turbulent restructuring, which ultimately made one of our key partners, the <sup>fi</sup>re manager, virtually unavailable to us.

## Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting

At Lund and Gothenburg, we assessed the outcomes of applying the proposed design approach to organizational decision making. The results highlighted that the interventions had rendered both anticipated and unanticipated consequences. Overall, our attempts to transform discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives by stimulating managers to adopt a design attitude were largely successful. In both organizations, managers understood better the shortcomings of a one-sided <sup>fi</sup>nancial approach focused on technical issues. They also learned about the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of our design approach for discovering and evaluating IT alternatives. Our idea of engaging managers as designers to help their organizations to measure the tangible and sense the intangible was appreciated as an effective means to establish a new agenda for IT investment decision making.

With regard to anticipated consequences, several managers noted the advantages of an integrative approach. The exposure to a wide range of business values through the multi-indicator model was seen as a critical success factor when developing value cases. In all three organizations, it provided a framework that helped managers rethink their extant <sup>fi</sup>nancially and technically oriented agenda. They came to understand that bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from IT investment decisions often are intangible, thus suggesting that discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives by necessity must look beyond narrow quanti<sup>fi</sup>cations of costs. They also argued that focusing on technical issues generated judgments that overlooked cultural, political, and social dimensions inherent in IT investment decisions. As an illustration, the IT manager serving at the functional level of the Lund FRS characterized IT investment decision making as a socio-technical analysis of the opportunities presented by IT:

Now I think differently and have new reference points. Previously, I used to restrict value from IT investments to direct benefits and costs. The former concerned what an IT application could potentially accomplish given its technical features, while the latter dealt primarily with hardware and software. My current understanding, in contrast, is more socially informed in that it centers around intended and unintended consequences for users and citizens alike, and much less on the technology per se.

The understanding of the multifaceted nature of discovery and evaluation of business value from IT investments proved helpful in supporting horizontal and vertical communication within the organization. This in turn helped managers appreciate the important role of stakeholders, and also to recognize the centrality of life-cycle thinking. A business manager serving at the functional level of the Gothenburg FRS articulated this:

Serving as an enabling platform for evaluation activities, this design approach has leveraged the creation of a professional language that helped bridge conversations between managers occupying different roles at various levels. As a result, my understanding of the usefulness of stakeholder involvement and its potential contribution was based on a more complete and robust approach to value assessment of IT investments. Yet another lesson is that a process view is necessary in that it recognizes effects of IT investments that emerge after their implementation, including license and maintenance costs.

While learning had occurred at both Lund and Gothenburg, progress was particularly evident at the Gothenburg FRS. Here, the organization had begun to internalize and practice the basic tenets of our proposed approach and the efforts had stimulated other managers to change their IT investment decision making. The value case was applied to guide investments in emergency support systems and six out of the eight indicators were used to identify tangible and intangible business values of a mobile system acquisition and a stationary system upgrade. The involved managers argued that the business value conversations enabled by the value case created broader views of these IT alternatives, of which some came as a surprise. The discovery and evaluation of these alternatives led to a thorough understanding of their potential business impact and eventually to decisions to invest in them. Some managers traced this positive outcome to improved collaboration between IT and business managers, as the IT manager at the functional level explained:

It’s evident that the approach fills the gap we’ve experienced. The value case actually serves as a filtering mechanism that helps us judge the appropriateness of new enterprise system initiatives. It has also made people more aware of the reasons why investments went wrong in the past. As a boundary object, I refer to the value case when discussing with business managers, and it seems like we nowadays reach agreements on cost calculations somewhat more effectively nowadays.

We were pleased to see that our efforts gained momentum and begun to bear fruit. However, we were also keen to investigate whether these emerging practices to discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives would persist over time or, if the organization would gradually return to its previous decision-making behavior. We therefore initiated a follow-up study a couple of years later. In 2012, it seemed like the structures we had promoted actually were reinforced rather than weakened. Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on the sustained effects of the action research effort, the IT manager at the functional level said:

We’re currently using the value case primarily for evaluating investments in new IT systems [not updates] … it also plays a role in our conversations with the steering committee. The value case is in fact part of the appendices of our general IT investment directive. Furthermore, the IT department uses it when negotiating its annual budget, but also as support for project revisions at the municipality level. We’ve also included some of the indicators of the value case into our project models… we did this back in 2009 or 2010. Some of the indicators have been slightly modified though. For example, the stakeholder analysis has been adapted so that it not only recognizes those being directly affected by the investment but also people who would benefit from simply knowing what’s happening. In terms of future actions, we’ll actively apply the value case for guiding the investment of a Business Intelligence solution for our dispatching central. Given that some 30% of our rescue operation require more advanced IT support to actually manage them, our organization has decided that we must improve our analysis capability.

In addition to anticipated consequences, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed unanticipated consequences of our interventions. Despite the reduced gap between IT and business managers at the Gothenburg FRS, the action research led to a situation where opposing forces had to be reconciled in IT investment decision making. While the IT side of the organization started to adopt a multifaceted view of business value, the overarching organization, SFRA, continued to promote technology-focused <sup>fi</sup>nancial value measures. Consequently, business managers were caught in the middle of these con<sup>fl</sup>icting views, as the IT manager serving at the functional level highlighted:

In the midst of the action research effort, SFRA approached us and suggested our FRS should make a multimillion [SEK] investment in novel media technology. Remarkably little, if any, attention was given to the value-creation dimension of it. Instead, they focused on the technology and its inherent features and the costs for hardware and applications. These were communicated to the organization, but service agreements and reinvestments, for example, didn’t matter at all. Still it’s not clear why we invested in this technology … and its actual usage is limited. Changing business managers’ attitude is difficult as long as the SFRA prevents it.

A second unanticipated consequence of our action research was the dynamics that emerged between organizational levels. The fact that the proposed design approach transformed discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives to also address cultural, political, and social dimensions ultimately meant that new managerial capabilities were needed at all levels for effective IT investment decision making. The IT manager serving at the functional level at the Lund FRS noted:

As managers, my colleague and I have an academic background and knowledge of IT investment evaluation, so for us the approach turned out to be both understandable and useful. But given the fact that its rationale requires people at the operational level to take part and contribute too, we learned that composition of working groups presents a challenge in itself. While it’s necessary to pick participants who have prior experience of IT investment evaluation, access to diverse people with an ability to understand those thoughts and ideas is equally important.

The institutionalized command and control thinking that tends to impregnate top-down procedures in many public organizations made it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to develop the requisite capabilities on all levels. Moreover, many managers had limited knowledge of how to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from experiences and knowledge emanating at different levels when orchestrating discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives. This gap between required cross-level interactions and learning and existing rigid hierarchical procedures was a source of frustration for several participants.

A third unanticipated consequence concerned the resources required to practice design thinking in organizational decision making. While our approach increased the opportunities for successful IT investment decision making, it also added extra burdens and worries. Generating and handling a larger amount of diverse information for analytic purposes turned out to be more demanding than expected. The IT manager at the functional level of the Gothenburg FRS noted:

I believe that IT can make the utilization of the value case more effective because some of its parts are pretty time consuming to deal with. For example, there’s an agreement between the employer and the unions that regulates the impact of IT investments on work conditions … if we’re updating an existing system or replacing an old system with a new one, I need to inform union representatives as well as department managers about the project plan. Then they analyze it and discuss its potential impact with the employees. Sometimes a more careful risk analysis led by a HR manager with the adequate expertise must be performed to identify consequences. In any case, I’ve clearly underestimated the time needed and the amount of work required. An additional area where IT could actually help us concerns cost analyses … we’re actively seeking a more appropriate cost model by building on the Life Cycle Cost concept to distribute costs over time and by taking IT support costs into account.

Pursuing a value case process is very labor intensive and this manager wanted to understand how to apply supportive tools and technologies. Recent digital advancements did provide new opportunities to gather, store, analyze, and access information and these needed to be explored in the context of discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives.

## Discussion

Some 20 years ago, Eisenhardt & Zbaracki (1992) outlined a research agenda they hoped would break the shackles of the prevailing choice perspectives and encourage studies that could transcend these perspectives to more realistic views of decision making. More recent research has argued that such studies need to integrate different perspectives (Elbanna & Child, 2007) to detail our knowledge of how decision making plays out in real-world organizations (Sutcliffe & McNamara, 2001). These studies are essential because they enable organizations to adopt new approaches that promote more creative and adaptive decision processes (Eisenhardt & Zbaracki, 1992). Accordingly, we have addressed how managers, in the particular context of IT investments, can discover and evaluate IT alternatives by measuring the tangible and sensing the intangible, and as a result we have proposed a new design approach to organizational decision making.

While IT investment decisions are made by most organizations on a very regular basis (Boonstra, 2003), they can have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on organizational performance (Mithas et al, 2011) and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability (Mithas et al, 2012). Discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives play an important role in such decision making, but are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult undertakings because there are tangible as well as intangible paths to IT-enabled business value creation (Kohli & Grover, 2008). However, managers engaged in IT investments tend to draw primarily on <sup>fi</sup>nancial methods and focus on technical, measureable indicators (Ward & Daniel, 2006), while ignoring the cultural, political, and social dimensions and the consequential business values that surround organizations’ use of IT (Jones & Hughes, 2001; Stockdale & Standing, 2006). Accordingly, organizations still struggle to pursue discovery and evaluation activities that leverage their IT investment decision making despite the fact that we have largely moved beyond the IT productivity paradox (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996; Mithas et al, 2012).

To cope with the complex realities involved, IT investment research has sought to replace the dominating <sup>fi</sup>nancial and technical register with a more holistic and re<sup>fl</sup>ective perspective (Bannister, 2005). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it has been suggested that the interpretive perspective must be placed central stage (Jones & Hughes, 2001; Stockdale & Standing, 2006; Walsham, 1993, 1999), opening up for new approaches that emphasize the pluralistic dimension of business value (Symons, 1991) and support discovery and evaluation of alternatives as collective endeavors that involve stakeholders at multiple organizational levels (Walsham, 1993). Hence, Stockdale & Standing’s (2006) interpretive approach supports the use of existing indicators, while at the same time allowing new ones to be incorporated. Further, by including stakeholders to in<sup>fl</sup>uence decision processes and outcomes, their approach engages a wide range of organizational perspectives in discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives.

We have picked up the baton from Stockdale & Standing (2006) to help transform IT investment decision making in organizations. They argue that comprehensive approaches are required to understand how cultural, political, and social dimensions in<sup>fl</sup>uence the <sup>fi</sup>nancial bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of an IT investment decision. Bannister (2005) anticipates that transcending the prevailing agenda involves development of new approaches that build on different philosophical positions. Consistent with the application of design thinking in management (Simon 1969; Boland and Collopy, 2004; Boland et al, 2008), our approach abandons a view of organizational decision making as choice between alternatives and emphasizes the creative processes through which alternatives are discovered and evaluated. Re<sup>fl</sup>ective of this idea, we set out to explore whether managers acting as designers (cf., Wastell, 2010) would be able to discover and evaluate IT alternatives by measuring the tangible and by sensing the intangible.

At the heart of our proposed <sup>fi</sup>ve-stage approach is the value case, which re<sup>fl</sup>ects a pluralistic understanding of business value in the considered context and supports stakeholders’ discovery and evaluation of speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT investment alternatives. As such, it represents a negotiated and agreed-upon idea about the envisioned business value of a particular IT investment decision. Given our dual goal of advancing IT investment research and transforming organizational decision making, the approach was applied through a Collaborative Practice Research project (Mathiassen, 2002) with three Swedish public organizations. Inspired by prior research (Bannister, 2001), our goal was to help these organizations break with current approaches to discovery of alternatives and replace them with processes that re<sup>fl</sup>ected pluralistic business value thinking. Our partnering organizations deemed this ambition crucial to increase the positive impacts of their IT investment decisions on business performance.

In two of these organizations (Lund and Gothenburg), our approach to decision making was received as a valuable mechanism that promoted a design attitude to discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives and helped managers rethink their decision processes. As for the two investment projects at the Gothenburg FRS, it was clear that the participating managers had discovered and evaluated business value indicators that previously were largely unknown to them. The considered IT alternatives were deemed appropriate, and eventually the organization decided to invest in them. Despite the fact that most public organizations are bureaucracies that tend to resist change (Bannister, 2001), our design approach helped the organization to start transforming IT investment decisions by changing its decision culture. The approach brought together people who understand IT investment with both structured and unstructured data and offered a problemsolving technique to exploit them. In this way, it leveraged decisions based on combinations of data that most of the involved managers felt would help the organization move toward more creative and adaptable IT investment decision making (cf., McAffe & Brynjolfsson, 2012). In addition to these anticipated consequences of our approach, we also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a number of unanticipated consequences. We believe these may trigger researchers and practitioners alike to add more <sup>fl</sup>esh to the bones of our proposed approach to decision making.

First, improving discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives requires changing a complex system of actors on multiple organizational levels. Yet, most practical and theoretical efforts that dealt with these decision activities have largely ignored the multilevel dynamics involved (Walsham, 1993). Our close collaboration with the three partner organizations demonstrated that effective discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives must be organized to leverage perspectives, experiences, and skills across different organizational roles and levels of management. Such multilevel practices draw attention to the context where behavior occurs and facilitate behaviors that traverse organizational levels (Hitt et al, 2007). The traditional, dominating decision logic is that managers at higher levels exert strong downward in<sup>fl</sup>uences, while their grassroot counterparts only exert weak upward in<sup>fl</sup>uences. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the proposed approach to decision making allows for reciprocal and strong upward in<sup>fl</sup>uences. However, little is currently known about how to orchestrate discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives so that lower-level in<sup>fl</sup>uences actually help shape investment decisions and their subsequent organization-level outcomes.

Second, improving discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives requires managing a large bulk of structured and unstructured data. However, past efforts have largely focused on the former (Hirschheim & Smithson, 1999), that is one-dimensional <sup>fi</sup>nancial and technical indicators such as additional sales, cost savings, and system reliability. In contrast, our approach also deals with the latter by incorporating unstructured data that people with domain expertise supply based on the generated value case. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our study suggest that incorporation of diverse value indicators of potential business impacts requires new professional languages during the IT investment discourse. By explicating a broad repertoire of business value indicators, organizations can better recognize decision outcomes and their antecedent conditions (Belcher & Watson, 1993; Kohli & Grover, 2008). However, our study also shows that it is challenging to move from astract notions of multidimensional decision making to concrete, measurable business value indicators in speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts. Novel routines are therefore needed to help managers maintain and practice pluralistic thinking when they discover and evaluate IT alternatives. Moreover, to maintain consistent, reliable, and valid indicators over time, such routines must cater for <sup>fl</sup>exible adjustments by helping managers assess and respond to any relevant change in their environments.

Third, improving discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives requires exploiting the potential of advanced technology to leverage organizational intelligence in decision making (cf., Leidner & Elam, 1995). So far, little attention has been paid to how such IT systems may provide actionable information delivered at the right time, at the right location, and in the right form to support IT investment decision makers. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the proposed approach to decision making paves the way for adoption of business intelligence systems in organizations. The objective of such systems is to improve timeliness and quality of inputs to the decision process (Negash, 2004), and they may help organizations consolidate, analyze, and manage business-critical structured and unstructured data (Clark et al, 2007; Chen et al, 2012). Hence, an important area for future research in business intelligence systems ( Jourdan et al, 2008) concerns their application to discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives that can help organizations involve multiple stakeholders in making decisions about large scale, complex investments.

## Conclusions

We have developed a new design approach to organizational decision making in the particular context of IT investments. Prior research has so far only generated a few interpretive IT investment approaches to complement the prevailing <sup>fi</sup>nancial ones, and it has largely ignored to develop the glue that can bind different perspectives together. This is a serious problem given the emergence of increasingly digitalized business environments where it is challenging to understand how technologies are implicated in complex socio-technical assemblages that span organizational levels. Contemporary technologies create discontinuous rather than evolutionary changes, while their embeddedness often makes them disappear from the immediate attention of users. Finding adequate approaches to discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives in investment decisions has therefore become increasingly complex and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our study suggest that the proposed approach helped the involved organizations to improve their discovery and evaluation of IT alternatives by measuring the tangible and by sensing the intangible. While we are strong in our belief that this improvement will gradually lead to transformed IT investment decisions in these organizations, the approach still needs to be tested and further developed in a wider range of decision situations. Indeed, we have outlined a set of implications for future work that applies not only to discovery and evaluation of IT investment alternatives, but also to organizational decision making in general.
