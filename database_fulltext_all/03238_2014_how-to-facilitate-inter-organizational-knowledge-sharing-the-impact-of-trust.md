# How to facilitate inter-organizational knowledge sharing: The impact of trust

Ying-Hueih Chen <sup>a,1</sup>, Tzu-Pei Lin <sup>a,2</sup>, David C. Yen <sup>b,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> Department of Computer Science and Information Management, Providence University, 200 Chungchi Rd., Shalu 433, Taichung, Taiwan <sup>b</sup> School of Economics and Business, 226 Netzer Administration Bldg., SUNY College at Oneonta, Oneonta, NY 13820, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 13 April 2012 Received in revised form 20 March 2014 Accepted 29 March 2014 Available online 18 April 2014

Keywords: Shared goal Relational embeddedness Influence strategy Trust Collaboration Knowledge sharing Relationship marketing

## A B S T R A C T

In this era of the network economy, inter-organizational knowledge sharing is one key driving force required to streamline value chain activities and maximize operational benefits. Knowledge sharing can be realized when the involved business partners successfully develop trust and build long-term partnerships. In this study, a model of knowledge sharing across the supply chain is constructed. Factors such as shared goals, social relational embeddedness, and influence strategy are investigated to determine whether they act as major driving forces to develop inter-organizational trust among the various supply chain members. The survey is based on 226 managers located in major industrial parks in Taiwan; the results suggest that trust is enforced when organizations develop shared goals, form social relational embeddedness, and initiate influence strategies. In addition, inter-organizational trust leads to better inter-organizational collaboration and knowledge sharing. Theoretical and practical implications are also discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Inter-organizational knowledge sharing and leverage are strategic means to achieve competitive advantage in rapidly changing business environments [7]. Knowledge sharing refers to leveraging information assets across various business partners to concentrate on supply chain activities, identify market opportunities, and/or capture business value [35,39]. Effective knowledge sharing enables supply chain partners to streamline the flow of information, money, and products across organizational boundaries, in turn improving the agility, adaptability, and predictability of the supply chain [39].

Despite the strategic importance of inter-organizational knowledge sharing in supply chain management, effective knowledge sharing remains a challenge. Inter-organizational knowledge sharing is a complex issue built on inter-organizational relationships [3,11]. Trust is a catalyst that facilitates strategic business interactions and knowledge sharing among independent firms [11,23,35]. For example, Ke and Wei [27] examine knowledge sharing from both the transaction cost and social-political theory perspectives and highlight the strategic importance of inter-organizational trust, as well as firm power, in facilitating knowledge sharing across the supply chain. Panteli and Sockalingam [36] note that trust is the cornerstone of knowledge-based collaboration. While these studies have identified key knowledge sharing issues, most studies in this area overlook the important questions of how and why organizations develop collaborative relationships and employ knowledge sharing in the supply chain context. Understanding the relational factors that drive knowledge sharing across various supply chain partners remains a major concern for both researchers and practitioners.

By considering knowledge sharing as a pattern of interorganizational interaction and the resulting outcome of social relationships, this study seeks to explore the relational factors that drive effective business to business (B2B) knowledge sharing across supply chain members. Relationship marketing suggests that organizational relationships can be built, maintained, and enhanced by developing collaborative, trustworthy, and reciprocal business partnerships [35]; inter-organizational trust is a necessary condition for relational exchange [5,27,36]. The level of knowledge sharing in a supply chain is determined by the perceived benefits of information exchange, agreements among independent firms, social satisfaction, and dependence among partners [11]. Following this vein of thought, we propose shared goals, relational embeddedness, and influence strategies as major relational antecedents of interorganizational trust, which in turn diminish the probability of opportunistic behaviors, strengthen the social bond between supply chain partners, and secure exchange benefits. Shared goals are the fundamental force that lead to the formation of homogenous behavior logic in the B2B context [35]; relational embeddedness is a major factor that shapes economic behavior among and between trading entities [1] and influences strategy in terms of how organizational power influences the decision making process of business partners [3]. These factors capture the plurality of interorganizational interactions and thereby provide insight into the driving forces of knowledge sharing.

According to the empirical results obtained by Chow et al. [14], supply chain members constantly face challenges related to knowledge sharing and collaboration as they attempt to respond to changing customer demands and business competition. The current study focuses on Taiwan manufacturing firm supply chains for two reasons. First, according to the Industrial Development Bureau of Taiwan, Taiwan is one of the world’s largest manufactures of high-tech products and a major business partner of the US. Many multinational organizations have manufacturing firms or business partners in Taiwan. Understanding supply chain practices in Taiwan can enhance knowledge integration across business boundaries. Secondly, Taiwan is one of the major players in the Greater China Region (China, Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan) and a major trading partner of China/Hong Kong, the US, Japan, Singapore, and Korea. As such, a clearer understanding of supply chain practices common in Taiwan is useful for firms who wish to focus on the booming Asian economy.

This study, which utilizes a quantitative research method for model testing, is important for two reasons. First, while previous studies have highlighted the importance of inter-organizational relationships in terms of capturing the benefits of knowledge sharing, most of these studies focused on technological, economic, and/or political aspects that affect knowledge sharing behaviors [10,11,27,28]. Very limited research has explored the factors that drive inter-organizational trust and knowledge sharing through a relational perspective such as shared goals, relational embeddedness, and influence strategies. The current study identifies and empirically examines these salient aspects of trust development, and highlights the effects of trust on knowledge sharing across the supply chain. Second, this study complements prior research in this subject area in that the quantitative data and findings can be generalized to provide actionable guidelines for business partners looking to better coordinate and integrate information assets.

The remainder of this paper is as follows: the next section presents the theoretical background through a review of the related literature. Section three discusses the research model and hypotheses, while section four outlines the research methods. The final sections summarize the findings and provide research implications.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Relational factors and knowledge sharing

In the digital economy, advanced telecommunication technologies provide communication platforms that enable business partners to jointly create, retain, transform, and transmit valuable knowledge [27]. Inter-organizational knowledge sharing is a critical factor for collaborative resource coordination, allocation and integration across different members of a supply chain [28,39]. Such knowledge sharing activities allow the organization to expand their resource pool, deliver value-added products or services, detect emerging opportunities and capture business benefits in a hypercompetitive business environment [27,28]. The organizational motivation to develop in-depth, inter-organizational alliances and collaboration is truly essential to accrue such a value-added network [36].

Relationship governance is fundamental to knowledge-based network arrangements. Relationship governance refers to the maintenance of strategic partnerships among different supply chain partners [11]. In the context of supply chain relationships, B2B relationships are often reactive and subject to market pressure and changing competitive positions [23]. Effective B2B interactions depend on the social relationship and trust levels developed between business partners [23]. Developing a collaborative partnership is essential for inter-organizational knowledge sharing since partnerships sustain the competitive advantage of the supply chain as a whole.

While relationship governance unfolds the importance of maintaining and developing a collaborative relationship, ‘‘trustcommitment theory’’ explains the details of how the relationship is developed. Trust-commitment theory, which places the focus on establishing, developing, and maintaining successful relational exchanges, is one of major theoretical perspectives used to analyze collaborative relationships in terms of B2B interactions [34,35]. Further, trust-commitment theory highlights the importance of collaboration across networks of firms to compete effectively within dynamically changing business environments [35]. The major features of B2B relationships are composed of such factors as shared goals, cost vs. benefits of the business relationship, communication and reciprocal behavior. Relationship commitment and trust are two key factors that encourage organizations to resist opportunistically alternative actions and hence, preserve long-term exchange benefits [35].

Prior studies have identified several relational factors that facilitate information sharing and knowledge exchange activities. For example, Dayasindhu [17] studies knowledge transfer in the Indian software industry and suggests that relational embeddedness acts as a major social mechanism to coordinate and safeguard social relationships. Morgan and Hunt [35] propose that similarities in terms of goals, policies, and strategies are an important factor in enhancing the predictability of the counterparty and strengthening the inter-organizational supply chain relationship. Furthermore, Hausman and Johnston [21] show that influence strategy is a useful vehicle to promote inter-organization interactions, and affects decision-making across relational partners in the supply chain context. While these aforementioned studies have identified and proposed diverse factors related to knowledge sharing across business networks, trust remains a core element.

Two key points emerge from the above review. First, in the B2B context, collaborative relationships can be developed and nurtured either through creating a joint business goals or by employing tacit strategies. Secondly, both collaborative relationships and interorganizational trust may be seen as detrimental factors that influence the effectiveness and sustainability of value-adding knowledge sharing within business networks. Trust is particularly important in terms of facilitating knowledge sharing to enhance the performance of inter-organizational partnerships [36].

## 2.2. Trust

Inter-organizational trust is the cornerstone of business partnerships, and nurtures the intention of knowledge acquisition and sharing outside organizational boundaries [36]. Pavlou [37] (p. 218) defines inter-organizational trust as the subjective belief with which organizational members collectively assess that a population of organizations will perform potential transactions according to their confident expectations, irrespective of their ability to fully monitor them.

Inter-organizational trust rests on the subjective belief and prediction that collaborative partners intend to meet obligations; it is the basis upon which organizations maintain strategic alliances, and facilitate inter-organizational communication and interactions. Based on trust, business partners determine the extent and nature of knowledge sharing [36].

Prior research has investigated the factors that cultivate organizational trust. Lewicki and Bunker [29] argue that trust exhibits distinctive characteristics at different stages of a relationship, and identify three sequential steps of trust development: calculus-based trust, knowledge-based trust, and identificationbased trust. Calculus-based trust, based on the conditions of economic exchange, is the most fragile type of relationship—it resembles the early stage of a conditional bond. Calculus-based trust is developed when the benefits of the business partnership outweigh the costs of maintaining the relationship. With conditional trust, business partners can engage in formal and explicit knowledge sharing [36]. Knowledge-based trust is the result of historical interaction and mutual understanding, where the behavior of the counterparty is predictable. Knowledge-based trust actually implies enhanced understanding and shared goals, which lead to more effective personal and tacit knowledge sharing. Finally, identification-based trust, derived from shared values and goals, emphasizes the emotional connection between the parties. Identification-based trust is, however, unconditional and hence, it requires a minimum control for parties. Panteli and Sockalingam [36] argue that this type of unconditional trust enables the business partners to achieve effective, value-adding knowledge sharing, which maximizes the collective benefits of knowledge sharing.

Trust is a dynamic phenomenon; the transformation of trust, from conditional to unconditional, is strongly associated with the evolution and maturity of the social relationship [36]. Previous research has investigated the impact of organizational mechanisms on the formation of trust. Morgan and Hunt [35] suggest that shared values synchronize behavioral logic and business goals across business partners, contributing to trust and long-term business partnerships. Chien et al. [12] explore consumer trust in terms of both structural and relational aspects and highlight the importance of relational embeddedness in trust development. These situational cues not only enhance the social bond, but also increase the general tendency for party trust.

In the context of supply chain management, trust is critical to promoting a strong partnership and facilitating collaboration, and knowledge sharing within the supply chain [30]. Inter-organizational trust not only influences the nature of a business relationship, but more importantly impacts strategic alliance outcomes in terms of information exchange and knowledge sharing.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Researchers have highlighted the great potential of knowledge sharing in terms of the success of supply chain management and sustaining competitive advantage [5]. Relationship management is a major determinant of business partnerships and social exchange within the B2B settings of supply chain management [23]. Knowledge sharing is carried out when business entities develop close partnerships and commit to long-term collaboration. The current study investigates the impact of shared goals, relational embeddedness, and influence strategy on trust, and the subsequent impact on collaboration and knowledge sharing. These factors affect the process and explain how and why supply chain partners develop and maintain collaborative partnerships and proceed with knowledge sharing. ## 3.1. Shared goals and trust

Morgan and Hunt [35] (p. 25) define shared values as the ‘‘extent to which partners have beliefs in common about what behaviors, goals, and policies are important or unimportant, appropriate or inappropriate, and right or wrong’’. Shared goals act as normative, enduring, and fundamental behavior guides to ensure the diverse goals and interests of the channel members converge in terms of behaviors and common goals [25]. In the context of supply chain management and knowledge sharing, the existence of shared goals is a desirable controlling mechanism that motivates the members to contribute information assets and undertake in-depth collaboration to maximize joint business performance [25].

Shared goals are an important relational resource and also a necessary condition to facilitate meaningful communication between parties in an exchange relationship [13,30]. Shared goals increase the organization’s capability to predict and assess the counterparty’s motivation and intentions with respect to the business relationship. Shared goals direct interactions between supply chain members and motivate the members to contribute, share, and combine their intellectual capital, which in turn augment the anticipation of value that can be realized through collaborative resource sharing [13,30]. Business partners with shared goals tend to develop positive expectations toward the exchange relationship. Morgan and Hunt [35] argue that shared goals are a major driving force that facilitates the development of inter-organizational trust and relationship commitment. Based on these grounds, the following relationship between shared goals and trust is proposed.

H1. Organizations that embrace shared goals tend to develop inter-organizational trust.

## 3.2. Relational embeddedness and trust

Bonner et al. [4] (p. 1373) define relational embeddedness as ‘‘the degree to which the firm’s alliance relationships are facilitated using trust, mutuality, and flexibility.’’ Relational embeddedness, comprised of social attachment and affiliation, is a strong social tie that significantly influences the economic behavior of the involved parties [17,31]. Such a social bond between business partners is an important avenue for creating differential advantages, because it reduces communication and business barriers [42].

Further, relational embeddedness is an informal, strong social relationship developed based on prior exchange experiences. Strong social exchange relationship is an essential condition to reduce uncertainty and promote tacit knowledge sharing within a supply chain. In the supply chain context, relational embeddedness actually increases motivation in terms of long-term collaboration and strategic partnership. Parties connected by relationally embedded ties are more likely to share tacit information, engage in non-opportunistic behaviors, and develop mutual understanding, leading to long-term partnerships [4,35]. In fact, parties that develop strong social relationships tend to develop a group culture and establish a governance system that can reinforce and safeguard the collaborative partnership as well as the resulting resource sharing [8].

The strong social ties not only enable business partners to better predict the behavior of their counterparts, but also foster a sense of affiliation and reciprocity among the exchange parties [17,35]. Organizations that develop strong social exchange relationships tend to have positive expectations and a strong sense of confidence about their business partners. Such a relational embeddedness will promote the sharing of information and resources, resulting in increased transparency in terms of business interactions [41]. Furthermore, relational embeddedness increases the predictability of the exchange parties and hence, ensures the integrity of the counterparties. Chien et al. [12] argue that relational embeddedness is an important antecedent to foster inter-organizational trust in an uncertain context. Supply chain members who develop strong social ties tend to have a greater propensity to pursue alliance relationships, and subsequently develop inter-organizational trust. On these grounds, it is suggested that the formation of relational embeddedness increases the level of trust.

H2. Organizations that develop relational embeddedness tend to develop inter-organizational trust.

## 3.3. Influence strategy and trust

Influence strategy refers to a wide range of different ‘‘means’’ used by the stakeholder to motivate target organizations toward joint activities and overcome resistance [19]. Influence strategy is the art of developing and maintaining diverse relational networks. Organizations utilize influence strategy based on how they assess their own power relative to the target organizations [40]. Influence strategy is in fact a strategic mechanism through which organizations can exercise power to encourage specific behavior and achieve inter-organizational coordination across supply chains. Prior research classified influence strategy as either a coercive strategy or a non-coercive strategy. The former includes such actions as threats, promises, and legalistic pleas to impose pressure, which serve to communicate the adverse consequences of non-compliance [21]. In contrast, the latter seeks to affect behaviors through communicating the positive consequences of compliance. Common non-coercive strategies include recommendations, requests, and information exchange [21]. Due to its effect on the decision-making process and activities across different relational partners, influence strategy is an important element in terms of accomplishing joint action and enhancing the efficiency and effectiveness of a supply chain.

Empirical research on supply chain management suggests that a non-coercive influence strategy positively enhances the integrity and reliability of the business partners, while a strong coercive influence strategy may damage future relationships [21]. While a non-coercive influence strategy is essential to develop mutual trust in the exchange relationship, the legal protection offered through a contract is often utilized to ensure the business partners follow the consensus agreement built for knowledge exchange. By achieving consensus on joint goals, organizations can better predict their various partners’ future behavior and build stronger long-term relationships. Based on this information, it is proposed that:

H3. Organizations that utilize an influence strategy tend to develop inter-organizational trust.

## 3.4. Trust and collaboration

Trust is an enabling condition for inter-organizational collaboration. Trust symbolizes the positive psychological state in which a trustee believes in a counterpart’s benevolence and integrity in terms of upholding their promise to achieve joint outcomes. Trust is the fundamental condition required to eliminate uncertainty within the supply chain coordination and interaction processes.

Hill et al. [22] (p. 188) define collaboration as ‘‘interactive and relational behaviors that occur between members of a work group and that are directed at task achievement’’. In the current digital economy, collaboration has become a strategic action where business partners engage in the processes of coordinating, communicating, and working with other business partners to accomplish a joint task. Collaboration is an operational mechanism through which supply chain members can maximize the business value using resource coordination and integration. While collaboration is essential to compete in a rapidly changing environment, it often involves some degree of uncertainty and risk. Trust is the core issue that sustains a collaborative relationship. By developing relational trust with all strategic supply chain partners, organizations become more willing to share resources, and ultimately are able to obtain collaborative advantages. Based on the discussion above, trust is the precondition required to initiate the collaborative agreement and facilitate the progress of the dependence relationship, which leads to the fourth hypothesis.

H4. Inter-organizational trust influences collaboration positively.

## 3.5. Collaboration and knowledge sharing

Knowledge is the core factor that can be utilized to drive effective and efficient decision-making. Further, knowledge sharing is one of the major factors contributing to the creation of a competitive advantage in terms of a flexible supply chain [39]. Knowledge sharing is an interactive process through which organizations accumulate and develop new knowledge; it enables business partners to detect and understand associated business problems and develop viable solutions. Moreover, effective knowledge sharing is a social-technical issue that involves both the utilization of information technology for information exchange and coordination of the various business partners within the supply chain. To this end, collaboration is required to speed up knowledge sharing.

A long-term collaborative business relationship is a necessary condition for effective knowledge sharing in B2B supply chains [34]. B2B collaboration is an important element to shift supply chain management from transaction-oriented to relationshiporiented coordination and integration. Collaboration forces the involved parties to develop common goals and formulate joint strategies, which help to eliminate concerns regarding the loss of valuable assets through the process of knowledge sharing. To this end, collaboration facilitates efficient and effective operation and interaction at each level of the supply chain. Knowledge sharing occurs when organizations develop strong partnerships and coordinate various organizational efforts toward common goals. Thus, the following is proposed.

H5. Inter-organizational collaborative relationships influence knowledge sharing positively.

## 3.6. Trust and knowledge sharing

Trust is also an important predictor of knowledge sharing, since it affects the perceived ability, benevolence, integrity, and predictability of the trading parties to comply with business norms [33]. Trust also reflects the scope and depth of relationships among partners and vendors, and hence, can lead to stronger collaborative partnership and better knowledge sharing [43].

In the business supply chain context, organizations develop social attachments with exchange partners through different types of social interactions and business collaboration. Trust enables the exchange parties to develop collaborative partnerships, and thereby strengthens the critical supplier-seller relationship. The perceived integrity and benevolence of the trading parties also eliminates concerns regarding opportunistic behavior and facilitates knowledge sharing across the business entities. Given that trust is the basic foundation for resource sharing and information exchange, we propose that trust contributes directly to knowledge sharing.

H6. Inter-organizational trust influences knowledge sharing positively.

## 4. Research method and design

## 4.1. Questionnaire design and pilot study

This study adopted the quantitative research method to verify theoretical constructs and investigate the relationships between variables. Research constructs were validated through theoretical, empirical, and construct examinations [6]. Initial constructs were developed and validated via prior published studies. Two professors with substantial experience in this subject area helped us to examine the appropriateness and clarity of the content wording.

A total of 27 EMBA students who had experience in supply chain management assisted with the pre-test questionnaire; two incomplete submissions were eliminated. This group of students was relatively homogeneous in terms of years of industry experience, project management experience, and involvement in supply-chain coordination. Their experience allowed them to offer industry-based feedback on the questionnaire items, as well as the validity and wording of the items. Cronbach’s a was applied to test the consistency of the measurement constructs. A Cronbach’s alpha of 0.7 or higher is commonly considered as a benchmark for reliability testing [20]. The Cronbach’s alphas for the pre-test exceeded 0.8 across all measurement variables.

## 4.2. Research sample

The manufacturing industry was chosen as the sample frame for this study. The manufacturing industry plays a major role in terms of the supply chain context, as it transforms raw materials and components into products. Further, the manufacturing industry was seen as an ideal target due to the intensive communication and interaction that occurs between suppliers, wholesalers, and retailers.

In Taiwan, industries consist of many small- and mediumsized organizations that specialize in a narrow range of value chain activities. Organizations constantly engage in participation, extension, and connection with industrial network systems. More specifically, Taiwanese firms serve as valuable sources of information for several reasons. First, according to Chow et al. [13], Taiwan is one of the major business partners of the US, and is somewhat similar to the US in terms of technological and business models. Second, many Taiwanese managers are educated in the US, resulting in similar business practices. Third, Taiwan’s manufacturing sectors, such as computer and semiconductor manufacturing, are key players in the global production network. For these reasons, Taiwan’s manufacturing industry was chosen for this investigation into knowledge sharing and inter-organizational collaboration practices.

This study targeted manufacturing organizations located in the major science and industrial parks in the northern, central, and southern regions of Taiwan for data collection. The research sample firms were selected using publicly available resources such as the park administration centers. In general, the selected firms had experience in cross-organizational data exchange and knowledge sharing. Managers and IT professionals were utilized as informants for this study because they often become involved in knowledge sharing activities when they interact with business partners.

A total of 623 firms were contacted and requested to participate in this study. Following the data collection process suggested by Dillman [18], each firm was contacted multiple times. First, a cover letter explaining the purpose and importance of this study was provided together with the actual questionnaire to solicit participation. Second, we made a follow-up phone call two weeks later to encourage participation. We requested that the questionnaire recipients to forward the questionnaire to the managers responsible for decisions pertaining to inter-organizational knowledge sharing and collaboration.

Two rounds of data collection were performed in this study. In the first round, we received responses from 234 of the 623 firms; however, we eliminated four questionnaires with missing values and 35 that were answered by employees rather than managers. We tested the research model using the first round data and found that both shared goals and relational embeddedness were highly correlated, and exhibited low discriminant validity. To understand whether or not the low discriminant validity was due to insufficient data or the research design, we conducted a second round of data collection to solicit participation from those who did not respond during the first round. In total, we received 31 valid responses, for a total of 226 usable questionnaires (a 36.3% response rate).

## 4.3. Descriptive data analysis

Table 1 depicts both the respondent and firm characteristics. According to the Taiwan Research Institute, firms with annual sales less than 100 million New Taiwan Dollars (equivalent to USD 3.3 million) or with less than 100 employees are classified as small- to medium-sized business. As such, 64.6% of the responding firms were considered large, with more than 100 employees; 67.7% of the responding firms were considered large, with annual sales exceeding 100 million New Taiwan Dollars; 42.9% of the respondents were middle or top managers with a job title of director or higher in terms of employee hierarchy; and 82.3% of the respondents considered cross-organizational knowledge sharing as important or very important.

It should be noted that the respondent profiles were comprised of middle-top managers, line managers and professionals. Therefore, we split the data into two categories based on the different task content and involvement in corporate decision making: middle-top managers and others. To examine the differences between these two groups, we performed a chi-square test (<sup>2</sup>) to test the homogeneity of the proportions. The test results did not reveal a significant difference across the research variables except for the two measurement indicators related to influence strategies. Since line managers and professionals are seldom involved in corporate strategies, we decided to retain these questions.

## 4.4. Model testing

To analyze the data, we employed structural equation modelling (SEM) using AMOS 7.0. SEM is a powerful multivariate analysis technique used to measure latent variables and test causal relationships among variables. In particular, SEM allows the researcher to conduct confirmatory factor analyses for theory testing and development. As such, SEM was deemed suitable to test the research hypotheses in this study.

This study also utilized confirmatory factor analysis to validate the proposed measurement instruments. According to Hair et al. [20], an ideal factor loading threshold is 0.7 or above. The factor loadings of our measurement items exceeded 0.7, which indicates high convergent validity. Further, the squared multiple correlations (SMC) were calculated to examine the reliability of the measurement indicators [20]. Two indicators of relational embeddedness and two indicators of influence strategy presented in this study failed to reach the threshold of 0.5 during SMC testing, and were eliminated from further analysis. The eliminated indicators may suggest that sampled organizations do not utilize a coercive influence strategy to force business partners to share knowledge. Additionally, research variables related to calculus-based trust also failed to reach the threshold. This analytical outcome reflects the typical Chinese culture, where networks of relationships among various parties are essential to ensure cooperation [9]. It also suggests that the effectiveness of knowledge sharing is often determined by the social satisfaction gained by the exchange parties, and reflected in the well-developed business relationships within Chinese society. As such, we eliminated calculus-based trust variables, and retained only the knowledge-based and identification-based trust variables.

The average variance extracted (AVE) represents the measurement items of the respective constructs. Hair et al. [20] suggest that an AVE of 0.5 or higher indicates adequate convergence. The AVE for each construct in this study exceeded 0.5, which supports their discriminant validity. Furthermore, the composite reliability (CR) was tested to measure the reliability and internal consistency of the measured variables representing latent constructs. A CR value of 0.7 or higher is seen as ideal based on Hair et al. [20]. The CR values in the present study all exceeded 0.75, indicating internal consistency. Table 2 presents the measurement indicators and the results of confirmatory factor analyses.

This study further tested the convergent and discriminant validity of the research constructs. Table 3 depicts the correlation coefficients between the research variables. The high correlation between shared goals and relational embeddedness remained a concern for discriminant validity.

To determine whether the research constructs satisfied the basic requirements for discriminant validity, we conducted a factor analysis and chi-square difference test [20]. The Bartlett test of sphericity was significant, indicating the appropriateness of conducting this factor analysis. Further, no cross-loadings were found. However, two measurement indicators of relational embeddedness were loaded on to shared goals. We still decided to retain all four measurement indicators of relational embeddedness since these measurement constructs were developed based on prior studies. Table 4 outlines the exploratory factor analysis results. A chi-square differences test was further conducted to test the discriminant validity of the research construct [2,20]. Table 5 reports the result of three pairwise comparisons of correlation constrained with correlation unconstrained models. The results showed significant differences of shared goals, relational embeddedness and influence strategy, suggesting strong support for the discriminant validity. We also conducted a t-test to examine if they were theoretically independent from each other. The results were significant and support the discriminant validity of the proposed research constructs.

We further tested the model fit of the entire research model. All the measurement indicators exceeded the threshold value [20] except for GFI (=0.848), which was due to an insufficient sample size in terms of the sampling distribution. Despite this result, the measurement model still exhibited good fit across many different fit indices within a reasonable range [20], as shown in Table 6.

## 5. Data analysis and research findings

## 5.1. Control variables

We controlled for two variables that might affect interorganizational trust and knowledge sharing: annual sales and number of employees. The former, measured as the sales volume, was controlled for since it may be highly correlated to the interorganizational trust and knowledge sharing. The latter, which is one of the major indicators of firm size, was controlled for to eliminate its impact on inter-organizational trust and knowledge sharing. The linear regression results suggest that neither annual sales (b = -0.088, t = -0.871, p = 0.385, VIF = 2.262) nor employee numbers (b = 0.046, t = 0.456, p = 0.648, VIF = 2.262) had a significant impact on inter-organizational trust. Moreover, annual sales (b = 0.070, t = 0.693, p = 0.489, VIF = 2.262) and employee numbers (b = -0.120, t = -1.195, p = 0.233, VIF = 2.262) were not significant in terms of knowledge sharing.

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

This study contributes to the growing body of literature exploring the impact of influence strategies, shared goals, relational embeddedness, and inter-organizational trust on inter-organizational knowledge sharing and collaboration. The research findings are not only consistent with prior research results, but also offer new empirical insights on how relational and culture issues drive inter-organizational collaboration and knowledge sharing.

This study extends our understanding of the social impact of inter-organizational knowledge sharing and collaboration. This study supplements prior research in that we incorporated strategic and relational issues in inter-organizational research and examined their varying effects on the development of inter-organizational trust. Prior e-commerce and information systems studies have seldom examined the mixed effects of strategic, relational, and cultural issues on the development of inter-organizational trust. Our study extends the research scope from consumer behavior to the impact of relationships within an inter-organizational context. The empirical findings suggest that exchange parties should develop shared goals and bounded relationships to enhance the perceived trustworthiness of the trading partners [35]. Further, the results suggest that dominant firms can request that their business partners share important knowledge and utilize legal protection such as contracts to strengthen the inter-organizational trust. This study extends the theoretical scope of organizational trust and highlights the strategic importance of social, institutional, and cultural factors in terms of inter-organizational interaction. Organizations that intend to streamline value chain activities and take advantage of the business benefits should endeavor to develop bonded business relationships, positions of dominance, and shared visions in a timely manner.

Furthermore, this research study also contributes to interorganizational research by integrating institutional, knowledge management, and social theory perspectives. Our results highlight the important role of inter-organizational trust in collaboration and knowledge sharing between and among the value chain organizations. Our study is one of few that attempts to empirically investigate the factors that drive inter-organizational knowledge sharing. Prior studies have predominantly focused on knowledge sharing within the organizational context. Our results indicate that inter-organizational trust is the cornerstone required to develop and establish business collaboration and cross-organizational data exchange and knowledge sharing. As a result, organizations that seek to take advantage of business opportunities through interorganizational knowledge sharing and data exchange should endeavor to develop mutual trust.

## 6.2. Practical implications

Research in information systems (IS) frequently highlights the strategic importance of IS to creating and capturing business benefits. Despite the sky-rise investment in IS, the operational efficiencies provided by IS are limited unless the organization can effectively use IS to achieve strategic alliances across organizational boundaries. Knowledge sharing and inter-organizational collaboration are critical to achieving competitive advantage through IS. As such, it is important for managers to understand the underpinning driving forces behind enhanced inter-organizational resource sharing.

By exploring the driving forces of inter-organizational collaboration and knowledge sharing, this study suggests that organizations can decrease the uncertainty and risks associated with knowledge sharing, and nurture inter-organizational trust by using the influence strategy, developing embedded relationships, and creating shared goals with supply-chain partners. Our findings suggest that organizations should consider embedded relationships as more important than either influence strategy or shared goals in terms of developing inter-organizational trust. This result provides empirical insights for organizations intending to pursue competitive advantages through supply-chain collaboration and cooperation. Recent research investigating institutional factors associated with eSCMs adoption has suggested that dominant organizations exercise power to motivate business partners to adopt eSCMs [26]. Our findings suggest that organizations can also exercise non-coercive strategies that include legal protection to achieve knowledge sharing and collaboration within the supply chain context. Organizations that intend to strengthen business partnerships through supply-chain integration should focus on developing embedded relationships to increase organizational trust.

The research results also suggest that decision makers that focus on using inter-organizational information systems for resource coordination and leverage should develop deep trusting relationships with their business partners. The positive association between inter-organizational trust and knowledge sharing also implies that trusting relationships may further motivate the exchange parties to share resources and information, and thereby contribute to the efficiency of joint performance. Managers or employees who are in charge of inter-organizational communication and interaction can build trust by constructing social platforms and developing shared goals.

In summary, the current study contributes to the growing body of literature exploring the impact of institutional factors, social relationships, cultural factors and trust on inter-organizational knowledge sharing and collaboration. This investigation provides a framework that illustrates the relationships between influence strategy, relational embeddedness, shared goals, organizational trust, collaboration and knowledge sharing. More importantly, this study extends trust research from individual consumer behavior to the inter-organizational context. It details the important issues underpinning the growth and strengthening of business coordination and collaboration, and also provides empirical evidence for market planners and decision makers to help them balance technological investments and social relationships, which can lead to even greater inter-organizational knowledge sharing and collaboration. Finally, the research findings are valuable for industrial players who seek to improve the operation of supply chains.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

While this study enhances our understanding of the driving forces of inter-organizational knowledge sharing and collaboration, three research limitations need to be highlighted. First, this study focuses on the manufacturing industry in Taiwan. Although this industry represents an ideal supply-chain context and information source, the research sample demographic characteristics might limit the generalizability and applicability of the research findings to different contexts. For example, it is wellknown that Chinese culture differs from western culture to the extent that Chinese are collectivist, possess a high power distance, and focus on social relationships [24]. Chinese people develop relationships at an inter-personal level, whereas westerners usually focus on building an inter-organizational relationships [38]. In Chinese society, inter-personal relationships play a determinant role in terms of business interactions. Thus, the antecedents of inter-organizational trust proposed by this study should be further examined in a western context to determine the generalizability of the findings. Also, we gathered information from managers and professionals without considering their level of involvement or experience in inter-organizational interactions. Of critical concern is that these factors may have influenced the perceived impact of influence strategy, perceived relational embeddedness, or trust, which in turn may have influenced the research outcomes. Future research is needed to control for these factors and to test the research findings in diverse contexts.

Second, this study investigates the impact of influence strategy, shared goals, and relational embeddedness on the inter-organizational context––other possible antecedents were not considered. While these three factors are important in terms of discovering the major driving forces of developing inter-organizational trust, there are many other potential factors. For example, Dayasindhu [17] argues that structural embeddedness provides an informal mechanism for building social relationships, while Hausman and Johnston [21] highlight the strategic effects of non-coercive strategies in trust development. Future studies can extend the research scope to identify and test other factors that may influence inter-organizational trust and consequent knowledge sharing and collaboration.

In addition, while the current research findings outline important relational factors that affect enhanced collaborative relationships and increased knowledge sharing, the research method may still require further refinement to improve the predictability of the research variables. For example, the selfreported questionnaire may have introduced common method bias [16]. Future research studies should devise a novel data collection method to minimize the potential for common method bias. Also, as noted above, the research model failed to pass the GFI fit statistic examinations. While our research model still shows a good model fit between the research theory and sample data, larger samples would help to revalidate the model.

Finally, according to Lewicki and Bunker [29], trust is developed in calculus-based, knowledge-based, and identification-based stages. However, the current research findings only confirm the importance of knowledge-based and identificationbased trust. There are two possible reasons for the lack of support for calculus-based trust. First, this outcome reflects the unique relationship development and management practices common in Chinese cultures. According to Shaalan et al. [38], Chinese societies focus on building inter-personal relationships. To this end, the survey respondents may have developed some close friendships within the supply chain context, and subsequently extended these personal relationships into the business interactions. Secondly, Lewicki and Bunker [22] argue that calculusbased, knowledge-based, and identification-based trust tends to develop sequentially; calculus-based trust matters most in the early stages of inter-organizational interaction. Organizations that have developed a collaborative relationship and pursued knowledge sharing may have already migrated from calculusbased trust to a more advanced type of business relationship. The static model used in this study is only able to capture a snapshot of inter-organizational trust; as such, future research should observe and test a more dynamic developmental process.
