## Highlights

 Economic and hedonic benefits impact trust and commitment within the brand fan page

 Trust and commitment toward a brand fan page influence brand relationship quality

 Brand relationship quality mediates trust and commitment on fan page word-ofmouth

 Young and female fans having a strong relationship with the brand spread positive WOM

 Fans with a low participation in the fan page are more sensitive to hedonic benefits

## Abstract

This study seeks to clarify the antecedents and consequences of trust and commitment within the brand fan page context on Facebook, examining a sample of 210 respondents using structural equation modeling. The results highlight the positive effect of economic and hedonic benefits on trust and commitment within the brand fan page. Mediation analysis reveals that trust and commitment developed within the brand fan page will be transformed into positive “word of mouth” for the respective brand if fans have a strong relationship quality with the brand. Further, we found that young and female fans with a high level of engagement, having a strong relationship with the brand, spread positive WOM. Our findings broaden ways for developing relational governance in a firm-initiated virtual brand community by providing new levers and guidance for marketers to build strong customer relationships.

Keywords: trust, commitment, brand fan pages, Facebook, brand relationship quality, wordof-mouth, hedonic benefits, economic benefits, virtual brand community

## 1. Introduction

Facebook is a dominant social network site with more than 2,167 billion monthly active users<sup>1</sup> attracting more and more marketing investments with \$5.09 billion in revenues (Syncapse, 2013). Moreover, it provides an easy-to-use interface for individuals to establish and maintain social relationships. It is a tool that has perceived usefulness (Ngai et al., 2015).

However, given the growth in the volume of brand fans (e.g., often in the millions for major brands), the risk of letting consumers entirely rule online conversations has urged companies to adopt an active social media presence for customer relationship reasons, attempting to get closer to customers (Magniant & Lévy-Béncheton, 2012). Indeed, no brand is immune to damaging content from Internet users.

Consequently, many firms have created virtual brand communities on social networking sites in the form of brand fan pages. These brand public profiles are established to create a community of consumers who interact with a company through liking or commenting on brand posts or through sharing information (Bushelow, 2012). For many firms, brand fan pages outperform official websites in terms of consumer traffic (Neff, 2010; Zastrow, 2013).

As such, Facebook is seen as “the biggest relationship-marketing provider” for many brands (Neff, 2010). Facebook’s characteristics “provide unique and interesting conditions for investigating the interaction of multiple selves and the incorporation of brands in consumer self-expression” (Hollenbeck & Kaikati, 2012, p. 396).

The brand fan page can be defined as a firm-initiated virtual brand community (FiVBC, hereafter), which is a “specialized, nongeographically bound community based on a structured set of social relationships among admirers of a brand” (Muniz & O’Guinn, 2001, p. 412). The goal is to build and develop relationships with current and potential consumers by inducing dialogue and productive feedback around brand-related topics (Magniant & Lévy-Béncheton,

2012) including the relational dimension (Sung et al., 2010). Beyond the number of fan page members, which has become a brand awareness indicator, the understanding of relational mechanisms, such as trust and commitment within the FiVBC and relationship quality that govern the relationship between fans and the brand, is becoming a prerequisite to customer relationship management (CRM).

Many scholars stress the usefulness of managing the conversations (Greenberg, 2010) within the FiVBC (e.g., a brand fan page) to impact the return on the social marketing investment. From this perspective, the FiVBC may lead to a variety of related benefits: (1) a direct and interactive communication with fans (e.g., Muniz & Schau 2005; Bagozzi & Dholakia, 2006), (2) a greater community engagement (Algesheimer et al., 2005), (3) influencing fellow consumers about products, (4) peer recommendations (Smith et al., 2005, Beukeboom et al., 2015), (5) innovation and faster adoption of new products (Thompson & Sinha, 2008), and (6) improved loyalty to the company that owns the brand (Porter & Donthu, 2008). Taken together, the studies on these benefits suggest that the FiVBC could influence the competitiveness of the company. Indeed, as brand fan page membership does not require prior approval (Muniz & O’Guinn, 2001), fans—current customers or those who have never owned the brand—may be members of many brand communities, even within the same product category, intensifying the rivalry among direct competitors (Thompson & Sinha, 2008). Against this background, a positive word-of-mouth (WOM) is particularly relevant for brands and companies, as fellow consumers are seen to be more credible sources of information (Kozinets, 2002).

This shift leads inevitably to relationship marketing implications because the conversations must be managed so that they bring a return on the social marketing investment. By making efforts to provide access to quality content, to foster member embeddedness, and to encourage interaction, firms can support relationship development and drive favorable outcomes from virtual communities (Porter & Donthu, 2008; De Vries et al., 2012).

In addressing the issue that marketers are not satisfied with the metrics currently available to evaluate their social media investments (eMarketer 2012), several studies point to the need for a better understanding of how consumers are influenced by different relational (Balasubramanian & Mahajan 2001; Dholokia et al., 2004; Porter & Donthu, 2008).

Despite Barwise and Meehan’s (2010) study on the increasing effect of social media on company reputation and Colliander and Dahlén’s (2011) work on improving brand publicity in social and “traditional” digital media (e.g., online magazines), little research has been conducted on how social media networking business initiatives, such as brand fan pages, influence virtual community attitudes (Gummerus et al., 2012; Sung et al., 2010), improve the view of brand relationship quality, and spread positive WOM (Chu & Kim, 2011; Coulter & Roggeveen, 2012).

Indeed, the growth of social media and its popularity among consumers (Schivinski & consumers (Hutter et al., 2013; Jahn & Kunz, 2012). In this sense, WOM has been found to be a powerful force in persuasion (Sen & Lerman, 2007; Coulter & Roggeveen, 2012), and it is paramount to identify the drivers leading members of brand fan pages to diffuse positive WOM about the brand. A lot of research has been devoted to the drivers of the participation of consumers in online communities. However, as brand fan pages are viewed as volitional social networking pages bringing together admirers of brands, we should go beyond the precursors of participation. In particular, there is very limited research about the relationship between consumers’ goals for being members of brand fan pages, such as finding reliable information in real time, gaining access to promotions—economic benefits—or escaping from challenges, gaining intrinsic cultural and aesthetic enjoyment, and seeking emotional release—hedonic benefits,—and their attitudes, in this case, trust and commitment within the virtual brand community, and their behavior relative to that brand.

This study attempts to fill the abovementioned gaps. Based on prior research, we investigate economic and hedonic benefits, trust and commitment related to fan pages on Facebook, brand relationship quality, and WOM and its moderators (gender, age, and engagement). More specifically, the study attempts to answer two overarching research questions:

(1) Do hedonic and economic benefits drive trust and commitment toward the brand fan page on Facebook? Further, to what extent does the induced trust and commitment toward the brand fan page cause the consumer to view the brand relationship quality more positively?

(2) What is the mechanism through which trust and commitment within the brand fan pages and the brand relationship quality affect WOM? Further, does the brand relationship quality mediate the moderation effect of engagement, gender, and age on WOM?

By investigating these questions, the aim of this research is to identify the determinants of trust and commitment within the FiVBC setting as well as their proximal (relationship brand quality) and distal outcomes (WOM). This becomes even more important as digital marketing communication budgets are reaching 30% in some organizations. Thus, understanding these issues within FiVBCs is a top priority of marketing communications (Valos et al., 2016).

The next section presents the theoretical background followed by the conceptual framework and its relational constructs. After detailing the analysis and the results of a quantitative survey of 210 brand fans on Facebook, this article presents some implications, research limitations, and possible future research directions.

## 2. Literature review and hypotheses

## 2.1. Theoretical framework

In the literature, several streams have been used to inform our understanding of user behavior. Of specific importance are social exchange theory (SET, hereafter; Thibault & Kelley, 1959), social capital theory (Bourdieu, 1985), and uses and gratifications theory (UGT, hereafter; Blumler & Katz, 1974).

At the confluence of psychology, economics, and sociology, SET asserts that human behavior and social interactions entail exchanges of material and non-material goods including symbols of approval or prestige (Homans, 1974). Characterized by reciprocity, such exchanges go beyond the commercial sphere (Blau, 1964); that is, social exchanges result from voluntary acts by individuals, motivated by the return that they predict their actions will generate, through a response from the exchange partner. These motivations often reflect a desire to obtain a gain (economic or noneconomic) or at least limit loss (e.g., financial costs, capital investments, and time). Finally, according to SET, the tangible and intangible benefits obtained, or any rewards provided by the exchange, must increase over time; otherwise, the actors gradually disengage and terminate the exchange relationship.

Recent studies have demonstrated that when a consumer engages regularly with a brand through social networks and co-creates content, the relationship improves (Culnan et al., 2010). Arguably, fans minimize the cost of their communications with the brand—“liking” costs nothing—and get, beyond tangible rewards, more knowledge and updates about the brand. The fan can feel like a partner and becomes more faithful to the company’s existing products, as well as ready to test new offerings and more resistant to disparaging communications about the brand. The existence of a mutual social exchange and reciprocity between fans and brands means that fans and brands primarily want to create or improve their relationships (Piskorski, 2014). Accordingly, it is important that the brand maintains a strong, quality relationship based on trust, commitment, and satisfaction with its fans.

UGT, from a user-centric perspective, addresses two central aspects: why and how people actively look for a specific media to meet their needs better (Blumler & Katz, 1974). UGT was first applied to understand mass communication in traditional media. Thereafter, in social media searches, UGT has been used to identify precursors to satisfying consumer needs and to increasing their participation in social networks (Dholakia et al., 2004; Porter & Donthu, 2008). The gratification applying to fan pages on Facebook could be related to the content and topics about the liked brand as well as to the process (experience of use and the benefits that ensue). Moreover, gratification could result from shared identities and affiliation with likeminded members interacting with the same brand (Muntinga et al., 2011).

Social capital theory (Bourdieu, 1986) explains the influence of social connections and social relations on goal achievement and corresponds to the “resources embedded within, available through and derived from the network of relationships possessed by an individual or social unit” (p. 376). According to Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998), the relational dimension of social capital theory refers to the trust, obligation, and identification (commitment) present in personal relationships between people or the mutual trust, respect, and friendship that arise out theoretical lens, insofar as trust represents a fundamental element and is developed through a history of interactions (Granovetter, 1985; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). The strength and homophily (the degree to which “actors” in the social networks perceive each other as close and similar and reciprocally connected) represent the two main features of relational capital. In the context of virtual brand communities, a common interest (the brand) that strengthens members’ ties (Lin & Lu, 2011) may lead to active information-seeking behaviors (e.g., sought after WOM), as strong ties are crucial and more important to the flow of influence than those perceived as weak (Brown & Reingen, 1987). However, especially in an FiVBC on Facebook and when the brand is well known, with online review platforms of products increasingly available, instead of making the effort to actively seek information, consumers, even by adopting a passive strategy, follow reviews of other fans. In particular, when people share information, positive or negative experiences with respect to the brand, Facebook receives high scores (Wetzer et al., 2007). When trust is built through repeated transactions (e.g., posts on the fan page or purchases of the brand), fans are more willing to share information and to engage in open communication with the brand (Porter & Donthu, 2008).

## 2.2. Antecedents of trust and commitment within the FiVBC

## 2.2.1. Benefits of the FiVBC

As a volitional social networking site, the primary goal of being a member of an FiVBC on Facebook is to interact with the brand, also implying an expectation of tangible or intangible returns.

In addition to community identification (Tsai & Men, 2013) and desirable personal image projection as part of that network (Boyd & Ellison, 2008; Cova & Pace, 2006), the benefits sought by members of the FiVBC on Facebook could be considered as both process and content rewards. They entail economic and hedonic aspects. Using a content analysis of 89 and games, as well as economic benefits like contests or sweepstakes, were used. Seeking those benefits causes, in turn, more regular and frequent interactions in the FiVBC on Facebook between the brand and the fans. It further demonstrates the brand’s willingness to maintain twoway communications by listening and responding to its fans and supporting them if needed. Furthermore, the fan’s perception of the regularity of benefits derived from such interactions associated with a very low cost can produce reciprocal behavior (Surma, 2016) such as a sense of trust and commitment toward the FiVBC on Facebook.

## 2.2.2. Trust

#

Based on the Ability, Benevolence, Integrity (ABI) model (Mayer et al., 1995), online trust is defined as a set of positive beliefs or expectations with respect to the competence, integrity, and benevolence of the firm in an online environment (McKnight et al., 2002). Accordingly, this study considers trust as based on the perception of the competence, integrity, and benevolence of the FiVBC. Online trust is not fundamentally different from offline trust anonymous nature of the person managing the FiVBC (Ching & Ellis, 2006), and the risk of private data being hacked make trust between the fan and the FiVBC even more complex. In the context of a brand fan page, centered on a specific mutual concern (e.g., the brand) (Ridings et al., 2002), the FiVBC may be assessed based on its competence to provide fans with appropriate responses. Likewise, integrity refers to the belief that the FiVBC will respect brand fans by fulfilling its promises and being sincere (Gundlach & Murphy, 1993; Doney & Cannon, 1997) without any opportunistic behaviors. Finally, benevolence, as good intentions, support, and caring shown toward fans, may be expressed by taking their safety and security into account (Ridings et al., 2002; Ba & Pavlou, 2002).

## 2.2.3. Commitment

Commitment represents “the consumer’s psychological attachment toward the online service provider, along with his/her willingness to maintain the customer–firm relationship” (Liang & Chen, 2009, p. 222). Allen and Meyer (1990), within an organizational framework, identified three forms of commitment: affective, based on an assessment of and identification with a partner; continuance or instrumental, which results from the calculation of switching costs and the dependence associated with being in a relationship with a partner; and normative, referring to the sense of a moral obligation to remain in the relationship. The affective component, as represented by emotional attachment, is the most widely used facet in prior research that seeks to explain commitment to a partner or a brand because it implies a desire to maintain a lasting relationship (Moorman et al., 1992; Morgan & Hunt, 1994). When consumers come to like (or, in some cases, love brands or service providers), they are experiencing the psychological state of affective commitment. This affective commitment develops because the consumer identifies with and likes the brand and the FiVBC (Cater & Zabkar, 2009; Vesel & Zabkar, 2010). It is, therefore, an attachment based on the individual’s desire to visit and use the FiVBC and continue the relationship (Bansal et al., 2004; Brun et al., 2014). This definition seems appropriate for the present study context as well.

## 2.3. Hypothesis development

Because the FiVBC is a channel of interaction, communication, and sharing with others, it provides fans with more opportunities to develop and maintain relationships with the brand as well as with like-minded members (Lin & Lu, 2011). Labrecque (2014), for example, found that perceived interactivity and openness of the brand could influence consumers, improving their relationships. Interestingly, even when this social interaction is somewhat restricted between members, the relational dimension is expressed through indirect consumer inquiries on various topics (e.g., product availability, employment, and detailed information about promotional events) and responses by the brand via its FiVBC. By establishing the brand as a social entity, it implies some reciprocity in the relationship and mutual rewards across parties. The interaction between members and the brand via the fan page is crucial to assess trust and commitment. We investigate below those relational construct cornerstones in developing a good relationship between the brand and the fans.

## 2.3.1. The effect of economic benefits on trust

The majority of FiVBCs provide sales promotions, contests, sweepstakes, and coupons for community members as well as opportunities to experience a free trial of a new product of the brand (Baird & Parasnis, 2011). Sung et al. (2010) found that consumers use brand communities in order to obtain rewards and incentives in exchange for their community participation. In accordance with SET, successful social exchanges that induce a benefit that is concrete facilitate the development of a relational benefit like trust. Feelings associated with the brand through favorable repeated interactions develop into trust. In other words, in the context of a brand fan page, centered on its core asset, the brand itself (Ridings et al., 2002), FiVBC provides fans with appropriate economic benefits, it reinforces both its perceived competence and evinced trustworthiness (Doney & Cannon, 1997). Likewise, by fulfilling its promises (Gundlach & Murphy, 1993; Doney & Cannon, 1997) in its incentive propositions, the brand buttresses its integrity and reduces fans’ perceptions of any opportunistic behavior from the firm. Finally, by supporting and caring about its fans, expressed by taking their “incentive-seeking” motives and well-being into account (Ridings et al., 2002), the brand shows benevolence toward its fans.

H1: Economic benefits positively affect trust in a fan page brand.

## 2.3.2. The effect of economic benefits on commitment

Gwinner et al. (1998) demonstrated that to create successful relationships, the parties must believe that they have won something; the consumer, thus, is motivated to enter into a relationship (i.e., continuance in the FiVBC) to obtain benefits. According to SET (Thibault & Kelley, 1959), any rewards provided by the exchange must increase over time; otherwise, the actors gradually disengage and terminate the exchange relationship. Indeed, the FiVBC offers more than detailed information about the focal brand’s products to the community as it provides additional favors (economic benefits) to virtual community members. In a recent study of 21 international online brand community members, Dessart et al. (2015) stated that some fans “admit being part of online brand communities, mainly for the discounts or rewards they can reap” (p. 37). SET indicates that successful exchanges affect the relationship and can cause one individual to become committed to another (Blau, 1964). Accordingly, as one exchange partner feels a personal obligation to pay back the favor to the other, economic benefits offered by the FiVBC can play a crucial role by feeding continuous supportive exchange (Jin et al., 2010). Arguably, fans will commit to the relationship if they perceive that beyond sharing similar interests with the other FiVBC members, the relationship with the brand provides economic benefits. From the above, we predict that:

H2: Economic benefits positively affect commitment to a fan page brand.

## 2.3.3. The effect of hedonic benefits on trust

Hedonic benefits stem from escaping from challenges, gaining intrinsic cultural and aesthetic enjoyment, and feelings or affective states, such as pleasure, fun, and entertainment, gained through consumer experiences (Wang & Fesenmaier, 2004, Oh et al., 2016). Unlike Facebook. Instead, prior research has focused on information-based communities (e.g., Hennig-Thurau et al., 2004; Porter & Donthu, 2008). Generally, social networking sites can be seen as a source of pleasure. Users continue using them because they provide them with enjoyment (Sledgianowski & Kulviwat, 2009). It has long been argued that a large number of members engaged in diverse activities in online communities are pleasure seekers, attaching interest to the hedonic benefits (Armstrong & Hagel, 1995; Dholokia et al., 2004). Affective gratification and playtime (hedonic attributes) on fan pages create positive emotions similar to gameplay. According to Wicks et al. (1999), positive emotions are a sine qua non in building trust, as they allow the actors to expect that the trust will be honored. However, the introduction of hedonic activities may also erode trust that already exists around the incumbent brand’s website (Muniz & O’Guinn, 2001). Given these conflicting findings, we hypothesize that:

H3: Hedonic benefits positively affect trust in a fan page brand.

## 2.3.4. The effect of hedonic benefits on commitment

Moon and Kim (2001) defined enjoyment as a playfulness state: “when individuals will find the interaction intrinsically interesting: they are involved in the activity for pleasure and enjoyment rather than for extrinsic rewards” (p. 220). Van der Heijden (2004) identified enjoyment and pleasure as motors for “interacting with a hedonic system” that “is designed to be an end in itself” (p. 696). Moreover, Bagozzi and Dholakia (2006) also demonstrated that the positive feelings of enjoyment and happiness considerably improved the desire to commit in a community of members. Individuals’ enjoyment of a virtual community has been shown to positively affect the intention to display and receive helping behavior (Chan & Li, 2010). In the same vein, it has been argued that people should reciprocate by repaying not only those who provide direct help but also the community itself (Muniz & O’Guinn, 2001; Chan & Li, 2010). These perceived benefits, in turn, should influence members’ assessments of their commitment to the FiVBC community and to the brand. This leads us to the following hypothesis:

H4: Hedonic benefits positively affect commitment to a fan page brand.

## 2.3.5. The effect of trust on brand relationship quality

Brand research consistently indicates the presence of a form of brand anthropomorphism (Aaker, 1997), which not only facilitates the identification of brands and people but also provides a context for discussing the “social” relationships between brands and consumers. This personification strategy is fairly widespread in the business world, such as in the form of mascots (human, animal, or symbolic), avatars on websites, and celebrity spokespersons. Insofar as relationships between fans and brands exist and because conversations between a company and individual members are displayed and shared with all other members who visit the FiVBC, fans—whether or not they are current users of the brand—could collectively affect, define, and redefine the consumer–brand relationship (Fournier, 1998). Brand relationship quality generally manifests through relationship-oriented behavior such as the development of trust, commitment, and satisfaction toward a brand (Grégoire et al., 2009; Morgan & Hunt, 1994; Aurier & N’Goala, 2010).

“Brand trust refers to the willingness of the consumer to rely on the ability of the brand to perform its stated function” (Chauduri & Holbrook, 2001, p. 82) despite the risk or uncertainty associated with that brand (Becerra & Badrinarayanan, 2013). Brand trust is critical to relational marketing (Albert et al., 2008) and has been found, in the online context, that it increases belief in products/services and the intention to purchase (Becerra & Badrinarayanan, 2013). More specifically, brand trust is reinforced when customer beliefs are likely to derive from past experiences with the brand. Fans can increase their knowledge of the brand, its product-improving cognition, and their “consciousness of kind” (Bagozzi & Dholakia, 2002). The latter refers to the feeling that binds every member to the other fans around the brand, which helps in reducing uncertainty related to the use of the brand’s products (Ha & Perks, 2005) and thus, should contribute to fostering brand trust. Furthermore, Gamboa and Gonçalves (2014) found that Facebook enhances the relations by increasing (among other mechanisms) trust, customer satisfaction, and commitment. Thus, we predict that:

H5: Trust within the FiVBC has a positive effect on brand relationship quality.

## 2.3.6. The effect of commitment on brand relationship quality

Studies on commitment to a VBC tend to adopt a social, normative perspective (Allen & Meyer, 1990; Raïes et al., 2015), according to which members commit to others as their attachment and duty to the community develop gradually through exchanges (Casaló et al., 2008). However, the commitment to the FiVBC as a desire to maintain a lasting relationship

#

with a partner or a brand (Moorman et al., 1992; Morgan & Hunt, 1994) is represented by emotional attachment. The symbiotic relationships of individual consumers with objects, or the worship of products and brands, offer a tool for the construction of social identity (Baudrillard, 1970). Several authors have recognized the similarities between interpersonal exchanges and exchanges that bind a consumer to a brand (Fournier, 1998; Aaker et al., 2004; Hennig-Thurau interactions in the FiVBC (Casaló et al., 2008), has been shown to positively influence consumer behavior toward the brand (Andersen, 2005; Raïes, 2015). The FiVBC multiplies the interactions between consumers and the company. This repeated exchange demonstrates the presence of a relationship that re-emphasizes the notion that goods/services and prices are only two elements of the exchange. In the FiVBC, the consumer can signal the importance of the brand’s products to the community manager, the marketing agents, or institutions that manage the brand. As revealed by recent empirical findings, frontline staff whose behavior is congruent with the brand can effectively reinforce brand meaning as they interact with customers (Sirianni et al. 2013). Thus, relational elements are at the heart of any brand issues, requiring effort to promote communication and engage in positive dialogue with consumers.

As presented earlier, brand relationship quality consists of trust, commitment, and satisfaction with a brand. Brand commitment is defined as an enduring desire to continue the relationship combined with a willingness to make an effort toward that end (Morgan & Hunt, 1994). The affective component, as represented by emotional attachment, is the most widely used facet in prior research that seeks to explain commitment to a partner or a brand because it implies a desire to maintain a lasting relationship (Morgan & Hunt, 1994). In a B2C setting, Fullerton (2003) defines the affective dimension of commitment as follows: “The essence of affective commitment is that customers come to acquire an emotional attachment to their partner in a consumption relationship. When consumers come to like (or, in some cases, love)

brands or service providers, they are experiencing the psychological state of affective commitment” (p. 334). Insofar as we deal with real brand fans, this definition seems appropriate for the present study context. We argue that in the context of increased global competition, increased price transparency (i.e., easy online comparisons) causes volatility and lowers consumer loyalty; commitment within the FiVBC can help to strengthen brand relationship imitate as they create sustainable competitive advantages (Roberts et al., 2003).

Brand satisfaction is considered an appraisal of satisfaction with and happiness in the relationship (Aaker et al., 2004) as well as a comparison of relationship performance against expectations (Oliver, 1997). This psychological state features various aspects of the relationship exchange process. Since FiVBCs are centered on relationships, the use of fan brand pages will ultimately lead to the building of significant relationships (Tsai & Men, 2013). Following Morgan and Hunt (1994), a relationship in which both parties trust each other generates enough value so that the parties will be committed to the relationship (e.g., Garbarino & Johnson, 1999). Therefore, we predict that:

H6: Commitment within the FiVBC has a positive effect on brand relationship quality.

## 2.3.7. The effect of brand relationship quality on WOM

Recommendations made by peers within a virtual context are considered more trustworthy and dependable and encourage potential buyers to purchase that specific brand (Chu & Kim, 2011; Coulter & Roggeveen, 2012; Hajli, 2014). Recommendations, opinions, and advices are all forms of WOM issued by peers, which may provide reliable communication to people who receive them (Day, 1971). When a consumer is satisfied with the brand (Oliver, 1997), or is committed to, or trusts it (Ranaweera & Prabhu, 2003), she or he is more likely to

spread positive WOM about it. In addition, social media features effectively help to disseminate positive WOM about the brand; on these platforms, consumers can share their brand experiences with peers whom they trust (Greenberg, 2010). Interestingly, customers with higher relationship dispositions tend to recommend more and generate positive WOM (Zeithaml et al., 1996). From investigating consumers’ responses to WOM on social networking sites, Coulter and Roggeveen (2012) deduced that creating brand pages on social network sites, including in establishing long-term relationships. According to Becerra and Badrinarayanan (2013), the willingness to endorse and promote the brand is induced by trust. It would, thus, be useful for companies to identify the impact of brand relationship quality on WOM. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H7: Brand relationship quality has a positive effect on spreading positive WOM about the brand.

## 2.3.8 The mediating role of brand relationship quality

Customer commitment and trust are shown to be important predictors for WOM as commitment and trust in a specific fan page is a prerequisite for spreading positive WOM about the brand (De Matos & Rossi, 2008; Ruiz-Mafe et al., 2012). This is supported by the view that customers who are committed to and trust a brand’s FiVBC are more likely to engage in reciprocal activities that may eventuate in (positive) WOM about the brand (Jung et al. 2013). However, trust itself may not be sufficient to induce positive WOM about the brand but it does help to create and maintain relationship continuity between the customer and the brandespecially in environments where trust becomes a replacement for experiential cues such as the physical product, atmosphere, or the sales force (Verma et al., 2016).

Consequently, trust-triggered relationship continuity in a brand’s FiVBC enhances brand relationship quality and strengthens the relationship between the customer and the brand (i.e., relationship marketing efforts; Verma et al., 2016), encouraging consumers to engage in reciprocal activities, facilitating (positive) WOM (Jung et al., 2013). This supports the mediating effect of brand relationship quality on the relationship between FiVBC trust and WOM. Commitment is likewise shown to be important in establishing a strong relationship with customers (Morgan & Hunt, 1994). In the FiVBC environment, customer commitment (i.e., an enduring desire to maintain a valued relationship with the brand) may enhance customer satisfaction with the brand, emotional attachment with the brand, sense of identification with the brand, and personal meaning for the customer (Garbarino & Johnson, 1999; Sung et al., 2010), thereby providing an extended platform for customers to relate to the brand and other customers (Jung et al., 2013). Thus, brand relationship quality may mediate the relationship between FiVBC commitment and WOM.

## 2.3.9 The moderated mediation of brand relationship quality

Engagement within the FiVBC as a moderator. Consumer engagement within online communities corresponds to the participation in the community as well as the level of activities performed by members in the community. It creates and reinforces relationships likely to enhance the emotional bonds that consumers have with objects, with the community, or with a brand (Raïes et al., 2015). Level of participation is less prevalent in prior literature and has often been confused with consumer commitment (Raïes et al, 2015; Algesheimer et al, 2005). Dholokia et al. (2004) specify that participation level can be measured in terms of frequency and duration, which, in turn, affects members’ familiarity with the community and with the brand. High FiVBC trust shows the willingness of a consumer to rely on the ability of the brand’s virtual platform to perform its stated function (Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001). A consumer showing a high level of engagement with a brand’s FiVBC shows a higher FiVBC trust, which enhances brand–customer relationship quality (McAlexander et al., 2002), which, in turn, stimulates the consumers to show more active presence in a brand’s virtual community (Laroche et al., 2013). However, a consumer with a low level of engagement in a brand’s FiVBC may express lower FiVBC trust, leading to a looser brand–customer relationship, eventuating in lower willingness to actively participate in the brand’s virtual community. Committed customers have strong relationships with brands that bond them to products/services and companies (Moorman et al., 1992). These relationships can be further enhanced with a FiVBC is more likely to induce a higher FiVBC commitment that enhances strong brand relationship quality, which, in turn, facilitates WOM (Jung et al., 2013; Lujja et al., 2017; Sung et al., 2010). In contrast, a customer with low engagement with a brand’s FiVBC may not build strong behavioral, emotional, and cognitive bonds with a brand and the online community surrounding the brand (Lujja et al., 2017), which, in turn, leads to a lower brand relationship quality, eventuating in a moderate WOM (Verma et al., 2016). We hypothesize:

H8a: The relationship between FiVBC trust–brand relationship quality–word-of-mouth is stronger for a high level of engagement within the FiVBC compared to a low level of engagement within the FiVBC.

H8b: The relationship between FiVBC commitment–brand relationship quality–word-ofmouth is stronger for a high level of engagement within the FiVBC compared to a low level of engagement within the FiVBC.

Age as a moderator. Age has been found to have an impact on behavior in online settings in general. Pfeil and Zaphiris’s (2007) findings reveal that older people enjoy building a sense of community in an online setting where they exchange all levels of supportive communication. Ellison et al. (2006) showed that Facebook does appear to play an important role in the process students use to form and maintain social capital. However, age differences between different user groups in the behavior and usage of FiVBCs like Facebook are not tackled. Research shows that young users often visit and update their social network sites and examine other users’ pages,

#

which allows them to interact with others; in turn, this facilitates participants gaining or maintaining peer acceptance (Dunne et al., 2010). Furthermore, social network sites may help young users to make friends more easily and “feel part of the group” (Dunne et al., 2010). As young users share information about their lifestyles, interests, states, and experiences more readily, they offer valuable insights about their behavior and preferences to brands (Boyd & information about their lifestyles and consumer habits (Chakraborty et al., 2013). This infers that younger consumers may trust the FiVBC more, which enhances brand–customer relationships and eventuates in enhanced WOM. However, older consumers may interact less with a brand’s virtual community, have higher risk perceptions associated with a brand (Chaudhuri & Hollbrook, 2001), which represents as lower FiVBC trust, eventuating in moderate WOM. Similarly, compared to older consumers, younger consumers are more active in online communication (Zaphiris & Sarvar, 2006), show a strong sense of belonging to a brand’s virtual community, and care more about a brand’s long-term success (Dunne et al., 2010). This leads to stronger consumer–brand relationships and intensifying WOM. Stated formally:

H9a: The relationship between FiVBC trust–brand relationship quality–word-of-mouth is stronger for younger people compared to older people.

H9b: The relationship between FiVBC commitment–brand relationship quality–word-ofmouth is stronger for younger people compared to older people.

Gender as a moderator. Research shows that social roles emphasized by the social role theory prevalent in the offline world also apply in the online world (Lin et al., 2016). Indeed, by liking or commenting on a post, fans express their own positive self-identity through the brand’s achievements (Lipsman et al., 2012) and, thus, enhance their social experience and social roles. Gender has been found to be a predictive moderator in users’ social network site continuance

For women, the tendency to spread positive WOM might be important because they are, according to Verhaagen (2005), more friendly, unselfish, and bounded together than men (e.g., in participation in online chat rooms). Women also share more easily information about their personal life and express solidarity toward each other more strongly and deeply. In contrast, men favor a pattern of assertive and independent behaviors (Lin et al., and a higher commitment toward the FiVBC, which strengthens brand relationship quality, eventuating in higher WOM. Conversely, men with their assertive, independent, and “caring less about others” behavior may show a lower trust in and a lower commitment toward the FiVBC, which leads to a weaker customer–brand relationship and, in turn, results in lower WOM. The formal testable hypotheses are:

H10a: The relationship between FiVBC trust–brand relationship quality–word-of-mouth is stronger for women than for men.

H10b: The relationship between FiVBC commitment–brand relationship quality–wordof-mouth is stronger for women than for men.

## 3.1. Sampling frame

Although we used convenience sampling, our sample was required to meet certain essential criteria. To this end, we formed our sampling according to recent studies by Harris Interactive (2012) that identified fan profiles for fan pages on Facebook. Before creating the questionnaire, we identified the appropriate sample according to the following criteria:

Respondents must be fans of at least one brand on Facebook and have visited the VBC at least once in the previous two weeks (whether to read information or participate).

The population should include both men and women, although a Harris survey (Etude Harris Interactive, 2012) indicated that slightly more women were present on brand-oriented fan pages (52%).

The population should feature different age groups, considering the increasing maturity of fans of these pages. A Harris study (Etude Harris Interactive, 2012) showed that although fans of these pages were mostly younger than 35 years (60%), those between 35 and 49 years of age represented 24% of the population, and those older than 50 years constituted more than 16%.

Data collection was performed between September and December 2014. An online questionnaire design was used to collect sample data. The computer-aided survey employed allowed for continuous contact with the respondents, for monitoring the stages of completion, for segmentation by behavior, and for delivering targeted messages to respondents. The online questionnaire was sent by email to contacts on social networks, like Facebook, asking them to share the link with their own contacts on Facebook. We also contacted fans via Viadeo or by directly emailing brand fans on Facebook (100) (Coca Cola, Oasis, Nike, Apple, etc.).

In summary, participants received an email invitation or a post placed on their banner Facebook page and provided with a link to the online questionnaire. The 243 responses

#

collected were subjected to the initial filter question—having visited a fan page during the last two weeks—to identify “real” fan followers. According to this criterion, 33 responses had to be discarded, so the final sample comprised 210 completed, usable questionnaires.

Convenience sampling was used to reach the population of online brand fan pages. This convenience sample does not represent the community of fans following brands on Facebook but can shed light on the mechanism underlying how consumers get engaged with online brand fan pages. Brand fan pages in the sample were either representing consumer electronic goods producers or companies marketing fast-moving consumer goods (FMCGs) (e.g., Coca Cola, Oasis, Nike, Apple, and other internationally well-recognized brands). The diversity of the brand portfolio in our sample allowed us to obtain a more diversified picture of how consumers relate to brand fan pages. In terms of the frequency of their visits to VBCs, the 210 respondents were evident fans: 122 (58.1%) visited VBCs several times per month and 88 (41.9%) visited them several times per week. Therefore, the respondents were regular users of VBCs on Facebook.

The majority of the respondents were between 18 and 35 years of age (58.6%). In addition, 87 respondents (39.9%) were students, a rate consistent with the general usage trends for Facebook. General laborers represented 25.7% of the respondents, followed by managerial professionals (22%) (see Appendix 1).

Among all the brand types with communities on Facebook, the VBCs most followed by consumers related to clothing, accessories, or shoe brands; 104 respondents (49.5%) chose this type of brand as their focus in answering the questionnaire. ## 3.2. Measurement

Measurement scales were taken from the literature and adapted to this research context. All scale items were derived from the prior literature on virtual communities and branding. Moreover, they have been used extensively across different contexts (Finland, Korea, US, etc.), indicating satisfactory validity and reliability. Their psychometric qualities have also been confirmed in the context of our study, which reinforces their generalizability. The scale of economic benefits was assessed with four items (to get bonuses, to participate in lotteries, to get better service, and to get fast responses) borrowed from Gummerus et al. (2012) and Dholakia et al. (2004), and the scale of hedonic benefits with five items (to relax, to pass the time when bored, to be entertained, because it is enjoyable, and to play) taken from Sung et al. (2010). FiVBC trust and FiVBC commitment were measured with three items for trust (this brand fan page is reliable and dependable, I have a lot of respect for this brand fan page, and I feel safe and secure when I use this brand fan page) and three items for commitment (I am proud to belong to this brand community, I feel a sense of belonging to this brand community, and I care about the long-term success of this brand community), which were derived from Garbarino and Johnson (1999) and Sung et al. (2010). Three components (brand trust, brand commitment, and brand satisfaction) of brand relationship quality as a second-order factor were assessed with four items for brand trust (I trust the product quality of this brand, buying this

#

brand’s products is a safe bet, this brand is sincere with consumers, and this brand is honest with consumers) adapted from Gurviez and Korchia (2002); three items for brand commitment (I feel emotionally attached to this brand, this brand has a great deal of personal meaning for me, and I feel a strong sense of identification with this brand) taken from Fullerton (2003); and three items for brand satisfaction (I am satisfied with this brand’s products, I am satisfied with this brand, and I am pleased with this brand) adapted from Aaker et al. (2004). The measure for WOM comprised three items (I will say positive things about this brand to other people, I will recommend this brand to someone who seeks my advice, and I will encourage friends and relatives to do business with this brand) derived from Zeithaml et al. (1996). All the scales were assessed with five-point Likert scales with anchor points of 1= “totally agree” and 5= “totally disagree.” (For a detailed description of measurement items, please refer to Appendix 3.) Three multi-group moderators were used in the model, with engagement with VBCs measured on a four-point Likert scale (how many times, on average, do you visit a brand fan page?) adapted from Raïes (2015), and age and gender assessed with single-item measures.

## 3.3. Method

The data analysis followed two sequential steps: (1) confirmatory factor analysis to assess the discriminant validity of latent constructs and (2) structural equation modeling for hypothesis testing. For this purpose, AMOS 22.0 was used. In the confirmatory factor analysis, a 28-item measurement model with eight latent constructs was built (Appendix 3) and tested using the sample data. Altogether, three measurement items (V7, V8, and V13) were removed from the initial factor structure due to low factor loadings (<0.60) and/or high modification indices. The purified model with the remaining 24 items indicated a good fit to the data with $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f } { = } 1 . 9 3 2$ (p<0.001), CFI=0.945, NFI=0.894, GFI=0.849, and RMSEA=0.067. All item loadings on their corresponding latent constructs were significant at the 0.05 level (shown in Appendix 3), demonstrating adequate convergent validity. When assessing factor structure, each measure’s variable was related to its respective construct, showing no cross loading with other constructs in the factor structure. Thus, unidimensionality was achieved.

By assessing discriminant validity, the procedure outlined by Fornell and Larcker (1981) was applied. For this, the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) for each of the latent constructs was compared to the between-construct correlations (i.e., shared variance). An examination of the AVE showed that for each pair of variables, the extracted variance was greater than the squared correlations between the constructs. As such, discriminant validity was achieved (see Appendix 2). Construct reliability was measured with Cronbach’s alpha where all constructs exceeded the 0.70 level recommended by Nunnally (1978).

Next, a structural equation model was built to test the hypotheses. Here, brand relationship quality was treated as a second-order construct composed of brand trust, brand commitment, and brand satisfaction. Considering the small sample size and the relative complexity of the structural model, the fit indices showed a reasonable fit to the data (χ<sup>2</sup>/df=2.457, p<0.001; CFI=0.909; NFI=0.856; GFI=0.803; and RMSEA=0.083) (Hair et al., 2006). The structural model could explain 68.4% of the variance in the exogenous variable. Moderating effects were assessed using categorical moderator variables (Baron & Kenny, 1986); structural equation modeling followed the logic of the multi-group analysis (i.e., the moderator affecting every relationship in the model) (Hair et al., 2014). The mediation effects in the general theoretical model were tested using bootstrapping (MacKinnon et al., 2004). The main effect findings of the structural equation modeling together with multi-group moderations are presented in Tables 35.

## 4. Discussion and implications

The aim of the study is to empirically investigate the determinants of trust and commitment within the VBC in the FiVBC setting and its consequences, namely positive WOM. The study specifically examines the mediating role of the brand relationship quality on the relationships between trust and commitment within the fan page and WOM and the moderating role of gender, age, and engagement on the above relationships.

This study sought to respond to calls for unpacking the multifaceted role that the brand’s social network may play in marketing activities as well as for jointly investigating customers online community attitudes, namely trust and commitment within the FiVBC and offline relationship quality with the brand (John et al., 2017; Surma, 2016; Nambisan & Watt, 2011). The study further responds to the need to integrate consumer profiles and their level of

#

engagement to show differences in their attitudes and behaviors (Ruiz-Mafe et al., 2014). As consumers are often willing to “like” brands (John et al., 2017), identifying the relational mechanisms by which the FiVBC affects offline consumer attitudes and increases positive marketing outcomes (e.g., WOM) remains a key challenge. This is all the more important as prior research underscores that Facebook “Likes” and the brand consumption are not its outcomes is worthy of a more thorough exploration (Nelson-Field et al., 2012). Furthermore, by treating WOM as a dependent variable and elucidating the path by which positive WOM is generated, our research responds to the call from Wien and Olsen (2012) to examine the determinants of WOM as prior research has focused largely on understanding the process and outcomes of WOM communication.

Using structural equation modeling, our findings show the positive impact of brand fan pages on fostering consumer–brand relationship quality. Our study not only confirms the prior findings that WOM and social media (e.g., Facebook) are inextricable forms of marketing (Kozinets et al., 2010) but also extends these by underlying the importance of relationship marketing variables. More specifically, our results highlight that hedonic benefits and, to a lesser extent, economic benefits are found to positively affect trust and commitment within VBCs, while the brand relationship quality mediates the relationship between trust and commitment within VBCs and the positive WOM on the brand. Furthermore, individual characteristics (gender and age), as well as level of engagement with VBCs, are shown to moderate the mediation of brand relationship quality on the relationship between trust and commitment within VBCs and WOM.

Within this context, and in line with the recent study of Lin et al. (2016), it is also important to recognize that gender-based roles and gender differences affect the usage of fan pages on Facebook. The management of fan pages on existing social network sites can be more effective by considering these gender differences and by tailoring content accordingly.

Results of the mediation analysis show that FiVBC trust and FiVBC commitment increase brand relationship quality, which increases WOM. On Facebook fan pages, trust and commitment within the FiVBC impact the brand’s relationship quality significantly and marketers and adds theoretical insights to the relational mechanisms by which the brand relationship quality can be managed. Furthermore, the present study predicted that brand relationship quality mediates the FiVBC trust and commitment and positive WOM. This confirms the importance of brand relationship quality in the real environment as a construct for assessing consumer–brand relationships (Fournier, 1998; Grégoire et al., 2009) and further extends the literature by identifying how brand relationship quality mediates trust and commitment within the FiVBC and WOM as well as by highlighting its relevance for social media marketing.

Our findings are in line with Kumar et al.’s (2016) results who indicate that investing in results reveal that brand recommendation is susceptible to the alignment between the online relationship within the FiVBC and the offline brand relationship quality. This is all the more important as 78% of fans are current brand users (Syncapse, 2013).

## 4.1. Theoretical implications

The growth of social media websites and their popularity among consumers (Schivinski & Dabrowski, 2016) has urged firms to create presences on popular platforms such as Facebook. Fans that are more connected can spread brand messages better across their social networks than non-fans (Wallace et al., 2014). However, as evidenced by Culnan et al. (2010), the existence of a VBC (e.g., a brand fan page) will not guarantee that a company will establish successful on-going relationships with fans and build sustainable communities. From this perspective, our study contributes to the existing research in three ways.

First, by identifying the determinants of trust and commitment within a VBC, our research underlines the importance of the economic and hedonic benefits for members of a brand fan page in fostering trust and commitment within the VBC. Prior research tackled the intrinsic enjoyment and excitement that customers might derive from being around their main object of interest, such as a brand (Nambisan & Watt, 2011; Jahn & Kunz, 2012), as well as the effect of relationship benefits on participation (Dholokia et al., 2009). Our findings, in the specific context of brand fan pages, go further by stressing the direct role of the economic and hedonic benefits provided by the firm that initiate trust and commitment within the VBC. This result likely indicates that fans do not see a direct commercial interest behind the actions of the firm but rather the desire to maintain a good relationship with consumers or to serve them better.

Second, our research shows that the relationships within the FiVBC do not stand separate from the real relationships of the fans with the brand. We demonstrated that brand relationship quality is influenced by trust and commitment generated within the brand fan page. Our results are in line with Gamboa and Gonçalves’s (2014) study revealing that Facebook enhances trust, customer satisfaction, and commitment, among other relational constructs, and that these relations are stronger for fans of the brand than for non-fans. Our study brings empirical evidence for the SET paradigm, where trust and commitment between fans and the FiVBC develop through exchanges in which members (fans) reciprocate a positive personal impact. Likewise, fans having a strong relationship quality with the brand are willing to engage in online WOM communication in order to give the brand something “in return” for the good experience. Hence, the aim of a positive WOM communication activity is that the brand will become or remain successful (Hennig-Thurau et al., 2004).

#

From the social capital theory perspective, our findings broaden ways for developing relational capital by offering new levers for customer relationship management (CRM). The social capital of a brand, such as trust and commitment within the fan page, is found to help build stronger ties with the respective brand and, in fine, promote its dissemination.

Third, insofar as WOM has been found to be a powerful force in persuasion (Sen & customers and their readiness to use social networks to warn other customers or seek revenge (De Matos & Rossi, 2008), the brand, according to our results, can shape consumer attitudes favorably by promoting trust and commitment within the FiVBC, thereby improving relationship quality and inducing positive WOM. This suggests maintaining consistency between the brand’s online and offline actions because the mediation of the brand relationship quality cannot be circumvented. This finding also suggests that brands could use referral marketing at first to encourage fans—current users or those who have never owned the brand— to refer their brand fan pages to friends and family. Trusov et al.’s (2009) study of WOM communication strategies in social media underlines that these strategies enjoy a higher financial incentive than traditional WOM does. In the context of a brand fan page, our study highlights how fans within trusted and committed VBCs are in a position to be intrinsically motivated to generate positive WOM. As such, creating a fan page has become a cornerstone of effective social media marketing, enabling WOM and promoting a firm’s relationships with potential consumers effectively and efficiently.

## 4.2. Managerial implications

Based on our findings, managers can better calibrate their marketing tactics in terms of action, content, and communication. Moreover, it would be interesting to determine whether consumers who are members of the FiVBC spread WOM solely because of their trust and commitment within the brand fan page and/or because of their brand relationship quality.

“Facebook fan pages for brands are an easy way to engage with consumers in a forum where they've chosen to engage with us,”<sup>2</sup> said an executive at P&G. Brand managers via their online brand communities on Facebook seek to build stronger relationships that go beyond a mere transaction. In this respect, in light of our findings, practitioners could reinforce their customer relationship management (CRM) by using their fan pages on Facebook to communicate their marketing messages to their target customers with specific actions.

Combining the FiVBC and brand relationship quality reveals to marketers that they must strengthen members’ trust and commitment within VBCs. Put differently, firms have to maximize the synergies between online and offline resources in order to consolidate their overall relational capital with their customers.

First, brands should market their VBCs and include elements that enhance perceptions of trust and commitment such as informative, honest, and benevolent content. Such features encourage members to return more regularly to the brand fan page and proximity can help reduce uncertainty and improve trust. Engaging and exciting activities, where fans can share their experiences on the brand fan page, lead to liking, and commenting on and sharing content. Finally, information and activities should match members’ expectations as determined by surveys and provide hedonic benefits that are highly valued by fans.

How can a brand determine what is attractive to fans and what might encourage them to visit the VBC regularly? The idiosyncratic nature of VBCs on Facebook suggests the need to rely on the Facebook Insights tool to determine which types of activities prompt the most likes or shares. Identifying the posts that have led members to communicate openly is a good way for brands to adapt their content strategies to the expectations of their members. According to the principle of reciprocity, if brands show that they listen to their members, they can develop a sense of community, which is essential for commitment to a VBC.

#

Second, in light of the significant moderating effects of personal characteristics (age and gender), managers should be aware that young and female fans are more prone to diffusing positive WOM and provide these targets with appropriate content. Moreover, older people are found to be more committed when they enjoy fan brand pages. Moreover, fans with a low frequency of participation in the FiVBC are sensitive to hedonic benefits. Managers should provide more entertainment services to these categories in order to foster their commitment and trust within the VBC. Thus, it is useful and worthwhile for fan page managers to initiate within the FiVBC homogeneous sub-communities based on profiles around common interests, for example, the Jeep for Cure community devoted primarily to women (Facebook, 2018), an audience that is predisposed to be receptive to selective communication actions.

The brand community manager should also seek mainly to accurately target fans on the FiVBC, inform them about products exclusively, and respond to problems that they might encounter with branded products (i.e., provide customer service through the VBC). Using a brand fan page to improve the brand relationship quality is worthwhile because the communities tend to be less mercantilist and more instantaneous, such that they often become a preferred channel of communication between the brand and the consumers. The objective is to support the transfer of the VBC’s trust and commitment to the relationship quality of the brand because this link represents a dual benefit for the company and also encourages customers to spread positive WOM about the brand.

Third, the economic benefits that refer to the consumer’s on-going relationship with the brand could, for example, offer rewards for participation or help to save time through a special treatment that supports faster decisions (Gummerus et al., 2012). However, providing economic benefits should be coupled with a strategy that primarily provides entertainment to fans. Firms are advised to design webpage features and functions of their brand fan pages in order to better deliver more hedonic benefits; that is, they should, e.g., offer daily content, such as videos, photos, texts, games, and Facebook Live (a live streaming video channel to keep users updated as to what is happening on Facebook with positive information about the brand), with a fun and entertaining character. This provides an opportunity to convert the VBC into a trust and commitment hub for lasting brand relationship quality.

## 5. Conclusion

This study sought to clarify the relational equation between members of a brand fan page on Facebook and the brand that initiated the VBC. The results show that the relationships between VBCs and the companies that own the brands follow both singular and subtle patterns. Trust and commitment within the VBC are driven by economic and hedonic benefits and determine the brand’s relationship quality. Furthermore, the WOM effect appears to be more sensitive to the relationship quality of the brand than to trust and commitment within the brand fan page. This counterintuitive result suggests that the passage from real to virtual ultimately involves a reallocation of the benefits earned in the VBC toward the brand, which consolidates relational assets. By contributing to the life of the virtual community and participating in its fulfillment, the brand can establish and maintain successful, reciprocal social exchanges with its fans in order to create relational capital.

Our study has several limitations that leave avenues for future research. First, our analysis concerned brand fans in France. Many global brands are present in many countries of the world, especially developed countries. Therefore, the results here could be generalized better by replicating this study with more countries including different types of cultures. Cultural differences may moderate the effect of the relationships between fans and brands.

Second, due to low representativeness of different brands in our sample and the relative complexity of our structural model, we were not able to run subgroup analysis on each brand separately. Future research may capitalize on this limitation by controlling for the type of consumer good (e.g., FMCG, consumer electronic goods, clothing, health-care products, etc.) when investigating fans’ attachment to virtual brand communities.

Third, results of the mediation analysis show that (1) FiVBC trust and FiVBC commitment increase brand relationship quality, which, in turn, increases WOM; (2) despite the progress in the study, there is room for future work accounting for the direct effect; and (3) a negative indirect path.

Fourth, for the fan participation, we used the frequency of visiting the fan page as a proxy. However, consumer participation might be either active or passive. Passive participation implies a “quiet membership” that usually features a non-interactive behavior. Active participation instead involves posting, producing content on the VBC, and interacting with others in community activities. It would be interesting to integrate these features by using active behavior on social networking site measurements as well as a broader measure of participation such as followed by Wu et al. (2015).
