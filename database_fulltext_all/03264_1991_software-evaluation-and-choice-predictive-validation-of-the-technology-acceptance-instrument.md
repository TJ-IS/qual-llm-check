# Software Evaluation and Choice: Predictive Validation of the Technology Acceptance Instrument

## Introduction

Information technology (IT) researchers repeatedly insist upon the rigorous validation of measuring instruments (e.g., Benbasat, 1991; Straub, 1989); yet, most IT instruments remain at an early stage of development (Zmud and Boynton, 1991). A lack of instrument standardization is one of the reasons IT research suffers from “conflicting” results and precludes the generation of coordinated research programs (Jarvenpaa, et al., 1985; Teng and Galletta, 1990). However, progress is evident in the recent psychometric work of Davis (1989) who initiated reliability and validity testing on an instrument measuring perceived usefulness and ease of use (U/EOU) as determinants of IT acceptance. In investigating the predictive validity of the U/EOU instrument in a software evaluation and choice scenario, the research reported in this paper represents a continuation and enhancement of the validation research program begun by Davis (1989).

As the use of prepackaged software (e.g., spreadsheets, database management systems, word processors) becomes widespread in organizations; the need for evaluating and choosing among several viable options increases. While a number of methods have been proposed to aid decision makers in their selection of software, many are quantitatively complex, costly and timeconsuming to administer, and difficult to manage with large groups of potential users (Anderson, 1990). An alternative approach to the evaluation and choice process focuses on user input of the qualitative properties of the software (Goslar, 1986; Klein and Beck, 1987). Specifically, the U/EOU instrument has been suggested as a suitable way to measure such qualitative properties (Adams, et al., 1992; Davis, 1989).

A theory identifies constructs and their relationships. While researchers are interested in predicting the relationships suggested in the theory, practitioners are interested in the prediction of an outcome and how well an instrument can predict that outcome. Both of these interests are involved in predictive validity—for the researcher it is a form of construct validation and for the practitioner, a forecaster of a future behavior, the criterion. When determining predictive validity, the criterion variable must represent the objective of the instrument (Cronbach, 1971). In this research, both the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, et al., 1989) and the applied decision-making situation (software evaluation and choice) provide a basis for determining an appropriate criterion variable.

The Technology Acceptance Model indicates that perceived usefulness and ease of use will influence an individual's intentions to use an information technology. This research introduces a criterion variable that measures intentions as a choice among several alternatives with subjects actually expecting to use their choice.

## Previous Literature

Research on the U/EOU instrument by Davis (1989) began with construction and initial validation testing using the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) as a theoretical basis (Davis, et al., 1989). Davis (1989) defined perceived usefulness as “the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance” (p. 985). Perceived ease of use is “the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free of effort” (p. 985).

Construction of the U/EOU resulted in a 12-item instrument that (1) was shown to be reliable, (2) consisted of the two factors usefulness and ease of use, and (3) was correlated with intentions to use and self-report usage (Davis, 1989; Davis, et al., 1989). The technologies used were electronic mail, a text editor, word processing, and graphics software. The U/EOU was then subjected to further psychometric testing (Adams, et al., 1992) indicating that the U/EOU was: (1) reliable, (2) construct valid, (3) composed of two distinct factors—usefulness and ease of use, and (4) associated with self-report usage for electronic and voice mail, word processing, a spreadsheet, and a graphics package. Additional support for the U/EOU instrument was offered and found to be test-retest reliable with a database management system and a spreadsheet (Hendrickson, et al., 1993). Although focusing primarily on testing TAM by means of the U/EOU, Mathieson (1991) provided support for the reliability of the U/EOU and its association with self-reported intentions to use spreadsheet technology. Evidence for the reliability of the U/EOU instrument has been collected for a number of different information technologies, and support for its construct validity has accumulated. While previous research has demonstrated the association of usefulness and ease of use with intentions and usage, the methods used to measure the constructs have always been self-report.

Difficulties associated with self-report measures, particularly when they are used for constructs highly correlated with each other (i.e., U/EOU with intentions and self-report usage), have been extensively documented (Podsakoff and Organ, 1986). One of the limitations of self-report measures used in combination with other self-report instruments is the occurrence of common method variance, the contamination of a potential relationship by the subjects' desire for consistency or social desirability (Podsakoff and Organ, 1986). Employing an indicator for the dependent variable that does not share the self-report method of the U/EOU instrument would, therefore, strengthen assertions about the overall validity of the U/EOU instrument (Cronbach, 1971).

The purpose of this investigation is to provide evidence for the predictive validity of the U/EOU instrument by measuring intentions as the actual choice a subject makes. The research proposes that choice behavior is a stronger measure of intentions than subject-reported intentions because the choice measure is an evaluation of the subjects' commitment to perform a behavior, while intentions are merely a self-report on the potential for a future behavior. Instead of self-reporting intention to use a particular software package, the subject's actual choice among several packages was used as the criterion variable.

## Methods

The subjects were 47 MBA students enrolled in a required management information systems course. As a portion of their coursework, they were assigned an application development project that included a software evaluation/choice component. The application developed was a bibliographic database. The subjects evaluated several DBMSs and selected one with the understanding that they might use it to develop the bibliographic database. The database management system software was selected as the information technology to investigate because of its widespread use among end users in organizations. Further, it has not been used in any previous predictive validity investigations of the U/EOU constructs and instrument.

The subjects were randomly divided into 10 groups of four, five, or six and randomly assigned one of nine PC-based DBMSs listed in Table 1.

Each individual in every group was responsible for becoming familiar with the DBMS assigned and demonstrating the features of that DBMS package for his/her group. The features to be demonstrated were assigned in advance and were identical for all the presenters so that the demonstrations would be equivalent. Then each member of the group completed the U/EOU instrument for the DBMS demonstrated immediately following each demonstration. Consequently, each subject was exposed to four, five, or six DBMS packages and filled out a U/EOU instrument for each. After having seen demonstrations for four to six DBMSs, each individual chose the DBMS package that he/she wanted to use to build the bibliographic database. The choice was made one week subsequent to the DBMS demonstrations, which significantly reduced, if not eliminated, any remaining common method bias.

The U/EOU instrument used in this study was the 12-item measure constructed by Davis (1989) with wording modifications to reflect the DBMS technology. Each item was assessed with a 7-point scale with end points of highly likely/highly unlikely.

## Results

The sample for the study was 231, based on 47 subjects rating up to six DBMSs. Reliability for both the usefulness and ease of use scales was high, with a Cronbach's coefficient alpha of .95. To determine factorial validity—whether the usefulness and ease of use items form distinct constructs—the study applied a two-factor principal components factor analysis that confirmed the two factors of usefulness and ease of use. The item loadings for the usefulness scale ranged from .77 to .92 and from .64 to .93 for the ease of use scale.

For predictive validity, it is necessary to determine if the U/EOU can predict the choice of the DBMS package from scores on the U/EOU instrument. The U/EOU instrument provided a score for each subject on each of the packages evaluated. The highest U/EOU score for each subject should indicate the individual's preference of software packages and be the same as his/her stated choice if the U/EOU instrument is accurate. Out of the 47 U/EOU indicated preferences, 33 were the same as the subject's stated choice, suggesting the instrument has an accuracy rate of 70 percent compared to 17 percent to 25 percent (1 out of 4, 5, or 6 packages) by chance. Using 21 percent as an average percentage hit rate from chance, a z-statistic was 6.28 (p<.001), indicating that an overall 70 percent accuracy rate is much better than the rate obtained by chance alone (Huberty, 1984). The formula for the z-statistic is:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} {z} & {= (o - e) N ^ {1 / 2}} \\ & {(e (N - e)) ^ {1 / 2}} \end{array}
$$

where, o is the number of observed cases, e is the number of cases expected by chance, and N is the sample size (Huberty, 1984).

In addition to the descriptive statistics indicating predictive validity, a discriminant analysis was performed to determine if the U/EOU could discriminate among those preferring a particular package from those preferring other packages. Since the same software packages must be considered across subjects for this test, the subject responses utilized were those that evaluated packages 1, 3, and 4 who also chose either package 1, 3, or 4, yielding a sample size of 37.

Table 2a gives the results of the discriminant analysis for the entire U/EOU instrument. The discriminant classification was adjusted using the priors to account for unequal group sizes. The percent of total correct classifications, or the hit rate, is 80 percent (1.00 minus 0.20, total error rate). The hit rate expected by chance alone would be 33 percent. A z-statistic of 3.95, significant at p < .001, determined that the experimental hit rate was better than what could be obtained by chance (Huberty, 1984). Because the groups are uneven, the additional use of the maximum chance criterion method is recommended (Huberty, 1984). This method assumes that all cases are assigned to the largest group by chance rather than an equal division among all the groups. Applying this more conservative analysis, the z-statistic obtained was 2.07, still significant at p = .02. This finding suggests that the U/EOU instrument is a good discriminator among software packages 1, 3, and 4.

The results of separate discriminant analyses performed for the usefulness and ease of use scales are shown in Tables 2b and 2c. For the usefulness scale, the hit rate was 80 percent. The z-statistic was 3.95 (p<.001), and with the more conservative maximum chance criterion, the z-statistic was 2.07 (p = .02). For the ease of use scale, the hit rate was 74 percent. The z-statistic was 3.12 (p < .001) and with the maximum chance criterion, 1.18 (p = .12). Both scales appear to be relatively good discriminators of software packages although the ease of use scale did not reach significance when the maximum chance criterion was applied.

Several tests were performed to eliminate certain alternative explanations for the results obtained. First, to test whether an individual tended to choose the package that he/she demonstrated, a chi-square analysis was used. Because the groups looked at an average of 5 packages, and each of the 47 subjects demonstrated and chose one package, 9.4 subjects could be expected to pick any one particular package (i.e., the one he/she demonstrated). In this case, 12 subjects picked the package that he/she demonstrated, yielding a chi-square of .72 (p = .43). Therefore, it was not more likely that an individual chose the package that he/she demonstrated over any of the other packages.

Second, to test whether the subjects' choice of package was influenced by its sequence in the demonstrations, a chi-square test was performed. Because of unequal group sizes, only the first five demonstration positions were used, yielding a sample size of 43. Of these, eight picked the package demonstrated first, seven for the second, 11 for the third, seven for the fourth, and 10 for the fifth. The expected value for each of these, given the choice is independent of the sequence of the demonstration, would be 8.6 (43/5). The chi-square was 1.54 (p = .23), indicating that the sequence of the demonstration appears to be independent of the choice.

Third, there is the potential for subjects to be influenced by the rest of the group in their choice. To determine intragroup bias, a chi-square was performed to test whether the proportion for a choice across groups is the same as the overall sample for a package. DBMS number one was used for the analysis because it was chosen by 63 percent of the subjects. If the same proportion exists within the groups as in the overall sample, then there would not be a bias due to group membership. The chi-square was 8.54 (p = .48) indicating that subject choice was not influenced by group membership.

## Conclusion

This study has determined that the U/EOU instrument demonstrates reasonably good predictive validity. Since the dependent variable, choice, extends prior work via this test of predictive validity, Adams, et al. (1992), Davis (1989), Davis, et al. (1989), and Mathieson (1991) receive additional support through this study. In addition, the results help to triangulate on the IT acceptance phenomenon because they were obtained using: (a) a behavior-oriented, rather than self-reported, variable (choice), (b) a different software technology (DBMS), and (c) different analysis technique (discriminant analysis).

The U/EOU instrument has been used to predict or assess the usage of a number of different information technologies. Most of these can be classified as end-user applications: word processors, spreadsheets, graphics, electronic mail, voice mail, database management systems, and a text editor. The robustness of the U/EOU instrument demonstrated across these different technologies may allow its application to other information technologies such as group decision support systems, computer conferencing, multimedia, and CAD/CAM.

The U/EOU instrument has been shown to have predictive validity for (1) intentions to use, (2) self-report usage, (3) self-predicted usage, (4) attitudes toward use, and (5) choice. The fifth element has particular relevance to practitioners, who frequently have few options in evaluation and choice methods for software decisions. Because targeted users of software packages often participate in software evaluation and choice decisions but typically have little training in the use of complex quantitative models for multi-criteria decision making, a method is needed (a) that potential users of the technology can understand, (b) that organizations can administer efficiently at low cost, and (c) that takes into consideration qualitative aspects of the software. The U/EOU instrument has been empirically shown to be reliable and valid under a number of different situations with several different technologies and, as shown in this study, has been able to predict the choice behavior of subjects in a software evaluation project. It is, therefore, a logical candidate for use in the evaluation and choice of software packages.
