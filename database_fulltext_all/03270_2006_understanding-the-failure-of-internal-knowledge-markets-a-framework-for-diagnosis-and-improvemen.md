# Understanding the failure of internal knowledge markets: A framework for diagnosis and improvement

Michael Brydon <sup>a,\*</sup>, Aidan R. Vining <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Faculty of Business Administration, Simon Fraser University, Burnaby, BC V5A 1S6, Canada <sup>b</sup> Faculty of Business Administration, Simon Fraser University, Vancouver, BC V6B 5K3, Canada

Received 15 February 2005; received in revised form 25 September 2005; accepted 3 September 2006 Available online 17 November 2006

## Abstract

In writing this paper, our objective was to use the concept of internal market failure to explain why many knowledge management initiatives fall short of expectations. We re-examined the conventional view of knowledge as a pure public good and developed a typology of knowledge as a heterogeneous public good. This permitted us to identify the different sources of internal market failure that impeded knowledge creation and sharing within firms. We then analyzed generic managerial responses to internal market failure and showed how the effectiveness of each response was limited by the nature of knowledge as a tradable commodity. We concluded by presenting a preliminary framework for knowledge management based on the enforcement of dynamic internal property rights. The objective of a dynamic response to internal knowledge market failure was seen as an attempt to balance individual incentives with the need to create and share knowledge throughout the organizational. <sup>#</sup> 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Knowledge management; Internal knowledge markets; Public goods

## 1. Introduction

The creation and dissemination of knowledge within firms is vital for sustaining competitive advantage, especially in knowledge-intensive industries [41]. However, effective knowledge management is difficult. Only 13% of the executives in a recent survey of European and US organizations felt that they effectively transferred knowledge between units [34] and many, probably most, knowledge management initiatives fail [38].

Much research in this area has focused either on technology or on ways of knowledge elicitation [5,13]. Although both are important, we focused on incentives for knowledge creation and sharing. Exchanges within the firm’s internal knowledge market could and often have resulted in a variety of organizational inefficiencies; i.e., internal knowledge market failures [37]. Our underlying assumption was that many managers have underestimated the complexity of the incentives for knowledge creation and sharing by employees and therefore misunderstood the sources of failure. The internal market view of knowledge management has emphasized the fundamental importance of costs and prices in eliciting knowledge [21]. It also recognized that new technologies can disrupt the existing internal market by changing individual incentives for creating and sharing knowledge, raising or lowering the costs of enforcing property rights, reducing or relocating transaction costs, supporting institutional mechanisms (such as, reputation and rating systems), and facilitating complex price-discovery techniques. Managers must therefore understand the standard responses to market failure and exploit emerging technologies in order to achieve a balance between individual incentives for knowledge supply and the organization’s desire for free knowledge sharing.

## 2. A typology of knowledge public goods

An efficient internal market for a private good – a homogeneous depletable good with well-defined property rights – operates efficiently when it is supplied by many employees. In such ideal circumstances, consuming employees willingly pay some ‘‘price’’ (P) (which may be in non-monetary units of exchange) equal to the supplying employee’s marginal, or opportunity, cost (EMC), so that

$$
P = \mathrm{EMC}.
$$

Internal knowledge markets, however, seldom resemble efficient markets for private goods because knowledge often has public good properties. Public goods lack both rivalry and excludability. The lack of rivalry determines the extent to which a good, once supplied at a given level, can be used without affecting other consumers. The lack of exclusion defines the degree to which a supplier (or property right owner) can deny the good to potential consumers within the organization. Knowledge is often regarded as a pure public good [3]. However, this view ignores two important issues: variable excludability and the presence of externalities.

The first issue is variation in the extent to which exclusion can be enforced. Knowledge that is tacit (or implicit) is excludable since any communication depends on the voluntary participation by the owner of the knowledge [27] and owners often have incentives to withhold their knowledge [30]. Indeed, they may find it advantageous to withhold their knowledge, leave the firm, and sell their knowledge in the external market. Failure to share tacit knowledge is clearly a serious problem in knowledge-intensive firms [24]. Whether tacit knowledge remains in an employee’s mind or is shared depends on the level of organizational support— involving incentives, norms, and processes. Sometimes firms develop non-monetary incentives through internal markets; however, the mechanisms for tacit knowledge transfer, such as social interaction, apprenticeship, and mentoring, do not scale well. Each transfer involves relatively high marginal costs for both supplier and consumer. Firms may invest in knowledge management systems (KMS) as a means of reducing their reliance on purely tacit knowledge. Once in place, a KMS reduces the marginal cost of supplying the knowledge to almost zero. However, such systems require the owners to organize and transform their knowledge into a suitable form for distribution. The initial cost of codification – the ‘‘first-copy cost’’ – is typically significant [36] and, once codified, the knowledge may have public good properties that make it difficult for the owner to recoup these costs. Emerging technologies such as digital rights management (DRM), however, make excludability of codified knowledge and the enforcement of property rights increasingly feasible.

The second issue is the potential presence of externalities due to knowledge exchange. A negative externality occurs when an exchange imposes costs on employees not participating in the exchange. For example, if one employee is consuming written knowledge, it is difficult for other employees to consume the same knowledge unless there are duplicate copies. The existence of negative externalities thus depends on managerial decisions about the media in which the knowledge is stored and the capacity of the firm’s technological infrastructure. Encoding the same knowledge using an electronic medium might eliminate the effective rivalry caused by the use of physical media, such as paper. A different type of externality arises whenever knowledge or expertise becomes less valuable to the supplying employee when it is shared. The diffusion of specialized knowledge throughout a firm is the main objective of knowledge management initiatives; however, making such expertise a commodity may result in a transfer of economic surplus from individuals to the organization. This potential decrease in value to the employees is termed a pecuniary externality. Unlike normal negative externalities that impose indirect costs, pecuniary externalities affect relative prices. Their existence and impact on knowledge sharing are variable; depending on any incentive systems and informal culture of the firm.

We developed the typology of knowledge goods shown in Fig. 1 to help understand the implications of variable excludability and negative externalities on internal knowledge markets.

## 2.1. Pure public goods: non-excludability without externalities

The top-left quadrant represents ‘‘pure’’ public knowledge goods. Knowledge that is a pure public good does not tend to be shared (or even created) in an internal market without incentives. TeenShare, a crossdivisional forum for sharing knowledge about the teen market in a consumer products firm illustrates the risks faced by knowledge management initiatives. The system failed to provide ‘‘nontrivial rewards and recognition for interunit knowledge transfer’’ and was disbanded after 2 years due to lack of use [8].

Supply of knowledge in such systems requires that employees code their tacit knowledge and incur a fixed first-copy cost. However, each employee’s marginal benefit (EMB) for consumption and use of the knowledge may be small in comparison to its cost. Inefficiency and a loss of organizational surplus occur because no one employee is willing to underwrite this cost, even though the aggregate benefit to the organization is potentially large. The loss of organizational surplus is aggravated if exchanges of knowledge have the potential to generate positive externalities (knowledge spillovers).

## 2.2. Open access goods: non-excludability with negative externalities

Open access knowledge results whenever managers respond to a pure public good failure by making firmlevel investments in knowledge and knowledge-transmission infrastructure. The potential for inefficiency exists whenever:

(a) the firm under-invests in infrastructure,

(b) exclusion is costly or infeasible.

As the capacity limit of the knowledge transmission infrastructure is approached, users incur congestion costs (there are too many consumers or they intensify their use). Congestion does not reduce the amount of the knowledge available for consumption by others but is a negative externality—it raises the marginal cost imposed on other internal consumers through, for example, longer delays or increased unreliability of access. Unfortunately, congestion frequently affects systems that add the most value to an organization. When Ernst & Young developed the Center for Business Knowledge as the core of its knowledge management initiative, usage of the center exceeded capacity and led the firm to impose usage levies [35].

Fig. 2 illustrates the congestion costs created by an upward shift in demand for a popular system. The total marginal cost faced by employees consuming knowledge from a congested source is equal to the internal market price P of the knowledge (zero for open access goods) plus a congestion cost, C(Q), which is a function of Q, the aggregate quantity of knowledge consumed. When Q is low, congestion costs are zero. However, the congestion cost curve slopes upwards as Q approaches capacity and becomes vertical when Q reaches the system’s limit. The total quantity of open access knowledge consumed within the firm is determined by the point at which the congestion cost curve for the marginal employee i intersects the aggregate demand schedule for the organization. ## 2.3. Priced goods: excludability without negative externalities

Knowledge is ‘‘priced’’ when excludability permits suppliers to restrict consumption to only those employees who are willing to pay a positive price, P. Organizational surplus is reduced when knowledge is priced above its marginal cost of supply because the knowledge is potentially valuable to all employees $( \mathrm { E M B } > 0 )$ yet only those willing to pay for it $( \mathrm { E M B } \ge P )$ are granted access. In addition, employees who possess scarce and valuable knowledge will be tempted to act as internal monopolists and price the knowledge well above its EMC. The result is underconsumption of knowledge by employees and a transfer of surplus from the firm to the individual knowledge supplier.

Although losses of organizational surplus make it appear that the enforcement of excludability is counterproductive, the alternative is reversion to a pure public good and the risk of undersupply. Excludability permits an internal market to place a price on knowledge. Employees who possess knowledge that is valued within the firm can recoup their knowledge-generating investments and are therefore more likely to share.

Price-based internal markets for tacit knowledge can sometimes evolve without measures to enforce excludability. One study of knowledge sharing within a firm observed that employees ‘‘help most willingly when they perceive a reciprocal benefit to themselves’’ [32]. Achieving a self-sustaining internal market for codified knowledge is more difficult. The high first-copy cost means that the average cost to the supplier is positive, even though the marginal cost of supply is effectively zero. Thus, suppliers can only recoup their average costs if they can enforce excludability for all copies of the knowledge. However, the enforcement of excludability is generally seen as being costly or infeasible, especially when the knowledge is in digital form.

The problem of high first-copy costs and nonexcludability has been addressed historically by the granting intellectual property rights, such as copyrights and patents. Such mechanisms give the knowledge owner a restricted monopoly over the application of the knowledge in exchange for its disclosure. Although this approach has been effective, enforcement is often cumbersome and expensive. Emerging technologies to enforce the excludability of each copy of knowledge offer much better control. Access control technology can be used to require potential users of a knowledge repository to authenticate their identity before being granted specific access rights. Several well-established digital content standards, such as Adobe’s PDF and Microsoft’s Office formats, already incorporate DRM infrastructure. The degree of excludability for codified knowledge is a decision rather than a property of the knowledge itself.

## 2.4. Hoarded goods: excludability with negative externalities

Hoarding may occur when knowledge sharing results in a pecuniary externality for knowledge suppliers. Specifically, specialized knowledge becomes a commodity once it is shared throughout the firm. Although this is precisely the firm-level objective of a KMS, it decreases the value of the knowledge by undermining an employee’s status as an expert. As Orlikowski observed in a consulting firm: ‘‘in an environment where ‘knowledge is power’, many consultants believed that any sharing of expertise . . . would hurt, not help, their chances of generating some unique expertise and consequently of securing the sought-after promotions’’ [28].

Pecuniary externalities have traditionally been internalized through price-based internal markets. However, this is typically imperfect because knowledge suppliers must be compensated for both their first-copy cost and the loss of its value once it has been shared. An employee that can exclude others from specialized knowledge may therefore act as a monopolist and secure rent by limiting supply of the knowledge to a level that raises the price. However, for reasons similar to those of congestion, individual knowledge suppliers fail to consider the aggregate cost on other knowledge suppliers. As a consequence, formal or informal cartels often emerge to enforce knowledge hoarding. Craft apprenticeship provides many historical examples of group-enforced restrictions on knowledge sharing. Superficially, the apprenticeship system offered an efficient price-based market for knowledge—the master traded knowledge to apprentices in exchange for cheap labor. However, an early feature was the emergence of powerful guilds to limit the number of apprentices for a single master [23].

## 3. Barriers to effective management of internal knowledge markets

Different sources of inefficiency and market failure exist in all four quadrants of our knowledge typology. Managers may intervene in internal knowledge markets in an attempt to minimize the impact of such failures. However, managers must contend with two additional sources of failure.

## 3.1. Uncertainty

Uncertainty about the returns on organizational investments in KMSs is a well-recognized problem [33]; however, individual employees may face uncertainty about the ultimate value of their knowledgegenerating activities. Without explicit contracts or a support culture (a form of implicit contract), employees are unwilling to make investments, especially if they face opportunistic behavior, such as reward reneging, by managers. The universality of this internal failure is illustrated by the difficulty that firms have in matching innovation, especially radical innovation, of new entrants into the industry [9]. One means of reducing uncertainty is to rely on ‘‘demand pull’’ rather than ‘‘supply push’’ in knowledge transfers. Google Answers and the ‘‘urgent request’’ feature of Siemens’ internal ShareNet KMS [26] have relied on questions from knowledge consumers to initiate the knowledge creation and sharing process. In the case of Google Answers, consumers pay for the knowledge explicitly.

## 3.2. Information asymmetry

Internal markets can involve search, experience, or post-experience goods [39]. Goods for which consumers can determine the relevant characteristics prior to use are search goods. In contrast, employees can only determine the quality of experience goods on reception and use. Post-experience (or credence) goods are similar to these, except that their value is difficult to determine even after use. The pervasiveness of uncertainty about the value of knowledge suggests that knowledge is generally a post-experience good.

Suppliers in internal transactions typically have superior information about the quality of the good being supplied and may have strong incentives to distort information about its value in order to further their goals [12]. Employees may contribute low-quality knowledge whenever their firm rewards the quantity of contribution rather than its quality [18]. Users over-consume the good if the quality of the knowledge good is believed to be greater than it really is, leading to organizational surplus loss, as shown in Fig. 3.

$\mathrm { E M B _ { U } }$ represents an estimate of employee marginal benefit from a resource when the employee lacks perfect information about its quality. $\mathrm { E M B } _ { \mathrm { I } }$ represents the informed estimate—the amount that an employee with perfect information would consume. Uninformed employees consume at the intersection of $\mathrm { E M B _ { U } }$ with the supply schedule, S. This amount, $Q _ { \mathrm { U } } ,$ is greater than $Q _ { \mathrm { I } }$ and the shaded area abc equals the firm cost resulting from the over-consumption. The supplier of the knowledge benefits from an increase in producer surplus, denoted by the area $P _ { \mathrm { U } } c a P _ { \mathrm { I } } ,$ and consequently has no incentive to reveal to users that they are overconsuming.

The opportunism enabled by information asymmetry can be reduced if there is repeated sampling of knowledge quality from ongoing exchanges, and if suppliers have incentives to develop a reputation for providing high-quality knowledge. But, if consumers perceive that internal knowledge suppliers are not maintaining their reputation (for example, they do not expect to be penalized for being unreliable), then suppliers of high-quality knowledge may have insufficient incentives to participate in the internal knowledge market [1]. Indeed, there is evidence that internal markets have an across-the-board discount for risk and the value of knowledge-generating ‘‘stars’’ is often recognized more quickly by the external market [14].

## 4. Seven responses to internal knowledge market failures

The literature has identified a number of generic responses to market failures [42]. We now consider seven of the responses that are relevant to internal knowledge markets.

## 4.1. Using internal knowledge subsidies or taxes to alter incentives

The purpose of internal taxes and subsidies is to correct internal inefficiencies by altering the relative prices of internal knowledge goods. Taxes raise the employee cost of supplying goods that they oversupply, while subsidies lower the cost of supplying goods that they undersupply. Managers use subsidies in the pure public good quadrant to address the problem of undersupply and in the priced and hoarded goods quadrants to offset high prices imposed by monopolists. Conversely, managers may use negative subsidies (taxes) in the open access quadrant to discourage excessive usage of knowledge infrastructure. Chargebacks for processing time, for example, are routinely used to reduce congestion.

Well-tuned incentives can increase the supply of valuable knowledge for codification [40]. However, if marginal costs and benefits are not well understood, then inefficiencies will either be over or under corrected. Siemens Corporation attempted to increase the supply of knowledge objects in its ShareNet KMS by rewarding contributions with redeemable points. Later, when resources for such initiatives became scarce in the wake of the telecommunications downturn, managers had difficulty justifying the <sup>s</sup>400,000 worth of ‘‘ShareNet points’’ already awarded to knowledge suppliers.

Subsidies provided by management may also generate ‘‘goal displacement’’ [20]. Information asymmetry allows employees to exploit simplistic knowledge sharing incentives that reward quantity rather than quality. The result is often a new negative externality, knowledge pollution. The search cost borne by knowledge consumers increases if out-of-date, repetitive, or low-quality knowledge is contributed to the knowledge base [16]. According to one survey, knowledge pollution is a serious problem: five of the top ‘‘drawbacks to knowledge sharing and effective decision making’’ involve issues of knowledge priority, quality, quantity, and timeliness [11]. Some firms have been forced to introduce costly mechanisms such as editors and user ratings, to reduce information asymmetry and curb knowledge pollution.

## 4.2. Establishing internal knowledge rules and routines

Internal knowledge rules and routines serve two distinct purposes. First, they can enhance efficiency by strengthening internal property rights. Second, they can govern internal knowledge contracts to regulate the price, quantity, and quality of shared knowledge. Many firms use rules and routines to overcome the lack of explicit incentives to supply knowledge. Firms may also use rules in the open access goods quadrant to reduce congestion. For example, Canadian Tire, a retailer, experienced difficulty when the popularity of its data warehouse led to higher than anticipated use by internal business analysts and resulted in long delays and unreliability for all users. In response, managers considered imposing processing time quotas on users and mandating the use of precomputed queries [31]. However, it is difficult to encourage knowledge activities by ‘‘top–down’’ monitoring and regulatory mechanisms. Regulation will usually generate input distortions. As many firms have discovered, simple rules based on monitoring mechanisms, such as counting the number of times an employee accesses a KMS or contributes a knowledge object, are easy for employees to manipulate. Corporate culture, which is an implicit rule structure, yields better compliance, but is difficult to control.

## 4.3. Outsourcing knowledge supply

Many firms outsource the production of goods for the market. Similarly, firms can outsource some knowledge activities in order to remedy internal inefficiency and undersupply [17]; e.g., McKinsey outsources some of its visioning to leading academics; and Motorola develops technologies that are critical to its success in-house, but outsources other technologies to suppliers. Outsourcing may seem paradoxical for knowledgeintensive firms, but knowledge-intensive firms should be the most proficient because they are more likely to ‘‘know what they do not know’’. This reduces information asymmetry and enables more effective monitoring of suppliers.

Outsourcing knowledge production can help alleviate undersupply. In addition, the existence of potential external suppliers of knowledge reduces or eliminates the excess rents that an internal monopolist can extract. The risk of outsourcing knowledge supply is that externally-acquired knowledge may be of limited value to the firm because external sources do not possess knowledge that is complementary to the specific business or industry.

## 4.4. Provision of knowledge sharing infrastructure

Investments in IS infrastructure can reduce congestion and thus move knowledge from being an open access good to either a pure public or priced good. Other infrastructure investments, such as editors, knowledge librarians, search facilities, and quality rating systems, can reduce search costs and knowledge pollution. It is important to recognize, however, that the technological infrastructure itself has public good characteristics. The key organizational problem that arises when providing public goods at the firm-level is that employees have little incentive to reveal their marginal valuations for the infrastructure. They recognize that all employees receive the knowledge unless measures are taken to exclude use. Internal users may anticipate having to help pay to supply a knowledge sharing infrastructure and thus have an incentive to understate their marginal valuations. Alternatively, if users have positive valuations, but anticipate that their contributions will be independent of their revealed valuation, they have an incentive to overstate their marginal valuations.

## 4.5. Internal insurance for risky knowledge investments

Risk arises within firms because risky activities typically cannot be set out in written contracts. As a consequence, employees may fail to engage in knowledge-generating activities that they see as risky. Firms can encourage risk taking by providing employees with ‘‘insurance’’. For example, executives can establish a reputation for placing weight on the validity of knowledge-generating activities in addition to outcomes [15]. However, care must be taken to limit any moral hazard. Insurance may induce employees to take even greater risks or initiate costly activities that are not appropriate from the firm’s perspective.

## 4.6. The creation of knowledge clubs

The defining characteristics of ‘‘club goods’’, such as swimming pools and golf courses, are their high initial cost (which favors a larger number of paying members) and the potential for congestion once some threshold level of use is reached (which favors a small number of members and exclusion). Several popular knowledge management practices can be considered to be clubs. Subunits within firms routinely build and maintain unitlevel intranets and data marts to support their own unique knowledge requirements. Other clubs may be selfselecting. In a community of practice (CoP), an interest in, and specialized knowledge about, a particular topic determine membership. Thus, a CoP dedicated to ‘‘inquiry-based teaching and learning among math and science teachers in Indiana’’ [22] is effectively limited to a small group. In other cases, formal membership requirements may be imposed, including nomination and a sustained level ofcontribution. The most explicit means of allocating club membership is through subscription fees. Commercial knowledge portals, such as Experts-Exchange.com, provide examples of fee-based knowledge clubs.

Knowledge clubs offer three advantages over pure public knowledge goods and priced knowledge goods. First, the club’s relatively small scale facilitates monitoring and increases the feasibility of other generic responses, such as subsidies and rules. Second, a club bundles multiple knowledge objects into a single one and thereby reduces the high variance in consumers willingness-to-pay [4]. Third, in those clubs in which ‘‘dues’’ are assessed, the decision to join or leave provides a reliable signal about the aggregate value of the knowledge that is being shared. The primary disadvantage of knowledge club goods is that they can lead to fragmentation of a firm’s knowledge [7].

## 4.7. Facilitation of price-based internal knowledge markets

Managers may facilitate price-based internal knowledge markets either through fixed internal transfer prices [2] or internal auctions [6]. Where there are functioning internal markets for tacit knowledge, the motivation for employees to participate is often intrinsic. The ‘‘prices’ for the tacit knowledge are thus embedded within the organization’s reciprocity norms. The encouragement of price-based markets for tacit knowledge where they have been previously discouraged or prohibited, however, may result in extrinsic motivators, such as monetary payments, ‘‘crowding-out’’ the established intrinsic motivators [29]. As consequence, many firms have little incentive to encourage the development of formalized price-based markets for tacit knowledge.

Firm have less choice of extrinsic motivators in markets for codified knowledge due to first-copy costs.

However, firms that codify their knowledge and develop a price-based internal market for it often discover that these markets require significant firm-level intervention and investment. They have to define and enforce an internal property rights regime to ensure that the knowledge is excludable. In addition, firms must provide infrastructure for locating knowledge, reputation mechanisms to signal quality, auction protocols to facilitate price discovery and allocation, and procedures for contract enforcement and dispute resolution [10].

## 5. A dynamic view of knowledge management

The absence of risk-free responses to internal knowledge market failures suggested that a context-sensitive, dynamic managerial response should be more effective. The question that firms should ask is: in which quadrant should our knowledge be located? The state transition diagram in Fig. 5 shows how managerial responses trigger transitions between quadrants of the knowledge typology. Clearly, those on the right side should be avoided, due to the losses of organizational surplus imposed by congestion and knowledge hoarding. Similarly, the risks of undersupply in the pure public good quadrant are well understood. However, the lack of price-based internal markets for knowledge in current practice suggests that many firms are reluctant to enforce exclusion in order to address undersupply [25]. We believe that there are two solutions to this. The first is the development of knowledge clubs in which club members can create and share knowledge without restriction (shown by the dotted circle midway along the excludability dimension). The second is a dynamic internal property rights regime in which the enforcement of exclusion is limited in much the same way as the scope and duration of exclusion is limited in both patent and copyright law.

The diverse institutional arrangements used by commercial knowledge exchanges illustrate how dynamic property rights and variable exclusion can be achieved. At issue is the precise nature of the transition from the priced goods quadrant (in which private incentives to create and share knowledge exist) to the pure public goods quadrant (in which all employees can freely access and use the knowledge). At one extreme, some commercial knowledge exchanges like Ingenio.com keep their knowledge in the priced goods quadrant. Excludability is enforced by requiring that most knowledge transfers occur through telephone conversations which Ingenio’s membership agreement prohibits consumers from recording. At the other extreme, Google Answers immediately moves new knowledge from the priced goods quadrant to the pure public good quadrant. Exchanges that occur through Google Answers are initiated by consumers who post-questions at a fixed bid price. Once a question is answered, the response is made available to anyone who visits the Google Answers web site. A large number of people may thereby benefit from the investment of a single ‘‘knowledge patron’’. Other commercial knowledge exchanges use less abrupt transitions from the priced goods to the pure public goods quadrant. Knexa’s public knowledge auction [19] relies on exclusion and the passage of time in an effort to approximate Lindahl prices for knowledge goods. The key to achieving efficiency is to partition the public good so that each consumer experiences a differentiated copy and is therefore willing to pay a differential amount to consume the provided knowledge. Knexa’s approach involves selling multiple copies of the knowledge (in an electronic document) using a descending price auction. Since the price for a copy of the document decreases over time, time-sensitive consumers pay more for early access while less time-sensitive consumers wait and pay less.

## 6. Conclusions

While scholars have written about internal knowledge markets and their problems, the typology described here provided a textured view of knowledge. Our typology recognized that excludability and negative externalities during knowledge exchange can result in different knowledge market failures. We explored the knowledge management challenges that arose in each quadrant of the typology and described the inevitable collateral risks associated with static managerial responses. We concluded that internal knowledge markets are best managed by (1) avoiding externalities in the right-hand quadrants and (2) balancing individual incentives with organizational welfare through the use of limited and dynamic property rights.

There are several practical implications. First, knowledge management initiatives that fail to account for the motivations of individual employees are likely to fail. Successful knowledge managers must recognize both the significant first-copy costs and any pecuniary externalities that employees face when making the decision to contribute to the repository of knowledge. Second, providing appropriate incentives to knowledge suppliers is difficult because of information asymmetry and uncertainty. Thus, we advocate price-based internal knowledge markets as a means of valuing knowledge and providing appropriate incentives for knowledge creation and sharing. Third, we emphasized the role of IT in enabling variable excludability and dynamic internal property rights for knowledge. We conclude that sophisticated internal knowledge auctions may come to play an important role in corporate knowledge management practice.
