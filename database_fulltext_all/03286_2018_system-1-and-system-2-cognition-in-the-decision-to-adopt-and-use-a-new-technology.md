# System 1 and System 2 Cognition in the Decision to Adopt and Use a New Technology

## Abstract

Most models of technology adoption and use assume a rational decision maker engaged in thoughtful deliberate consideration of the new technology. However, recent research in psychology concludes that such deliberate, rational, conscious decision-making (termed System 2 cognition) has less influence on behavior than originally thought; nonconscious automatic cognition (termed System 1 cognition), which is often influenced by personality characteristics and pattern matching based on past experience, also plays a key role in most decisions. As users adopt and use new technologies time and time again, a set of general expectations about new technology adoption begins to emerge. A user’s personality combined with this pattern of positive and negative experiences creates System 1 heuristics that are triggered when a user faces a similar decision in the future. The focus of this paper is to examine the extent to which the predispositions produced by System 1 automatic cognition – both enabling and inhibiting – versus the deliberate technology assessment produced by System 2 cognition influence technology adoption and use. We found that enabling predispositions influences the formation of intentions to use a new technology, and both enabling and inhibiting predispositions influence an individual’s ultimate follow through in acting on his or her intentions and actually using new technologies. Our research suggests that concepts previously seen as “determinants” of technology adoption and use (e.g., performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions) are not really determinants but rather are important partial mediators in a larger nomological network that includes both automatic System 1 cognition and deliberate System 2 cognition.

Keywords: enabling beliefs, inhibiting beliefs, technology readiness contributor, technology readiness inhibitor, technology adoption, duality, predisposition, System 1 cognition, System 2 cognition, automatic cognition, deliberate cognition, unified theory of acceptance and use of technology

## 1. Introduction

Is the decision to use a new technology entirely based on rational formation of beliefs? The technology or information system is the product of rational evaluations held by individuals. Past research using this perspective has been very successful in predicting the intention to adopt a new system or technology, explaining as much as 70% of the variation in intentions [1]. It has been less successful in predicting actual use behavior (accounting for only 25–30% of the

variance), suggesting that much happens between a deliberate rational decision to do something and actually undertaking that behavior [2].

Recent research in psychology concludes that human behavior is controlled by both deliberate, rational, conscious decision-making (termed System 2 processes) and nonconscious automatic cognition (termed System 1 processes) [3]. System 1 is automatic and not controllable; it continually influences decision behavior and does so much faster than System 2 [3]. System 1 processes evolved to enable humans to react to stimuli in the environment, especially dangers, much faster than our conscious System 2 can react. System 1 typically presents an approach or avoid response to any stimuli (animate or inanimate) in less than 1 second, sometimes faster [4-6].

System 1 conclusions can be overridden by System 2 but only if the individual is motivated to engage in conscious deliberate thinking [3]. The results of System 1 subtly influence the conclusions reached by System 2 by leading individuals to assess objects in different ways and weigh criteria differently. Many individuals deny the influence of System 1 on their decisions but the empirical research about its effects is clear [3, 7]. The extent to which System 1 influences behavior is under debate, with leading psychology researchers concluding that System 1 influences as much as 90% of all human decisions [3]. Even the strongest skeptics conclude that at least 50% of human decisions are controlled by System 1 [3]. Regardless of whether one believes that System 1 controls 50% or 90% of behavior, the unmistakable conclusion is that System 1 has a major influence on behavior.

However, our models of technology adoption are based on theoretical models of deliberate and rational System 2 processes. They do not consider how System 1 processes influence the decision to adopt and use technology. In this paper, we build a model of technology 2

adoption that includes System 1 processes to better explain technology adoption decisionmaking and use behavior. System 1 processes are built on simple heuristics based on past associations [3]. As we encounter a situation, engage in a behavior, and experience an outcome, our System 1 gradually builds heuristics that bind the three together to help guide future enact past behaviors. The construction of these heuristics is affected by a myriad of factors including personality.

Consider, for example, that a police officer approaches you. If you have had good past experiences with the police, or have vicariously had positive experiences through news reports or stories from friends, then your System 1 will generate a positive approach response faster than your System 2 can interpret the current situation. Conversely, if you have had negative experiences with the police, or negative experiences have dominated news reports or stories from friends, then your System 1 will generate an instant negative avoid response. This positive or negative conclusion from your System 1 will influence how your System 2 interprets the available facts as it begins to make sense of the situation.

We argue that the same processes are at work in technology adoption decisions. Prior research suggests that each new technology that individuals are exposed to typically triggers both positive and negative responses [8]. For example, a technology purchased to save time can also waste time [9]; social networking sites that connect people together can also isolate them [10, 11]; and new mobile technologies that can improve users’ performance can also impair their functioning [12]. These positive and negative responses are not merely opposites of each other, but instead coexist in a duality [13].

As users experience new technologies time and time again, their System 1 gradually builds a set of general heuristics about technology adoption and use [14]. That is, a pattern of positive and negative beliefs triggered by experiences with prior technologies combines with innate personality characteristics to produce a set of heuristics that are used by System 1 to produce an immediate response – or a predisposition – to a technology adoption decision. This immediate response can be overridden by System 2, but the System 1 response colors subsequent technology assessments that influence whether new technologies would be adopted and ultimately used. This process is similar to the formation of one’s disposition to trust, which evolves from a pattern of personal experiences over a lifetime to create generalized trust beliefs [15]. These heuristics induce repeatable patterns of behaviors, which are often experienced as predispositions – when an individual encounters a situation, he or she is predisposed to behave in a certain manner.

The focus of this paper is to examine the extent to which these heuristics that create predispositions to behave in certain ways influence the decision to adopt and use a new technology – both those that enable and inhibit adoption and use. Do these past System 1 heuristics dominate the decision to adopt and use a new technology, or do beliefs about the specific new technology under consideration formed by deliberate System 2 cognition dominate use behavior? That is, do the System 1 heuristics and the predispositions they produce predict the decision to adopt and use so that users do not even consider the new technology itself, or do beliefs about the effectiveness of a specific technology formed through System 2 play such an important role that System 1 heuristics and the predisposition they produce do not influence the decision? Or is it some combination of the two?

We found that an individual’s enabling predispositions (System 1) have a direct effect on behavior (i.e., intentions and use) over and above the deliberately formed beliefs (System 2) about the specific technology itself (i.e., performance expectancy (PE), effort expectancy (EE), social influence (SI), and facilitating conditions (FC)). These enabling predispositions also 4

influenced the deliberately formed beliefs, such that two users experiencing the same technology walked away with significantly different PE, EE, SI, and FC, depending on their general heuristics about technology adoption and use. Interestingly, we found that both enabling and inhibiting predispositions moderated the relationship between the decision to enabling predispositions are important in the formation of behavioral intentions (BI) to use a new technology, but both enabling and inhibiting predispositions are important in influencing an individual’s ultimate follow through in acting on his or her intentions and actually using new technologies.

Our findings have two important implications. First, our research shows that the factors previously argued to be “determinants” of adoption and use (i.e., the System 2 cognitive response known as PE, EE, SI, and FC) [1] are not “determinants” but rather are factors that partly mediate in a much larger nomological network in which enabling and inhibiting predispositions to technology influence the cognitive response about new technologies and ultimately whether those new technologies are adopted and used. Second, because the effects of enabling predispositions on BI and use are not just opposite to those of inhibiting predispositions, our research highlights the importance of conceptualizing enabling and inhibiting predispositions as mutually independent. Conceptualizing them separately provides opportunities to examine unique antecedent and consequent effects, thus helping us better understand the decision of whether to adopt the system and the actual use behaviors that follow.

In the following sections, we first build theoretical arguments for the proposed research model and then present our hypotheses. We present the instrument development and characterize the participants of this study. We next present the empirical test of our proposed model. We conclude the paper with implications for research and practice.

## 2. Theoretical Background

We first summarize the paradigm employed in this research, individual acceptance of technology using intentions and usage. Our conceptual framework builds on the existing paradigm to include both enabling and inhibiting predispositions to technology.

Researchers have long argued that there are two fundamentally different forms of cognition. Many dual process models have been proposed under a host of different names; see Evans [16] for an analysis. Kahneman [3] calls these System 1 (automatic cognition) and System 2 (deliberate cognition). System 1 runs continuously and delivers conclusions automatically and involuntarily; “it cannot be turned off” [3]. Behaviors that are automatic or appear as predispositions or intuition are essentially System 1 cognition [17]. System 1 is your “autopilot” when you do not engage in deliberate cognition.

System 2 is familiar from prior information systems research (see de Guinea and Markus [18] who call for a change to this). System 2 is the commonly assumed, deliberate, rational, effortful thinking that plays a central role in most information adoption research. Any theory that argues that deliberate attention is applied to some information and a conclusion is drawn based on a thoughtful process that balances the issues present in the situation has assumed that System 2 is operating (e.g., [1]).

One analogy to explain the difference between System 1 and System 2 is driving a car. When you first learned to drive, you used System 2 cognition. You had to devote considerable

attention and cognition to driving. Everything was effortful, and you had few spare cognitive resources to engage in a conversation. After several years of driving, your System 1 developed a set of heuristics for driving and you now rely on it to drive; you no longer have to devote System 2 attention to driving. As you drive to work, you now let your System 1 control your driving, so your System 2 is freed up for conversation or to think about what awaits you when you arrive. The System 1 heuristics you develop are based on your experiences and your innate characteristics, which means that there are both similarities and differences in how people drive (and people from different regions may exhibit distinct patterns based on the experiences they have had in their region). Sometimes unexpected events occur (e.g., a failed traffic light or an animal crossing the road), and your System 1 alerts your System 2 that it now needs to pay attention and take over; hence, you switch from System 1 to System 2 cognition.

## 2.1 System 2 as a Driver of Behavior

There are several broad streams of research in information systems that examine how and why individuals adopt new information technologies from the lens of System 2 cognition (see [19, 20]), such as implementation success at the organizational level [21] and task-technology fit [22]. We focus on the decision to adopt a new technology (i.e., intentions) and the actual usage behavior that follows as the dependent variables [1, 23, 24].

Under this paradigm, users consider technology design features and first develop a cognitive response to technology (i.e., PE, EE, SI, and FC [1]), which then influences their behavioral response (i.e., BI and usage) [25]; see Figure 1. PE refers to an individual’s beliefs that reflect the degree to which using a system will help improve job performance. PE is rooted in

constructs such as perceived usefulness [23, 26], extrinsic motivation [27], job-fit [28], relative advantage [29], and outcome expectations [30, 31]. EE refers to an individual’s beliefs related to the degree of ease associated with the use of the system and is based on constructs such as perceived ease of use [23], complexity [28], and ease of use [29]. SI is the extent to which an individual believes that organizational and technical infrastructure supports the use of the system [1]. FC is the extent to which an individual believes as important and others believe that he or she should use the system [1]. Prior research has examined the behavioral response in terms of both BI and usage (see, e.g., [32, 33]) and has found that users’ intentions to use the system significantly affect actual usage [34].

Figure 1: Adoption of New Technologies; adapted from Davis [25]

The cognitive and behavioral response to technology may be either positive or negative, or more likely, both, because to most users technologies have both positive and negative characteristics [8, 13]. Psychologists have long debated the degree to which conflicting states can be simultaneously experienced. One perspective suggests that the ability to experience conflicting states simultaneously is limited because positive and negative states represent opposite dimensions on a bipolar scale [35]. The second, and now dominant, perspective suggests two mutually independent dimensions, valence and arousal [36-39], with the horizontal dimension in this spatial metaphor being a pleasure-displeasure dimension (valence) and the vertical dimension being the level of activation-deactivation (arousal) [40]. While pairs of

states separated by a few degrees are considered to have a positive correlation (e.g., elated and happy), those separated by 180° are assumed to have a perfect negative correlation (i.e., a correlation of −1). While a perfect negative correlation implies a linear relationship, a correlation other than −1 implies an L-shaped function [39], thus, positing that positive and negative states are independent of one another [41].

In summary, the cognitive and behavioral responses to technology adoption decisions include a set of positive responses that are enabling and a set of negative responses that are inhibiting. This set of responses coexists simultaneously in a duality [13].

## 2.2 System 1 as a Driver of Behavior

System 1 exists so we can react faster than we can think [3]. When presented with a stimulus, our System 1 cognition automatically generates a response in less than 1 second [3], often as quickly as 300 milliseconds [42]. This process is nonconscious and unavoidable; we cannot prevent it [3]. System 1 does not require deliberate “attention” [43]. Instead, it runs continuously and supplies these assessments to our System 2, even though they are not asked for [3, 44]. System 1 is a pattern-matching system driven by the stimuli available in the current context. System 1 matches stimuli to the heuristics it has stored in memory, and these heuristics produce the

Some heuristics are based on personality differences, and some are learned based on experience [44, 45]. We repeat behaviors that have been successful in the past and avoid those that have not [17, 44]. The essence of learned heuristics is reinforcement learning [46, 47]. Given a certain situation, we perform a behavior and assess the result. This assessment is often heavily affect-laden [46] and heavily anchored in outcomes, even when the outcomes have a large random component [17].

Thus, personality plays a large role in how we interpret the cause and effect relationship that builds our heuristics from past experience; two individuals may experience the same situation and come away with different heuristics because they attribute the outcomes to different causes.

Nonetheless, it is this series of learning by doing that creates our System 1 heuristics [17, 47]. When we are successful, we form the integrated situation-response in a heuristic to guide emerging heuristic [17, 47], although it may take many repetitions to build – and especially change – heuristics [48].

An individual is exposed to many different technologies over their lifetime of technology use. With each exposure to a technology, he or she will have a corresponding set of positive and negative cognitive and behavioral responses. Some technologies trigger predominantly positive responses, whereas others trigger predominantly negative responses. As users experience new technologies, a pattern emerges such that a new technology adoption decision is viewed in light of exposure and responses to prior technologies. This is because as an individual repeatedly encounters a situation and forms a similar response, that situation and response become matched in memory as a heuristic to guide behavior [49].

Over time, individuals encounter numerous technology adoption decisions. As they make decisions about whether to adopt a technology and they experience the outcomes of these decisions, their System 1 begins to build heuristics – heuristics that both enable and inhibit adoption. When a technology adoption situation is encountered again, these heuristics are triggered and the matching response is formed automatically by our System 1 [49-51]. Even if the situation is not a perfect match, System 1 will produce the response because its job is to

produce an answer in less than a second, regardless of the correctness of the answer; it prioritizes speed over accuracy [3].

This automatic response of System 1 is experienced as a set of predispositions or “generalized beliefs” [15]. We say we are “predisposed” to take certain actions because when we encounter a situation, the closest matching heuristic(s) are triggered and influence us to behave in a certain way. They are the default outcome if we choose not to invoke the deliberate cognition of System 2. When we encounter a new technology adoption decision, the situation triggers the enabling and inhibiting heuristics before the technologies themselves are experienced. That is, our System 1 produces an adoption decision in less than a second, before we begin to consider the specific details of the new technology. Thus, our System 1 response is largely independent of the detailed nature of the new technology.

For better or worse, our System 1 conclusions – our predispositions – also influence how we perceive the details of the new technology under consideration. Research on confirmation bias [52, 53] shows that we tend to focus on information that supports our initial beliefs and discount information that opposes them [51, 54]. If we choose to override our System 1 response and devote cognitive effort to deliberately applying System 2 cognition, the initial System 1 conclusion colors our System 2 cognition [53]. We see the available information but tend to discount information that does not support the initial – and potentially inaccurate – System 1 result.

System 1 heuristics that are experienced as predispositions; when encountering a new situation, System 1 will often recommend behaviors that match past “successful” behaviors and oppose behaviors that match past “unsuccessful” behaviors. These System 1 conclusions are presented in less than one second and precede System 2’s deliberate and rational consideration of the specific technology being considered. These System 1 conclusions will then influence System 2 as it develops thoughtful and deliberate cognitive response to the specific new technology. Thus, the cognitive and behavioral response of an individual to technology n is influenced by his or her predispositions formed over the prior time period, 1…n−1, of using different technologies.

Figure 2: Role of Technology-related Predispositions in Adoption Decisions

These predispositions have been observed in the past. For example, Rogers [55] notes that the diffusion of innovations, whether technology or not, often follows an adoption curve where some individuals are early adopters, others adopt with the majority, and others are late adopters. This propensity to adopt innovations early or late, is driven by our predispositions towards the innovation.

One commonly used conceptualization of technology predispositions is technology readiness [14, 56]. Technology readiness uses duality of beliefs to suggest that individuals simultaneously harbor positive and negative beliefs about technology-based products and services [23, 57-59]. Technology readiness has been applied in a variety of contexts including consumer markets [56], online services [60, 61], educational choice [62], health care [63], and website usability [64].

Technology readiness includes four components, two of which (optimism and innovativeness) are technology readiness contributors (referred to as TRCo), while the other two (discomfort and insecurity) are technology readiness inhibitors (referred to as TRIns) [14]. TRCo captures the notion that technology is a “good thing” and that an individual believes that he or she is at the forefront of trying new technology-based products and services. TRIn encapsulates a general paranoia about technology and distrust and skepticism that may be associated with technologybased products and services. Optimism is a belief that technology offers people increased control, flexibility, and efficiency in their lives. Innovativeness reflects the extent to which an individual believes that he or she is at the leading edge of trying out new technology-based products or services. Discomfort is a perceived lack of control over technology and a feeling of being overwhelmed by it. In this study, individuals believe that technology is not really designed for use by ordinary people and that it is too complicated. While both discomfort and anxiety cause a feeling of lack of control, anxiety related to discomfort refers to how an individual feels that others view him or her (rather than the individual’s discomfort with technology). Finally, insecurity reflects an inherent distrust of technology and doubt about its ability to work properly.

## 3. Hypotheses Development

In this research, we seek to explore how users’ predispositions to technology produced by

System 1 cognition can directly influence behavioral responses such as adoption and use decisions and also influence the more deliberate and thoughtful cognitive responses produced by System 2; see Figure 3.

We test several different models for the behavioral and cognitive responses to technology. We first examine how System 1 cognition (assessed by the individual’s enabling and inhibiting predispositions, TRCo and TRIn, respectively) directly influences the behavioral response to technology (in the absence of System 2) in terms of use behavior (Model 1a) and then both intentions and use behavior (Model 1b); see Figure 4. We then add System 2’s cognitive responses to the model; hence, we can investigate how the enabling and inhibiting predispositions produced by System 1 affect users’ System 2 cognitive responses (Model 2), which in turn influence the behavior responses; see Figure 5.

In summary, we argue that there are (1) direct effects of System 1’s enabling and inhibiting predispositions on the behavioral responses to a new technology (both intentions, i.e., the adoption decision and actual use behavior); (2) moderating effects of System 1’s enabling and inhibiting predispositions on the relationship between intentions and use; and (3) direct effects of System 1’s enabling and inhibiting predispositions on System 2’s cognitive response to technology (both PE and EE).

## 3.1 System 1 and the Behavioral Response to Technology

Figure 4 summarizes the relationships examined in the baseline System 1 models, Models 1a and 1b. System 1 is a set of subsystems that run in parallel triggered by the stimuli in the context [44, 45]. Because it has many subsystems operating in parallel, it simultaneously uses several heuristics, each of which produces an answer to be considered [44].

In the arena of technology adoption and use, we have the potential for both heuristics that encourage a user to adopt and use a new technology and heuristics that inhibit adoption and use because technology has both positive and negative aspects [8]. Both enabling and

inhibiting heuristics can exist simultaneously because many people have a history of both positive and negative experiences [13].

A new adoption and use situation would trigger these System 1 heuristics, and these heuristics would produce an instant answer. The specific design features of the technology would be irrelevant, except to the extent to which they would trigger different heuristics. Thus, when presented with a situation requiring a technology adoption and use decision, the user’s System 1 would produce similar results across similar technologies, results that would be seen by an observer to be a predisposition to act in a certain manner.

We examine these effects empirically in two steps. In the first baseline model (Model 1a), we examine the effects of System 1 on the usage behavior. Our second baseline model (Model 1b) adds the decision of whether to adopt (BI) because much prior research has examined the role of BI as a predictor of the usage behavior (e.g., [65, 66]). Note that the hypotheses tested in Models 1b, H3, H4, and H5 are additive to those presented in Models 1a, H1, and H2.

## 3.1.1 Usage Behavior

System 1’s enabling predisposition (TRCo) refers to a user’s underlying heuristics that lead to a System 1 belief that technology is a positive thing. Technology may increase control, flexibility, and efficiency, and the supporting heuristics are formed based on a history of successful experiences with technology adoption decisions. It refers to the feelings that with technology one can accomplish more than what one has in the past and that it gives one greater convenience and control. The innovativeness-related TRCo beliefs are driven by the desire to seek out new stimuli (see also [67]), guided by willingness to try technologies on one’s own or keenness of being the first to try new technologies. Prior research suggests that users who are technology

optimists, novelty seekers, and technology thought leaders have intrinsic motivation to use new systems and intrinsically enjoy trying new approaches to old problems [68, 69]. We thus hypothesize that users high in TRCo, who believe that technology increases control, flexibility, and efficiency and who seek to be at the forefront of trying new technology, will tend to decide to use the system to a greater extent.

H1: TRCo will have a direct positive effect on the usage behavior.

System 1’s inhibiting predisposition (TRIn) refers to an individual’s distrust in technology that is based on the belief that innovation is exclusionary and is formed on the basis of a history of unsuccessful experiences with technology adoption decisions. It pertains to a perceived lack of control over technology and a general paranoia that an individual has for technology-based products and services. TRIn also refers to an individual’s skepticism about the ability of technology to work properly. Prior research suggests that technostress reduces the productivity of an individual [70] and that individuals with greater TRIn believe that technology is often too complicated to be useful [71]. Thus, we hypothesize that users’ high in TRIn will tend to use the system to a lesser extent.

H2: TRIn will have a direct negative effect on the usage behavior.

## 3.1.2 Behavioral Intentions

Prior research suggests that BI include both direction (to do X or not to do X) and intensity (time and effort that someone is prepared to expend to do X) [2]. Having formed BI, a user considers factors that could influence their performance and then employs those estimates to undertake behavior [66]. BI are a measure of an individual’s motivation to perform a behavior [2].

Prior research suggests that intrinsic motivation is a significant determinant of BI [27]. Thus, we expect that intrinsic dispositional optimism (i.e., higher levels of TRCo), for example, notion that “technology is good,” will increase BI.

H3: TRCo will have a direct positive effect on behavioral intentions.

However, technological innovations that are radical can also result in apprehension in users [72]. For example, computer anxiety has been found to have significant impact on intentions [73]. Susskind and Stefanone [74] found that perceptions of general internet apprehensiveness show a negative association with on-line information-seeking behavior. Thus, apprehension, distrust, and skepticism (i.e., higher levels of TRIn) are expected to lower BI. Thus, those high in inhibiting predispositions (TRIn) will have lower motivation to perform their behavior. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H4: TRIn will have a direct negative effect on behavioral intentions.

3.1.3 System 1 and the Relationship between Behavioral Intentions and Usage Behavior Prior research suggests that individuals who possess well-formed intentions, i.e., the extent to which they have thought through the outcomes and obstacles associated with their decision, are of trying out new technologies – i.e., individuals with greater TRCo – will have greater motivation to perform behavior, regardless of their intentions. Because of keenness of being at the forefront of new information technologies, individuals with greater TRCo would not perceive unforeseen obstacles as overwhelming. Users with greater TRCo are intrinsically motivated to be the first to try new technologies and thus are willing to make the requisite effort. Because users with greater TRCo have greater motivation and involvement and are more willing to make the effort, we expect that users with greater TRCo will be more likely to actually act on their

intentions than those with lower TRCo. Thus, TRCo will strengthen the relationship between BI decisions and use behavior.

H5a: The relationship between behavioral intentions and the usage behavior will be strengthened for individuals with greater TRCo.

Individuals with greater TRIn perceive technologies to be too complicated to be useful and are overwhelmed by how much they need to know to use them [71]. Because of their belief that technology is exclusionary, individuals with greater TRIn who encounter unforeseen disadvantages or difficulties would be more likely to perceive them as overwhelming. Considering that users with greater TRIn are characterized by perceived lack of control over technology and skepticism about technology’s ability to work properly, such individuals will have lower motivation to perform behavior and thus would have lower motivation to follow through on their intentions. Any set back in acting on their intentions would be noticed and would be more likely to break the link from intention to successful completion. Hence, we expect that with greater TRIn, the relationship between BI and use will be weakened.

H5b: The relationship between behavioral intentions and the usage behavior will be attenuated for individuals with greater TRIn.

## 3.2 System 1 Effects on System 2

Model 2 (Figure 5) expands the baseline System 1 models (Figure 4) to include users’ System 2 cognitive responses to technology. The hypotheses for Model 2, H6, and H7 are added to those in Model 1b (H1-H5). In this section, we present only the additional hypotheses, i.e., H6 and H7 argue that System 1 predispositions influence System 2 cognition so that different individuals will perceive the same technology in different ways. It is well known that System 2 cognitive responses of PE, EE, SI, and FC influence the behavioral outcomes of intentions and use [1]; hence, we include these relationships in the model but offer no formal hypotheses.

System 1 processes run constantly and automatically and present conclusions faster than the slower and more deliberate processes of System 2 [3]. Before a user can deliberately and rationally form a conclusion, for example, about performance or effort expectations of a new technology, System 1 has already presented a complete and well-formed conclusion about whether the technology should be adopted or not – a conclusion that is relatively independent of the technology itself. Of course, an individual can choose to deliberately assess the new technology and use System 2 cognition to corroborate or override the conclusion of System 1 [76].

The initial conclusion presented by System 1 has an influence on System 2 [49, 76, 77]. Individuals often use “anchoring and adjustment” as an important decision-making heuristic [78]. Individuals begin with the initial System 1 conclusion that serves as an anchor, whether consciously or not. As individuals gain additional information, they adjust their conclusions from the initial anchor [79, 80], so that positive information moves them closer to an adoption decision and negative information moves them closer to a decision not to adopt. This shift is relative to the initial anchor presented by their System 1, so understanding this initial System 1 anchor is important in understanding the ultimate decision; a set of positive information triggering good PE and EE is likely to lead to adoption for individuals with an initial neutral or positive System 1 conclusion, but for others, whose System 1 has presented a very strong negative conclusion, even an unbroken set of positive information may not be sufficient to overcome the initial System 1 conclusion.

But the effects of System 1 do not stop there. An individual’s interpretation of a situation is often biased by their initial System 1 beliefs [49]. The initial System 1 conclusion colors how an individual perceives the situation as he or she applies the deliberate and rational cognition of System 2 [81, 82]. Individuals unwittingly tend to focus on information that supports their initial System 1 beliefs and discount information that opposes them [79, 83]; we are predisposed to see what we expect to see, so we interpret the situation using the initial anchor suggested by System 1. Thus, the net effect of neutral information that presents both positives and negatives is often to strengthen the initial conclusion formed by System 1, whether positive or negative, because information that supports the initial conclusion is attended to and information that does not support it is overlooked [79, 83, 84].

Therefore, in summary, the initial System 1 predispositions (TRCo and TRIn) will influence the cognitive response to System 2 (PE, EE, SE, and FC). The effects may be simple and direct. A positive approach response produced by System 1 may inflate the four cognitive responses to a specific technology postulated in unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (PE, EE, SI, and FC), while a negative avoidance response may decrease them.

Conversely, the System 1 predispositions may act on the antecedents of the four cognitive responses. For example, higher levels of TRCo are based on stronger beliefs of control [85] and convenience [86], which increase intrinsic motivation to use technology; increased intrinsic motivation can act as an anchor in the evaluation of technology, so that assessments of PE are higher [87, 88]. Conversely, a lack of trust in technology (i.e., higher levels of TRIn) is related to the lack of realization of benefits [89], which acts to lower

PE. Openness to technology (i.e., higher levels of TRCo) may improve perceptions of EE [1].

Therefore, we theorize that System 1 enabling and inhibiting predispositions will have a direct effect on System 2’s cognitive response to a specific technology:

H6: TRCo will have a direct positive effect on a) performance expectancy; b) effort expectancy; c) social influence; and d) facilitating conditions.

H7: TRIn will have a direct negative effect on a) performance expectancy; b) effort expectancy; c) social influence; and d) facilitating conditions.

## 4. Method

We conducted a field survey of actual users of an e-textbook system.

## 4.1 Participants

Participants for our research were 172 junior and senior students drawn from a set of business courses at a large state university in the US. The university introduced an e-textbook initiative, which included the launch of a new e-textbook system. The use of the e-textbook system was voluntary. The students were provided access to an e-textbook through the learning management system but had the option to use a printout of the textbook or order a low-cost printed version. About 34% of our respondents were female, and the average age was 21.5 years.

## 4.2 E-textbook System

The e-textbook system was a commercial product developed by Courseload, Inc., which provided delivery of pedagogical content using a web-based system and included several navigation, text interaction, and sharing features. These features provided a different paradigm to the way students traditionally read textbooks. Students could navigate using several options, which are as follows: (1) selecting a chapter in the table of contents that is linked to the book chapters; (2) using the search textbox to find keywords in the textbook; (3) using the

“go to page” textbox to go to a specific page of the textbook; and (4) using the on-screen navigation or physical keyboard arrows to move either page-by-page or line-by-line. With regard to navigation, students could also re-arrange the display of the textbook, including zooming and fitting, to the display size of their screen.

As students read the e-textbook, they could also highlight, annotate, and add notes. Highlighting was done through a “click and drag” over the desired text. An annotation was an extension of a highlight as a student could also add a text-based note affiliated with their highlight. A student could choose to make a “sticky note,” which allowed the student to place a note anywhere on the page. The examples of text interactions above indicate how the e-textbook feature could be helpful to students as they interacted with the content in an e-textbook.

Another feature of the e-textbook system was the ability to share one’s comments in the textbook (e.g., highlighting and annotating the book) with the instructor or other students in the class. While students could only choose to “share” or “not share” all their interactions with the instructor, students had the ability to select specific students in the class to share their text interactions with. The students also had the ability to view the interactions made by the instructor or other students in their respective copies of the e-textbook.

## 4.3 Survey Instruments and Survey Administration

The survey items for BI, PE, EE, SI, and FC were adapted from the work of Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, and Davis [1]. The items for the TRCo and TRIn constructs were adapted from the work of

Parasuraman [14].<sup>1</sup> The full list of survey items used can be seen in Appendix A.

We collected data at two points in time. The first survey included items for BI, PE, EE, and TRCo and TRIn. The first survey was conducted during the first week of classes after students were given a 20-minute training on the e-textbook system during the class. The second survey included an item for the subjects to provide their use of the system. The second survey was conducted 15 weeks later during the last week of classes.

SmartPLS 2.0 M3 [93] was used for the analysis, and bootstrapping was employed to determine the significance of measurement loadings and paths in the structural models.

## 6. Discussion

In this research, we examined how System 1 predispositions influence a user’s System 2 cognitive responses to technology, and how together the System 1 predispositions and the System 2 cognitive response affect BI and actual usage behavior. Table 4 summarizes

our results. As hypothesized, we found that the System 1 enabling predispositions (TRCo) directly influence BI and usage behavior. However, we found that the System 1 inhibiting predispositions (TRIn) do not directly influence BI or usage behavior. Interestingly, we also found that the System 1 enabling and inhibiting predispositions both attenuate the relationship between BI and usage behavior. Finally, we found that the System 1 enabling predispositions influence the System 2 cognitive responses of PE, EE, SI, and FC (which in turn affected the behavioral responses).

In summary, our research shows that users’ System 1 predispositions have a direct effect on their technology adoption decisions and actual use, over and above the deliberate and rational cognitions of System 2 that have dominated past research (i.e., PE, EE, SI, and FC). Our research also shows that System 1 predispositions subtly influence users’ System 2 cognitions (PE, EE, SI, and FC) so that they shape how users perceive the new technology. We now expand on the key findings.

First, we expected that the System 1 enabling predispositions (TRCo) would directly influence BI and usage behavior. We found that TRCo directly influences BI and usage behavior, above and beyond the System 2 cognitive response to technology (i.e., PE, EE, and SI). For individuals who have formed the same assessment of PE, EE, and SI, individuals with greater TRCo are more likely to use the system.

The fundamental theoretical core of current technology adoption theory and research is based on a rational and deliberate System 2 assessment of the new technology and the situation in which it will be used. However, our research shows that the enabling predispositions from System 1 influence intentions and use above and beyond these rational and deliberate System 2 assessments. The total explained variance on intentions in our study was comparable to prior research (i.e., $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } { = } 0 . 7 4 )$ . In our study, System 2 assessments accounted for about 50% of the variance on intentions, with direct effects of the predispositions from System 1 explaining another 25% (and another 25% remaining unexplained). Of course, the predispositions from System 1 also influenced the System 2 assessments (ranging from a low value of 7% for SI to a high value of 25% for EE). We conclude that the rational and deliberate System 2-based models are useful but are incomplete. By adding System 1 factors into System 2-based models, such as technology-related predispositions, we are more likely to better understand and predict intentions and usage behavior.

Second, we expected that System 1 inhibiting predispositions (TRIn) would directly influence BI and usage behavior. However, this turned out not to be the case. Although TRIn does have a significant and negative correlation with BI (see Table 3), these effects disappear in the presence of TRCo (see Figure 8). TRCo has significant bivariate correlations with PE, EE,

SI, FC, BI, and usage behavior (Use). TRIn has significant bivariate correlations with PE, EE, and BI. Therefore, we conducted an additional analysis to examine the mediation effects of PE, EE, and SI separately for TRCo and TRIn on BI, believing that PE, EE, and SI were mediating some of their effects on BI.<sup>3</sup> We also assessed the mediation effects of FC on TRCo and TRIn separately.<sup>4</sup> These results show that when considered separately, TRIn does have significant direct effects on Use and BI. With regard to TRIn and BI, PE is a partial mediator and that EE is a complete mediator. This is because the mediated path of TRIn through PE to BI accounts for 59.31% of the total effect of TRIn on BI, and the mediated path of TRIn through EE to BI accounts for 91.70% of the total effect of TRIn on BI; see Appendix B. However, when both TRCo and TRIn are in the model (as shown in Figure 8), only TRCo has a direct effect on PE, EE, SI, FC, Use, and BI. This is not surprising considering that the bivariate correlation between TRCo was at least double the bivariate correlation of TRIn with other latent variables in our model; see Table 3. Thus, this additional analysis helped us confirm the importance of TRIn and better understand its impact on BI and Use.

We therefore construe TRCo as a suppressor variable [98], which occurs when two variables independently have significant correlations with a dependent variable, but when the two variables enter a model together, only one variable has a remaining significant effect. In some cases of suppressor effects, it may be parsimonious to drop one of the variables [99];

however, we decided to retain both variables for theoretical and statistical reasons. Theoretically, we argued that TRCo and TRIn are two constructs that simultaneously exist as part of an individual’s predisposition toward technology. Statistically, we assessed the predictive relevance of TRIn and TRCo, checked for issues related to multicollinearity, and also compared the variances of TRCo and TRIn. We established the predictive relevance of both TRIn and TRCo to our final model using Stone-Geisser’s $\mathrm { Q } ^ { 2 }$ [92] and found that both TRCo and TRIn had predictive relevance for use. We also assessed the effects of multicollinearity in our dataset, and we found that all construct variance inflation factors revealed values less than 5 indicating that any bias in our structural results due to multicollinearity would be minimal [100, 101]. Finally, the comparison of variance of TRCo and TRIn revealed that TRIn had a standard deviation of 0.77, which was greater than that of TRCo in our dataset indicating that this was not an issue due to lack of variation in our sample.<sup>5</sup>

Third, we also found that System 1 inhibiting predispositions (TRIn) weakened the relationship between BI and usage, thereby indicating that predispositions not only influence the formation of BI but also whether an individual actually acts on those intentions and ultimately uses the new technology. In a context outside IS, Sheeran [2] concludes that while intentions can explain behavior, they account for only 28% of the variance in actual behavior, suggesting that much happens between the formation of an intention to do something and actually doing it.

Sheeran [2] found that as much as 47% of individuals had positive intentions but failed to act on them, whom he termed “inclined abstainers.” Individuals with greater TRIn are more likely to be “inclined abstainers.” For these individuals, any set back is likely to sidetrack their intentions and lead to lower use.

Fourth, contrary to our expectations, we found that System 1 enabling predispositions (TRCo) also attenuates the relationship between BI and usage. At first glance, this does not make sense; after all, we hypothesized that individuals high in TRCo should be more motivated to follow through on their intentions and actually use the system, analogous to the opposite way that high TRIn weakens this link between intentions and usage. The key difference is that TRCo has a significant direct impact on usage, separate from intentions (unlike TRIn that does not). This means that individuals high in TRCo are more likely to use the system, independent of their stated intentions to do so. For these individuals, their stated intentions (measured by BI) have less of an impact on actual usage because they are more likely to use the system regardless of their stated intentions, and thus, there is a weaker relationship between stated intentions and actual use (i.e., a negative beta coefficient).

Fifth, we found that the System 1 predispositions influenced the System 2 cognitive response to the new technology. In other words, innate characteristics and past experience with other technology adoptions changed how users viewed the new technology. Their rational and deliberate assessments of the PE, EE, and SI of the new technology were subtly shifted to be more positive, while FC were subtly shifted to be more negative. Past research has argued that PE, EE, SI, and FC are “determinants” of intentions and use [1]. In contrast, our research

suggests that these System 2 constructs are intervening constructs that in some cases mediate a more fundamental relationship between System 1 predispositions, and adoption and use; see Appendix B. We conclude that PE and EE are important constructs, but they are not “determinants” per se; they are not the starting point in the adoption and use of new technology but rather are influenced by deeper System 1 constructs developed over a lifetime.

We believe that our research makes three contributions. First, it shows that System 1 predispositions to technology play an important role in users’ behavioral response to technology. Predispositions directly influence intentions and use, above and beyond the traditional System 2 cognitive response to the technology (i.e., PE, EE, SI, and FC).

Second, our research shows that these System 1 predispositions also shape the System 2 cognitive response to technology that has dominated prior technology adoption research. There is a more fundamental relationship at work, such that these System 2 “determinants” of adoption decisions and use behavior are more correctly important partial mediators, not “determinants.” Thus, for many users, the adoption and use of new technologies is influenced

Third, our research highlights the importance of conceptualizing positive and negative responses to technology as independent because we found that the System 1 enabling predispositions (TRCo) and inhibiting predispositions (TRIn) had different effects. Prior research suggests that new information technologies can evoke both positive [102, 103] and negative [104-106] perceptions that can directly or indirectly influence beliefs and behaviors toward technologies. Our research suggests that positive and negative predispositions are independent and may or may not have opposite effects.

Before we discuss the implications of this research, it is important to recognize some limitations. While self-reported usage measure may not be an ideal surrogate for actual usage [107], prior research has widely used self-reported usage [23, 33]. Self-reported usage was measured 3 months after the measurement of BI, and thus, common-method variance was minimized. One might argue that the actual number of pages read would be a better measure of use, but this measure is highly dependent on the number of pages a student intends to read, which is independent of the technology; some students intend to read all of the assigned pages, some intend to read very little (studies of our student population show that on average, students read 50% of the assigned pages). Thus, we believe the best measure is the self-reported percentage of material read on computer versus on paper. We studied university students adopting a technology designed for student use (e-textbooks). While this field study employed actual users of the system who used the e-book system to learn, university students tend to be younger and more intelligent than the general population. Future research should examine the generalizability of our findings to the general population as different relationships could emerge with different situations.

## 6.1 Implications for Research

Our research has five implications for future research. First, it shows that one’s System 1 predispositions to technology significantly influence the behavioral response to the technology (i.e., the intentions to use and actual usage). Individuals are likely to decide to adopt and use it differently depending on their innate characteristics and past experiences with numerous other prior technology adoptions. How we respond to a new office system, a new enterprise system, a new smart phone, etc., will be indelibly shaped by our personality and experiences with countless other technologies that may or may not be related to the technology in question. We believe that this calls for more research investigating how System 1 predispositions influence the adoption and use of new technologies.

Second, much prior research has considered the deliberate and rational System 2 constructs of PE, EE, SI, and FC as important “determinants” of intentions and use [1, 23]. Our research suggests that these in fact are not “determinants” but rather are partially mediating variables that cognitive response to the technology itself. In other words, two individuals experiencing the same technology are likely to form different System 2 perceptions of it because of their personality and history of prior experiences that have shaped their System 1 predispositions. We believe this calls for more research on how System 1 factors that influence the deliberate and rational System 2 cognitive responses to technology, such as PE, EE, SI, and FC.

Third, considering that System 1 enabling and inhibiting predispositions are independent, future research needs to consider both positive and negative predispositions as factors that can explain adoption and use. Recent research that has examined enabling and inhibiting perceptions has considered object-based beliefs about the technology artifact [13, 108]. Our research takes this one step further to show that enabling and inhibiting predispositions influence both an individual’s cognitive appraisal of the situation and his/her behavioral response. Future research should examine enabling and inhibiting beliefs more deeply to better understand how this duality influences adoption and use.

For example, perhaps early adopters are individuals who have typically had positive past experiences and have a strong influence from enabling predispositions (TRCo) and weak (or no) influence from inhibiting predispositions (TRIn). Laggards might be the opposite case with weak (or no) influence from enabling predispositions and strong influence from inhibiting predispositions; due to many past adoption and use failures.

Finally, future research should examine how System 1 predispositions influence users intentions to continue using information systems (see [110]) and their propensity to explore a new system’s features (see [111-113]). Prior research suggests that behaviors can be driven by habits instead of intent [114, 115] and that habits have been found to directly affect usage behavior over and above intentions, attitudes, subjective norms, and behavioral control [116]. Future research needs to examine how System 1 predispositions help shape the formation of habits themselves.

## 6.2 Implications for Practice

Our research makes two contributions for practice. First, we found that System 1 predispositions are important considerations in adoption and use. Practitioners should consider TRCo and TRIn as they undertake an IT implementation. Social profile data, behavioral data, and customer lifecycle data can all be used to understand the predispositions of users. For example, Amazon’s innovations such as “recommended products” and “users like me also bought” that predict the users’ interest leverage predispositions<sup>6</sup>. System 2 factors such as PE and EE influence

intentions, but they are less important for individuals with greater TRCo, who are more likely to intend to use a system, regardless. Intentions influence use, but those with greater TRIn are less likely to follow through on intentions; users with greater TRIn should be considered as “fragile users,” i.e., users for whom any setback will lead them not to follow through on their intentions. user base can help better understand adoption or lack of adoption.

Second, our research suggests that the rollout of new technology should consider both positive and negative predispositions of the user base, although positive predispositions have a greater impact than negative predispositions. Considering that users’ predispositions are relationships between predispositions and intentions and use. Developing interventions designed to strengthen the impact of enabling predispositions and ameliorate the impact of inhibiting predispositions can increase the likelihood of successful adoption and use. For example, training that draws out past successful adoptions to strengthen positive predispositions may strengthen the impact of enabling dispositions. Specifically the training might look to highlight the positive effects of a past successful adoption.

We believe this implication can go beyond an IT adoption and use context. For example in a marketing context, promotional material introducing a new product innovation might be framed in the context of previously well-received and successful innovations. Similarly, in a non-profit service setting, a healthcare provider might introduce a new healthcare innovation to its staff and patients by highlighting previous past successful innovations.

## 7. Conclusion

In this research, we found that the automatic cognition of System 1 directly influences technology adoption decisions and usage behavior and also acts indirectly by shaping the deliberate and rational cognition of System 2 (i.e., PE and EE). Thus, the traditional and often researched System 2 “determinants” of technology adoption and use are better thought as important partial mediators, not “determinants,” of technology adoption and use. Our results indicate that System 1 positive and negative predispositions had separate and different effects, indicating that they are different constructs that need to be conceptualized independently. Finally, our advice for practitioners striving for a successful technology rollout is somewhat different from past research: our research shows that the chance of successful adoption and use decisions is likely to be increased by activating and strengthening the impact of users’ System 1 positive predispositions.

## Appendix B: Additional Mediation Analyses

We assessed the mediation effects of effort expectancy (EE) and performance expectancy (PE) for both enabling predispositions (TRCo) and inhibiting dispositions (TRIn) on Behavioral Intentions (BI). We followed the guidelines of Hair, Hult, Ringle, and Sarstedt [117] [see their Exhibit 7.18 on p.224], which operationalize the recommendations of Preacher and Hayes [118] to PLS-SEM for assessing mediation. Per these guidelines, we did the following: (1) Assess the significance of the independent variable on the dependent variable without the presence of the mediator; (2) Assess the indirect effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable; and (3) Assess the variance accounted for (VAF) by the mediator compared to the direct effect. The results of our analysis are found in Table A1 below.

Table A1 shows the six mediation paths tested along the rows, and the steps of the guidelines described above as the columns. We use the first row of the table to explain the table. For each mediation path tested, our first step was to assess if the independent variable (e.g., TRCo) had a significant effect on the dependent variable (e.g., BI) without the presence of the mediator in the model (e.g., PE). This direct effect was significant, thus we continued our analysis. The second step was to assess the indirect effect of the independent variable (e.g., TRCo) on the dependent variable (e.g., BI) through the mediator (e.g., PE). This indirect effect was significant, so we continued to the last step of the analysis. The last step was to assess whether the VAF by the indirect effect as opposed to the direct effect (both paths were present in the model for step 2) accounted for greater than 80% (i.e., full mediation), between 20% and 80% (partial mediation), or less than 20% (no mediation) of the total effect of the independent variable (e.g., TRCo) on the dependent variable (e.g., BI). In the first row of the table, we see that the indirect effect (e.g., TRCo through PE to BI) accounts for between 20% and 80% (e.g., 53.82%) of the total effect of the independent variable’s (e.g., TRCo) total effect on dependent variable (e.g., BI); hence, we conclude there was partial mediation.

Step 1 of our results in Table A1 indicates that the direct effects of both our TRCo and TRIn have significant direct effects on BI/Use; TRCo has a positive relationship, whereas TRIn has a negative relationship. Step 2 of our results in Table A1 indicates that the indirect effect of our predispositions through both PE and EE is significant in the same direction (e.g., positive/negative) of the effect as Step 1 and less than the effect of Step 1. The indirect effect of TRCo and TRIn through SI and FC was not significant and thus not further evaluated. Finally, Step 3 indicates that PE is a partial mediator of our predispositions on BI, and EE is a full mediator of our predispositions.

## Author Biography

Vijay Khatri is an Associate Professor of Information Systems in the Operations and Decision Technologies department in Kelley School of Business. He earned his PhD from the University of Arizona. His current research interests His research interests center on data management and technology adoption. He is interested in understanding the cognitive aspects that influence the analysis and design of databases. He is also interested in exploring how technology and user characteristics influence use behavior, and ultimately, technology adoption. His work has been published in journals such as Information Systems Research, Information Systems, IEEE Transactions on Knowledge and Data Engineering, and IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, among others. He is a member of AIS, ACM and Informs and a senior member of IEEE.

Binny M. Samuel is an Assistant Professor at the University of Cincinnati. He earned his Ph.D. from the Kelley School of Business at Indiana University. He also holds a Bachelor of Science in Business and M.B.A, both with emphasis in Accounting and Information Systems. Prior to his doctoral education, he worked in IT roles at Ford Motor Company and at Indiana University. His current research interests focus on human factor issues in order to 1) improve the ways in which the information processing needs of an organization are analyzed, designed, and maintained and 2) understand how cognition and emotions affect an individual’s decision to adopt and ultimately use systems. His work can be found in Journal of Information Technology, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, and MIS Quarterly among others.

Alan R. Dennis is Professor of Information Systems and holds the John T. Chambers Chair of Internet Systems in the Kelley School of Business at Indiana University. He was named a Fellow of the Association for Information Systems in 2012. Prof. Dennis has written more than 150 research papers, and has won numerous awards for his theoretical and applied research. His research focuses on three main themes: team collaboration; IT for the subconscious; and digital innovation. He is Editor-in-Chief of Foundations and Trends in Information Systems, Editor-in-Chief of AIS Transactions on Replication Research and Vice President for Conferences for the Association for Information Systems. Prof. Dennis also has written four books (two on data communications and networking, and two on systems analysis and design) and is leading an NSF-funded project to gamify information systems education. He has co-founded five start-up companies, the most recent of which is NameInsights.com.
