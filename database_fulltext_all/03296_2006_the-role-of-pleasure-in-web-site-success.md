# The role of pleasure in web site success

Kristof De Wulf, Niels Schillewaert, Steve Muylle <sup>\*</sup>, Deva Rangarajan

Vlerick Leuven Gent Management School, Reep 1, 9000 Gent, Belgium

Received 26 November 2004; received in revised form 18 June 2005; accepted 29 October 2005 Available online 15 December 2005

## Abstract

We developed and empirically validated a process model of web site success in an online shopping context by identifying the role of pleasure as a key mediating variable. Web site evaluations (of content, organization, and technology) were posited as affecting the success (involving satisfaction, commitment, and trust) of a multi-dimensional web site. Pleasure was introduced as a key variable, mediating the relationship between web site evaluation and web site success. We found that pleasure partially mediated the evaluations–success relationship and also found significant support for direct relationships between web site evaluations and success. Additionally, satisfaction was found to be instrumental in helping predict user commitment and trust that online shoppers placed on the site owner. <sup>#</sup> 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Web site pleasure; Web site success; Web site design

## 1. Introduction

Based on a literature review and empirical study, we present a process model for assessing web site success in an online shopping setting. In it, we identify web site evaluations as a key independent variable contributing to the success of a web site, both directly and indirectly. We suggest that pleasure experienced by online shoppers when using a web site is a key mediating variable that affects the way that web site evaluations result in web site success. Fig. 1 shows our model.

In our study, web site evaluations refer to features of the web site seen and experienced by the end-user. While previous research focused primarily on studying individual features of web sites, very few studies integrated these features. We identified content, organization, and technology as the major constructs of web site evaluations. Subsequently, drawing from the literature on affective states of individual users, we see pleasure as a key mediating variable between web site evaluations and success. In keeping with extant research, we felt that web site success was a multidimensional construct composed of satisfaction, commitment, and trust. We extended previous literature by focusing on the relationships between these dimensions and empirically tested our model using a panel of online shoppers.

## 2. Web site evaluations

The possibility of a higher order factor structure influenced by lower order constructs has long been recognized. However, structural equation modeling was the first to show that higher order factor structures could be used in measuring complex constructs [20]. We identified web site evaluations [15] to be a multidimensional construct involving three higher order dimensions: content, organization, and technology, as shown in Table 1.

## 2.1. Content

This is viewed as a second-order construct with credibility, currentness, relevance, and sufficiency as its underlying dimensions. Credibility is the extent to which the contents of the web site are considered trustworthy. Currentness refers to the recent nature of the information in the web site. Relevance is the extent to which contents of the web site are useful. Sufficiency is the extent to which contents are complete.

## 2.2. Organization

This is considered a second-order dimension, corresponding to the effective structuring and display of web site content. Navigation, readability, speed, design, and interactivity are its main lower order constructs. Navigation refers to the structural consistency of the web site. Readability refers to the extent to which text is legible. It is an important dimension as different studies have shown that reading from a computer screen may be slower and less accurate [47,66]. Speed refers to time spent in loading and displaying the site. Design affects the visual appeal of the web site. Interactivity is the extent to which the site is personalized to the user.

## 2.3. Technology

While previous studies have predominantly focused on information and system features, less attention has been devoted to the technical aspects of web sites [58]. In our study, the extent to which the technology simplifies the site and makes the visit successful is evaluated for both reliability and progressiveness, where reliability refers to the enduser’s perception of the adequate technical functioning of the web site and a web site is progressive if it is state-of-the-art. In this research, a progressive site depends on the richness of the web site and availability of advanced tools, such as search and decision aids [1,55]. According to media richness theory, multimedia interactive formats provide capabilities richer than text and photographs of traditional brochures or catalogs. Web sites can take advantage of this by the use of audio, video, and the ability to link pages.

## 3. Web site pleasure

We consider pleasure to be a major attitudinal outcome and an antecedent of success. Pleasure is the extent to which the visitor perceives the web site visit to be enjoyable and is considered a prerequisite for a successful site. Empirical studies have shown that, while users may wish to use an online service, the design of the user interface often impacts them to do so in an enjoyable manner [7,8,38,42]. Chen et al. suggested that the level of entertainment offered by a web site is a key predictor of user attitude towards the site. Childers et al. [10] argued that the extent to which a web site evoked hedonic feelings significantly affected the Internet shopping experience of the customer. This was further confirmed by Eroglu et al. [25]. The importance of pleasure was also prevalent in the notion of flow in hypermedia computer-mediated environments [35]. Web sites allow visitors to experience ‘‘flow,’’ a virtual experience characterized by fun, in which visitors interact with the site in a recreational manner. As such, the visitor–web site interaction is viewed as playing a vital role in enhancing pleasure, and hence building loyalty. Therefore, we proposed pleasure as a key outcome variable of web site evaluations and considered it to be an anteceding factor impacting web site success.

## 3.1. Relationship between web site evaluations and pleasure

Support for the use of pleasure as an important outcome of web site evaluations factors can also be found in studies on Internet marketing. Indeed, the significance of providing relevant, up-to-date, complete, and trustworthy information in web sites has been shown by many authors [2,34,43,33]. Likewise, the importance of site organization through adequate readability, hyperlink structure, and accessibility has been discussed in many studies [5,61]. Also, the provision of multimedia elements, such as graphics, audio, video, and backgrounds, is considered significant in enhancing web site content and increasing attractiveness [6]. Further, using interesting themes, flashy graphics, or appealing site design may make a web site experience entertaining and increase visitors’ likelihood to conduct business. Personalization is also believed to enhance marketing outcomes in online environments. Interestingly, research conducted in a shopping environment, indicated that greater customer control of the shopping experience increased the pleasure and convenience of shopping, which had an impact on customer satisfaction.

We therefore suggested that online shoppers who visit a web site that contains up-to-date information matching their needs have a feeling of positive affect on visiting the site. Similarly, the better the content is organized and presented, the more likely the visitor will experience a positive association with the web site. Also, the more a web sites uses progressive technology, such as multimedia elements, the more likely it is for customers to purchase and increase the chance of a pleasurable experience. Thus, we hypothesize that:

1a. Web site content is positively related to pleasure.

1b. Web site organization is positively related to pleasure.

1c. Web site technology is positively related to pleasure.

## 4. Web site success

Based on web usability and design and media richness studies [14], Palmer [52] identified user satisfaction, likelihood of return, and frequency of use as three key sub-dimensions of web site success. Similarly, integrating the expectation-disconfirmation paradigm with user satisfaction theories, McKinney et al. studied the effects of perceived Web System and Web Information Quality performance on satisfaction. Another important outcome involved the trust that web site users placed on the site. Trust has been studied frequently as an outcome variable in online environments [37,41] and was found to be essential for the effective functioning of firms in electronic commerce [4]. Thus, we identified web site success as a multidimensional construct with satisfaction, commitment, and trust as its key factors.

## 4.1. Satisfaction

End-user satisfaction is important in IS research, as it is considered a significant factor in measuring IS success and use [19,16,57]. Muylle et al. defined satisfaction as the attitude toward the web site by a hands-on user of the site. In our study, we used it as an attitudinal measure.

## 4.2. Commitment

Our main focus was on attitudinal loyalty [51] rather than action/behavioral loyalty [18], which represents an enduring commitment of a user to the site, that cannot be inferred by observing users’ revisits and online buying behavior. Indeed, this can occur without commitment, if for example, users are indifferent or have no viable alternatives. Since committed customers typically show higher purchase intent, better resist counter-persuasion, are more willing to pay a premium price, and are more eager to recommend the service provider to others, we considered commitment to be a crucial outcome variable that should drive web site development efforts.

## 4.3. Trust

Trust is of significant concern to users. Friedman et al. [30] stated that trust is based on the expectation that a trustee will act in the interests of the trustor without a guarantee and that it is involved with minimizing the risk of harm. Hanson asserts that a welldesigned site can build trust and confidence. Generally, control trust can be distinguished from party trust [63]. We take the latter perspective and view trust as the web site user’s perception of confidence in the site owner’s reliability and integrity.

## 4.3.1. Relationship between pleasure and web site success

While most research on web site success has focused on site features as antecedents, some authors are looking at the contribution of affective feelings on web site success. Drawing from the literature on hedonic motivations and experiential value [44], Eroglu et al. stressed the importance of the effect of hedonic aspects (pleasure) on online shoppers attitudes and behaviors. In an empirical study, they found evidence that pleasure plays an important role in predicting user satisfaction among online shoppers. Other evidence for the positive effect of pleasure on satisfaction exists in psychology [65] and marketing [26].

Hoffman and Novak asserted that users who exhibit high degrees of pleasure while using a web site spend a longer time visiting it and were more likely to revisit it. While this indicated action/behavioral loyalty, we believe that this action is due to the development of a favorable attitude. This is in keeping with our definition of commitment as an attitude.

Studying the role of consumer trust, Jarvenpaa et al. suggested that individual customers tried to avoid indulging in situations that result in more pain than pleasure. Similarly, Eroglu et al. suggested that pleasure experienced by online shoppers directly affected their risk seeking/avoidance behavior. This implied that users are more likely to develop a relationship based on trust when the web site gives rise to feelings of pleasure, thereby reducing their worry about risk. Based on these arguments, we suggest the second set of hypotheses:

2a. Pleasure is positively related to satisfaction.

2b. Pleasure is positively related to commitment.

2c. Pleasure is positively related to trust.

## 4.3.2. Relationship between web site evaluations and web site success

Prior research on satisfaction in online environments has mainly focused on web site features. McKinney et al. highlighted the importance of Web Information and Web System Quality variables in predicting satisfaction. Their findings were similar to those of Muylle et al., who considered information and organization as key predictors of web site user satisfaction. We decided that the extent to which endusers perceive technology to be reliable and progressive would impact their satisfaction. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

3a. Web site content is positively related to satisfaction.

3b. Web site organization is positively related to satisfaction.

3c. Web site technology is positively related to satisfaction.

Drawing on the work by Jap and Ganesan [40], we considered user commitment to a web site to be an attitudinal rather than a behavioral outcome. In an online survey of 602 Korean customers of online stores, Park and Kim [54] suggested the importance of information and user interface quality as a key antecedent to user commitment. Sarel and Marmorstein [56] suggested that customers involved with online banking tended to evince more commitment in the hope that the technology would improve over time. Further, the Theory of Reasoned Action [27] suggested the importance of beliefs and evaluations in developing attitude. Thus, we have hypotheses:

4a. Web site content is positively related to commitment.

4b. Web site organization is positively related to commitment.

4c. Web site technology is positively related to commitment.

Urban et al. [64] pointed to the deficiencies of most web sites in building customer trust and suggested the importance of information, organization, and technology in gaining confidence and belief. Based on research in marketing and human–computer interaction, Egger and de Groot [23] identified web site informational content as a factor likely to influence the development and maintenance of trust. Likewise, Dutta et al. [22] indicated that providing the customer with useful information contributed to being trusted by a customer and then maintaining trust. Similarly, various organizational features, such as layout, readability, and structure, as well as technical features, like reliability and functionality, have been identified as sources of trust in e-commerce [9]. Hence, we have the hypotheses:

5a. Web site content is positively related to trust. 5b. Web site organization is positively related to trust. 5c. Web site technology is positively related to trust.

## 4.4. Relationship between web site success factors

Research in marketing has dealt with the positive relationship between satisfaction, commitment, and trust [32]. Mohr and Spekman [48] and Ganesan [31] showed empirically that satisfaction played a key-role in positively predicting commitment. Satisfaction was also shown to positively predict trust in some marketing studies. In an online setting, Bauer et al. found strong positive effects of satisfaction on user commitment and trust. Thus, we hypothesized that:

6a. Satisfaction is positively related to commitment.

6b. Satisfaction is positively related to trust.

6c. Commitment is positively related to trust.

## 5. Method

We conducted our research in two steps. The first involved exploratory research consisting of two stages that identified the various first-order and the secondorder factors of web site evaluations as well as the items that best represented the constructs. Concurrent verbal protocol studies (Stage 1) were followed by an empirical validation of the constructs (Stage 2) in the first step. In the second, we conducted an empirical study to test the proposed model. This involved a different sample of online shoppers, using factor items derived from the exploratory research, and then testing the hypotheses.

## 5.1. Web site evaluations

Research into web site evaluations has built upon various perspectives, including uses and gratifications, attitude, quality, and user satisfaction. Various dimensions and items spanning the domain of this construct have been proposed, as shown in Table 1.

In order to compare and accumulate the various approaches, we considered it useful to adopt Churchill’s [12] approach to developing measures of marketing constructs. This, which is particularly well suited for investigating behavioral relationships from a fundamental and applied perspective, considers the specification of the domain of the construct as a prerequisite for developing measures with desirable reliability and validity properties. Thus, we needed to be exacting in the conceptual specification and to determine what was and was not included in its domain. Our objective was thus to investigate, at the conceptual level, how the underlying dimensionality of web site evaluations was structured, which is discussed next.

## 5.1.1. Exploratory study

Stage 1. In order to elicit evaluative dimensions of a visitor’s web site experience a concurrent verbal protocol study was conducted with 18 Internet users [24]. The respondents were sampled for heterogeneity in terms of socio-demographic and web-use characteristics in order to obtain insight into the factors that affect web site evaluations [13]. The sample size was in line with case study based research [67].

Verbal protocols have proven to be useful for exploratory purposes, theory development, and hypothesis formulation concerning the use of specific information technology. During a concurrent verbal protocol session, participants are asked to ‘‘think out loud’’ while performing a task. Here, respondents were asked to report their thoughts while surfing their favorite and disliked web sites. If respondents had not mentioned dimensions identified in previous studies by the end of the session, they were probed in terms of these dimensions. Each protocol lasted for approximately 90 min, was tape-recorded, transcribed, and subjected to a thematic content analysis using established qualitative coding techniques [62]. The authors conducted the coding independently and then corresponding coding results were confirmed and discrepancies resolved through mutual agreement [46]. As a result, 15 categories and 72 measurement items were identified. In addition, five academic researchers and four Internet business practitioners evaluated the face and content validity of the discovered dimensions and items. These people were asked to assign the individual items to the web site evaluations dimensional constructs and to evaluate each item in terms of wording. Only items that were consistently classified by all experts and that did not pose any wording problems were maintained. In total, 12 dimensions involving 53 items were retained.

Stage 2. In order to add to the validity of the results of the qualitative content analysis and expert survey, a quantitative study was conducted among 260 Western European Internet panel members of an Internet marketing research company. We made use of a research panel consisting of people who voluntarily opted to participate. We recruited the panel via high traffic web sites, allowing for continuous refreshment and good coverage of the active Internet population. Participants were asked to select a product category of their interest from a varied list of 11 categories. Each was assigned a task to perform on one specific web site; the set had been carefully selected to represent various levels of commerce support, ranging from plain-vanilla to fully-fledged transaction enabled sites [36]. Following completion of the task, respondents were asked to fill out a questionnaire containing the 53 items, measured on a 7-point Likert scale. The order of the 53 items was randomized and alternated between respondents to assess reliability and validity. Ninetytwo panel members provided usable answers resulting in a response rate of 35%. Reliability (Cronbach alpha >0.7) and principal components analysis (item factor loading above 0.65 with cross loadings below 0.30) led to a final set of 11 dimensions and 49 items.

## 5.2. Empirical study

In order to test the model, new data were collected using the same procedure as for the quantitative stage of the pilot study. Six hundred and ten members of the same Internet marketing research agency panel were asked to fill out the online questionnaire containing the final 49 scale items, representing the 11 proposed dimensions. The panel sample had 64% male and 36% female online shoppers. The median age was 33 and the median net income after taxes was between <sup>s</sup>1000 and 2500 per month. The median value of previous Internet usage showed that the respondents had been using the Internet for more than 3 years. In addition, items for the web site success factors satisfaction, trust, and commitment, adapted from a study by De Wulf et al. [17] were included in the final questionnaire. Pleasure was measured using the scale adopted from Donovan and Rossiter [21]. Two hundred and nine respondents (a response rate of 34%) provided usable answers. The hypothesized model was estimated using a partial least squares (PLS) approach to account for the effects of measurement error. In this, the predictor and dependent variables are viewed as latent constructs that cannot be observed directly. Multiple observed indicators were used to operationalize these latent constructs.

PLS is gaining active interest and use by virtue of it being able to model latent constructs under conditions of non-normality and small to medium sample sizes. Because it makes no distribution assumptions, it is robust to violations of multivariate normality. PLS is also preferable to other techniques, such as regression, that assume error free measurement.

According to Chin and Newsted [11], sample size requirements can be calculated by selecting: ‘‘(1) the block with the largest number of formative indicators (i.e., largest measurement equation), or (2) the dependent latent variable with the largest number of independent latent variables impacting it (i.e., largest structural equation). Using a regression heuristic of 10 cases per indicator, the sample size requirement would be 10 times either (1) or (2), whichever is greater.’’ Our sample size meets these requirements.

The PLS procedure allowed us to assess the predictive validity of the structural models. Tests of significance were analyzed using a Bootstrap resampling procedure in PLSGraph and 500 re-samples were used. The software used for the analysis was PLSGraph Version 3.0.

## 5.2.1. Measurement model

The measurement model was checked using confirmatory factor analysis in order to test for the reliability and the validity of the constructs. Table 2 summarizes the results on the measurement model.

The standardized loadings of each of the measurement items underlying a construct are shown with their composite reliability and the average variance extracted. As these indicate, the measurement model performed well. They are all above the recommended minimum of 0.40 [28]. The composite reliabilities are all above the recommended minimum of 0.70. The average variances extracted range from 0.67 to 0.9 (with a recommended minimum 0.50 [29]). Thus, all of the factors demonstrate good internal consistency and reliability.

The measurement model can also be used to evaluate discriminant validity. Constructs are valid if the variance extracted for each is higher than the squared correlation between the constructs. We examined each pair of constructs and found that all were valid. In addition, the existence of positive correlations between the first-order factors suggests that there is convergent validity where each factor represents different dimensions of the same concept.

## 5.2.2. Structural model

In order to test for the mediating effects of pleasure and the hypotheses, we use the competing models analysis procedure suggested by Singh et al. [60]. This involves estimating and comparing a direct effects and partial mediation model. The first involves studying the effects of content, organization, and technology on the web site success factors: satisfaction, commitment, and trust. The partial mediation model involves estimating (1) all the relationships in the direct effects model, (2) the effects of web evaluations factors on pleasure, and (3) the effects of pleasure on the factors of web site success. The hypothesized mediating effect of pleasure is supported when, compared with the direct effects model, the partial mediation model yields higher explained variance, a significant relationship between web site evaluations factors and pleasure, diminished or insignificant effects of web site evaluations on web site success, and significant effects of pleasure on web site success factors.

Table 3 reveals the results of the analysis. It is evident that the partial mediating model explains higher variance than the direct effects model. The differences in variances range between 7.8 and 10.8%. In addition, the effects of all the web site evaluations factors on pleasure are highly significant at $p < 0 . 0 1$ level. Similarly, pleasure has significant effects on web site success at $p < 0 . 0 1$ level. Finally, compared with the direct effects model, the partial mediation model attenuates the effects of web site evaluations on web site success. While there is no drop in significance level, there is a definite reduction in the magnitude of the path coefficients. All these, taken together, suggest that the partial mediation model is an improvement over the direct effects model and thereby substantiates the mediating role of pleasure.

## 5.2.3. Results for Hypothesis 1

Table 3 presents parameter estimates and t-values for the hypothesized model. The results indicate that all of the hypothesized paths were significant at $p < 0 . 0 1$ Thus, if end-users perceived that a web site provided them with adequate, quality information, on a well organized and state-of-the-art web page, they were likely to exhibit higher levels of pleasure with it.

## 5.2.4. Results for Hypothesis 2

The results of our analysis indicated that all of the hypothesized paths were significant at $p < 0 . 0 1$ . Endusers who experienced high levels of pleasure demonstrated higher levels of satisfaction with the web site. In addition, pleasure induced higher levels of commitment of the end-users to a web site and also resulted in them developing trust in the site owner.

## 5.2.5. Results for Hypothesis 3

The partial, unmediated effects of web site evaluations on web site success, after controlling for pleasure, are also shown in Table 3. The results indicated that all the hypothesized relationships were significant at the $p < 0 . 0 1$ level. However, a comparison of the direct effects model and the partial mediation model indicated that the magnitude of the path coefficients is reduced when pleasure was added to the model. This suggests that web sites characterized by higher levels of content, organization, and technology are likely to enhance levels of satisfaction exhibited by web site end-users.

## 5.2.6. Results for Hypothesis 4

The partially mediated model in Table 3 indicated that hypotheses $\mathrm { H } _ { 4 \mathrm { a } }$ and $\mathrm { H } _ { 4 \mathrm { c } }$ were significant. Note that, when compared with the direct effects model, $\mathrm { H } _ { 4 \mathrm { a } }$ was significant only at the $p < 0 . 0 5$ level. This indicated that pleasure partially mediated the relationship between web site content and commitment. Similarly, the path coefficient of $\mathrm { H } _ { 4 \mathrm { c } }$ reduced in magnitude for the partial mediation model, suggesting that there was a partial mediation effect of pleasure on the web site technology–commitment relationship. Interestingly, the effect of web site organization on commitment was not significant $( p > 0 . 0 5 )$ . This was in direct contrast to the direct effects model where the relationship was significant at the $p < 0 . 0 1$ level. This offered proof that pleasure completely mediated the relationship between organization and commitment. Thus, Hypothesis 4 was only partially supported.

## 6. Discussion

The key contribution of our study is the development of a structural model that links beliefs about a web site with affective feelings towards the site that ultimately result in attitudinal and behavioral intentions with regard to the web site. We achieve this mainly through the analysis of the mediating variable of pleasure. To the best of our knowledge, very little empirical research has focused on the role of pleasure as a key factor that links online shoppers’ beliefs about a web site to corresponding behavioral outcomes associated with the web site. Another contribution involves the investigation of the relationships between web site success dimensions.

We found that the content, organization, and technology features of web sites were significantly related to pleasure. We also found a positive, significant effect of pleasure on the success variables. The beta coeffcients in Table 3 showed that the most important effect of pleasure was on the satisfaction experienced by online shoppers. The effect size of the pleasure– commitment relationship also indicated the important role of pleasure in an online shopping experience. While this would suggest a mediating role of pleasure, we found that in addition to predicting pleasure, there was a significant relationship between most web site evaluations and success variables. Thus, in keeping with Baron and Kenny [3], we conclude that pleasure partially mediates the evaluations–success relationship.

We extend previous research by focusing on the interrelationships between web site success variables. Satisfaction with the web site is key in helping predict user commitment to the web site and the corresponding trust that users place in the site owner. It is of interest to note that while the effect sizes of the previous relationships (H1 through H5) would be categorized as between small to medium, the effect sizes of the relationships between the outcome variables satisfaction–commitment $\mathrm { ( H _ { 6 a } ) }$ and satisfaction–trust $\mathrm { ( H } _ { 6 \mathrm { b } } \mathrm { ) }$ could be characterized as large.

## 6.1. Managerial contribution

Based on our study, web site owners should understand the importance of creating web sites that induce affective feelings of pleasure in the online shopper thereby contributing to user satisfaction, commitment, and trust in the site owner. Thus, care should be taken to ensure that the information provided on the site should be applicable, believable, sufficient, and up to date. The designers should pay attention to the structure and layout of the site and build appropriate tools. More attention could also be paid to using multimedia tools to increase levels of pleasure for shoppers. Personalization of the web site could increase user interactivity and efforts must be taken to reduce content download times.

## 7. Limitations

The key limitation of our study was the self-selection bias resulting from the use of data from members of an existing panel. This was alleviated somewhat by freedom given to the participants to choose a web site of their preference. Another limitation involved the respondents providing the data. This could have been resolved by complementing subjective evaluations with objective data. Another limitation was the panel sample used to qualify survey outcomes. Clearly, the use of multiple sample sets in various settings could enhance the external validity of our results.
