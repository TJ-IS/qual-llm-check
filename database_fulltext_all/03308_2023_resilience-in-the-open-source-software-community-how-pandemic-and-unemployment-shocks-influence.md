# RESILIENCE IN THE OPEN SOURCE SOFTWARE COMMUNITY: HOW PANDEMIC AND UNEMPLOYMENT SHOCKS INFLUENCE CONTRIBUTIONS TO OTHERS’ AND ONE’S OWN PROJECTS<sup>1</sup>

Onkar S. Malgonde G. Brint Ryan College of Business, University of North Texas Denton, TX, U.S.A. {onkar.malgonde@unt.edu}

Terence J. V. Saldanha Terry College of Business, University of Georgia Athens, GA, U.S.A. {terence.saldanha@uga.edu}

Sunil Mithas

Muma College of Business, University of South Florida Tampa, FL, U.S.A. {smithas@usf.edu}

Contributions by individual open source software (OSS) community members are the lifeblood of the OSS projects that power today’s digital economy and are important for the very survival of such communities. Individual contributions by OSS community members to others’ projects and their own determine whether OSS communities are resilient in the face of major shocks. Arguably, if crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic prompt users to reduce their contributions to others’ projects relative to the contributions to their own projects, such behavior can have implications for the overall resilience of the OSS community. Therefore, whether and how individuals change their contributions in the face of a crisis is an important question. We examine whether members in an OSS community increased or decreased their contributions to others’ projects relative to their own in the face of the COVID-19 pandemic, a sudden and unexpected global health-related shock that has affected almost everyone. We also compare and contrast this behavior when the OSS community faced increasing unemployment, an economic cyclic shock that is arguably and relatively more personal. Drawing on the concept of prosocial behavior and conservation of resources (COR) theory, we hypothesize that the pandemic increased OSS community members’ contributions to others’ projects relative to their own; on the other hand, the threat of rising unemployment decreased OSS community members’ contributions to others’ projects relative to their own. Our empirical analyses of a longitudinal dataset of over 18,000 OSS community members on GitHub, with more than 1.4 million member-day observations, support our hypotheses. This study contributes by uncovering the differential effects of exogenous health-related and economic shocks on the resilience of the OSS community. We conclude with a discussion of our findings’ implications for OSS community resilience.

Keywords: Open source software community, IT knowledge workers, IT professionals, digital resilience, open source software, pandemic, unemployment, exogenous shock

## Introduction

Open source software (OSS) communities play an important role in developing critical software systems that are used by the general population and across industries and have numerous application areas, such as data analytics, operating systems, automation, and cloud computing (Roberts et al., 2006; Towers-Clark, 2019). Many firms rely on OSS software, and OSS projects have played a central role in facilitating the response to many crises and societal challenges (Wang et al., 2020). Considering the critical importance of OSS projects and communities in delivering high-quality and reliable software, it is important to understand how OSS communities stay resilient in the face of exogenous natural and economic shocks through adaptations at the individual level by OSS community members.

Because OSS communities survive and thrive on members’ contributions to others’ projects and their own, they serve as a good context for examining whether and how such communities exhibit resilience to exogenous shocks through changes in contributions by OSS community members to others’ projects and their own. <sup>2</sup> Arguably, exogenous shocks (e.g., a pandemic) can lead OSS community members to focus more on their own projects and can reduce their contributions to others’ projects, potentially affecting the resilience of the OSS community. Thus, how individuals change their contribution behaviors regarding others’ projects versus their own in the face of exogenous shocks or crises is an important question with implications for the resilience of OSS communities.

OSS communities recently faced the COVID-19 pandemic as an exogenous health-related shock (Boh et al., 2023; Rai, 2020). The pandemic also caused other economic effects such as a sharp rise in unemployment (Falk et al., 2021).<sup>3</sup> However, the COVID-19 pandemic, as a health-related exogenous shock that affected individuals’ professional and personal lives, is conceptually different in nature (at least in its origin) from other economic exogenous shocks such as unemployment in that a rise in unemployment threatens the economic and job security of both unemployed and employed individuals (Kummer et al., 2020; Luechinger et al., 2010).

Against this backdrop, our primary objective is to understand whether OSS community members increase or decrease their contributions to others’ projects (relative to their own) in the face of a pandemic. Our secondary objective is to contrast OSS community members’ contribution behaviors in response to a pandemic with their behaviors when faced with rising unemployment. Drawing on the concept of prosocial behavior and conservation of resources (COR) theory (Hobfoll, 1988, 1989), we theorize that there are fundamental differences regarding changes in the contribution behaviors by OSS community members, depending on the type of exogenous shock in the environment. We argue that due to the different nature of the shock of a pandemic versus that of rising unemployment, different motivations play different roles in influencing the contribution behaviors of OSS community members. We hypothesize that facing a pandemic increases OSS community members’ contributions to others’ projects relative to their own, whereas rising unemployment decreases OSS community members’ contributions to others’ projects relative to their own. We found support for our hypotheses by using a unique longitudinal dataset of over 18,000 OSS community members in United States (over 1.4 million workerday observations) in the February 2019-May 2020 time frame, combining data from GitHub (an OSS platform) and other archival datasets. Our findings are robust to alternative econometric specifications and alternative ways of measuring the pandemic and unemployment. Moreover, they are robust at the hourly level and weekly level of aggregation, and robust to various sensitivity analyses.

This study makes two main contributions. First, we contribute to the literature on resilience by studying the resilience of an OSS community in the form of members’ contributions to others’ projects versus contributions to their own projects. Distinguishing between contributions to others’ projects and one’s own complements prior OSS research that has heretofore focused on contributions more broadly and has identified the need to “analyze the nature of [the] contributions” (Kummer et al., 2020, p. 816). Second, we contribute to the discourse on contributions to public goods by identifying the nuanced and opposite effects of different types of exogenous shocks (pandemic and economic) on OSS community resilience in terms of different types of knowledge contributions. Our findings suggest that different exogenous shocks invoke different motivations of IT knowledge workers to contribute to others’ OSS projects versus their own.

Overall, this study contributes to our understanding of the digital resilience of OSS communities, as reflected in the changed contribution behaviors of community members to OSS projects when faced with a pandemic (a health-related exogenous change) and rising unemployment (an economic exogenous change) by highlighting changes in behavior rather than merely documenting a restoration or increase in activity levels (Forsgren, 2020). Our study has implications for the resilience of OSS communities, as well as for project owners, managers, IT knowledge workers, and teams more broadly as they contribute to digital strategies and transformation of their organizations or broader ecosystems. Our findings suggest that managers must identify the incentives or interventions that compensate for the different resources and motivations of OSS community members in response to different types of shocks (e.g., a pandemic or rising unemployment). Our results also provide implications for addressing issues related to employee well-being arising from isolation or reduced social interaction, where contributing more to others’ projects can facilitate social interaction.

## Theory and Hypotheses

## Background and Conceptual Framework

Two streams of literature are relevant to our study: (1) the effects of exogenous shocks on motivations to contribute to public goods and (2) digital resilience in OSS communities. The first stream explores the role of exogenous events or shocks in motivating contributions to digital public goods. For example, Kummer et al. (2020) found that in response to high unemployment during the 2008 economic crisis, monthly consumption of and contributions to Wikipedia increased in German districts and European countries. Zhang et al. (2019) found an increase in Wikipedia contributors after exogenous shocks related to a focal Wikipedia article. Keegan et al. (2013) highlight the role of early contributors to support Wikipedia pages related to breaking news events. As exemplified in these studies, this literature stream has largely focused on the effect of a single exogenous shock on macrolevel (e.g., district, country) or aggregate contributions without contrasting the effects of different types of exogenous shocks. Moreover, these prior studies do not inform how individuals adjust their contributions to others’ projects versus their own ⎯a key focus of our study. Although studies at the district or country level (e.g., Kummer et al., 2020) have provided useful insights regarding the effects of economic shocks on content generation, they can potentially suffer from “ecological fallacy” in that the results from an aggregate unit are attributed to individuals (Robinson, 1950). To the extent that underlying mechanisms invoke arguments at the individual level, our study at the individual level better aligns theoretical arguments with the unit of analysis in the data for a better understanding of contributions in OSS communities while avoiding concerns related to ecological fallacy.

The second literature stream we draw on relates to the notion of digital resilience in the broader literature on resilience in the organization sciences in general (Duchek, 2020), where it focuses on organizational entities (e.g., organizations, ecosystems) and how they relate to their environment, drawing on ecological theories (Lorenz, 2013). The concept of resilience has its origins in the medical sciences and psychology, which typically focus on individuals. We draw on the information systems (IS) literature that emphasizes the role of “people” in understanding the notion of resilience as a “sociotechnical concept encompassing people, information, technology” for developing strategies and processes to protect services (Park et al., 2015, p. 320). Our focus on the responses of OSS community members to exogenous shocks extends current conceptualizations in the resilience literature and contributes to the human aspect of information systems (Heeks & Ospina, 2019).

Although prior studies in these two streams have provided important insights, they have not addressed how OSS community members prioritize contributions to others’ projects versus their own OSS projects when facing exogenous shocks (e.g., pandemic, rising unemployment). Our study addresses this question directly to provide insights at the level of the individual member by focusing on members micro-knowledge contributions to OSS projects. Our primary focus is OSS community resilience in the face of the COVID-19 pandemic, which was a relatively unexpected, once-in-alifetime, sudden, global, inflicted shared pain that affected virtually everyone to some extent. As a comparison, we study OSS community resilience in the face of an unemployment shock, which provides a useful practical and theoretical contrast to the shock from the pandemic to the extent that an unemployment shock is relatively gradual (compared to that of a pandemic), less unexpected, cyclical phenomenon whose impacts are more localized and likely to be perceived as personal. Such differences in the nature of these shocks may drive the motivations and responses of the OSS community to these two shocks.<sup>4</sup>

## Theory Development

## Digital Resilience in the OSS Community

As noted, the resilience of the OSS community is critically dependent on the contributions of members to others’ projects, in contrast to contributions to their own projects. Hence, it is important to operationalize and measure the resilience of the OSS community by considering their contributions to others’ projects versus their own. Such a distinction between the contributions to others’ OSS projects versus one’s own is a key point of departure of our study from prior research, which has not made such a distinction (Jiang et al., 2019; Kummer et al., 2020; Setia et al., 2020), or has focused on overall contributions, such as the number of edits, questions posed to the community, comments provided on issues, and answers (Peng, 2019; Wasko et al., 2009).

We classify a contribution as a contribution to others’ projects if (1) the contribution requires approval by the project owner to take effect, (2) there is interaction between the contributor and the project owner, and (3) the contribution is to an OSS project owned by someone else. Contributions to others’ projects require extensive preparation by the contributing focal member because such contributions are often preceded by wide-ranging actions such as the finalization or testing of changes and the development of documentation discussing the rationale for these changes. During this process, the project owner may request edits to the changes proposed by the focal contributor, resulting in increased time, effort, and interaction between the contributor and the project owner. This mechanism of OSS projects enables geographically distributed IT knowledge workers to contribute to others’ projects while allowing the project owner to maintain the project’s quality and vision. When contributing to others’ projects, the focal contributor’s responsibility is limited to the contribution. In our conceptualization (as well as the context and empirical operationalization), contributions to others’ projects involve interaction with the project owner.<sup>5</sup>

In contrast, OSS community members contribute to their own OSS projects when they are the owner of the project. Apart from the core contributions of code, OSS contributions often include auxiliary tasks such as answering questions about installation or usage, developing tutorials or alternative versions to support broader user groups, and maintaining documentation. IT knowledge workers’ responsibility for their own projects extends beyond the contribution to include the general upkeep of the project. In our conceptualization (as well as the context and operationalization), contributions to one’s own projects do not involve interaction with other IT knowledge workers.

Contributions to others’ projects and one’s own projects are also distinguishable based on project responsibility, which lies with a focal community member (one’s own project) or someone else (others’ project). Incorporating contributions (from oneself or others) into one’s own project requires IT knowledge workers to consider multiple issues (e.g., impact on other modules, coding style, quality of contribution). In the case of contributions to others’ projects, the contributor’s cognitive effort is typically more localized to the contribution and the challenges addressed by the contribution for the focal project. Contributions to others’ projects require taking the perspective of the project owner and investing effort to identify opportunities for contributing to such projects, thereby achieving a sense of community. The study of contributions to others’ projects relative to one’s own is consistent with the extant IS literature, which considers the notion of attention focus, where individuals shift their focus of attention from themselves to others (Lei et al., 2021).

Overall, the distinction between contributions to others’ projects versus one’s own is critical to understanding how OSS community members make resource allocation decisions when faced with a pandemic or other exogenous shocks, given that different types of exogenous shocks may make some resources more salient than others. As discussed earlier, the distinction between one’s own projects and others’ projects is important from a practical perspective because OSS communities thrive on the contributions of their members to other projects; hence, if OSS community members increase their contributions to others’ projects relative to own in the face of exogenous shocks, this would have significant implications for the resilience of the OSS community to shocks.

## Conservation of Resources after Pandemic and Unemployment Shocks

To theorize how OSS community members change their contribution behaviors in the case of a pandemic versus rising unemployment, we use conservation of resources (COR) theory. COR theory explains human motivations in terms of addressing the stressful loss of resources and articulates how humans are motivated to protect their existing resources and acquire new ones (Hobfoll, 1988, 1989). A core concept of COR theory is resources, defined as “anything perceived by the individual to help attain his or her goals” (Halbesleben et al., 2014, p. 1338). Resources are things valued by people such as “time, energies (e.g., physical, mental, and psychological energies), conditions (e.g., marriage, organization tenure), objects, and personal characteristics (e.g., self-esteem)” (Chen & Karahanna, 2018, p. 1026). COR theory suggests that the actual or potential loss of resources (more salient than resource gain) increases an individual’s strain and stress. In response, individuals act to avoid resource loss and/or invest resources to protect against resource loss, recover from losses, and gain resources (Hobfoll, 2001).

Participation in OSS projects is an avenue for resource investment and acquisition for OSS community members because it requires community members to invest cognitive resources of time and effort. In doing so, OSS community members are often required to interact (e.g., read or synthesize others’ thoughts, respond to questions/comments) with other members. Resources invested to participate in OSS projects, in turn, enhance skill development, reputation, and social interaction (Hann et al., 2013; Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003).

COR is an apt theoretical lens for our study because a pandemic and rising unemployment are exogenous changes that result in OSS community members experiencing the loss of different types of resources. First, let us consider the loss of resources caused by a pandemic. With the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, many organizations transitioned to alternative work arrangements, such as work-from-home policies, limited access to office space, and the alternative use of office space. Consequently, many OSS community members used some form of remote working, where physical co-location with colleagues was not possible. Although remote work has advantages such as reduced commute times and increased flexibility (Facilities, 2020; Singer-Velush et al., 2020), remote work introduces challenges such as reduced social interaction (Ford et al., 2020), disconnection from colleagues (Spataro, 2020), isolation, and the loss of community (Talboy, 2020)⎯cognitive resources of OSS community members. In addition to the work-related challenges of the pandemic, OSS community members potentially faced personal challenges such as the hospitalization of family members, increased demands for childcare and elder care, and restrictions on mobility. Such challenges can introduce technology-mediated interruptions, resulting in conflicts between work and personal lives (Chen & Karahanna, 2014, 2018).

Second, and in contrast, rising unemployment in the individual’s environment results in economic uncertainty related to one’s employment, which contributes to stress for individuals (Linn et al., 1985). The economic threat of rising unemployment can have long-term negative effects on individuals, their families (Westman et al., 2004), and the community (Gassman-Pines, 2020). The negative effects of rising unemployment impact both unemployed and employed individuals (Kummer et al., 2020; Luechinger et al., 2010). For IT knowledge workers, the stress related to existing employment and job searching is well known (Mantler et al., 2005). Thus, rising unemployment in the environment poses threats to the economic security and job security of IT knowledge workers.

As noted earlier, although economic shocks such as rising unemployment can also be caused by other exogenous shocks such as a pandemic, at least conceptually, these shocks are different in nature. Such conceptual distinctions are important in developing a theory of how OSS community members cope differently with various shocks. We argue that OSS community members experience different types of resource loss due to a pandemic versus rising unemployment. Whereas a pandemic leads primarily to the loss of interaction and community, rising unemployment threatens economic security. Because COR theory explains individuals’ motivations to protect against the actual or potential loss of their resources and to acquire additional resources (Hobfoll, 1988, 1989), it helps us theorize OSS community members’ preference to contribute to others’ projects versus their own projects in response to resource loss due to the exogenous shock of a pandemic versus the exogenous shock of rising unemployment.

## Hypotheses

We discuss how pandemic and unemployment shocks influence OSS community resilience through differential impacts on OSS members’ contributions to others’ projects versus their own.

## Influence of Pandemic on Contributions to OSS Projects

We theorize that a pandemic influences community resilience by increasing OSS community members’ contributions to others’ projects relative to their own for two reasons (See Appendix Table A2 for a summary of the key underlying mechanisms). First, psychology research suggests that prosocial behavior (defined as “a broad category of acts that are defined by some significant segment of society and/or one’s social group as generally beneficial to other people” [Penner et al., 2005, p. 366]) helps individuals buffer the impact of death and cope with it (Greenberg et al., 1997) because individuals perceive the lasting impact of their contributions to others (Peterson & Stewart, 1996). Prosocial behavior can positively impact the well-being and general happiness of individuals (Binder & Freytag, 2013). Contributions to OSS projects are a manifestation of prosocial behavior because engagement in OSS projects is a socially useful activity (Kummer et al., 2020). We argue that prosocial behavior among OSS community members is more salient and more strongly directed toward others during a pandemic because health-related crises invoke prosocial behavior to act in the interest of others (Serrano-Montilla et al., 2021; Syropoulos & Markowitz, 2021). Thus, salient prosocial behavior, focused on others during the pandemic, induces OSS community members to contribute more to others’ projects relative to their own.<sup>6</sup>

Second, due to norms or mandates for social distancing, a pandemic presents challenges, including reductions in social activities and the loss of interaction and social support that would otherwise be available (Halbesleben, 2006). Anecdotal evidence suggests that the lack of interaction was of significant concern to knowledge workers during the COVID-19 pandemic (Zaidi & Ali, 2020). Consistent with COR theory, in such cases, OSS community members would be expected to identify avenues to address this loss of interaction and community support. Because contributions to OSS projects are discretionary (Bitzer & Geishecker, 2010), contributing to OSS projects is one avenue for OSS community members to compensate for the loss of interaction and social support in other areas (Jiang et al., 2017).<sup>7</sup> As discussed earlier, contributions to others’ OSS projects require interaction and community acceptance (from project owners), thereby offering interaction opportunities for OSS community members. In contrast, contributions to one’s own OSS projects do not require interaction with others (because they only involve the focal worker) or the intervention of others for the contributions to take effect, given that the focal worker can set their own criteria to incorporate changes into their own OSS projects. Thus, contributions to others’ projects are more effective than contributions to one’s own projects in terms of helping OSS community members recover the lost resources of interaction and community acceptance during a pandemic.<sup>8</sup>

In sum, the pandemic’s nature as a health-related threat to general well-being (1) amplifies prosocial behavior directed toward others and (2) encourages interaction with others to overcome the lost resources of interaction and community acceptance. These factors induce OSS community members to contribute more to others’ projects than to their own. Hence, we posit:

H1: The shock of a pandemic influences the resilience of OSS communities in such a way that the pandemic shock increases OSS community members’ contributions to others’ projects relative to their own.

## Influence of Rising Unemployment Contributions to OSS Projects

We theorize that rising unemployment decreases OSS community members’ contributions to others’ projects relative to their own (See Appendix Table A2 for a summary of the key underlying mechanisms). Consistent with COR theory, unemployment threats lead to the actual or potential loss of resources such as economic security (Selenko et al., 2013). To recover lost resources or to protect against the loss of resources such as job security and economic security, IT knowledge workers can employ career-focused maneuvers such as participation in OSS projects, which may better position them in uncertain labor markets (Hann et al., 2013). Such actions represent resource investments that can directly benefit IT knowledge workers’ careers and improve their online reputations, which can then be exploited offline (Bitzer & Geishecker, 2010; Huang & Zhang, 2016; von Krogh et al., 2012; Xu et al., 2020).

We argue that in the face of an unemployment threat, contributions to one’s own projects provide a way for OSS community members to acquire resources that enable them to signal their labor market skills to a greater degree than contributions to others’ projects (Hann et al., 2013) for three reasons. First, compared to contributions to others’ projects, contributions to one’s own projects are more effective in helping OSS community members showcase their own abilities publicly and develop their reputation on the OSS platform (Fang & Neufeld, 2009; Roberts et al., 2006), which can be used as a signaling mechanism (Spence, 1973) if they, for example, include links on their profile page.<sup>9</sup> Profile pages on OSS platforms serve as aggregate signals of contributions on the platform and indicate the individual’s status. For example, when OSS community members contribute to their own projects, other platform users may subscribe to updates, bookmark projects for future reference, or follow members for updates on their activity. Second, contributions to one’s own projects are more directly visible and more easily attributable to the focal OSS community member by potential recruiters than contributions to others’ projects. Anecdotal evidence also supports this argument. For instance, a recruiter notes: “It’s an excellent opportunity to see what they are passionate about, their coding style— good or bad—and fun side projects. We love when developers see a need and just go ahead and code a solution to share with the community. We are looking for some amazing problem solvers on our team. This is hard to get from an interview or resume. But sometimes, we see someone’s GitHub library and think, ‘Wow, that is really cool and handy’” (Terdiman, 2012). In comparison, contributions to others’ projects are more indirect, involve more one-to-one contributions, are less easily discernible publicly, and are less easily attributable to a focal OSS community member.

Third, the economic frameworks of career concerns and signaling incentives (Holmström, 1999; Lerner & Tirole, 2002) highlight the importance of leadership positions in OSS projects that render “substantial status and careerconcern benefits of being a leader” (Lerner & Tirole, 2002, p. 214). As the owner of the OSS project, the focal contributor plays a central role in the evolution of their own project. Contributions to projects that improve the reputation of the contributor also serve as a signal to the labor market (Xu et al., 2020). In comparison, contributions to others’ projects, while beneficial to the contributor (e.g., more interaction opportunities), have a limited impact on the contributor’s reputation signals on the platform due to the difficulty of (1) analyzing the quality of such contributions since multiple contributors may be participating and (2) quantifying contributions to others’ projects that can be attributed to the focal contributor’s status. Thus, the effort contributed and the responsibility for the overall success of a project (contributions to one’s own projects) have a higher signaling effect, compared to specific contributions to others’ projects (Lerner & Tirole, 2002).<sup>10</sup>

Although unemployment threats can induce prosocial behavior in terms of overall contributions to digital public goods (Kummer et al., 2020), the effect of prosocial behavior in increasing contributions to others’ projects is less salient due to the economic insecurity faced by IT knowledge workers due to the unemployment threat. This is because prosocial behavior, by definition, implies a focus on what is “generally beneficial to other people” (Penner et al., 2005, p. 366) rather than to oneself. In the face of an unemployment threat, IT knowledge workers’ prosocial behavior toward others is dominated by their motivation to showcase their own skills by contributing to their own projects (as previously discussed).<sup>11</sup> Hence, faced with an unemployment threat, OSS community members would be expected to decrease their contributions to others’ projects relative to own due to the less salient effect of prosocial behavior toward others.

In sum, the nature of rising unemployment as an economic threat (1) encourages a focus on one’s own reputation to overcome potential or lost economic security and (2) reduces the salience of prosocial behavior directed toward others. These factors are likely to induce OSS community members to contribute less to others’ projects than to their own. Hence, we posit:

H2: The shock of rising unemployment influences the resilience of OSS communities in such a way that it decreases OSS community members’ contributions to others’ projects relative to their own.

## Method

## Data

We collected data from multiple sources to create a panel dataset for daily and weekly contributions to others’ projects and one’s own projects from GitHub involving a randomly selected set of users (i.e., OSS community members) during the period from February 1, 2019 to May 31, 2020. First, we collected objective secondary data from GitHub’s public application programming interface, which provides data on contributions to others’ projects and one’s own projects across public repositories and community members. To make the data collection process tractable, we collected the location information of users from GitHub for 654,512 randomly chosen contributors from our panel dataset. To avoid confounds due to cross-country differences and for tractability of the results with data on the pandemic and unemployment threats, we limited our dataset to contributors from the United States. Appendix Figure A1 details our approach to the data collection and random sample selection. We identified the location (state) of the contributors from the location information that users provided in their profile, and we dropped users for whom location data were not available. Second, we collected the monthly state-level unemployment rate from the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

Third, we collected data on the control variables from other data sources or imputed them from existing sources. We used machine learning algorithms to derive gender and ethnicity information following prior literature (Robles et al., 2014; Vasilescu et al., 2015). For gender, we used three Python packages: gender-guesser (a generic gender prediction engine), guess-Indian-gender (specific to users of Indian descent that are not covered in the gender-guesser), and namesex (specific to users of Chinese descent with Hanji script). For ethnicity, we used the 2010 U.S. census model from the Python programming package—Ethnicolr—to predict ethnicity (White, Black, Asian, Hispanic, and Native American) based on the name of the user. The accuracy of the algorithms is as follows: 81% for ethnicity using the Ethnicolr package (Sood & Laohaprapanon, 2018), 90% for Hanjin-script names using the Namesex package (Lu et al., 2019), and 88% for Indian-origin names using Gender Guess Indian Names (Vijayakumar, 2019). Similar approaches and algorithms to identify ethnicity have been used in prior studies (e.g., Kerr & Lincoln, 2010).<sup>12</sup>

Consistent with prior research, we excluded OSS community members who did not make any contributions to others’ projects or their own projects throughout the entire time frame of the study, as such users are likely to be inactive users (Moqri et al., 2018). Because we are interested in the contributions to others’ projects relative to the contributions to one’s own projects, we also excluded users who contributed only to others’ projects or only to their own projects during the study’s time frame. This prevented any potential bias arising from a worker’s inherent nature to contribute only to others’ projects or only to their own projects. The findings are substantively unchanged whether or not we excluded users with no contributions to their own or others’ projects.

Our final sample is a daily and weekly panel dataset for 18,869 OSS community members across all 50 states in the U.S. from February 1, 2019 to May 31, 2020, comprising over 1.4 million member-day (524,411 member-week) observations of member’s contributions to others’ projects or their own. Our sample’s composition in terms of gender and ethnicity is broadly representative of the user population on GitHub and is comparable with prior studies. For example, 88.57% of the users in our sample are male, and 24.73% of the users belong to minority ethnic groups (Blacks, Asians, Hispanics, or Native Americans). The gender proportion is similar to the roughly 10% female composition reported in prior studies on GitHub and surveys of OSS users (Bosu & Sultana, 2019; Robles et al., 2014; Zlotnick, 2017). The proportion of minority ethnicity users in our sample is also reasonably comparable with Zlotnick’s (2017) study on GitHub, which reported 14.63% of users from minority ethnic groups.

## Variables

The dependent variable (RelatCont) measures the coderelated contributions of OSS community members to others’ projects relative to their own projects.<sup>13</sup> Contributions to others’ projects is measured as the number of contributions initiated by the focal contributor to projects owned by others. The OSS project’s owner may accept, reject, or propose changes to the focal user’s contribution request. Contributions to own projects is measured as the number of commits (code-related changes) by a focal contributor to OSS projects owned by the focal IT knowledge worker.

RelatCont is computed as the ratio of an OSS community member’s contributions to others’ projects to the contributions to their own projects.<sup>14</sup> Consistent with prior studies, we added 1 and used the logarithmic transformation to reduce skewness and to avoid the logarithm of zero values (Xu et al., 2020) (The results are unchanged even without the logarithmic transformation). Thus, RelatCont is measured as:

$$
\text { RelatCont } = \log (\frac {1 + \text { Contributions   to   Others'   projects }}{1 + \text { Contributions   to   One's   Own   projects }})
$$

Intuitively, the higher the value of RelatCont, the higher the member’s contributions to others’ projects are, relative to the contributions to their own projects. This measure of RelatCont can be equivalently viewed as the difference between the (log-transformed) number of contributions by the OSS community member to others’ projects and the (logtransformed) number of contributions to their own projects. Although we conducted our main analysis at the daily level, for robustness, we repeated the analysis using RelatCont measured at the weekly level by aggregating daily contributions to the weekly level. The results from the weekly analyses are similar to the results from the daily analysis, thereby adding robustness to our findings. We also conducted an analysis at the hourly level and obtained substantively unchanged results (omitted for brevity).

Pandemic is a binary variable, defined as 1 for the days after March 11, 2020 (which is when the World Health Organization declared the global COVID-19 pandemic; prior studies have also used this date as the start of the pandemic— e.g., Song et al., 2021), and 0 otherwise. For robustness (discussed later), we repeated the analysis by considering the pandemic as starting on different days in each state based on the COVID-19 case severity in each state, i.e., based on when the cumulative number of COVID-19 cases in the state exceeded 500. Unemployment was measured as the unemployment rate (computed as a percentage) in the state (location) of the IT knowledge worker. For robustness, we repeated the analysis using Unemployment, measured as a shock, i.e., 1 for days after April 1, 2020 (which is when all U.S. states experienced a substantial increase in the unemployment rate), and 0 otherwise.

We included several control variables. We used Account age to control for the length of time that the IT knowledge worker had an account on GitHub. Since a worker’s contributions may be influenced by the number of other workers following them and by the number of workers they follow (Moqri et al., 2018), we controlled for the number of followers (Followers) and the number of other workers that the focal worker is following (Following). We controlled for gender (Female) because women and men may differ in their contribution patterns (Prana et al., 2022). We controlled for the Race of the focal worker, measured using four binary variables (Black, Asian, Hispanic, and Native American; White is the omitted category) because research suggests that different races were differently affected by the pandemic (Kochhar, 2020). Since contributions may differ during weekends (Forsgren, 2020), we included a dummy variable indicating whether the day was on a weekend (Weekend). We controlled for whether the focal worker was affiliated with a company (Companyflag). To control for the effect of the political leaning of the worker’s location, we controlled for the state’s political leaning (RedState). Finally, because more populated areas may witness more contributions, we controlled for the population of the state where the IT worker resides (Population).

Table 1 shows the measurement of the variables, and Table 2 shows the descriptive statistics and correlations. The mean of Contributions to one’s own projects is approximately 10 times higher than the mean of Contributions to others’ projects, underscoring the relative challenges of contributions to others’ projects, where individual IT knowledge workers must spend more effort and time than contributions to their own projects. This highlights the complicated nature of OSS projects, where contributions to others’ projects cannot be made in passing, unlike in online collaboration communities such as Wikipedia (e.g., deleting text or fixing grammatical mistakes), where individuals can draw on their existing knowledge. A total of 45.88% of the OSS community members were affiliated with an organization, which is consistent with the notion that OSS projects are growing in popularity in organizations, as they facilitate the exploration of alternative business models that utilize diverse skill sets from the OSS community (Ågerfalk & Fitzgerald, 2008).

## Empirical Models and Econometric Considerations

Due to the longitudinal nature of our dataset, we estimated panel regression models. We used the following panel model specification to test the hypotheses:

$$
\text { RelCont } _ {i t} = \text { f } (\text { Pandemic } _ {i t}, \text { Unemployment } _ {i t}, \text { control   variables }) \tag {1}
$$

To effectively isolate the effects of the unemployment threat and to overcome issues due to the potential effects of the pandemic on the unemployment threat, we tested the unemployment hypothesis (H2) using data for the time frame before the onset of the pandemic. This ensured that there was no confound of the pandemic in the analysis for testing the hypothesis related to unemployment. Thus, to test H2, we estimated the following model using data prior to the onset of the pandemic:

$$
\text { RelCont } _ {i t} = \text { f } (\text { Unemployment } _ {i t}, \text { control   variables })\tag{2}
$$

We also tested our unemployment hypothesis using the data subsequent to the onset of the pandemic, as shown in Equation (1) above. The results for our hypotheses remain unchanged.

We estimated Hausman-Taylor instrumental variable models, which allowed us to specify and estimate endogenous variables without the strong assumption (of random-effects models) that all the variables are uncorrelated with the unobserved individual-level effects (Hausman & Taylor, 1981). In our models, we specified Following, Followers, Account age, and Companyflag as endogenous variables because they can be affected by the idiosyncrasies of the focal IT knowledge worker. For example, a worker’s unobserved characteristics may influence the number of followers they have. We specified the other independent variables (e.g., Pandemic, Unemployment, Female, Population, and ethnicity variables) as exogenous because they are largely beyond the control of a focal worker.

An advantage of the Hausman-Taylor model is that external instruments are not needed but are instead constructed from within the model, based partly on variables that are considered exogenous or endogenous to (i.e., uncorrelated with) the unobserved individual-level effects (Hausman & Taylor, 1981). As discussed in Greene (2003), Hausman and Taylor (1981) show that deviations from the group means of timevarying variables that are considered endogenous to unobserved individual effects are valid instruments. Similarly, Hausman and Taylor (1981) showed that the group means of time-varying variables (e.g., Unemployment, Population in our study) that are uncorrelated with individual-level effects are valid instruments. Finally, variables that are time-invariant and uncorrelated with individual-level effects (e.g., Female and ethnicity variables) serve as their own instruments (Greene, 2003). Following recommendations from prior research, we used Hausman tests and Sargan tests to assess model consistency and instrument validity (Greene, 2003; Hausman & Taylor, 1981; Sargan, 1958). Hausman-Taylor models are also more efficient than fixed-effects models (Hausman & Taylor, 1981). In all the models, we clustered standard errors at the worker level. For robustness, we tested our hypotheses using fixed-effects models and obtained substantively unchanged results.

We also conducted difference-in-differences (DID) type analyses by leveraging geographical differences in an unemployment shock across U.S. states.<sup>15</sup> The underlying logic for this analysis is that whereas the health crisis aspect of the pandemic influenced IT knowledge workers in every state in the U.S., the IT sector is heterogeneously distributed across the U.S. Therefore, in states with many IT employers, the risk of unemployment may not be as high as expected for IT knowledge workers because it may be easier for them to find a new job in the same region. In contrast, in states with few IT employers, the influence of an unemployment shock may be higher for IT knowledge workers, especially those with geographical constraints. Hence, in our DID analysis, we considered the “treatment group” as comprising IT knowledge workers in states in the lowest quartile of IT business establishments per capita. We considered IT knowledge workers in other states as the “control group.” Based on our theoretical arguments, IT knowledge workers in the states in the treatment group would be more prone to exhibit the effects of rising unemployment following the onset of the pandemic. Thus, we created a binary variable, HighUnempRisk, defined as 1 for states in the lowest quartile (based on the number of IT establishments per capita in the state), and 0 for other states. We obtained data on the number of technology establishments in each state from the Cyberstates 2020 report produced by

The Computing Technology Industry Association (CompTIA, 2020). We estimated the following model:

RelCont<sub>it</sub> = f(Pandemic<sub>it</sub>, HighUnempRisk<sub>i</sub>, Pandemic<sub>it,</sub> × HighUnempRisk<sub>i</sub>, Unemployment<sub>it,</sub> control variables) (3)

A negative and significant coefficient on the Pandemic<sub>it</sub> × HighUnempRisk<sub>i</sub> interaction term would be consistent with H2, which posits that when faced with unemployment shocks, OSS community members reduce their contributions to others’ projects relative to their own projects.

For robustness, we repeated the DID analysis in multiple ways to assess the sensitivity of the findings in terms of how the treatment and control groups were defined. First, we considered the treatment group to be the lowest quartile and the control group to be the highest quartile in terms of the number of IT establishments per capita, thereby creating more separation between the treatment and control groups. Second, we repeated the analysis by using quintiles instead of quartiles. Third, similar to prior research (Kummer et al., 2020), we repeated the analysis by defining the treatment group as IT knowledge workers in the states in the top quartile in terms of an increase in the state’s average post-pandemic unemployment rate (i.e., after March 11, 2020), compared to the state’s average pre-pandemic unemployment rate (i.e., before March 11, 2020).

## Main Results

Table 3 shows the results. Columns 1-3 show the daily results, and Columns 4-6 show the weekly results. For brevity, we interpret only the daily results, although the weekly results are qualitatively similar. In Column 3, the coefficient of Pandemic is positive and significant $( \beta = 0 . 1 1 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , suggesting that the pandemic increased OSS community members’ contributions to others’ projects relative to their own projects. Hence, H1 is supported.

To test the unemployment hypothesis (H2), we interpret Column 1, which provides estimates using data from the timeframe before the onset of the pandemic. In Column 1, the coefficient of Unemployment is negative and significant $( \beta = - 0 . 0 6 1 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , suggesting that rising unemployment decreased the contributions of OSS community members to others’ projects relative to their own. The coefficient of Unemployment is also negative and significant in the model using data from both before and after the onset of the pandemic (Column 3). Thus, H2 is supported. Results using weekly data (Columns 4-6 of Table 3) are substantively unchanged using data from before the onset of the pandemic (Column 4), as well as data from before and after the onset of the pandemic (Column 6).

In all the models, the Hausman tests yielded nonsignificant test statistics (p > 0.10), implying model consistency and instrument validity (Greene, 2003; Hausman & Taylor, 1981). In all the models, the Sargan tests yielded nonsignificant statistics (p > 0.10), suggesting instrument validity and model appropriateness (Sargan, 1958). The mean and maximum variance inflation factors are 1.39 and 2.48, respectively, which are below the suggested limits, implying that multicollinearity is not a major concern.

We computed the economic significance of the impacts of the pandemic and rising unemployment. Based on the daily estimates, after the pandemic’s onset, the contributions by OSS community members to others’ projects relative to their own increased by 11.2%. A 1% increase in the unemployment rate results in a 6.1% decrease in daily contributions by OSS community members to others’ projects relative to their own. As expected, the effect sizes are even higher in magnitude when the daily contributions are aggregated to the weekly level. Based on the weekly estimates, after the onset of the pandemic, the contributions by OSS community members to others’ projects relative to their own increased by 20.1%. A 1% increase in the unemployment rate resulted in a 13% decrease in weekly contributions by OSS community members to others’ projects relative to their own. These estimates suggest that the effects of the pandemic and rising unemployment are economically substantive.

The signs and significances of the control variables are largely in the expected directions. For example, in many models, the coefficients of Female, Followers, Following, Account age, and Population are positive and significant. This suggests that OSS community members who are female and who have a greater number of followers, follow more people, have been on the GitHub platform for a longer time, and are in states with a higher population tend to contribute more to others’ projects than to their own. Some of these signs of the control variables are either obvious or as expected, based on prior literature. For example, OSS community members with more followers or those following more people may be more adept at identifying opportunities to contribute to others’ projects (Blincoe et al., 2016). Likewise, because women are often considered more empathetic than men (Eagly, 2009), women may be more likely to display prosocial behavior in terms of contributing to others’ projects.<sup>16</sup>

## Robustness Tests

We conducted several robustness tests to assess the stability of our main results and to enhance confidence in causal attribution (Mithas et al., 2022a, 2022b). First, we estimate fixed-effects specifications. The results (see Appendix Table A3) are substantively unchanged, suggesting that our findings are robust to alternative model specifications. Second, because our data cover the time period up to May 2020, a concern may be whether there is sufficient variation in the Pandemic variable. Although over 2.5 months of pandemic data out of a total of 16 months in our data provide sufficient variation, we repeated the analysis by restricting the pre-pandemic data to be the same length of time as the post-pandemic data. This ensured that our sample had the same amount of time and a similar number of observations before and after the onset of the pandemic. This is consistent with prior research that uses the same time period before and after an economic shock to examine the influence of the economic shock on contributions to Wikipedia at the monthly level (Kummer et al., 2020). The results are substantively unchanged (Columns 1 and 2 in Appendix

Table A4), mitigating the concern of insufficient variation in the Pandemic variable. For robustness, we repeat the analyses using a time period of four weeks before and after the pandemic. The results (Columns 3 and 4 in Appendix Table A4) are retained.

Third, as discussed earlier, we performed a DID analysis to tease out the effect of rising unemployment from that of the pandemic. We consider the treatment group as OSS community members in the states in the lowest quartile based on the number of IT establishments per capita (HighUnempRisk). Intuitively, IT knowledge workers in this treatment group face the highest risk of unemployment. Table 4 (Columns 1-2) reports the DID results. In the daily model, the coefficient of Pandemic × HighUnempRisk is negative and significant (Column 1, β = -0.084, p < 0.01), suggesting that compared to OSS community members in other states, OSS community members in states with high unemployment risk made fewer contributions to others’ projects relative to their own after the onset of the pandemic. This is consistent with H2. Consistent with H1, the net effect of Pandemic for OSS community members in states with a high unemployment risk is positive (0.192 – 0.084 = 0.108). Thus, the DID analysis helps tease out the effect of the unemployment threat. The results from the weekly analysis (Column 2 of Table 4) are consistent with the daily analysis.

To examine the parallel trends assumption of the DID analysis, we assess whether the trend in the treatment group matched that in the control group prior to the onset of the pandemic. Following prior research adopting a similar approach (Kumar & Telang, 2012), we compared the trends in the dependent variable (RelCont) for the treatment and control groups over multiple pre-pandemic periods. We split the pre-pandemic period into two periods and then created a binary variable (Post), which is 1 for the second period, and 0 for the first period. A significant Post × HighUnempRisk coefficient would indicate a differential trend in the dependent variable across the treatment and control groups. In our estimations at the daily and weekly levels (omitted for brevity), the Post × HighUnempRisk coefficient is nonsignificant (p > 0.10), indicating that the parallel trends assumption holds in our data (Kumar & Telang, 2012). For splitting the pre-treatment period, we chose the cutoff at the halfway point of the pre-pandemic period. For sensitivity, we experimented with other cutoffs and found substantively unchanged results. This finding suggests that the treatment and control groups had similar trends in the relative contributions to others’ projects and their own in the pre-pandemic period, thereby satisfying the condition of parallel trends. Appendix Figure A2 plots the dependent variable (RelCont) across time for knowledge workers in the “treatment” group (states with a high unemployment risk) and the “control” group (other states). The figure shows that prior to the onset of the pandemic, both groups exhibited a similar time trend in the dependent variable, consistent with the parallel trends assumption. We further assessed whether there is unobserved heterogeneity in the time trends between the treatment and control groups by testing whether these groups had different time trends prior to the pandemic. To assess the differences in such trends, we followed prior research (Bertrand et al., 2004; Foerderer et al., 2018) and estimated models where we interacted a continuous time indicator (TimeTrend) with the treatment indicator HighUnempRisk for the pre-pandemic period. Appendix Table A5 shows estimations of the pre-pandemic time trends for the dependent variable. The coefficient of HighUnempRisk × TimeTrend is nonsignificant (p > 0.10), suggesting that the time trend is identical for the treatment and control groups. The estimates support the parallel trends assumption and reinforce the claim that our empirical strategy effectively controlled for ex ante heterogeneity in the treatment and control groups.

To address a concern that our DID results may be sensitive to how the treatment and control groups were defined, we repeated this analysis using alternative approaches to define the treatment and control groups: (1) the treatment group as the lowest quartile and the control group as the highest quartile in terms of the number of technology establishments per capita in the state, and (2) using quintiles instead of quartiles. These additional analyses yielded similar results as those in Table 4 (Columns 1-2) and are omitted for brevity. As earlier discussed, we repeated the analyses by using the change in the average state unemployment rate post-pandemic, compared to the pre-pandemic rate, to define the treatment and control groups. Columns 3-4 of Table 4 show that the results are substantively similar to those in Columns 1-2 of Table 4.<sup>17</sup>

Fourth, although the DID analysis teases out the effect of rising unemployment from the pandemic, we conducted another robustness test to address the concern that the effects of the pandemic and rising unemployment may be intertwined. Drawing on the argument that if OSS community members were able to maintain their jobs before and after the onset of the pandemic, they may not have been affected by the unemployment threat as much, we teased out the effect of the unemployment threat by focusing on OSS community members who were associated with a company (i.e., Companyflag = 1) in the pre-pandemic and postpandemic time periods. The behavior of these members was likely influenced mostly by the health crisis and only moderately influenced by the economic (or unemployment)

aspect of the pandemic. The results (Columns 5-6 in Appendix Table A4) are substantively unchanged.<sup>18</sup>

Fifth, as discussed earlier, we repeated the analysis by considering the pandemic as starting on different days in each state, based on the case severity in each state, i.e., when the cumulative number of COVID-19 cases in the state exceeded 500. The results (Appendix Table A7) are similar to the main results, mitigating the concern that the pandemic started on different days for different workers based on their location.

Sixth, to assess the sensitivity of our findings to alternative operationalizations of the Unemployment variable, we conduct a robustness test where we considered the threat of unemployment to be a shock that occurred on April 1, 2020, because all U.S. states experienced a substantial increase in unemployment at that time. This analysis measures Unemployment as a binary variable that is 1 subsequent to April 1, 2020, and 0 otherwise. In the daily and weekly models (Columns 7-8 of Table 3), the coefficient of Unemployment is negative and significant, and the coefficient of Pandemic is positive and significant, consistent with H2 and H1, respectively.

Finally, we conducted robustness analyses to rule out alternative explanations of our findings: (1) We conducted a robustness analysis by considering contributions to projects where the IT knowledge worker was a member as contributions to others’ projects. The basis for this argument is that contributions as a member were ultimately credited to a project owned by another OSS community member. The results (Table 6) are substantively unchanged from the main results, suggesting that an alternative operationalization of contributions to others’ projects does not change the results.<sup>19</sup> (2) To assuage concerns that the findings may be due to changes in the nature of the projects after the onset of the pandemic, we reconducted the analysis by restricting the sample to projects started before the onset of the pandemic only. The results (Appendix Table A8) are similar to the main results. We also conducted an analysis restricting the sample to projects unrelated to the pandemic (based on a keyword search of the project’s description).<sup>20</sup> The results, shown in Appendix Table A9, are substantively unchanged, further mitigating the concern that changes in the nature of projects post-pandemic could explain our findings. Moreover, the proportion of projects related to the pandemic was minuscule (only 0.45%), further alleviating this concern.

## Exploratory Analyses to Test Underlying Mechanisms

We conducted exploratory analyses to strengthen our confidence in the theoretical arguments for our hypotheses, which drew on two key mechanisms: the prosocial behavior of OSS community members and the need for interaction. First, to test the prosocial behavior mechanism, we classified projects as community-focused or non-community-focused projects.<sup>21</sup> To classify a project as community-focused, we used a proxy measured as the number of community members who bookmarked the focal project on the GitHub platform (for all the projects in our sample, we classified projects that were at or above the 75th percentile of bookmarks received as community focused). This measure is appropriate because bookmarking a project not only allows a community member to have easy access to the project but also provides all updates related to the project. Using our bookmarking proxy, we could identify projects that had greater appeal to users and had high popularity on the platform. Thus, using bookmarks as a proxy to identify community-focused projects is justified because OSS projects that garner bookmarks from users of the platform signal interest from others regarding the project’s topic and signal the popularity of the project in media and other communities (Jiang et al., 2017). For this analysis, our dependent variable was the ratio of community-focused to non-community-focused contributions. The results (Table 7) suggest that contributions to community-focused projects relative to contributions to noncommunity-focused projects increased after the onset of the pandemic. In contrast, contributions to community-focused projects relative to non-community-focused projects decreased after the unemployment shock. This result is consistent with our argument that OSS community members’ prosocial behavior increases in response to a health-related shock but decreases after an economic shock.<sup>22</sup>

Second, we conducted three exploratory analyses to test the need for interaction as a theoretical mechanism for the pandemic hypothesis (H1). Our first analysis tests the effects of the pandemic on two types of non-code contributions. The first type of non-code contribution is comments made by workers on issues logged in the system. Such posted comments represent interactions with other workers in relation to a focal issue. In contrast, the second type of noncode contribution that does not require interaction is changes to the project description or “read me” files. The results (Table 8) show that the pandemic increased relative contributions to others’ projects for contributions involving interaction (comments on an issue), whereas it had no significant effect on contributions involving no interaction (changes to the project description). This is consistent with the argument that interaction is a mechanism by which the pandemic induced more contributions to others’ projects.<sup>23</sup>

Our second analysis restricts the sample to knowledge workers whose own projects did not involve any contributions by others. For such knowledge workers, the argument that they contributed to others’ projects due to increased interaction opportunities is likely to be stronger, given that such workers do not interact with others when contributing to their own projects. The results, shown in Table 9, are substantively unchanged. Finally, we conducted an analysis by specifically considering contributions to one’s own projects, where the knowledge worker’s contributions were the only contributions (i.e., for the worker’s own projects, the worker was working alone). The results, shown in Appendix Table A11, are qualitatively unchanged. Both these sets of findings further support our argument that interaction is a theoretical mechanism that explains why OSS community members contributed more to others’ projects relative to their own projects during the pandemic. ## Discussion

## Findings

Our goal in this study was to assess OSS community resilience in the face of a sudden and unexpected global pandemic shock that affected almost everyone in some way and provide a comparison with another relatively more gradual, cyclical, expected shock that may have had greater personal salience for some. We leveraged a longitudinal dataset of over 18,000 OSS community members on GitHub with more than 1.4 million member-day observations to assess OSS community resilience, as exhibited in individual contributions by OSS community members to others’ projects and their own projects. Our main finding is that after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, OSS community members increased their contributions to others’ projects relative to their own. In contrast, in response to unemployment, OSS community members decrease their contributions to others’ projects relative to their own.

Our study speaks to the theme of digital resilience in general and OSS community resilience in particular in several important ways. First, we broaden the discourse on digital resilience by considering the micro-behavioral responses of OSS community members in response to shocks that ultimately affected the resilience of the OSS community. Considering that the resilience literature in the organization sciences in general (Duchek, 2020), and in information systems in particular, is at a nascent stage, this contribution regarding the responses of “people” broadens the discourse on digital resilience beyond focusing only on organizational entities (e.g., organizations, ecosystems) and how they relate to their environment (Lorenz, 2013), or on the effects of digital artifacts alone. Although some ideas related to resilience in the recent literature largely draw on ecological theories and focus on broader entities, the concept of resilience, with its origins in the medical sciences and psychology (Lorenz, 2013), typically focuses on individuals. Indeed, some researchers consider a narrow focus on ecosystems or larger social entities for resilience as too limiting (Sakurai & Chughtai, 2020) and call for the consideration of resilience at multiple levels, including the individual level (Duchek, 2020). Our work here in understanding community resilience in terms of the micro-actions and adaptations of individuals is consistent with prior information systems literature that emphasizes the role of “people” in understanding the notion of resilience as a “sociotechnical concept encompassing people, information, technology…” for developing strategies and processes to protect services (Park et al., 2015, p. 320).

Second, by shedding the spotlight on how OSS community members allocated their time and energy in response to the pandemic and unemployment shocks, our study creates opportunities for understanding how individuals’ micro-actions eventually reflect in the emergence (defined as the “dynamic process of interactions among heterogeneous agents that unfolds and evolves over time, resulting in various kinds of unexpected novel individual- and group-level configurations and/or broader social structures” (Benbya et al., 2020, p. 5) of the “digital resilience” of OSS communities and in the form of the creation of OSS artifacts that provide resilience to organizations. The focus on the response of OSS community members to exogenous shocks extends current conceptualizations in the resilience literature and contributes to the human aspect of information systems (Heeks & Ospina, 2019).

Finally, although we recognize some value in operationalizing a measure of when one achieves or manifests resilience, it is important to also consider the “process” aspect of resilience (i.e., beyond considering a particular “state” of resilience). In some contexts, simply achieving the same or increased levels of a particular activity may not be most appropriate as manifestations of resilience (e.g., power plants emitting the same or increased levels of carbon emissions). Instead, what individuals (or entities) do in response to shocks and how they differentially allocate their time and energies during the process of change is just as important (if not more) in understanding how individual behaviors change to eventually manifest in a particular state of the community’s resilience. In that respect, one can view the pursuit of resilience as a never-ending or continuous journey (similar to the pursuit of excellence) because the goalposts are constantly shifting, as defined by the actors in the system or from the perspective of those outside it. We refer to change as an adaptation in response to exogenous shocks, which aligns with recent conceptualizations in the information systems literature that define resilience as “the capacity to tolerate disruption from major external shocks, while maintaining core attributes and continuing to learn, adapt and develop responses to change (Armitage, 2007; Holling & Gunderson, 2002). Resilience emphasizes an adaptive capacity to tolerate change without flipping into a qualitatively different state (Holling & Gunderson, 2002)” (Boh et al., 2021, p. 2). The process aspect refers to resource alterations by individuals or other collective entities to reach a state of resilience in response to exogenous shocks (Weber et al., 2021).

## Theoretical Contributions and Research Implications

Our study has three main contributions to the research on digital public goods and digital resilience. First, our key contribution lies in advancing our understanding of OSS community resilience by uncovering nuances in the changes in community members’ contributions to others’ projects relative to their own.<sup>24</sup> This distinction has theoretical implications and suggests that OSS community members use different resource investment and acquisition mechanisms in response to exogenous shocks. Our study complements prior literature on digital public goods (Kankanhalli et al., 2005a; Xu et al., 2020) that analyzes overall contributions, and responds to the call from prior research to “analyze the nature of contributions” (Kummer et al., 2020, p. 816). Our study shows that different types of shocks have different effects on contributions to others’ projects relative to one’s own projects. A theoretical implication relates to the conceptualization and measurement of phenomena related to user participation and success such as users’ network position, project success, and career moves (Huang & Zhang, 2016; Moqri et al., 2018; Peng, 2019; Singh et al., 2011). Distinguishing between contributions to one’s own projects and others’ projects can enrich our understanding of these phenomena.

Second, our findings contribute to the nascent literature on digital resilience and suggest that OSS community members adapt to exogenous shocks by altering the nature of their contributions in terms of developing information systems. This finding extends the current literature that suggests an increase in the use of technology in response to exogenous shocks (Liu et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023; Tremblay et al., 2023). By identifying the nuanced effects of health-related versus unemployment shocks on OSS community resilience regarding members’ contributions to others’ projects and their own, this study contributes to the understanding of resilience (Boh et al., 2023) in the context of different types of exogenous shocks. Our results suggest that a pandemic encourages prosocial behavior and encourages interaction with others to overcome the lost resource of community support. In contrast, an unemployment threat decreases the salience of prosocial behavior and encourages a focus on one’s own reputation to overcome potential or lost economic security. These conceptual underpinnings (see Appendix Table A2) provide a better understanding of OSS community resilience in terms of the differences in members’ contributions to others’ projects versus their own.

Finally, our results complement prior studies that have examined the effects of only one exogenous shock at a time on contributions to digital public goods (Kummer et al., 2020) and have not contrasted the effects of different shocks. Our theorization contributes to this literature by highlighting that health-related and economic shocks invoke different motivations, resulting in different types of resource loss. These theoretical mechanisms lead to different emphases that OSS community workers place on contributions to others’ projects relative to their own when faced with healthrelated and economic exogenous shocks. Health-related exogenous changes drive OSS community members to contribute more to others’ projects, driven by prosocial behavior and resource acquisition motivations related to community support. In contrast, economic shocks drive OSS community members to contribute more to their own projects, driven by resource investment and acquisition motivations related to reputation and career. Our findings provide a more complete and enriched view of OSS community resilience to these two types of shocks.

## Managerial Implications

Our study has three main managerial implications. First, we highlight the role of resources that have nuanced effects on how OSS community members change their contribution behaviors in response to exogenous shocks involving a pandemic and rising unemployment. Faced with different types of shocks (e.g., pandemic or economic), OSS community members may experience a significant loss of resources, resulting in stress. Our findings suggest that when faced with pandemic-type shocks, OSS community members increase their contributions to others’ projects relative to their own. Hence, it behooves managers to take steps to enhance the resilience of OSS community members in the form of contributions to their own projects. In contrast, when the threat of unemployment arises, managers would do well to encourage OSS community members to contribute to others’ projects. Because OSS communities thrive on contributions to others’ projects, our findings imply that to ensure that the community maintains resilience during different types of shocks, managers may need to identify incentives or interventions that compensate for different resources and motivations of OSS community members.

Second, a better understanding of the differences in OSS community members’ contribution behaviors (and, by extension, of the resilience of OSS communities) when faced with the exogenous shocks of a pandemic and rising unemployment can help OSS project managers and owners streamline their processes of integrating, testing, and deploying code in response to such adaptations by OSS community members. Finally, our study has implications for managing OSS community members, who often contribute to OSS projects as a discretionary activity (Bitzer & Geishecker, 2010). Since a pandemic may require employees to work from home, one might expect OSS participants to spend more time on OSS projects. Yet this may not be true, due to stresses and personal challenges arising from a pandemic. Our results can help shape project policies and timelines. Employee well-being is important for IT firms, where social distance may lead to isolation and a lack of social interaction. Our findings suggest that managers can encourage IT knowledge workers to contribute more to others’ projects to facilitate social interaction.

## Limitations, Future Research Directions, and Conclusion

Our study should be viewed within the boundaries of its limitations. First, our analysis is limited to one country (the U.S.). Although this improves the internal validity by overcoming issues due to cross-country differences, future work could assess the generalizability to other countries. Second, while our approach of using machine learning packages to infer the control variables of gender and race is well accepted in the literature (Robles et al., 2014; Vasilescu et al., 2015), and the accuracy of the packages is quite high (as discussed earlier), there is a possibility of prediction errors. Nonetheless, to the extent that the prediction errors are uncorrelated with our focal variables, they are unlikely to cause bias.

We discuss two additional mechanisms that point in the same direction as our theoretical arguments and thereby lend further theoretical support to our findings. For example, prior work has identified intrinsic and extrinsic motivations to participate in OSS projects (Benbya & Belbaly, 2010; Lerner & Tirole, 2002; Raymond, 1999; von Krogh et al., 2012).<sup>25</sup> First, the literature on intrinsic motivations supports our findings regarding the influence of the COVID-19 pandemic.<sup>26</sup> One intrinsic motivation to contribute to OSS projects is a sense of happiness and community fostered in OSS contributors, even if contributing is not in the person’s own self-interest (Jiang et al., 2017; Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003; Osterloh & Rota, 2007). In line with the idea that voluntary contributions to others are associated with greater well-being (Dunn et al., 2008; Post, 2005), contributions to others’ projects are a more effective way for OSS community members to develop a sense of community, happiness, and support of others’ well-being than are contributions to their own projects, which are more selffocused. Thus, the intrinsic motivations involving a sense of community and happiness drive OSS community members to contribute more to others’ projects than to their own during a pandemic. Another intrinsic motivation to contribute to OSS projects is altruism (von Krogh et al., 2012), given that OSS participants are willing to do something beneficial for others and not necessarily for themselves (Bitzer et al., 2007; Wu et al., 2007). Because altruism is an outgrowth of situations that involve suffering (Vollhardt, 2009), a pandemic can accentuate the altruistic motivations of OSS community members to contribute more to others’ projects than to their own.

Second, according to the literature, extrinsic motivations to contribute to OSS projects include career concerns and the offline benefits related to one’s online reputation (Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003; Xu et al., 2020). This supports our findings regarding the influence of the unemployment shock.<sup>27</sup> Because rising unemployment is an economic change that threatens the livelihood and economic wellbeing of OSS community members (Kummer et al., 2020), rising unemployment amplifies the salience of the extrinsic motivations of career and reputation that OSS community members can attain by contributing to their own projects. Compared to contributions to others’ projects, contributions to one’s own projects are more effective in helping OSS community members showcase their own abilities publicly and develop their reputation on the OSS platform (Fang & Neufeld, 2009; Roberts et al., 2006). Overall, these additional theoretical mechanisms, albeit not testable in our data, could be instrumental for the future theorizing of OSS project contributions and OSS community resilience.

Our study opens several avenues for future research. First, whereas we focused on OSS community resilience by examining changes in the contribution behaviors of IT knowledge workers in an OSS community, future studies could examine the effects of exogenous shocks on IT teams, IT firms, or IT industries as they address opportunities and challenges in the face of ongoing digital transformations involving artificial intelligence and other emerging technologies such as ChatGPT, and Metaverse (e.g., Angelopoulos et al., in press, Mithas et al, 2022c). Second, while we use the concept of prosocial behavior and COR theory to develop our hypotheses, future work could consider and test other explanatory mechanisms, such as intrinsic and extrinsic motivations, altruism, and reciprocity, plausibly using experimental, survey, or qualitative methods. Finally, future work could study contributions in other digital settings and platforms (e.g., online discussion forums, R&D projects) (e.g., Huang et al., 2018).

To conclude, this study was motivated by the effects of the exogenous shocks of a pandemic and rising unemployment on adaptations by OSS community members and responds to calls for research on digital resilience (Rai, 2020). Drawing on conservation of resources theory, we theorized the nuanced effects of the pandemic and rising unemployment on OSS community resilience, as reflected in changes in community members’ contributions to others’ projects relative to their own. Analyzing a unique dataset from an OSS community, we found that community members increased (decreased) their contributions to others’ projects relative to their own projects in response to a pandemic (rising unemployment). The novel insights from this study are important, especially in light of the COVID-19 pandemic, which amplified the need for resilience to different types of exogenous shocks.
