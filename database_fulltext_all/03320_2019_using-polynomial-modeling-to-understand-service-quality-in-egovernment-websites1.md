# USING POLYNOMIAL MODELING TO UNDERSTAND SERVICE QUALITY IN E–GOVERNMENT WEBSITES<sup>1</sup>

## Rohit Nishant

Département de systèmes d’information organisationnels, Faculté des sciences de l’administration, Université Laval, 2325, rue de la Terrasse, QC G1V 0A6, CANADA {rohit.nishant@fsa.ulaval.ca}

Shirish C. Srivastava Information Systems & Operations Management Department, HEC Paris, 1 Rue de la Libération, Jouy en Josas Cedex, 78351 FRANCE {srivastava@hec.fr}

Thompson S. H. Teo Department of Analytics and Operations, School of Business, National University of Singapore, SINGAPORE {bizteosh@nus.edu.sg}

As e–government websites grow in functionalities and capabilities, there is a need to better understand the nuanced role of service quality to enable governments to better address citizens’ online service needs. Such an understanding should help improve overall e–government use by citizens. Thus motivated, our paper investigates how users respond to the service quality perception–expectation gap in e–government websites. We draw on rational choice theory (RCT) to develop a theoretical model linking expected and perceived information systems (IS) service quality to continued e–government website use intentions. The proposed model is empirically tested using polynomial modeling and response surface analysis. The results indicate that, in contrast to the organizational context, for e–government websites, both agreement and disagreement between expected and perceived IS service quality are positively associated with continued use intention. In our sample, as high as 77 percent of respondents appear to be in the zone of tolerance, suggesting that users can tolerate wide variations in service quality before they consider seeking alternatives to e–government websites.

Keywords: Context, continued use intention, polynomial modeling, rational choice theory, response surface

## Introduction

Many countries have been investing significant resources in e–government initiatives to offer e-services to their citizens (UNPAN 2014). Despite the hype, e–government investments do not always correspond with initial projections in yielding long-term benefits (Anthopoulos et al. 2016). Similarly, initial adoption of e–government by citizens, although essential, may not necessarily result in sustained benefits (Teo et al. 2008). Instead, long-term benefits from e–government investments depend on citizens continuing to use e-services over extended periods of time (Srivastava et al. 2016). Hence, examining the factors that contribute to continued use of e–government websites by citizens will be of value to research and practice.

Prior research has shown that continued technology use depends largely on the quality of service that the information system (IS) offers, and this includes both organizational IS as well as e–government systems (see Benlian et al. 2011-12; Teo et al. 2008). But e–government websites differ from typical organizational contexts in that the objective is public service. In organizational contexts, if the newly implemented systems fail to provide the desired service, users can revert to the previously available systems (see Venkatesh, Thong, and Xu 2016). But in an e–government context, because using online services is more efficient (in terms of time and cost savings) than alternatives (such as telephoning or visiting government offices), users may continue to interact with government through online channels despite e–government services failing to meet their expectations. E–government websites are thus the dominant choice because any alternatives are considerably less desirable (e.g., Teo and Koh 2010) and even less digitally skilled citizens are using e–government services (Ebbers et al. 2016). As an illustration, the Singapore online business licensing (OBLS) system allows users to apply, update, renew or terminate any combination of licenses issued by multiple government agencies in a single online transaction, thereby saving time and money, as there is no need to make multiple trips to visit various government agencies. The cost to apply for a new business license online is just S\$300,<sup>2</sup> compared to total expenses of around S\$1200, if done over the counter, as one needs to visit various government agencies (Teo and Koh 2010). Such significant advantages offered by e–government as compared to offline alternatives make it a unique, dominant choice context calling for focused research attention.

Both the management (Johns 2001, 2006) and IS (Hong et al. 2014) literatures have emphasized the salience of context in theory development. Context generates “situational opportunities and constraints that affect the occurrence and meaning of organizational behavior as well as functional relationships between variables” (Johns 2006, p. 386). Consequently, context can determine behaviors (Johns 2001). In the IS domain, context refers to “the characteristics and use context of the technological artifacts” (Hong et al. 2014, p. 112). Thus, the unique contextual characteristics surrounding e–government websites could influence the role of service quality in determining citizens’ continued use behaviors.

IS service quality research comprising various theoretical lenses, such as expectation disconfirmation theory, cognitive dissonance theory, and prospect theory, unambiguously agree that negative gaps between perceptions and expectations are undesirable (Brown et al. 2012; Venkatesh and Goyal 2010; Venkatesh et al. 2011). Nevertheless, because of the significantly less efficient alternatives, e–government websites can be the dominant choice even in a scenario of negative gaps, implying that less than expected service quality could still be positively related to continued use intentions. This proposition offers an interesting opportunity to better understand the service perception–expectation gap and its implications for continued use intentions of e–government websites.

Examinations of continued use intentions for e–government websites have assumed that service quality gaps are linearly related to outcomes (Venkatesh, Thong, Chan, and Hu 2016). However, linear relationships often fail to account for complexities in the joint effects of independent variables on dependent variables and this can lead to distorted and erroneous conclusions (Benlian et al. 2011-12; Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). Expectation disconfirmation may thus have more complex relationships with outcomes than the usually conceptualized: simple linear relationships (Brown et al. 2012; Klein et al. 2009; Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). Moreover, nonlinear models explain relationships more richly and completely (Brown et al. 2014). Consequently, recent IS research suggests the use of nonlinear techniques to investigate the impact of the relationship between expected and perceived service quality on different outcome variables (Appendix A). The marketing literature also recognizes that service quality can have nonlinear, dynamic implications (Falk et al. 2010).

Against this backdrop, the dominant choice context of e–government websites presents an interesting scenario where examining nonlinear relationships between antecedent and outcome variables could provide rich insights. The two key questions that we examine in this research are

RQ1: In the dominant choice context of e–government websites, what is the nature of the nonlinear relationship between perceived/expected service quality and continued use intention?

RQ2: In the dominant choice context of e–government websites, what proportion of respondents are in the range of the zone of tolerance where continued use intention of e–government websites remains relatively stable despite differences between user perceptions and expectations with regard to service quality?

We employ the service quality (SERVQUAL) model (Parasuraman et al. 1988) and use polynomial modeling to better understand the complex relationships between component measures of expected and perceived service quality and the outcome variable of continued e–government website use intentions. For a deeper understanding of the polynomial modeling results, we use response surface analysis for visual representation and statistical tests (Edwards and Parry 1993).

We make several key contributions to understanding e–government website operations and contrast it with traditional organizational, commercial, and individual IS use contexts. First, we theorize that perceived and expected IS service quality have nonlinear relationships with continued use intentions. We use rational choice theory (RCT) which contributes to our understanding of the continued use intention by highlighting that users’ response to service quality gaps is predicated on the availability of comparable options. RCT also helps to explain the dominant choice context of e–government websites where other alternatives tend to be less efficient in terms of time and cost savings. Second, we extend the service quality literature’s primary focus on linear relationships by focusing on the role of agreement/disagreement between perceptions and expectations, the direction of disagreement, and changes in outcomes. We provide empirical evidence that the results of past studies on organizational IS may differ from the dominant choice context of e–government websites. Third, we examine the zone of tolerance, that is, the range for which the behavioral responses to disconfirmation of expectations remain fairly stable (Johnston 1995; Liljander and Strandvik 1993). We find that a significantly large proportion of e–government users are in the zone of tolerance. Overall, our context-based theorization using RCT and polynomial modeling advances the knowledge and understanding about the nonlinear relationships between service quality gaps and continued use intentions in the dominant choice context of e–government websites.

## Theory

Despite the many noteworthy developments in the service quality literature, e–government use presents a unique context that may require revisiting and extending current understanding on IS service quality. With this end in view, in this section, we first discuss the service quality literature in relation to the e–government context. Next, we review the key theories used in prior IS expectation disconfirmation research and highlight the need to use the rational choice theory for better understanding the dominant choice context of e–government websites. We subsequently use this contextualized understanding to develop our hypotheses.

## Service Quality

Early consumer behavior literature was built on the concept of expectation confirmation/disconfirmation to better understand consumer satisfaction. For example, customers are more satisfied when their expectations compare favorably to the services they receive (Oliver 1977). Parasuraman et al. (1985) proposed the “gap model of service quality” using the SERVQUAL instrument to measure perceived service quality according to the differences between customer perceptions and expectations. Despite several concerns, the gap model and the SERVQUAL instrument are widely used for assessing service quality (Babakus and Boller 1992; Lupo 2013; Parasuraman et al. 1988).

SERVQUAL has been adapted to assess IS service quality (Kettinger and Lee 2005) in offline (e.g., Jiang et al. 2002), online (e.g., Cenfetelli et al. 2008; Xu et al. 2013), and organizational contexts (e.g., Banwet and Datta 2002; Benlian et al. 2011-12). Moreover, IS researchers have extended the model and developed the E-S-QUAL to focus on online contexts (Parasuraman et al. 2005). Researchers have also examined factors that facilitate IS service quality (Watson et al. 1998), perceptual congruence between IS professionals and users regarding service quality satisfaction (Benlian 2013), and ways to integrate service, information, and system quality (Tan et al. 2013).

In an alternative to the gap model view, some IS research has measured only perceptions of service quality (SERVPERF; Cronin and Taylor 1994). Similarly, e–government research has used the perceived service quality component of the SERVQUAL gap model to investigate antecedents and consequences of perceived service quality (Tan et al. 2013). The underlying premise is that users’ evaluations of service quality are essential (Barrett et al. 2015).

In the e–government context, IS service quality is defined as “the extent to which services delivered via an e–government website assist citizens in completing their governmental transactions” (Tan et al. 2010, p. 3). E–government research has often viewed quality as reflected in user perceptions regarding service design, service attributes, preferences for attributes (Venkatesh et al. 2012), beliefs (Fassnacht and Koese 2006), attitudes (Santos 2003), or transactional features such as ease in submitting electronic forms or filing tax returns (Tan et al. 2013; Tan and Pan 2003; Venkatesh et al. 2012).

Recently, the focus has turned to deconstructing e–government service quality into service content and service delivery (Tan et al. 2013). Researchers have tried to identify key service attributes (Venkatesh et al. 2012), to investigate ways to reduce uncertainty (Venkatesh, Thong, Chan, and Hu 2016), and to examine joint impacts of perceptions regarding information quality, system quality, and service quality on continued use intentions (Xu et al. 2013).

Despite the focus of IS research on service quality perceptions, few researchers have argued that the role of service quality expectations is far more complex as users tend to lack a single point of expectation. Instead, they have a range of expectation levels falling within a zone of indifference or tolerance (Goode et al. 2017; Kettinger and Lee 2005) representing the difference between expected and minimally adequate service. Disconfirmation falling outside the zone attracts the most attention (Benlian et al. 2011-12; Lankton et al. 2014), but this zone is highly contextual and can vary in size for different uses and over time. Moreover, users can differ in their zones of tolerance depending on their personal attributes. Clearly, IS service quality research needs to be further enriched in this direction but this calls for contextspecific theorizing to better understand and explain consumer/ user behavior in different contexts, including e–government.

## Need for an Alternative Perspective: Rational Choice Theory

The IS literature suggests that research on agreement/ disagreement between service quality expectations and perceptions comprises expectation disconfirmation theory, cognitive dissonance theory, and prospect theory (Brown et al. 2014). The theories have been leveraged to postulate the contrast model, the generalized negativity model, the assimilation model, and the assimilation–contrast model (Brown et al. 2014; Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). Table 1 shows the relationships between the different theories and the postulated models.

From Table 1, we conclude that when perceptions are lower than expectations, the negative effect is stronger than the positive effect (which occurs when perceptions exceed expectations). However, this generalization fails to consider the dominant choice context, such as e–government or online banking, which tends to be significantly more efficient in terms of time and expenses as compared to offline alternatives. In such situations where available alternatives may not necessarily be preferred despite the focal technology not providing the expected level of service, a fresh theoretical lens is needed to reexamine how service quality perceptions and expectations relate to use behaviors.

We also must closely examine user rationality in making decisions and considering alternatives, predicated on the cost– benefit analysis of various options for maximizing well-being (Charki et al. 2017). Healthcare research, for instance, reveals that patients make informed choices by considering convenience, past experience, and service quality (Victoor et al. 2012). Thus, service quality and option comparisons are crucial. Consequently, we focus on a rational choice theory (RCT) of consumer behavior (Mas-Collel et al. 1995).

Rational choice implies that consumers first identify available options and choose the most preferred by balancing costs against benefits and selecting the option that maximizes personal advantage (McCoy et al. 2012). RCT has often been used in studying choice preferences under various conditions (for a detailed discussion, see Weiner 2000) and in analyzing whether consumers make optimal choices (Levin and Milgrom 2004). Marketing research often assumes that consumers act rationally to maximize utility (Stüttgen et al. 2012). However, utility maximization has been debated, prompting researchers to propose that consumers adopt choice rules (Gilbride and Allenby 2004) in which they select alternatives that are “good enough” to provide satisfaction (Stüttgen et al. 2012). Another suggestion is the index strategy in which consumers order brands and select options that are close in optimal utility (Lin et al. 2014). Models involving choice rules still emphasize consideration of the best among available options. Hence, researchers have often used utility-based theoretical arguments rather than psychological theories for understanding user behavior (Sun et al. 2014). The key tenets underlying RCT are

(1) Consumers must choose from a set of alternatives. In the e–government context, they can choose to use the website, call the government office, or visit the office.

(2) For each alternative pair, consumers must have a preference or be indifferent to the choice (axiom of completeness).

(3) If A is preferred to B, and B is preferred to C, then A is preferred to C (axiom of transitivity).

In our context, the citizen’s choice to interact with the government through a physical visit, the telephone, or an e–government website will primarily depend on the amount of time, effort, and money expended for each of the alternatives. Clearly, among the three alternatives, the e–government website stands out as the cheapest and most efficient mode, making it the dominant choice. Mathematically, preference reflects utility functions. Past studies have modeled service quality as a utility function (e.g., Sampson 1999). However, similar to prior IS research that used economic theory (e.g., Brown et al. 2012), we draw on RCT (e.g., Charki et al. 2017) to theorize continued use intentions. In the IS context, RCT explains intentions to return to specific websites (McCoy et al. 2012; Rejón-Guardia and Martínez-López 2014). The focus has been on continued use intention and users can evaluate the utility of specific options in the examined contexts. RCT has also been used to understand employee compliance with IS security policies (Li et al. 2014) and unethical IT use (Charki et al. 2017). Consumers tend to choose according to their expectations and subjective assessments (perceptions) (Fountain 2001; Johnson et al. 1995) by rationally evaluating the quality of utilitarian services (Jiang and Wang 2006) or assessing alternatives according to performance information (Inman et al. 1997). Within the broader realm of decision-making literature, RCT explains that consumers prefer alternatives that maximize utility (Tversky and Kahneman 1986).

IT continuance has been viewed as a rational evaluation that includes disconfirmation (Bhattacherjee et al. 2008). Recent studies have discussed the rationality argument and utility perspective in conjunction with expectation disconfirmation theory to argue that users update their beliefs rationally when they access new information or experience the product itself (Wagner and Zhang 2014). Thus, RCT’s economic foundation provides theoretical rationale for understanding why users prefer e–government websites for interactions with the government.

## Hypothesis Development

E–government websites are the dominant choice, being inherently superior to visiting or calling (by phone) government offices by offering convenience without time and geographical constraints. Consequently, users would respond to disagreements between perceptions and expectations depending on the relative utility of the available alternatives. Users might experience (1) positive disagreements when perceptions exceed expectations or (2) negative disagreements when perceptions fail to meet expectations. Positive and negative disagreements represent two directionally opposite facets of disagreement, with agreement at the baseline (Figure 1).

Prior examinations of how the two types of disagreements impact outcome variables have shown similar and dissimilar impacts (Brown et al. 2012; Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). Similarly, positive and negative disagreements between perceptions and expectations have potentially similar or dissimilar relationships with intentions to continue using the technology. When users’ perceptions match their expectations, they will judge the e–government website as having acceptable service quality and as providing greater utility than the inferior alternatives. Such an RCT-based argument is consistent with expectation disconfirmation theory research describing baseline agreement situations (Brown et al. 2012, 2014), but when perceived service quality exceeds expected service quality, we move into the zone of positive disagreement in which the acceptable minimum level is exceeded. Consequently, e–government users will further explore its functionalities, generating continued use (see Brown et al. 2012). Thus, expectation disconfirmation theory argues that positive disagreement is positively related to continued use intention (Venkatesh et al. 2011). Moreover, predicated on RCT, we argue that users are likely to focus on the positive experience of being pleasantly surprised in the positive disagreement zone. Further, such positive disagreement is likely to enhance the dominant choice context of the e–government website because alternatives tend to be much inferior to the e–government website. For instance, the processing time and cost for submitting an application for a resale apartment through the e–government website could be at least 20 percent less relative to over-the-counter application (eGL Singapore 2011), thereby enhancing the positive experience with the website, which further reinforces its already positive rational evaluation.

## H1a. In the e–government context, positive disagreement between perceived and expected service quality is positively related to continued use intentions.

According to expectation disconfirmation theory and the SERVQUAL model, when perceived service quality fails to meet expectations, negative disagreement occurs, along with negative continued use intentions. Further, prospect theory suggests that when positive and negative disagreements are compared, negative disagreements have a stronger negative influence on intention to use (e.g., Brown et al. 2012). These prior studies were conducted in organizational IS contexts where employees invested substantial time and effort in systems training, expecting positive contributions to their productivity. If these systems failed to meet users’ expectations, users felt that their time and efforts were wasted.

In contrast, relatively less time and effort may be needed to start using e–government websites, as such websites are often easy to use, especially in countries with mature e–government services (Meskell 2014). In conventional organizational contexts, users may be disappointed and refrain from using the system when their perceptions fall short of expectations (Brown et al. 2012). Similarly, in the e–government context, users may feel disappointed when perceived service quality fails to meet expected service quality, but alternative means would require extra time, effort, and commuting expenses (Venkatesh, Thong, Chan, and Hu 2016). For example, visiting government agencies instead of using e–government websites entails commuting expenses, effort to find a specific location, traveling, and waiting time. This would motivate users to continue using the e–government website despite the negative disagreement, thereby prompting users to behave in way that is different from that observed in organizational contexts.

To better understand the mechanisms explaining the nature of e–government user reactions to negative disagreements, it would be worthwhile examining the source of negative disagreements in the specific context of e–government. In the case of e–government, negative disagreements could arise from two distinct scenarios: (1) high expected service quality and average or low perceived service quality or (2) low expected service quality and even lower perceived service quality. In the present day, however, in most developed countries with advanced e–government systems, users would at least have somewhat good, rather than low, perceptions about e–government service quality. Thus, high, rather than low, expectations would contribute to the negative service quality disagreements between perceptions and expectations as described in the first scenario.<sup>3</sup> Prior research has acknowledged that expectations could influence outcomes in a complex manner (Lankton et al. 2016).

We examine an e–government user’s continued use intention as the outcome, which implies that the current use of e–government might be tempered by the user’s prior experiences with the specific e–government website. As noted earlier, in countries with mature e–government systems, user perceptions of prior experiences would at least tend to be satisfactory. If the prior experiences have been satisfactory, users would tend to increase their expectations. These higher expectations would contribute to increasing negative disagreements, but users would still intend to continue using the e–government website. In fact, this intention will become stronger because now users may explore the website more deeply to see if the government has incorporated additional features into the website. Further, users may visit e–government websites for a specific purpose with certain expectations. When these expectations are higher than perceptions, users might be motivated to look further and explore new functionalities in the e–government websites in order to better understand what e–government websites offer and how it can be used to satisfy their purpose of visit. This might influence their continuance intention positively as well as further temper their future expectations. Consequently, users would recognize the higher utility of e–government, especially in comparison to the offline alternatives because of their dominant choice nature, which contributes to e–government’s utility notwithstanding negative perception-expectation gaps.

Users, being rational actors, will prefer the best alternative among the possible options and discount the fact that the perceived service quality fails to meet their high expectations. Consequently, negative disagreements, which are primarily due to enhanced user expectations for accessing greater functionalities, may increase continued use intentions, given the dominant choice context of e–government websites. Nevertheless, continued use intentions might be impacted negatively if the perceptions differ too much from expectations. Such extreme situations are highly unlikely, especially in the repeated use scenario where previous experiences with e–government websites are likely to temper user expectations.

## H1b. In the e–government context, where IT offers more utility in comparison with alternatives, negative disagreement between perceived and expected IS service quality is positively related to continued use intention.

Our next hypothesis focuses on the proportion of respondents who belong to the zone of tolerance. Previous research (e.g., Gorla 2012) found that generally less than 50 percent of respondents are within the zone of tolerance (the zone where the outcome does not vary much with gap/disagreement), but these studies were conducted in the organizational context where users might have comparable alternatives. E–government websites are more convenient and available than the alternatives (Venkatesh, Thong, Chan, and Hu 2016). The potential alternatives for e–government websites do not offer convenience, efficiency, and 24/7 availability. Specifically, in countries with mature e–government services, e–government websites are designed to meet the general expectations of users (Meskell 2014). From a rational choice perspective, despite disappointment in terms of service quality perceptions, using e–government websites would provide greater relative utility. Because users evaluate the quality of service and compare associated costs–benefits relative to the utility of the available alternatives (Charki et al. 2017; Inman et al. 1997; Jiang and Wang 2006), they are likely to tolerate negative disagreements and may continue to use e–government websites. Consequently, a majority of respondents are likely to be within the zone of tolerance where response to disagreement remains relatively stable.

## H1c. The proportion of respondents within the zone of tolerance for the dominant choice context of e–government website will be significantly greater than 50 percent.

Together, H1a and H1b suggest that as positive and negative disagreements increase, so do continued use intentions. For positive disagreements, e–government websites will exceed users’ expectations regarding utility. As the positive disagreement increases, utility relative to expectations will increase. For negative disagreements, even when perceptions lag behind expectations, e–government websites offer convenience and 24/7 availability and thus are perceived as having higher utility than alternatives. Further, as explained earlier, higher expectations could cause negative disagreement and motivate users to explore additional website functionalities. Consequently, users might realize the true utility of the service, discover more benefits, and have increased perceptions regarding the websites’ utility. H1c suggests that a majority of respondents would belong to a zone of tolerance where continued use intention remains relatively stable despite positive and negative disagreements. It follows that continued use would have a U-shaped relationship with disagreement in two-dimensional (2-D) space and a convex surface in three-dimensional (3-D) space (see Figure 2).

H1. In the e–government context, perception/expectation disagreement has a U-shaped relationship (in 2-D space) or convex surface (in 3-D space) relationship with continued use intentions.

Positive and negative disagreement could have differing relative impacts on continued use intentions. Our hypotheses have suggested that both positive and negative disagreement are positively related with continued use intention. In the organizational context, prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky 1979) has empirical support showing that negative disagreement has stronger negative effects on outcomes in comparison to positive disagreement (Brown et al. 2012). However, in the e–government context, RCT explains the contextual differences. That is, the dominant choice context mitigates the negative effect of negative disagreement. When perceptions exceed expectations, RCT suggests that e–government websites will offer higher utility by exceeding the acceptable level of service quality. Even when perceptions lag behind expectations, e–government websites will still remain the dominant choice. For instance, many governments offer online service for filing income tax returns. Even if a citizen finds the income tax return filing portal to be below his/her expectation, the online service will still be more efficient and cost-effective as compared to traveling to the income tax office and waiting in queue to be served.

Nevertheless, users will be sensitive to the direction of disagreement between expected and perceived service quality. When positive disagreement increases, users’ perceived service quality relative to their expectation increases. Then, the dominant choice context may enhance positive disagreement by providing more than the desired services, pleasantly surprising users, and sharply increasing continued use intentions. In general, the presence of negative disagreement has the effect of decreasing continued use intention. However, due to the dominant choice context of e–government websites where alternatives tend to be less efficient, e–government websites could still offer higher relative utility. However, due to the disappointment arising from perception lagging expectation, continued use intention would show smaller increases in the negative disagreement zone (Figure 2). In sum, negative disagreement will cause less increase in continued use intentions than will positive disagreement.

## Method

Singapore provides an excellent context for this study because, rather than calling or visiting government agencies, users view Singapore’s well-developed, world-class e–government systems as their dominant choice (Waseda University 2017). Moreover, the public is increasingly transitioning from offline alternatives to Singapore’s e–government websites (Yu 2014). We collected data by surveying 214 e–government website users at a large university in Singapore (see Appendix B for detailed survey measures). Some survey items were similar to those of Teo et al. (2008) but we used different variables: rather than system quality, perceived service quality, information quality, trust, continued use intention, and user satisfaction, we used expected and perceived service quality, and continued use intentions. We also used a different theoretical lens and empirical approach (polynomial modeling and response surface analysis).

We measured service quality using the shorter version of SERVQUAL (Landrum et al. 2009) on a seven-point Likert scale. Respondents indicated their expectations, perceptions, and continued use intentions regarding an e–government website with which they had prior experience. To negate response bias and to provide a psychological separation between the measures (Srivastava et al. 2015), we placed several intermediate questions between the questions on expected and perceived service quality.

Following Brown et al. (2012), we controlled for gender and age. We also controlled for Internet experience and prior e–government experience, as these variables may influence continued use intentions. The average age of participants was about 22 years. They averaged 7.9 years of Internet experience and 3.4 years of experience using e–government websites. Of the respondents, 57.9 percent were men, close to the actual proportion of Internet users in Singapore, thereby eliminating potential gender ratio bias. We discarded 16 incomplete responses among the 214 responses. We also discarded an additional 13 responses based on outlier criteria during regression analysis. Hence, we used 185 responses (86.45 percent response rate) for our regression analysis.

We followed the procedure suggested by Atwater et al. (2005) to test the polynomial model. We mean centered the terms before computing interaction effects and quadratic terms, as this reduces the potential problem of multicollinearity (Aiken and West 1991). The values of the variance inflation factor (VIF) ranged from 1.07 to 3.14 (all VIF values were below 5) indicating that the influence of multicollinearity confounding our results is not a concern (Hair 2006). Appendix D provides the interconstruct correlations. The high mean for expected service quality (4.95) and perceived service quality (5.12) also supported our contextualized argument that there is less probability of low expectation and even lower perception. We tested for common method bias using Harman’s single factor test (Podsakoff et al. 2003) and concluded that common method bias does not confound our results.

If polynomial regression analysis (PRA) gives significant results, a response surface plot could help in interpreting higher-order terms in the presence of linear terms (Edwards 2002; Shanock et al. 2010). In addition, theoretical considerations and the potential to interpret complex relationships motivate the use of a response surface (Edwards 2002). Other studies suggest using PRA and a response surface when theoretical arguments indicate a nonlinear relationship,

the anticipated relationship should first be examined with PRA to determine if any coefficient on the higher-order terms is significant. If not, then the model should be built using the guidelines for linear expectations (Klein et al. 2009, p. 818).

Quadratic terms in our nonlinear model were significant, supporting the use of response surface analysis where we can observe and analyze the change in continued use intention with changes in positive and negative disagreements. As a robustness check, we ran the analysis using scale-centered variables and found that the same variables were significant. The variance explained by the quadratic equation was significantly different from zero (F = 14.21). The quadratic model explained significantly higher variance than the linear model (F-test value = 3.54, p < .05). We also tested a cubic model to check whether it explains more of the variance, but no cubic terms were significant.

The response surface analysis graphs the results of a PRA in a three-dimensional space (Edwards and Parry 1993), characterized by three key features, namely stationary point, principal axes, and slopes along line of interest such as the confirmation and disconfirmation axes. The stationary point is the point at which the slope of surface is zero and the principal axes run perpendicular to each other and intersect at the stationary point (Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). Four surface test values were computed: ${ \bf a } _ { 1 }$ and ${ \bf a } _ { 2 }$ measure the slope and curvature of the line when two predictor variables completely agree with each other (confirmation) (Shanock et al. 2010), whereas ${ \bf { a } } _ { 3 }$ and ${ \bf { a } } _ { 4 }$ measure the slope and curvature when predictors disagree with each other (disconfirmation) (Shanock et al. 2010; Venkatesh and Goyal 2010; see Appendix E for the detailed formulas used to compute various statistics). These slopes and curvature along the confirmation and disconfirmation axes can be used to understand the relationship of agreement/disagreement with the outcome variable.

A significant ${ \bf a } _ { 1 }$ indicates that the line of agreement has an additive relationship to the outcome variable. A positive ${ \bf a } _ { 1 }$ indicates that the outcome variable increases as both predictor variables increase, whereas a negative ${ \bf a } _ { 1 }$ indicates that the outcome variable decreases as both predictor variables increase. A significant ${ \bf a } _ { 2 }$ indicates support for a quadratic relationship of the outcome variable to the predictor variables. It indicates a nonlinear slope for the line of perfect agreement between the predictor variables, thereby suggesting that more than a linear increase or decrease will occur in the outcome with corresponding changes in the predictor variables. A significant ${ \bf { a } } _ { 3 }$ indicates support for the relationship between perception/expectation disagreement and the outcome variable. A positive ${ \bf { a } } _ { 3 }$ suggests a higher outcome variable when x (the predictor variable along the x-axis) exceeds $_ \textrm { y }$ (the predictor variable along the y-axis), whereas a negative ${ \bf { a } } _ { 3 }$ suggests a higher outcome variable when y exceeds x. A positive ${ \bf { a } } _ { 4 }$ suggests a convex surface, whereas a negative ${ \bf { a } } _ { 4 }$ suggests a concave surface (Venkatesh and Goyal 2010).

The surface test values show that $a _ { 1 } ( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { b } } = . 6 9 , \boldsymbol { \mathrm { p } } < . 0 5 )$ was positive and significant, whereas $_ { 1 _ { 2 } } ( 6 = . . 1 8 , \mathrm { p } < . 0 5 )$ was negative. The significant value of ${ \bf a } _ { 1 }$ suggests that continued use intention increased as both expected service quality and perceived service quality increased and agreed with each other. The 2- D graph (Figure 4a) indicates that continued use intention increased with an increase in agreement (perfect agreement). The slope of disagreement $a _ { 3 } ( 6 = . 2 8 , 9 < . 0 5 )$ was negative and significant. This signifies that the continued use intention will be greater when perceived service quality exceeded the expected service quality (i.e., positive disagreement; Shanock et al. 2010). Figure 3b shows that with an increase in positive disagreement, continued use intention increased. The increase in positive disagreement here is attributable to an increase in perceived service quality for the same level of expected service quality. Hence, H1a was supported.

Figure 3c shows that as negative disagreement increased, continued use intention also increased. Specifically, the figure shows that when perceived service quality was constant, but expected service quality increased with subsequent increase in negative disagreement, continued use intention increased. Figure 4c shows that when perceived service quality was zero, but expected service quality increased with subsequent increase in negative disagreement, continued use intention increased. Further, our 2-D graph (Figure 4b) suggests that as perfect disagreement increased, continued use intention increased, irrespective of the direction of disagreement. Therefore, H1b was supported.

The contour plot depicting predicted values for continued use intention corresponding to values for expected and perceived service quality (Figure 3d) showed that a majority of continued use intention values remained within a narrow zone for different values of expected and perceived service quality (i.e., for a range of disagreement), suggesting a zone of tolerance or indifference where users tolerated disagreement and continued use intention varied little.

## Discussion

We use RCT, polynomial modeling, and response surface analysis to examine the dominant choice context of e–government websites in terms of the relationship between expected/perceived service quality and continued use intentions. We find that both agreement and positive/negative disagreement, are positively related with continued use intentions. In addition, disagreement had a positive asymmetric effect on continued use intentions. Departing from previous research that reported negative use intentions for both positive and negative disagreements (Venkatesh and Goyal 2010), we find that disagreements, both positive and negative, are positively related to use intentions, albeit in a different context. Further, we find that a significantly large proportion of users are in the zone of tolerance, about 77 percent, for e–government websites, compared to other information systems in the organizational contexts (e.g., 48% for service quality of IS departments; Gorla 2012).

## Theoretical Contributions

First, this work contributes to IS research by empirically demonstrating that contextual elements are salient, specifically where available alternatives are considerably less preferred. We extend the work on expectation disconfirmation theory by using RCT in an e–government dominant choice context. We showed that both positive and negative perception/expectation disagreements were positively related to continued use intentions except when there is an extreme negative disagreement. Moreover, a significantly higher proportion of respondents falls within the zone of tolerance. In fact, the zone of tolerance can be viewed as a “counterweight” to pure economic rationality espoused by RCT. Hence, our work can also be viewed as a theoretical extension to RCT because our findings demonstrate that economic rationality can be, to a certain extent, elastic depending on circumstances.<sup>4</sup> In our study, the dominant choice context of e–government website makes the majority of respondents belong to the zone of tolerance.

Second, our research brings new perspectives to user choice behavior. In e-commerce websites, users may switch to other options. Even if they are loyal to specific websites, they may want to exercise their freedom to choose through “psychological reactance,” which describes the user’s motivation to exercise their freedom of choice to counter increasing dependence on a specific website (Murray and Häubl 2012). We observe that limited availability of alternative options to e–government websites may result in continued use intentions even when users have positive and negative perception/expectation disagreements. Future research could examine various conditions motivating psychological reactance.

Third, by examining the joint effects of expected and perceived service quality on continued use intention, our work contributes to the literature on service quality in general, and e–government service quality in particular. The results suggest that e–government service quality had a more complex relationship with outcomes than envisioned in prior research examining the antecedents and consequences of e–government perceived service quality. Both expected and perceived service quality had significant linear positive effects on continued use intentions. This implies that higher perceived service quality and higher expected service quality both contribute to greater continued use intentions. In contrast, a significant nonlinear positive relationship was also present for perceived service quality but not for expected service quality (see Table 4 and Figure 4d). This extends e–government service quality research that primarily ignores the joint impact of perceived service quality and expected service quality. Our findings are different from prior studies grounded in cognitive dissonance theory, which concluded that disconfirmation, in general, was undesirable (see Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). Those studies argued that users respond negatively to both positive and negative disagreement due to cognitive dissonance. Previous studies, such as Brown et al. (2012), found that negative disagreement has a stronger negative impact relative to the positive influence from positive disagreement. Past studies also suggest that satisfaction with IS use is the strongest predictor of continuance intention (Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). Different models or theoretical lenses found predominantly similar results for continued use intention, satisfaction, and use (Brown et al. 2014). In general, continued use intention, satisfaction, and use behaved similarly for positive and negative disagreements. The assimilationcontrast model grounded in cognitive dissonance was found to be the most appropriate model in terms of its ability to explain different relationships. Notably, studies often examined the role of disagreement in the context of “usefulness.” Our study highlights several contrasting findings such as that service quality disagreement differs from usefulness disagreement, and disconfirmation, in general, is not bad. This could be attributed to the dominant choice context of e–government websites. It is also plausible that “user satisfaction” and “continued use intention” may behave differently. For example, a user may not be satisfied with a particular e–government website, yet continue to use it because e–government is a superior option among alternatives to do the task. The option of using an e–government website that does not completely satisfy a user might still be considered a better alternative than physically visiting a government office to complete the task because of the extra time and effort that would be required. This also raises an interesting question: Under what situations does satisfaction not function as an antecedent to continued use intention? Our results suggest that the dominant choice context is one such situation. In fact, when we plot the surface response using satisfaction as a dependent variable, the shape of the plots is consistent with past research (Appendix H). Thus, the context of the research and the nature of the dependent variable need to be necessarily incorporated in theorization.

Traditionally, service quality research has focused on increasing the level of perceived service quality as a way to improve user outcomes. However, our study indicates that the mechanisms orchestrating user outcomes such as their continued use intention for e–government websites is far more complex than previously envisioned. In this specific context, despite increasing the negative disagreement, high levels of expectation could actually influence continued use intention positively. This, in turn, highlights the limitation of the GAP model, which specifies a positive association of positive disagreement (excess of perceived service quality over expected service quality) user outcomes without focusing on the actual levels of expected and perceived service quality. Our research suggests the imperative to appreciate the context and the reasons underlying the observed service quality disagreement (both negative and positive) to better understand the role of disagreements in influencing user outcomes.

## Limitations

First, this study is set in Singapore. Consequently, culture may account for the relationship between perceived/expected service quality and outcomes. Singapore has an advanced e–government infrastructure and services, so alternatives may be considerably less preferred. However, other countries with less developed e–government infrastructure and websites may be quite different. Also, in some developed countries, citizens simply abandon governmental transactions by forsaking planned activities. For example, rather than applying for a hunting license, they may simply choose not to hunt. Consequently, future research can extend the study to different countries and cultures. Studies, such as Hoehle et al. (2015), found that espoused cultural values at the individual level (rather than national culture at the macro level) play a significant role in moderating the impact of mobile social media applications usability on continued use intention, the effect being much stronger than a main effects only model and a model using country dummy variables. Future studies can examine if the findings can be extended to the e–government context. Second, although our measures were psychologically separated, our data could be biased because no time elapsed between when we collected data regarding expectations and experiences. Moreover, we did not explicitly control for respondents’ awareness of the availability of physical alternatives.

## Practical Implications

First, this study helps draw attention to the nature of nonlinear relationships between perceived and expected service quality with continued use intention. Managers should note that, although continued use intention increases as perceived service quality increases, the increase in continued use intention is steeper for higher levels of perceived service quality (see Table 4 and Figure 4d). Hence, managers should pay attention to enhancing the level of perceived service quality by focusing on enhancing the usability of the website as well as providing dependable and prompt services to users. Venkatesh et al. (2017) provide some recommendations on improving website usability. Website designers should specifically focus on design mechanisms to assist passive users because active users educate themselves through active engagement whereas passive users may require assistance.

Second, managers should also note that expected service quality has a linear relationship with continued use intentions. Perhaps users have higher expectations for e–government websites and are motivated to explore the functionalities, thereby improving their experiences. For example, users expecting e–government websites to facilitate tax payments would search for such functionalities and discover other features. Therefore, managers of e–government websites should conduct advertising campaigns to increase awareness about their potential and to manage expectations by providing detailed descriptions of the website functionalities. A survey that measures citizens’ attitude to e–government (Pew Survey 2002) found that, as expectations from e–government increased, U.S. citizens explored various available functionalities and avenues to leverage e–government websites. Such surveys provide further empirical evidence supporting the need to manage user expectations from e–government websites.

Third, results show that managers of e–government websites must balance expectations and perceptions because expected service quality negatively interacts with perceived service quality in influencing continued use intentions. Arguably, a diminishing-returns effect occurs: as both expected and perceived service quality increase, the related growth in continued use intention declines (Figure 4a). However, as the effect of perceived service quality appears to be stronger than expected service quality (Table 4), managers should focus on managing perceived service quality and ensure that it meets or exceeds expected service quality. One plausible way is to establish and execute high service standards beyond the expected service quality. This could be achieved through well-trained employees who clearly understand citizen needs. This will result in well-designed e–government websites that provide required information to users in an easy way, thereby enhancing their perceptions of service quality.

Fourth, despite the high proportion of respondents in the zone of tolerance, in situations where there is a large negative gap between perceived and expected service quality, continued use intentions have a tendency to taper off. Thus, managers should note that, although e–government websites have a greater tolerance for perception/expectation disagreement than organizational or commercial contexts, it is inadvisable to deviate too much from user expectations. It is important for managers of e–government websites to continually strive to increase perceptions of service quality and proactively manage the expectations of service quality rather than being complacent. Managing expectations does not necessarily mean lowering expectations, as doing so may cause citizens to be disinterested in exploring e–government websites. On the contrary, managers should strive to convey relevant information about various functionalities of e–government websites so that users can form realistic expectations. Such communication between managers and users would also be helpful in detecting unmet expectations so that further enhancements to e–government websites could be made.

Finally, managers should note that users will tolerate negative disagreement to a certain extent, so strategies should be devised to extend the zone of tolerance or increase the utility of services for users who are outside the zone of tolerance. Managers could ensure consistency of user interfaces across various portals so that users become habituated to the interfaces and are likely to continue using the websites even when perceived service quality fails to meet their expectations. In fact, a single unified portal to access all services is desirable, as it would make user interfaces consistent and integrated to increase the utility and perhaps the level of tolerance. Managers could also encourage users to explore e–government websites through quizzes or contests. This will help users become familiar with e–government websites and have a better understanding of their functionalities and limitations, thereby leading to higher tolerance for negative disagreement. Recent website launches, such as the U.S. e–government website healthcare.gov (popularly known as the Obamacare website), illustrates the implications of the lack of awareness campaigns. Healthcare.gov did not make potential users aware of the plausible limitations such as limited traffic-handling capacity of the website. Consequently, several users searched for insurance plans simultaneously, resulting in technical glitches. Users did not tolerate these glitches and expressed their disappointment (Anthopoulos et al. 2016). An awareness campaign on the functionalities and limitations of healthcare.gov could have improved users’ tolerance for negative disagreement and reduced their sense of disappointment. Venkatesh et al. (2014, 2017) studied healthcare.gov and found it to be wanting on a variety of attributes. In particular, dimensions of usability were critical to improving user satisfaction as well as intention to use the website. Design fixes such as complete information on the homepage without the need to scroll down will make users aware of all features of e–government websites, set correct expectations, and lead to higher tolerance for negative disagreement.

## Conclusions

This research was aimed at understanding how expectations/ perceptions regarding website service quality were related to continued use intentions in the dominant-choice e–government context. We found that positive and negative disagreements between perceived service quality and expected service quality relate positively to continued use intentions, having a convex surface (in 3-D space). We also found that the proportion of respondents in the zone of tolerance is fairly large (about 77%), thereby providing empirical evidence to the thesis that, in the dominant choice context of e–government websites where other alternatives tend to be less favorable, users have high tolerance for disagreement between perceived and expected service quality. Our results also suggest that the dominant choice e–government website context may result in different user behavior from traditional organizational contexts and it is imperative for future research to take context into account when examining user behavior.

${ \bf a } _ { 1 } = \left( b _ { 1 } + b _ { 2 } \right)$ , where $b _ { 1 }$ is the unstandardized coefficient for expected service quality and $b _ { 2 }$ is the unstandardized coefficient for perceived service quality. The significance of ${ \bf a } _ { 1 }$ is tested using $t = a _ { 1 } \bigg / \sqrt { \left( S E _ { b 1 } ^ { 2 } + S E _ { b 2 } ^ { 2 } + 2 C O V _ { b 1 b 2 } \right) }$

${ a _ { 2 } } = \ ( { b _ { 3 } } + { b _ { 4 } } + { b _ { 5 } } )$ , where $b _ { 3 }$ is the unstandardized coefficient for (expected service quality)<sup>2</sup>, $b _ { 4 }$ is the unstandardized coefficient for (expected service quality × perceived service quality), and $b _ { 5 }$ is the unstandardized coefficient for (perceived service quality)<sup>2</sup>. ## Robustness Checks

Like the estimates for mean-centered variables, the estimates for scale-centered variables also supported our findings. In addition to checking the data for outliers and using standard errors robust against potential issues of heteroscedasticity, we used robust regression, which is robust to violation of various underlying assumptions behind conventional regression. The estimates from the robust regression supported our findings. System quality and information quality may affect service quality (Xu et al. 2013). Also, information quality and system quality could influence use intention (Teo et al. 2008). Therefore, we tested a polynomial model with system quality and information quality as control variables. We controlled for heteroscedasticity and outliers. System quality and information quality were measured using scales adapted from Seddon and Kiew (1996). The estimates for our constructs (linear and higher-order terms of expected and perceived service quality) were similar to the estimates in our original polynomial model, thereby supporting the robustness of our estimates.

## Appendix G

## Post Hoc Analysis: Effect of Purpose of Visit

Users visit e–government websites for informational and transactional purposes (Teo et al. 2008). Informational use includes browsing, downloading, and passively observing and obtaining information. Transactional use includes activities such as messaging and transacting, where users actively engage with the government agency through the website. When visitors use e–government websites, they learn about the various functionalities and become more skilled in using IS in general. Such experiential learning, called service learning, occurs while human needs are addressed (Lester et al. 2005). Learning levels may differ according to purposes for visiting e–government websites. Dimensions of service quality such as reliability and responsiveness may have varying consequences depending on users, and so service quality may have different effects on continued use intentions. Thus, we examine whether purposes for visiting websites influence the relationships in our polynomial model. The analysis further contextualizes our findings. Results from the polynomial model suggest significant estimates fo perceived service quality. We extended our model to include the interaction terms of expected and perceived service quality (linear and quadratic terms) with use (active/passive). We asked respondents to list Singapore websites that they have accessed along with their broad reasons for doing so, aligned with specific options such as browsing, downloading, messaging, and transacting. We classified respondents as passive users if they accessed e–government websites for merely browsing and downloading. Others were considered active users. Our approach reveals the main functionalities. The estimates for the interaction terms of perceived service quality are significant. The interaction plot (Figure G1) suggests that for both active and passive users, use intention increases with an increase in perceived service quality. However, passive users show a steeper increase in use intention. Perhaps active users are more knowledgeable than passive users about the functions and limitations of e–government websites. Thus, service quality is more consequential for passive users.

## Appendix H

## Distinction between Satisfaction and Use Intention in the Context of E–Government Websites

We argued that in the context of e–government websites, the implications of agreement and disagreement between perceived and expected service quality for satisfaction and for use intention could be different. The underlying rationale is that the available alternatives are inferior to the focal IS in question (e–government websites). We examined whether our assertion is empirically valid by testing a quadratic model with satisfaction as the dependent variable. We measured satisfaction using four items based on Seddon and Kiew (1996). The results showed that the estimates for different explanatory variables are often opposite in direction relative to the estimates for use intention. The response surface for satisfaction (Figure H1) is concave in nature, similar to the response surface reported in prior research (Brown et al. 2012, 2014; Venkatesh and Goyal 2010).
