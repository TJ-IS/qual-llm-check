# Information Technology and Interorganizational Learning:

An Investigation of Knowledge Exploration and Exploitation Processes

## Abstract

In this paper, we study the impact of IT-enabled learning mechanisms and learning strategies on the long-term knowledge outcomes of a firm in an interorganizational setting. Consistent with prior research in this area, we use a computational simulation model to study four IT-enabled learning mechanisms: internal electronic communication networks, external communication networks, company knowledge repositories and portals, and interorganizational knowledge repositories and portals. We also explore the interactions between a firm's internal and external learning strategies and these learning mechanisms under three different scenarios of partner size symmetry.

Keywords: Interorganizational learning, Knowledge management, Computational simulation

## 1. Introduction

With the emergence of global markets and the increasing need to bring innovative products rapidly to market, the knowledge needed to meet market demands is no longer easily acquirable by a single firm (Van de Ven 2005). Supply chains and other business relationships, enabled by interorganizational information systems for sharing knowledge among the partners, provide firms with the ability to rapidly learn from business partners (Larsson et al. 1998; Scott 2000). Current literature points to various examples where firms rely on their partner's knowledge to improve performance in areas such as product design, marketing, logistics, and research & development (Dyer and Nobeoka 2000; Lincoln et al. 1998; Im and Rai 2008; Rai et al. 2009). In the organizational learning literature, the “exploration” and “exploitation” learning strategies have been shown to have important bearing on an individual firm’s knowledge outcomes (e.g., March 1991; Kane and Alavi 2007). In an “exploitation” learning strategy, the participants learn from an organization’s codified knowledge (a knowledge base or knowledge repository). In an “exploration” strategy, participants contribute to an organization’s codified knowledge. However, there is little research on understanding the impacts of learning strategies in an interorganizational setting.

Within a single organization, through systems such as Enterprise Resource Planning and Customer Relationship Management, best practices place emphasis on integrated knowledge repositories that allow sharing of knowledge across the organization. In the case of knowledge about customers, for example, the focus is on a global shared view of customer profiles across all points of contact within the organization, which has been shown to benefit the organization as a whole. In contrast, in an interorganizational relationship, each firm usually has competence on specialized knowledge domains (e.g., product design, sourcing, and customer service) and may share its knowledge with partners selectively based on collaborative agreements and its own learning strategies (Gosain et al. 2004). For example, Tesco shares its knowledge of customers (e.g., shoppers attracted to Walmart) obtained through its loyalty program database with Kimberly-Clark, its skin-care product maker so that Kimberly-Clark can use this knowledge to design products that meet Tesco's customer needs (Rohwedder 2006). In another example, Shaw et al. (2001) report on the partnership between Procter & Gamble (P&G) and Walmart and the various ways they share critical marketing knowledge. In spite of the pervasiveness of such knowledge sharing arrangements, the internal (intraorganizational) and external (interorganizational) learning strategies employed may be different and the impact of these learning strategies on the knowledge outcomes has not been established in research. Further, although different knowledge management ITs, such as knowledge repositories and portals (KRP) and electronic communication

networks (ECN), have been shown in a number of studies (e.g., Parise and Sasson 2002; Scott 2000) to be useful in interorganizational knowledge sharing, the interplay between the strategy and technology choices is not known. Specifically, KRPs are centralized knowledge repositories where digital knowledge documents are stored and are electronically accessible by employees of the partnering firms. ECNs connect domain experts and others in the firm(s) fostering knowledge sharing during problem-solving and decision-making, such as through email or social network applications.

Therefore, our research question is “How do different learning strategies and the choice of supporting IT (learning mechanism) in an interorganizational setting affect the knowledge outcomes?” Answering this question would not only fill the gap in the interorganizational knowledge management literature, but would also provide practical understanding of how to leverage a firm’s relationship with interorganizational partners in order to achieve long-term knowledge benefits.

To answer the research question, we investigate the interorganizational knowledge management phenomena in question by modeling a dyad of firms that use different learning strategies and IT-enabled learning mechanisms within and across their firm boundaries. Given the past evidence on the importance of relative partner size (Subramani 2004; Kumar et al. 1998; Hart and Saunders 1998), we model three different scenarios of partner size symmetry. As Kane and Alavi (2007, p. 789) point out, building on existing computational models is an effective approach for “validating existing work, developing a cumulative research tradition, and enabling deeper exploration of foundational ideas than would be possible through the continual creation of new models.” Computational simulation methods complement other research methods, such as surveys, lab experiments, and case studies, by providing better understanding of and deeper insights in social science problems (Burton and Obel 2011). In addition, simulation-based research can be used to explore new concepts, ideas, and boundaries even when empirical assessment is not readily available (Harrison et al 2007; Burton and Obel 2011). Furthermore, the agent-based modeling approach used in our study enables discovering unknown phenomena emerging from the interactions of individual actors (e.g., employees in an organization). Therefore, similar to prior studies investigating the behavior of social systems by modeling the behaviors of the agents and their interactions, we adopt the agent-based modeling approach. This allows us to take into account the complex and dynamic contexts in interorganizational learning and to examine the underlying mechanisms by which KM IT impacts firms’ knowledge performance. Our results indicate that, in order to get the best learning outcomes, a firm cannot simply replicate its internal learning strategies externally. This is one of the main contributions of this research with implications to both practice and theory.

This research contributes to the interorganizational learning literature by extending the research on the impact of IT-enabled KM from a single organization (Kane and Alavi 2007) to an interorganizational context. In addition, by allowing the modeling of asymmetric relationships among

## 2. Review of Related Research

Organizational learning through knowledge exploration and exploitation was studied by March (1991). The three primary components in March's model are an external reality, an organizational code representing the organization's belief about the reality, and individual knowledge representing an individual's belief about the reality. Exploitation occurs when individuals modify their beliefs to adapt to the existing organizational code. Hence, the exploitation process diffuses knowledge among individuals. Exploration occurs when the organizational code is modified by individuals whose beliefs match with reality, thus creating new knowledge in the organization. March observed that, although an emphasis on exploitation strategies can generate quicker knowledge gains in the short run than can the use of exploration strategies, a sole focus on exploitation can be detrimental to organizations in the long run. Thus, knowledge exploration and exploitation are considered complementary rather than competing processes that enable knowledge creation and application in an organization (Nissen 2004). Kane and Alavi (2007) studied the effect of IT-enabled learning mechanisms on exploration and exploitation and modeled three types of IT-enabled learning mechanisms used in a single organization - 1) Group-based learning technologies such as team rooms, 2) Individual learning technologies such as email and instant messaging, and 3) Organizational portals that are used to store and disseminate organizational-wide

# ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

knowledge. Kane and Alavi (2007) found that knowledge repositories/portals and team rooms lead to exploitative use of knowledge (knowledge increases in a short period and plateaus in the long run), while individual learning mechanisms tend to show an exploratory effect on organizational knowledge (slow but continuous increase of knowledge level). They also discovered a number of interaction effects between the learning mechanisms. For example, the overall performance of individual learning mechanisms degrades when other tools are added, and when a team room is combined with the use of the other tools, there is a continuous knowledge growth indicating an exploration process.

Kane and Alavi's extension to March's model can be highlighted by two main points. While March's model assumes that individuals do not interact directly with each other, but interact indirectly through a universal organizational code, in Kane and Alavi, individuals can learn from individuals or nonhuman repositories that contain the knowledge. Second, Kane and Alavi's extended model organizes individuals in teams, which takes into consideration the effects of organizational structures on organizational learning. Bray and Prietula (2007) extend March's model to study the effects of organizational hierarchies in a multi-tier organization and the use of knowledge management systems (KMS) on the average knowledge level of individuals.

The knowledge management literature has looked at knowledge management strategies and their impact on performance. Kim et al (2014) used the knowledge-based-view (KBV) to model and study four knowledge management strategies – external system-oriented, internal-system oriented, external person-oriented, and internal person-oriented. They used contingency approach to show that the most effective strategy depends on the organizational IS maturity and its environmental knowledge intensity. Don and Yang (2015) studied the US pharmaceutical industry and showed that IT enables the focal firms to explicitly learn from external partners. Eccles and Davenport (2011) present a case of Cognizant's knowledge management platform that uses an electronic communication network to enable global collaboration and sharing of best practices among different business units.

While organizational learning has been explored in great detail, there is little research on inter-organizational learning (Mariotti 2012) and how groups or pairs of organizations proactively learn (Easterby-Smith et al. 2008). Inter-organizational learning is a complex phenomenon and Mariotti (2012) describes the process as involving the development of a common repertoire of know-how from which the participating firms can draw. Cricelli and Grimaldi (2010), in developing a taxonomy of knowledge based inter-organizational collaboration structures, position such structures as between market and hierarchy to facilitate the generation and transfer of knowledge. Blome et al (2014) studied the impacts of knowledge transfer on supply chain flexibility and found that both internal and external knowledge transfers have positive impacts on flexibility. Loebbecke and Crowston (2012) describe a knowledge portal that provides functionalities to identify and connect users based on their expertise to allow them to share their knowledge. Shih et al. (2012) describe a Web-centric knowledge sharing model for increasing efficiency in supply chains. In spite of the interest in inter-organizational learning and the increasing importance of inter-firm learning for competitive success (Crossan et al. 2011), the internal mechanics of how the common knowledge is created, modified and maintained have not been explored in the literature. Understanding the mechanisms that enable better learning in an inter-organizational setting will fill this important research gap.

In order to extend the computational models of organizational learning to an inter-organizational context, our study takes into consideration the learning of individuals not only from within their organization but from the business partners of the organization. While March's original model and its extensions focus on a single general knowledge domain, our model explicitly considers the complementary relationship between partnering firms by introducing knowledge sub-domains. Each firm can focus on its own core competencies and the two firms can complement each other's core competencies by learning from each other.

## 3. Research Setting and Model

IT has become an important tool in organizational learning and knowledge management. Hence, IT-enabled learning mechanisms that can facilitate learning at both the individual level and the organizational level are modeled in our paper. Established research and practitioner literature (Kane and Alavi 2007; Parise and Sasson 2002; Peli and Booteboom 1997; Scott 2000) provide the basis for identifying the IT-enabled learning mechanisms. The four learning mechanisms studied by this research are characterized by the scope of knowledge (internal or external) made available by the learning mechanism and the source of knowledge (human or IS) used for learning. Thus, we have (1) internal Electronic Communication Networks (ECN), (2) external ECN, (3) Company Knowledge Repositories and Portals (compKRP), and (4) Interorganizational Knowledge Repositories and Portals (interKRP). Knowledge Repositories and Portals (KRP) refer to information systems that are used by organizations to store and disseminate organizational knowledge. We model two KRPs - a firm's own KRP (compKRP) and interorganizational KRP (interKRP). An example of compKRP is the company intranet through which employees share work best practices with their colleagues. Individuals from one of the firms in an interorganizational relationship do not have access to the compKRP of the other firm. Unlike the compKRP, the interKRP is a common and shared portal for use by both firms in the interorganizational relationship (Loebbecke and Crowston 2012, Shih et al., 2012). Since the interKPR is inter-organizational, knowledge sharing in it could use mechanisms other than best practices, such as market mechanisms, to value and encourage contributions from experts across the interorganizational firms. A real-world example of using interKRP for learning is the use of extranet portals for interorganizational partners to search for knowledge related to interorganizational operations.

Adapting from March (1991) on organizational learning (OL), this study conceptualizes a firm's OL strategy as one of four categories: 1) slow on both learning and contributing (Slow OL), 2) slow on learning, fast on contributing (Explorative), 3) fast on learning, slow on contributing (Exploitative), and 4) fast on both learning and contributing (Fast OL). Consistent with the previous research in organizational learning (March 1991, Kane and Alavi 2007, Bray and Prietula 2007), an agent-based computational modeling approach is used to study the four IT-enabled learning mechanisms and their interactions with firms' internal and external learning strategies. Mathematical tractability is easily lost due to additional variables and interactions when social behaviors such as organizational learning are modeled using conventional analytical approaches (Harrison et al. 2007). Moreover, empirical approaches are oftentimes infeasible because of the difficulty and high expenses of collecting usable data (Carley 2009). On the other hand, the computational modeling approach used in our study allows us to have better control over variables of interest through the design of experiments (Harrison et al. 2007, Carley 2009).

## 3.1 A Model of Inter-organizational Learning using IT-enabled Learning Mechanisms

Our study creates a hypothetical dyadic relationship between firm X and firm Y. Firms X and Y both face a universal reality that is composed of m knowledge dimensions. This is consistent with the

way March (1991) in his seminar paper, Kane and Alavi (2007), and Bray and Prietula (2007) represented knowledge. Also consistent with previous research, each reality dimension takes a value of either 1 or -1. In order for our study to focus on understanding interorganizational learning, the reality dimensions include only knowledge specific to the partnership's knowledge exchange. Other dimensions that do not concern the partnership (e.g., outsourcing payroll IT within the firms) are not considered as part of the reality.

In the partnership, each firm focuses on its own core competencies and the two firms can complement each other's core competencies by learning from each other. For example, in an interorganizational system between a retailer and a manufacturer, the retailer may have superior knowledge of the merchandising, marketing, customer service, purchasing, and inventory management domains of reality while the manufacturer is superior at the research and development, product design, order management, logistics, and manufacturing domains of reality. The retailer can gain knowledge about how the manufacturer conducts order management through its interactions with the manufacturer. This modeling choice reflects real-life interorganizational partnerships.

Each individual in a firm holds beliefs (knowledge) on the m dimensions of reality as shown in

Figure 1. Each dimension of an individual's beliefs is assumed to take a value of 1, 0, or -1. If an individual's belief is 1 for a particular dimension of reality, it means that the individual's knowledge matches that of reality for that dimension and a value of -1 indicates that the individual's knowledge is opposite that of the reality (i.e., wrong knowledge). A value of 0 indicates that the individual does not have any knowledge of that dimension. This way of coding the individual knowledge is consistent with March (1991), Kane and Alavi (2007), and Bray and Prietula (2007) and allows us to model the state where an individual has correct knowledge, no knowledge or wrong knowledge on a certain dimension of reality. The individual knowledge level (KL) is determined by the proportion of reality dimensions that is correctly represented by individual beliefs.

The individuals in each firm are organized into a group structure. A group is similar to the notion of a functional team or a department in an organization. This study assumes that each group in the firm focuses on a certain number of knowledge dimensions within the firm's core competency knowledge domain. These knowledge dimensions are considered as the group's internal focus domain (IFD). For example, a retailing firm can consist of three groups (departments), namely customer service, purchasing, and merchandising. Each of the three groups covers a particular functional area in the firm and those functional areas are referred to as the groups' internal focus domains. Our model allows different groups in the same firm to have overlaps of knowledge dimensions. The internal focus domains and overlaps of knowledge dimensions modeled in our study reflect reality. For example, one group in Firm X may focus on the internal focus domain $IFD_{x1} = \{1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 13, 14\}$ (the numbers in the brackets are knowledge dimension numbers) and the other group in the same firm may focus on internal focus domain $JFD_{x2} = \{1,$

Our model further assumes that a group focused on the knowledge within the firm's core competency knowledge domain, could also, to some extent, have access to the knowledge dimensions falling in the partnering firm's core competency domain (e.g., through professional networks or associations). For example, employees working in the marketing department in a fashion-clothing retailing company may pay special attention to the knowledge about product design that is part of the core competency domain of a vendor company. Although the marketing group in the retailing company may not be an expert in the area of product design, this group of employees has better knowledge in the design area than the employees working in other groups in the retailing company. The knowledge dimensions that do not fall into a firm's core competency domain, but are of special interest to a group in the firm are referred to as the group's external focus domain (EFD). Other than functional groups within each firm, our model also allows individuals to join an interest group consisting of individuals who share similar job interests from both of the partnering firms. The interest groups are modeled because individuals from one company are likely to communicate with others who share similar work-related interests from the partnering firm. Given the setup of the two firms in an interorganizational relationship as described above, IT-enabled learning mechanisms in our model are explained in the following paragraphs.

Knowledge Repositories and Portals (KRP): There are two types of knowledge repositories and portals, one for intraorganizational learning (compKRP) and the other for interorganizational learning (interKRP).

The compKRP is a knowledge vector that has m dimensions, with each dimension corresponding to a dimension of reality. Firm X and firm Y each have their own compKRP. Each compKRP is updated by individuals with superior knowledge in the respective firm and it disseminates knowledge to all other individuals in the firm. Individuals from one firm do not have access to the compKRP in the other firm. In flat organizations, compKRPs serve the role of the organizational code conceived by March (1991). A similar way of modeling KRP has been adopted by Bray and Prietula (2007). The processes by which the compKRP is updated and how individuals learn from the compKRP are described below.

(1) Domain experts contribute to the compKRP (i.e., the compKRP learns): Domain experts are defined as individuals who work in a group and have higher KL on the group's focus domains (both IFD and EFD) than the compKRP. Domain experts can only update their IFD and EFD in compKRP. In each round, the probability that a domain expert contributes to a particular knowledge dimension in compKRP is $p_2$ . The probability that each dimension is updated is independent. When the knowledge value on a

particular knowledge dimension in the compKRP is the same as the dominant belief among the domain experts, the knowledge value in the compKRP remains unchanged. When the knowledge value in the compKRP differs from the domain expert belief, the chance that the knowledge value in the compKRP remains unchanged at the end of the period is $(1 - p_{2})^{t}$ where t (t > 0) is the number of experts who hold different beliefs than the compKRP minus the number of experts who hold the same beliefs as compKRP. Essentially, the knowledge values in the compKRP are determined by the agreement among domain experts (represented by t) and by the individual contribution probability $p_{2}$ .

(2) Individuals learn from the compKRP: Individuals adopt the values in the compKRP according to a learning probability $p_1$ . In each simulation round, there is a probability of $p_1$ that the individual's belief on a particular knowledge dimension changes to the non-zero value in compKRP. Zero values in the compKRP do not affect individual beliefs.

Similar to the compKRP, the interKRP stores knowledge in $m_{1}$ dimensions, with each dimension corresponding to a dimension in reality. A domain expert from either firm can update the knowledge dimensions that fall into the expert's IFD. Meanwhile, the interKRP disseminates knowledge to all the individuals from both firms. The processes through which the interKRP is updated and through which individuals learn from the interKRP are described as following.

(1) Domain experts contribute to the interKRP: The simulation begins with an interKRP characterized by no knowledge on any of the dimensions. In the context of the interKRP, a domain expert is defined as an individual whose knowledge in his or her IFD corresponds to reality better than the interKRP. The simulation selects the domain experts who can update the interKRP based on each individual's KL on hir/her IFD only (not the combined IFD and EFD). An individual's EFD falls into the partnering firm's core competency. Because the interKRP can be accessed by both sides of the dyad, the simulation lets the individuals update only their own firm's core competency domain. In each round, domain experts contribute to the interKRP their knowledge of a particular dimension on the IFD according to an external knowledge contributing probability $\eta_{2}$ . The probability that each dimension is

updated is independent. When a knowledge value in the interKRP is the same as the majority of expert belief, the knowledge value remains unchanged. When a knowledge value in the interKRP differs from the majority of expert belief, the probability that the knowledge value in interKRP remains unchanged at the end of a round is $(1 - q_{2})^{\mathbf{t}}$ where t (t > 0) is the number of experts whose beliefs differ from the

interKRP minus the number of experts who agree with the interKRP.

(2) Individuals learn from the interKRP: The interKRP not only draws expertise from firm X and firm Y, it also allows each firm's expertise to be disseminated to both firms in the dyad. The probability that an individual from either of the organizations adopts the interKRP's knowledge value on a particular dimension is called the external learning probability $q_{1}$ .

Electronic Communication Networks (ECN): Individuals in organizations learn from each other through the ECN. The ECN refers to a group of individuals, from the same firm or from different firms in the dyad, who share common work interests and use communication technologies as the primary means to facilitate interaction and learning. An ECN allows individuals to discuss work-related issues, ask questions, or to exchange ideas. Each individual in the interorganizational relationship has his/her own ECN. Each employee's ECN is comprised of two types of individuals: the ones working in the same functional group as the employee and the ones belonging to the same interest group as the employee. An employee's interest group can include others from the interorganizational partnering firm. The ECN that is composed of individuals from the same firm is called internal ECN, and the ECN in which individuals also come from the partnering firm is called the employee's external ECN. The processes of how individuals learn from ECNs are based on Kane and Alavi (2007) and are described below and their step-by-step procedures are presented in Appendix B.

An employee's internal ECN consists of individuals who are from the same functional group or who are outside of the employee's functional group but belong to the same interest group within the employee's firm. When learning from the internal ECN, the employee first assembles a subnetwork of the individuals in the internal ECN to learn from. The probability that an individual will be included in the subnetwork is $b$ . Once the subnetwork of internal ECN is assembled, the employee assesses which of these individuals have higher knowledge levels on all knowledge dimensions than the employee herself. Finally, the individual adopts the majority value of the expert group on each particular knowledge dimension according to an internal learning probability $p_1$ .

An employee's external ECN consists of individuals from the partner's firm who share the same interest group with the employee. Similar to the steps involved in learning from the internal ECN, when learning from the external ECN, the individual first assembles a subnetwork of the individuals in the external ECN to learn from according to the probability of $b$ . Next, the individual identifies the expert group of individuals who have higher knowledge levels on all knowledge dimensions than the individual. Finally, the individual adopts the majority value of the expert group on a particular knowledge dimension according to the external learning probability $q_{1}$ .

Our model allows all employees in firm X and firm Y to access both the compKRP and interKRP. Because of the wide availability of communication technologies (such as email and Web) in firms, individuals' access to knowledge embedded in peers, both internal and external, is also allowed.

The simulation model allows variation in the degree to which the interorganizational firms use each IT-enabled learning mechanism. This variation enables us to isolate the distinct features of each learning mechanism and to examine how the functions interact with each other. In a given round, the simulation implements the usage of each of the four learning mechanisms through a predetermined probability of selecting internal vs. external mechanisms (scope of knowledge) and another predetermined probability of selecting KRP vs. ECN (source of knowledge). The internal vs. external probability determines whether an individual will choose to learn from an external or an internal knowledge source, which may be determined in part by the organizational learning culture. The KRP vs. ECN probability determines whether the individual will choose to learn from a human knowledge source or the KRPs. The choices of a learning mechanism in different rounds are independent. The probability of external learning and KRP learning can be determined by a firm's learning culture and its IT infrastructure capabilities. In addition, these probabilities abstract the learning process in interorganizational settings, which depends on accessibility, the quality of knowledge management systems, and knowledge dependency on the interorganizational partners.

## 4. Experiment Design

The proposed simulation model allowed us to run a set of experiments to explore the impact of learning strategies and IT-enabled learning mechanisms on learning outcomes in an interorganizational setting. For these experiments, C# was used to implement a computer simulation for the modeling firms' IT-enabled knowledge management in an interorganizational setting. Flowcharts depicting the simulation steps are presented in appendices A and B. In the simulation, the groups in the firms are set to be equal in size. Choosing equal-sized groups maintains simplicity in the model while allowing us to capture the way groups use knowledge management tools. Although in reality an employee may play the role of liaison that can belong to more than one group, the model assumes that an individual belongs to one and only one group. At the beginning of the simulation, the $n_x$ individuals in Firm X were divided into $d_x$ groups and the $n_y$ individuals in Firm Y were divided into $d_y$ groups. The number of individuals in a group is $n_x / d_x$ in firm X and $n_y / d_y$ in firm Y. In the beginning of the simulation, the compKRP started with a state of “no knowledge” on all knowledge dimensions. The number of knowledge dimensions in the IFD of a group in firm X (firm Y) was set to $\boldsymbol{K}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{K}_{\boldsymbol{y}})$ - the percentage of firm X's (firm Y's) core competency dimensions in which a group in firm X (firm Y) specializes.

## 4.1 Contexts for Experiments

A set of experiments was designed with the firms' organizational learning (OL) types and KRP use as the independent variables and the average employee knowledge level as the dependent variable. Because employees are the conduits of a firm's knowledge, this research uses the average employee knowledge level as a proxy measure for the firm's knowledge competency. The experiments were conducted from the point of view of a focal firm that plays the role of either customer or supplier in a dyadic interorganizational relationship.

The effects of the firms' OL type and the use of IT on the firms' knowledge competency were examined under three types of relationship symmetry. Existing literature (Subramani 2004; Kumar et al. 1998; Hart and Saunders 1998) and evidence from real-world cases have shown that the size of a firm as indicated by the number of employees in the firm and by the scope of a firm's business are important factors for the firm's dependence on its interorganizational partner. Hence, this study modeled a focal firm under three types of interorganizational dependence structure. In the first, the focal firm and its interorganizational partner are similar in size. The study calls this relationship a symmetric relationship (SYM). The case of Wal-Mart and Proctor & Gamble falls into this category. In the second type, the focal firm is the larger firm in an asymmetric relationship (ASYMLarge). An example for this type of focal firm is GM's relationship with one of its small auto parts suppliers. In the third type, the focal firm is the smaller firm in an asymmetric relationship (ASYMSmall), such as the one of a small auto parts supplier to GM.

## 4.2 Treatments

A focal firm with its internal learning strategy in place (e.g., Explorative) may seek to find the best external learning strategy (e.g., Exploitative) when it faces an opportunity for inter-organizational learning. Because the primary objective of the experiments is to identify the best combination of strategies and IT-enabled learning mechanisms, we ran analysis of variance (ANOVA), not only to test whether different learning strategies and the level of KRP use affect the average employee knowledge level, but also to examine the best fit between internal OL strategies and external OL strategies.

This research considers three treatments for the focal firm: 1) the focal firm's internal OL strategy, 2) the focal firm's external OL strategy, and 3) the level of the focal firm's KRP use. The firm's KRP use has three levels: low, medium and high. The four types of OL strategies (Slow OL, Exploitative, Explorative, Fast OL) are applied to both internal learning and external learning. Because there is no strong empirical evidence or theoretical foundation to postulate a relationship between firms' internal and external OL strategies, this research treats internal OL and external OL as independent of each other. Consequently there are a total of 16 OL strategy combinations that can be employed by the focal firm. Since we assume individuals learn from either KRP or ECN, a low use of KRP indicates a high use of ECN, and vice versa. ## 5. Results and Discussion

Three 4x4x3 experiments were conducted for three types of interorganizational relationship symmetry. Table 4 summarizes the findings. When two or more strategies do not show significant differences in the average employee knowledge level for a given internal OL strategy and interorganizational relationship type, we listed all strategies in the corresponding cell.

The simulation was also run using parameter values other than those used for the experiments in order to check the robustness of the findings. The results showed that the general knowledge level trend held, although the degree of knowledge level differed depending on the values selected as input. Overall, the results regarding the impact of IT and learning strategies on average employee knowledge were robust and can be generalized to similar parameter setups.

According to the findings of March (1991), an Exploitation learning strategy characterized by slow individual learning and fast organizational learning, does not create high long-term firm knowledge, while an Exploration learning strategy is better in generating long-term knowledge. However, when external learning and IT use are introduced into the picture, the effects of Exploitation and Exploration on firm knowledge change. Our results suggest that Exploitation dominates the best external learning strategy category. Especially, when firms' internal learning is slow, fast external learning can compensate for the slow internal learning and help the firms achieve better overall knowledge level. We discuss below the results and implications for each of the three types of interorganizational relationships.

## 1) INTOL = Slow OL

When employees in focal firms are slow in learning and contributing internally, the firm's internal OL strategy is considered Slow OL. Under this condition, the Exploitative external OL strategy created a better long-term average employee knowledge level than the other strategies across the three KRP use levels. In particular, the best knowledge outcome was achieved when Exploitative external OL strategy was used in combination with a low level use of KRP. The outcome was the worst when the external Slow OL was coupled with a high level of KRP use. The results indicate that, because Slow OL firms were slow at acquiring new knowledge inside, to achieve a better knowledge level across all knowledge dimensions firms had to quickly absorb the complementary knowledge from their partners to compensate for the slow learning inside. In addition, since KRP causes the knowledge to become homogeneous in the long run, a low level of KRP use - or a high level of ECN - coupled with quick learning from outside can achieve the best results for internally Slow OL firms.

## 2) INTOL = Explorative

The internally explorative firms are quick at contributing to their internal KRP, but slow at learning from it. The best knowledge outcome was achieved when employees used KRP or a mix of KRP and ECN to quickly learn from interorganizational partners. However, when quick external learning was coupled with little KRP use, the knowledge level of the firm was the lowest. The results suggested that although an Explorative internal OL strategy allows firms to achieve higher knowledge levels of their core knowledge competencies (March 1991), this strategy is not the best strategy for external learning. An Exploitative external OL strategy was more appropriate for improving the overall knowledge level. The results also indicate that a medium or high level of KRP should be used in combination with the Exploitative external OL strategy. Because employees in internally explorative firms are quick at contributing to internal KRP, KRP can serve as an effective technology to disseminate individuals' knowledge to the entire firm. If low KRP is used, new knowledge learned from the external sources cannot be effectively brought into the firm. When this happened, an exploitative process could only make employees learn from the same knowledge, significantly reducing the variances in knowledge and, therefore, expediting the convergence of knowledge to an even lower level.

## 3) INTOL = Exploitative

When focal firms have high internal learning probabilities and low internal contributing probabilities, they have an Exploitative internal OL strategy. For these firms, a low level of KRP use, i.e., a high level of ECN use, would generate the best result of long-term knowledge level for all four types of external OL $^{1}$ . The results indicated that when firms were learning fast internally, the external OL strategy would not matter as long as ECN was the dominant technology used to facilitate the learning. High internal learning probabilities can cause knowledge to quickly converge at a low knowledge level (March 1991). To alleviate the adverse effects of fast internal learning on the knowledge outcome, a strategy that can increase the variance in knowledge should be used. A high level of ECN use helps employees find diverse knowledge, and therefore, creates a higher knowledge level.

Knowledge level was the lowest when KRP was used along with a Slow OL external strategy. This is because the low external learning probabilities inherent in the Slow external OL strategy prevent the complementary knowledge from being brought into the firm and KRP further obstructs employees' opportunities to increase knowledge diversity.

## 4) INTOL = Fast OL

This type of firm has high internal learning probabilities and high internal contributing probabilities. Similar to the Exploitative type of firms, all four external OL strategies allowed these firms to achieve the best knowledge outcome when ECN was used. The slow external learning probabilities inherent in the externally Slow or Explorative OL strategies coupled with high KRP use could cause the low knowledge level.

## Implications

When firms employed Slow OL or Explorative OL strategies internally, an Exploitative external OL strategy was the best option to reach high average employee knowledge level in the long run. The fast learning inherent in the external Exploitative strategy seems to compensate for the slow learning inside the firms, thus increasing the overall knowledge level. Since the focal firm has a lower knowledge level than the interorganizational partners on the focal firm's complementary knowledge domain, the firm's chances of contributing to the complementary knowledge domain are low. For example, Apple (focal firm) and Intel can be considered to have a symmetric interorganizational relationship in designing PC/Laptops.

Apple has expertise in the knowledge dimensions of product design and demand whereas Intel has expertise in knowledge dimensions of processor design and manufacture. Thus, Apple and Intel are contributing to a similar number of dimensions in interorganizational learning. In this situation where the interorganizational partner has better knowledge of the firm's complementary domains, a better strategy for the focal firm to acquire the complementary knowledge is through Fast or Exploitative learning using KRP. As Kane and Alavi (2007) found, when a KRP is used, new knowledge can be disseminated among individuals quicker than with IT that facilitates community-based learning.

However, the matching learning mechanisms for the internally Slow and Explorative firms are different. Slow OL firms should use ECN as the main learning mechanism both internally and externally because ECN allows the employees to be exposed to knowledge with high variability, increasing the chances of employees learning new knowledge. Because of the low contributing rate in the Slow OL firms, the use of KRP would negatively impact the chances for employees to find and learn new knowledge. The Explorative type firms, on the other hand, should focus on the use of KRP both internally and externally, because the high internal contributing probability in those firms allows KRP to effectively disseminate new knowledge learned from the interorganizational partner among the employee population.

For the Exploitative and Fast OL firms, all four external OL strategies delivered the highest level of knowledge when ECN was used. So, for firms that are quick learners inside, the external learning strategies do not matter as long as ECN is the dominant technology to facilitate learning.

## 5.2 Larger Firms in Asymmetric Interorganizational Relationships

We examined the main effects of firms' internal OL strategy (INTOL), external OL strategy (EXTOL), KRP use (KRP), and their interactions on firms' average employee knowledge level. All three main effects and the interaction effects were significant. Figure ASYMLarge (1) to (4) in Figure 3 plotted the interactions between EXTOL and KRP under each type of INTOL to aid the interpretation.

For the larger firms in asymmetric interorganizational relationships, because their core knowledge domain is larger in scope than their complementary knowledge domain, learning from internal sources is more critical to improve their overall knowledge level than learning from interorganizational partners.

In terms of IT choices, the use of ECN produced better results than the use of KRP. The explanation for the adverse impact of the KRP on the knowledge level was that the low contributing rate inherent in Slow OL firms did not allow the KRP to pick up new knowledge effectively from the employee population. Therefore, ECN was more effective for internal and external learning. When external OL strategies were considered, high external learning probabilities coupled with low external contributing probabilities (Exploitative) would produce the best average employee knowledge in the longrun. High external learning rates allowed firms to absorb complementary knowledge quickly, contributing to the improvement of the overall knowledge level. Meanwhile, low external contributing rates prevented the firm's smaller partner from mastering the firm's core knowledge domain that was larger in scope than the smaller partner's own core knowledge domain.

The knowledge level was the lowest when Slow and Explorative external OL strategies were used in combination with a high KRP level. Slow external learning inherent in Slow and Explorative external OL strategies prevented firms from effectively learning from their partners. Moreover, KRP inhibits employees from finding diverse knowledge, further reducing the knowledge level.

## 2) INTOL = Explorative

For all four types of external OL strategies, a low level of KRP use generated better average employee knowledge than the high level use of KRP or a mix of KRP and ECN use $^{2}$ . Similar to the Slow internal OL strategy, the knowledge level was the lowest when Slow external OL strategy was used in combination with a high KRP level.

## 3) INTOL = Exploitative

Similar to the firms with an Explorative internal OL strategy, high ECN use created the best knowledge level regardless of the external OL strategies. The knowledge level was the lowest when Slow and Explorative external OL strategies were used in combination with a high KRP level.

## 4) INTOL = Fast OL

Similar to the firms with an Explorative or an Exploitative internal OL strategy, high ECN use created the best knowledge level regardless of the external OL strategies. The knowledge level was the lowest when Slow and Explorative external OL strategies were used in combination with a high KRP level.

## Implications

When the firm is the larger party in the interorganizational relationship, the number of dimensions of the firm's complementary knowledge domain (the interorganizational partner's core knowledge domain) is less than the number of dimensions of the firm's core knowledge domain. Therefore, to improve employee knowledge levels, the focal firm should focus on improving internal knowledge. For example, the interorganizational relationship between Apple and one of its relatively smaller suppliers is asymmetric, where Apple has expertise in a majority of the core knowledge dimensions in PC/Laptop design. In this situation, where the need is for a better design and integration of the supplied component into Apple's product, a higher level of ECN use by Apple's employees provides more opportunities to learn directly from their peers, increasing the variability in organizational knowledge. This finding is consistent with Kane and Alavi's (2007) result that a KRP can hinder long-term improvement of knowledge level because it can significantly reduce variance in the knowledge.

The results showed that a highly effective technology for improving the average internal employee knowledge level was ECN. The results pointed out that the larger firm in an asymmetric interorganizational relationship can always achieve the best long-term employee knowledge level when ECN was used, regardless of the firm's external OL strategy.

When individuals draw knowledge from a KRP frequently, the knowledge among employees quickly becomes homogeneous, decreasing the opportunities for new beliefs to emerge. Consequently, high levels of KRP use may result in low long-term knowledge levels. In comparison, communication technologies connecting individuals (ECN) allow individuals to establish their own human knowledge sources. Individuals can select their own learning sources at each time using the ECN so it is much more flexible than a KRP. More importantly, knowledge accessed using an ECN is much more diverse. Hence, although the ECN is inefficient in improving knowledge in the short run, it preserves variance in knowledge in the long run, leading to high knowledge levels.

Our results show that to improve their overall knowledge competency, firms should improve their core knowledge domain (through internal learning) as well as their complementary knowledge domain, i.e., their interorganizational partner's core knowledge domain (through external learning). For larger firms, improving knowledge in the core domain is much more critical than acquiring new knowledge from their smaller interorganizational partners. A high level of use of ECN enables the firm to preserve variability in knowledge and, therefore, effectively improves knowledge level. On the contrary, for smaller firms, learning from their larger interorganizational partners grants opportunities to survive and grow in the long run. But, external learning can also be challenging to the smaller firms as the scope of the complementary knowledge domain is larger than their core domain. The results demonstrate that to overcome the size barrier and learn effectively from their larger partners, smaller firms should use a mix of knowledge repositories and human networks. Although there are good opportunities to find new knowledge in an ECN, because knowledge learned from the ECN is ad hoc, counting on it in order to acquire a large amount of knowledge may be counterproductive.

## 5.3 Smaller Firms in Asymmetric Interorganizational Relationships

We examine the main effects of firms' internal OL strategy (INTOL), external OL strategy (EXTOL), KRP use (KRP), and their interactions on firms' average employee knowledge levels. All three main effects and the interaction effects were significant.

Four two-way ANOVAs, one for each INTOL type, were carried out to understand the effects of EXTOL and KRP. The interaction between EXTOL and KRP was significant in all four cases. Figure ASYMSmall (1) to (4) in Figure 3 plotted the interactions between EXTOL and KRP under each type of INTOL to aid the interpretation.

In contrast to the larger firms in the asymmetric interorganizational relationship, the smaller firm should focus on learning complementary knowledge as its learning priority. The results demonstrate that there was no dominant external OL strategy that could deliver the best knowledge outcome for the smaller firms. The effect of external OL type on the knowledge outcome depended on the choice of internal OL type and the level of KRP use by the firm.

## 1) INTOL = Slow OL

When firms' external OL strategy was Explorative or Exploitative, a high level use of ECN generated the best knowledge outcome. The knowledge outcome was the worst when a Slow external OL strategy was coupled with a mixed use of ECN and KRP, and when an Explorative external OL strategy was coupled with a high level of KRP.

## 2) INTOL = Explorative

Overall, a mixed use of ECN and KRP produced a better knowledge level than the other two types of IT use across all four external learning strategies. The best knowledge level was achieved when a mixed use of KRP and ECN was coupled with the Explorative or Fast external OL strategy. The worst knowledge outcome occurred when a high level of ECN was used to aid a Slow or Exploitative external learning strategy, or when a high level of KRP was used to aid an Explorative or Fast external learning strategy.

## 3) INTOL = Exploitative

Overall, a mixed use of KRP and ECN produced similar or higher knowledge levels than the other two IT use types. The best knowledge level was achieved when a mixed use of KRP and ECN was coupled with the Slow, Explorative or Fast external OL strategy, or when a high level of ECN was coupled with a Slow external OL strategy. When a high level of KRP was used to aid the Exploitative external OL strategy, the knowledge outcome was the lowest.

## 4) INTOL = Fast OL

Overall, a mixed use of KRP and ECN produced a knowledge level similar or better than the other two IT use types. The best knowledge level was achieved when a mixed use of KRP and ECN was coupled with an Exploitative external OL strategy. When a high level of KRP was used to aid the Slow or Fast external learning strategy, the knowledge outcome was the lowest.

## Implications

When a firm is the smaller party in an asymmetric interorganizational relationship, the number of dimensions in the firm's core knowledge domain (the interorganizational partner's complementary knowledge domain) is lower than the number of dimensions in the firm's core knowledge domain. Therefore, to improve employee knowledge levels, the firm should consider improving its external knowledge as its priority. A high ECN use by the internally slow learning firms or a mix of ECN and KRP use by the internally explorative, exploitative, and fast learning firms yielded the best knowledge outcome in our model. As a result, choosing ECN as the dominant KM technology may be the appropriate IT strategy for smaller firms in asymmetric interorganizational relationships.

As far as the external learning strategy was concerned, there is no overarching pattern that applies to the firms. For the internally slow learning firms, both Explorative and Exploitative external OL strategies create the highest knowledge level under conditions of both high ECN and medium ECN use. For the internally explorative firms, an Explorative external learning strategy is the best choice under conditions of both high and medium ECN use. For the internally exploitative firms, a slow external learning strategy is the best under the condition of high ECN. A slow, explorative, or fast external learning is the best under the condition of medium ECN use. Finally, for the internally fast learning firms, an exploitative external learning strategy is the best strategy when a mix of ECN and KRP is used. The inconsistent results regarding the best external OL strategies indicate that there may be an interaction between the external learning strategies of the small firms and their larger partners. Because the knowledge outcome of smaller firms depends highly on the firms' ability to learn from the complementary knowledge domain that is contributed by their larger counterparts, the external OL strategy of the larger counterparts becomes important in deciding how much the smaller firms can learn. Therefore, further research is needed to uncover the mechanisms of learning for smaller firms in asymmetric interorganizational relationships.

## 6. Limitations and Future Research

Our study focuses on the dynamics of IT-enabled learning mechanisms and firm's learning strategies in an interorganizational context. In order to accommodate the need to study these dynamics, an abstraction of complex knowledge management processes in the real world is unavoidable. As Burton and Obel (2011) cautioned, a good computational model is not a real-world-like model but rather a relatively simple representation of the real world built with clear purposes. The value of our model depends on its usefulness (Shapira 2011), and a computational approach is useful when mathematical tractability is difficult to achieve as it still allows exploring new ideas, concepts, boundaries, and limitations (Burton and Obel 2011, Carley 2009). Although, generalization of the detailed results presented in this paper needs to be done with extreme caution, we find that our main results are robust with varying set of simulation parameters. Such analysis for robustness of new insights combined with follow-up empirical studies will increase the usefulness of computational models, such as the one in this paper, and make it possible to apply the results to business practices. In addition to the prediction of the impact of IT-enabled learning mechanisms and learning strategies, our computational model can serve other purposes identified by Carley (2009), such as for example, to provide a test bed for new ideas in interorganizational learning, to develop a theory, to provide decision making aids, to suggest critical experiments for subsequent questions, to suggest the relative impact of different variables (factors), and to generate hypotheses for inter-organizational learning.

Although our simulation model tries to maintain validity by following well-established modeling frameworks (March 1991, Kane and Alavi 2007, Bray and Prietula 2007), our study still has some limitations and the results must be interpreted in the context of the modeling assumptions. One limitation in our study is that the KRP in our model stores employee knowledge only for a short term. In future research, the model can be modified so that the KRP can accumulate and consolidate knowledge in the long run. Another limitation of the research relates to the types of learning strategies. Based on March (1991), this research identifies four strategies for both internal learning and external learning. There may be other learning strategies characterized by different values of learning and contributing probabilities. Future research may include study of other learning strategies.

There are other ways to extend our research. First, as discussed earlier, there may be interactions among firms' external OL strategies. These interactions can cause inconsistent results regarding the best and worst external OL strategies for the small firms in asymmetric relationships. Therefore, further research is needed to investigate the effects of the interaction. Second, empirical research can be conducted to identify factors, such as organizational culture and management support, which may be the overarching factors in determining the relationship between internal and external learning strategies. Third, the model can be expanded by considering a changing external reality and employee turnover, which add variability to the closed system. Fourth, additional organizational structure and technologies supporting those structures can be modeled. For example, organizational hierarchies and technologies supporting learning from other tiers could be modeled. Fifth, the effects of interorganizational partner's external learning strategies on the knowledge competency of both firms as a whole remain an interesting topic to explore. Sixth, this research studies the complex nature of interorganizational learning in a dyadic interorganizational context. Future research can extend the study to a more complex network of firms in a supply chain. Finally, triangulation for a given question we investigate can happen through multiple methods and different approaches (Burton et al. 2011). We believe follow-up complementary studies may complete the triangulation process initiated by our study.

## 7. Conclusion

Firms are increasingly able to access complementary knowledge resources from their interorganizational partners. Technologies such as cloud computing and social networks allow partnering firms to collect and share vast amounts of data. Data analytics and visualization tools make it easier to analyze such data and codify the resulting knowledge that can then be easily shared both within the firm and with its partners. Even as internal and external learning strategies are becoming an imperative for competitive advantage, the interplay between an organization's learning strategy vis-à-vis its partners and the optimal choice of KM technology is not well understood in literature. In this paper, we have modeled the use of IT-enabled learning mechanisms (ECN and KRP) within and across firm boundaries and investigated the firms' internal and external learning strategies. This study puts interorganizational relationships under the microscope of computational simulation to discover the intertwining effects of learning mechanism and firms' internal and external learning strategies on the firms' long-term average employee knowledge level. Because employees can be the conduits in organizational learning and knowledge management processes, the average employee knowledge level is considered a reliable indicator of firms' knowledge competency. The results from our study suggest that ECNs – the IT-enabled learning mechanism that allows individuals to interact with each other - are more effective than KRPs in improving long-term employee knowledge level in focal firms. In addition, we found that, if the firm is in a symmetric interorganizational relationship or the firm is the larger firm in an asymmetric relationship, the positive impact of fast learning from interorganizational partners is the strongest when the firm's internal learning is slow.

Our study belongs to the Analyzer type of simulation studies (Zhang and Gable 2014), whose contributions are in “predicting what will happen when certain conditions are met or providing prescriptions predictive of future outcomes.” The findings of this study contribute to theory development in interorganizational learning by identifying important factors for IT-enabled interorganizational learning and the mechanisms by which those factors interact. A framework is developed for interorganizational firms to select appropriate internal and external knowledge management strategies, and to build a knowledge management technology infrastructure for supporting those strategies. Finally, the simulation research helps improve practitioners’ understanding of how to leverage a firm’s relationship with interorganizational partners in order to achieve long-term knowledge benefits.
