## Abstract

Microlabor markets engage workers in temporary employment contracts to complete short-duration tasks for micropayments. Because microlabor platforms often preclude worker interaction, independent microtasking communities have emerged to allow workers to exchange ideas and interact to improve their work performance. Research has yet to take an in-depth look at how workers utilize microtasking communities to mitigate unpaid coordination costs to improve their financial productivity. The present study uses political skill as a theorizing lens to investigate how microtask workers utilize the capabilities of these communities that influence their ability to avoid financia marginalization. Using pseudo-ethnography and thematic analysis, we employed a sequential mixed methods design to identify how community capabilities and ideological beliefs influence worker performance. These insights then informed the design of an empirical study using survey data from 253 Amazon Mechanical Turk workers who use microtasking communities to test our research model. We found that politically skilled individuals use community capabilities, subsequently influencing their hourly wage. We also found that microtasking ideology weakens the effects of political skill on community capabilities and their influence on hourly wages. We discuss several contributions to the political skill and microtask literature.

Keywords: Political Skill, Online Communities, Microtasking, Financial Performance, Mechanical Turk, MTurk, Information Utility, Social Support, Crowd Work, Microtasking Ideology

## 1 Introduction

Over the last several decades, labor markets have expanded to include digitally enabled labor platforms, referred to as microlabor markets, that facilitate transitory employment contracts between employers and on-demand workers to complete tasks online regardless of their physical proximity to the requester (De Groen & Masselli, 2016; Di Gangi et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2021). Commonly referred to as microtask workers, these workers “take on micro tasks posted on a web-based, third-party platform in an open call by organizations or individuals in exchange for micropayment” (Deng et al., 2016, p. 281).<sup>1</sup> Typically, these markets maintain tight controls over how microtask workers are paid and with whom they communicate (Bergvall‐Kåreborn & Howcroft, 2014; Brawley & Pury, 2016; Irani, 2015b).

Unfortunately, these controls can subject microtask workers to unfair labor practices if they restrict workers’ free exchange of information regarding employers and microtasks. Absent the ability to share information, workers may be exposed to unscrupulous employers who unfairly reject work or pay less than the value of the work (Martin et al., 2016; Silberman & Irani, 2016). For example, a study of Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) workers in the United States found that workers earn slightly more than half of the federal minimum wage that US employers are legally required to pay traditional workers (Keith et al., 2019). Microtask workers are generally not protected by labor regulations and must personally assume responsibility for managing their wages. Fortunately, online communities not controlled or managed by microlabor market platform owners (e.g., Amazon) have emerged as a resource for workers to combat unfair labor practices (Berg et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2018; Martin et al., 2016).<sup>2</sup> Research has found that these communities are critical to workers’ financial performance (Yin et al., 2016). However, the qualitative and empirical literature diverges on how to examine financial performance.

Qualitative microtask research highlights the systemic challenges of microtask work (Brawley & Pury, 2016; Silberman & Irani, 2016; Wood et al., 2018) and suggests that workers share information about available microtasks to improve their hourly wages (Fieseler et al., 2019; Gupta et al., 2014; Martin et al., 2016). Quantitative microtask research has found that community participation leads to higher average monthly or total earnings (Di Gangi et al., 2023; Di Gangi et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2021). Both literature streams suggest that workers are financially better off when they take advantage of the capabilities of online communities, although financial performance varies from worker to worker (Di Gangi et al., 2023; Di Gangi et al., 2022; Martin et al., 2014; Martin et al., 2016; Yin et al., 2016). Examining how microtask workers utilize the capabilities of online communities could improve the understanding of differences in worker performance. However, information systems (IS) research has only begun to consider which individual cognitive traits/abilities influence workers’ financial performance.

Political skill has emerged in the organizational behavior literature as an effective predictor that differs from many social effectiveness concepts traditionally studied within the IS discipline (e.g., personality, social capital, emotional intelligence) to explain why some workers perform better than others (Ferris et al., 2005a; Ferris et al., 2020; Ferris et al., 2005b; Maher et al., 2022; Munyon et al., 2015). Political skill “enables individuals to exercise selected behaviors in a manner that appears to be sincere, inspires support, and engenders trust, such that they can effectively influence and shape the responses of others” (Ferris et al., 2020, p. 5). Political skill also shapes how one internally perceives the surrounding environment (i.e., intrapsychically) and focuses on using environmental resources for personal goal attainment (Ferris et al., 2007; Munyon et al., 2015). Among IS scholars, political skill has been examined in traditional work settings (Gerow et al., 2017; Rutner et al., 2015) and has recently begun to be investigated in online communities (Di Gangi et al., 2022; Matook et al., 2015).

While some light has been shed on how workers leverage these communities to realize financial performance goals, IS research has not examined precisely how political skill influences workers’ perceptions of an online community’s capabilities and their impact on performance outcomes (Di Gangi et al., 2022; Martin et al., 2016). Such work is important because Wei et al. (2012) found that politically skilled workers access instrumental and expressive resources in a traditional work environment differently than less politically skilled workers, which positively impacts their performance ratings. <sup>3</sup> Within the IS field, Di Gangi et al. (2022) found that political skill positively shapes personal, intangible capabilities (i.e., opportunity recognition) for MTurk workers at the dimensional level. Di Gangi et al. (2023) also demonstrated that workers’ relational social capital and microtask information quality facilitated the development of opportunity recognition skills, which in turn influenced total earnings. However, neither study unpacked the specific community capabilities that affect workers’ financial performance.

To address the mixed findings in the microtask literature regarding financial performance and to understand how political skill influences microtask workers’ performance, this study used a sequential mixed methods approach to examine how politically skilled MTurk workers utilize microtasking communities and their capabilities. Our qualitative study seeks to understand the real-world context of how workers define their financial objectives and how they use political skill to leverage community capabilities to attain this goal. Using pseudo-ethnography and thematic analysis, we identified perceived system evolvability, information utility, and social support as critical community capabilities that facilitate hourly wage performance. Moreover, our observations found microtasking to be goal-oriented, making political skill a suitable theoretical lens for understanding how MTurk workers’ performance differs depending upon workers’ ideological views toward their work. We confirmed these observations through a quantitative study of 253 MTurk workers and found that political skill influences community capabilities, subsequently increasing hourly wage performance.

This study contributes to the literature on microlabor markets and political skill in several ways. First, we provide empirical support for microtasking communities as a resource by demonstrating how their capabilities influence workers’ hourly wages. Second, we extend political skill to the IS discipline by considering a construct-level perspective of political skill to emphasize its relevance to microtask workers who use online communities, as suggested by the organizational behavior literature (Munyon et al., 2015). This perspective extends prior IS research that has examined political skill on a dimensional level (Di Gangi et al., 2022; Matook et al., 2015). Third, we develop a formative measure of microtasking ideology that moderates the effect of political skill on perceptions of community capabilities and the subsequent effect on hourly wage performance. From a practical perspective, we provide guidance to community and platform designers seeking to support workers’ financial objectives. In the next sections, we review the literature that informs our study of the role of political skill within microtasking communities.

## 2 The Role of Online Communities in Microtask Labor Markets

Microlabor markets use digital platforms to provide geographically dispersed, on-demand workers with access to requests to complete microtasks in exchange for payment from employers (i.e., requesters) (Deng & Joshi, 2016; Hong et al., 2021; Taylor & Joshi, 2019). MTurk has garnered the most attention from scholars, given its placement within a larger class of microtasking platforms where work is performed entirely online and offers low wages along with limited autonomy (Bergvall‐Kåreborn & Howcroft, 2014; De Groen & Masselli, 2016). A variety of academic disciplines have studied microtasking, including industrial psychology (Schmidt, 2015), computer science (Irani & Silberman, 2013, 2014), social science (Cini, 2023; Wood et al., 2018), management (Aguinis et al., 2021), and IS (Deng & Joshi, 2016; Deng et al., 2016; Deng et al., 2023; Di Gangi et al., 2023; Di Gangi et al., 2022), as either a source of data, a phenomenon of interest for critical inquiry, or both.

Bergvall‐Kåreborn and Howcroft (2014) and Irani and Silberman (2013) describe the MTurk platform as one that sets boundaries around the interactive space among workers and with requesters through interface constraints. These constraints have led researchers to identify systemic issues regarding unpaid work due to the coordination costs associated with evaluating and locating suitable microtasks (Hara et al., 2018), pay fairness based on hourly wages (Keith et al., 2019; Ross et al., 2010), requester unresponsiveness (Brawley & Pury, 2016; Panteli et al., 2020), and unfair labor practices such as requesters unfairly rejecting work, leaving workers unpaid for their efforts and without recourse for dispute resolution (Brawley & Pury, 2016; Irani, 2015b; Maffie, 2020). Some researchers have speculated that these microlabor market problems stem from a problem of worker invisibility (Gupta et al., 2014; Irani, 2015b; Irani & Silberman, 2014; Savage et al., 2020). The digital platform anonymizes workers, creating a cloak of invisibility around workers that deindividualizes workers in the eyes of requesters and limits their ability to aggregate information with fellow workers, leading to increased unpaid coordination costs (Hara et al., 2018; Irani & Silberman, 2013, 2014; Savage et al., 2020; Wagner et al., 2021).

External microtasking communities have emerged to address the problem of worker invisibility and enable workers to engage in goal-oriented behaviors, such as sharing contextual information about microtask work (Fieseler et al., 2019; Savage et al., 2020; Wood et al., 2019; Wood et al., 2018). While these communities are not owned or operated by Amazon, they exist within MTurk’s microlabor market ecosystem and serve as multifaceted resources for workers (Di Gangi et al., 2023; Fieseler et al., 2019), providing a variety of benefits to workers who use them (Gray et al., 2016; Martin et al., 2014; Silberman & Irani, 2016; Wood et al., 2019). These communities allow workers to interact socially (Fieseler et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2021), access opportunities for peer learning and emotional support (Cini, 2023; Deng et al., 2016; Gupta et al., 2014; Joyce & Stuart, 2021; Panteli et al., 2020; Wood et al., 2018), increase the visibility of their concerns about microtasking work (Deng et al., 2016; Irani & Silberman, 2013, 2014), and improve their performance through the use of community resources (Gupta et al., 2014; Martin et al., 2014; Silberman & Irani, 2016). This research highlights issues related to microtask work that contribute to negative perceptions of pay equity among MTurk workers, such as worker invisibility and unpaid coordination costs that impact workers’ hourly wages (e.g., Fieseler et al., 2019; Gupta et al., 2014; Wood et al., 2019; Wood et al., 2018).

In contrast, empirical research has sought to understand the social dynamics of the microtask environment (Chen et al., 2023). For instance, Yin et al. (2016) used network analysis to map the communication network of MTurk workers and found that microtasking communities play a central role in their work process. This research has found that microtasking communities enable faster access to information about high-quality microtasks embedded in workers’ social networks, leading to speculation that such communities improve earnings and other work outcomes. More recently, empirical research on microtasking communities has linked active participation (Ma et al., 2021) and skill development (Di Gangi et al., 2023; Di Gangi et al., 2022) to worker financial performance, confirming that these communities lead to financial benefits, with some caveats (e.g., Ma et al., 2021, found that social posting negatively affects monthly earnings).

Interestingly, qualitative and empirical research has characterized microtask work as competitive due to the limited availability of microtasks that offer attractive pay from “good” requesters while recognizing the need to sustain community resources through collaboration (Di Gangi et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2021). However, empirical research on financial benefits has examined average monthly or total earnings (Di Gangi et al., 2023; Di Gangi et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2021) rather than hourly wages, which has been the focus of qualitative research (Keith et al., 2019; Ross et al., 2010). This disconnect between the rich narratives of qualitative research and the precision of empirical research on financial performance has limited the advancement of the understanding of how microtasking communities support workers. Future research could alleviate this disconnect through an indepth exploration of the evolving needs of workers and the resources that explain their goal-directed behaviors in microtasking communities.

## 3 Political Skill in Microtasking Communities

Political skill enables workers to understand others and themselves and apply that knowledge to achieve their personal objectives (Ferris et al., 2005a; Ferris et al., 2020; Ferris et al., 2012). This description is expansive because it assumes relevance in all workplace settings (e.g., in-person, online). Mintzberg (1985), whose work inspired much of the political skill research, noted that all workplaces are political arenas where office politics thrive. As many workplaces have migrated from the office to online environments over the past two decades, the politics of the “office” (online or otherwise) have also migrated. Microtasking communities, built by the informal networks of MTurk workers, have recreated the proverbial watercoolers and breakrooms where workers share essential information and interact socially. Political skill likely contributes to these communities’ rise and subsequent leveraging because politically skilled individuals strive to achieve their goals by efficiently using resources to engage in goal-directed behaviors (Di Gangi et al., 2022).

Research has shown that political skill is an effective predictor of a diverse set of work outcomes (Maher et al., 2022; Munyon et al., 2021; Munyon et al., 2015), including worker performance ratings, financial performance, career advancement, and burnout (Ferris et al., 2005a; Ferris et al., 2020; Ferris et al., 2007; Munyon et al., 2021; Summers et al., 2020; Venz & Gardiner, 2017; Wihler et al., 2017). Political skill influences work outcomes by impacting individuals’ goal-setting abilities at the intrapsychic level (Maher et al., 2022), allowing individuals to recognize and capitalize on opportunities in order to build strong support networks in ambiguous social contexts and thereby improve their performance (McAllister et al., 2018; Munyon et al., 2015). This ability is particularly relevant to microtask workers’ use of microtasking communities to combat coordination costs and improve productivity (Hara et al., 2018; Martin et al., 2014; Martin et al., 2016).

Political skill helps to explain interpersonal interactions in the workplace and associated goaldirected behavior. Due to the flexibility of political skill, a variety of academic disciplines have used it as a theoretical lens, including marketing (Bolander et al., 2015), healthcare (Montalvo, 2015; Montalvo & Byrne, 2016), entrepreneurship (Yetis-Larsson et al., 2015), sports management (Magnusen et al., 2014), and education (Taliadorou & Pashiardis, 2015), and IS (Di Gangi et al., 2022; Gerow et al., 2017; Matook et al., 2015; Rutner et al., 2015). However, this research has primarily studied hierarchical, face-to-face work environments (Ferris et al., 2008; Ferris et al., 2017; Ferris et al., 2020; Ferris et al., 2012; Ferris et al., 2007; Gerow et al., 2017; Munyon et al., 2015). IS researchers have recently found that political skill is effective in contexts where individuals interact asynchronously in non-hierarchical online communities (Di Gangi et al., 2022; Matook et al., 2015). While IS represents a new domain for the study of political skill, we expect that prior research findings will translate to an online community context. For instance, Di Gangi et al. (2022) argued that microtasking communities are sociopolitical arenas where individuals use resources to recognize and capitalize on opportunities to achieve their goals.

Researchers have likened political skill to how skilled chess players examine their resources (e.g., pieces) and identify optimal movements (e.g., openings) that will result in an optimal outcome (McAllister et al., 2015). Similar to chess, microtask workers must examine their community resources (e.g., tools and information about requesters/microtasks) and then select the optimal movements (e.g., choosing a microtask, setting a priority order for microtasks) that will result in high hourly wages. The best workers demonstrate the ability to successfully evaluate other players (e.g., requesters and fellow workers), their own position, and the current state of the board (e.g., available microtasks) to facilitate optimal performance and achieve their work objectives. A critical difference between traditional and online work environments is how resources are constructed and made available. Online communities rely on resources that are jointly developed through coproduction by their members—in contrast to traditional environments, where resources are provided through the organization and its hierarchical structure (Dahlander & Wallin, 2006; Daniel & Stewart, 2016; Weber, 2004). Nevertheless, there is a lack of theoretical guidance on how microtask workers should best draw resources from microtasking communities, and the impacts of the underlying structure of these communities on politically skilled workers is also poorly understood (Di Gangi et al., 2023).

Political skill is a multidimensional construct consisting of four dimensions: networking ability, social astuteness, interpersonal influence, and apparent sincerity (Di Gangi et al., 2022; Ferris et al., 2005a; Ferris et al., 2020; Ferris et al., 2005b; Summers et al., 2020). Networking ability refers to the ability of an individual to efficiently identify important networks or individuals who can help them achieve desired outcomes and develop relationships within these networks (Ferris et al., 2008). Inherent to networking ability is the ability to recognize information flows within the larger social structure of a workplace (Di Gangi et al., 2022). In traditional workplaces, networking ability helps workers decide who to associate with because of the informational benefits gleaned from transitory face-to-face conversations with a limited audience. In contrast, the digital trace data created in microtasking communities increases accessibility beyond those present when the information was initially created (Agarwal et al., 2008; Di Gangi et al., 2023; Di Gangi et al., 2022; Howison et al., 2011; Yin et al., 2016). Research has also shown that communities can create information overload issues, suggesting a need for filtering capabilities to identify information relevant to one’s objectives (Fadel et al., 2015). Networking ability may aid in such filtering, helping workers to decide whom to follow before directly associating with individuals in a traditional networking environment. Moreover, in the context of online health communities, Matook et al. (2015) found that networking ability can combat perceptions of loneliness by providing social support.

Social astuteness refers to how well workers understand their environment, particularly regarding underlying motives, hidden agendas, and the actions taken by fellow workers (Ferris et al., 2005a; Ferris et al., 2020; McAllister et al., 2015). Such awareness enables politically skilled individuals to cope with adversity in their work contexts and manage the behavioral expectations that emerge from their social interactions (Ferris et al., 2005a; Ferris et al., 2020;

Maher et al., 2022; Munyon et al., 2015). In contrast to face-to-face settings, microtask workers must rely on textual statements or images, which limits their ability to detect cues to these motives or agendas (Di Gangi et al., 2022). Thus, socially astute individuals may operate differently in environments where interaction extends over a series of statements or actions.

Whereas networking ability and social astuteness focus on how individuals position themselves to recognize opportunities to achieve their work outcomes, interpersonal influence and apparent sincerity focus on implementation through social action (McAllister et al., 2018). Interpersonal influence refers to how individuals calibrate their behavior to elicit desired actions from others—for example, by sharing relevant information (Ferris et al., 2005b; McAllister et al., 2018). People use this influence to ensure that their needs or interests are represented and help them achieve desired outcomes. In face-to-face settings, workers can receive immediate feedback on their influence tactics, while in online communities, feedback cues are limited and feedback delivery is delayed. Apparent sincerity refers to how an individual authentically delivers a message (Ferris et al., 2008; Ferris et al., 2020). Collectively, these dimensions work together to facilitate an individual’s ability to navigate the workplace to achieve their objectives (Di Gangi et al., 2022; McAllister et al., 2018; McAllister et al., 2015). However, contextual differences between traditional and community workplaces suggest that workers must overcome certain challenges when using political skill in environments with fewer cues.

Ambiguity exists concerning how political skill affects task performance (Munyon et al., 2015). Ferris et al. (2012) argued that the dimensions of political skill reflect a core social effectiveness construct based on its initial development as a unidimensional construct. Rutner et al. (2015) noted that political skill is often modeled at the construct level (e.g., Gerow et al., 2017; Treadway et al., 2014; Wei et al., 2012) and revealed unique insights compared to dimensional-level studies undertaken in an online context. For instance, Matook et al. (2015) examined networking ability but did not include the remaining dimensions of political skill in their study of online health communities. To the best of our knowledge, Di Gangi et al. (2022) is the only study that simultaneously examines all of political skill’s dimensions in an online microtasking context. Further examination of political skill and its impact within microtasking communities could benefit from a construct-level examination building on the literature from organizational behavior scholars. This study aims to understand political skill’s relevant range as a theorizing construct in online work contexts (e.g., microlabor markets) that can facilitate worker’s goal fulfillment.

## 4 Mixed Methods Design

Mixed methods create the ability to “address confirmatory and explanatory research questions” that can “provide stronger inferences than a single method” (Venkatesh et al., 2016, p. 437). We used a sequential mixed methods design to overcome the disconnect found in the microtask literature concerning financial performance and the limited theorizing on political skill in relation to IS phenomena (Teddlie & Tashakkori, 2009; Venkatesh et al., 2013; Venkatesh et al., 2016). To elicit an understanding of the role of political skill role in explaining how workers identify and leverage community capabilities and to frame the financial performance metric of interest to microtask workers (e.g., average monthly or total earnings versus hourly wage performance), we asked an initial qualitative question: What community capabilities do microtask workers leverage within microtasking communities to achieve their financial work objectives?

To address this question, we utilized a pseudoethnography where the first author undertook a participant-observer role within several microtasking communities. We utilized political skill as a sensitizing lens to understand how workers identified and leveraged community capabilities to facilitate their financial performance. We used two open-ended questionnaires employing thematic analysis to validate the author’s observations regarding the identified community capabilities and political skill’s ability to facilitate access to these capabilities, respectively. To empirically test the research model developed from our qualitative insights, we then asked a quantitative question (Creswell & Clark, 2007; Venkatesh et al., 2013): How does political skill influence the community capabilities identified in the qualitative study to impact microtask worker hourly wage performance?

This study adopted a multiparadigm perspective with a less dominant/dominant design, where the interpretive, qualitative component (i.e., less dominant component) informed the dominant positivist, quantitative component of the overall study (Venkatesh et al., 2016). We concluded with a mixed methods question synthesizing the inferences derived from the qualitative and quantitative questions: Are the community capabilities identified in the qualitative study supported by the results of the quantitative study? Our qualitative and quantitative samples were drawn from the same population (i.e., MTurk workers who utilize microtasking communities). Appendices A and B detail our design choices, which adhere to established criteria for mixed methods designs (i.e., Venkatesh et al., 2013; Venkatesh et al., 2016).

## 5 Phase 1: Qualitative Component

We used an interpretive, pseudo-ethnography approach to identify the relevant community capabilities that workers employ to achieve their financial objectives (Sarker et al., 2018a, 2018b; Van Maanen, 1988; Walsham, 1995, 2006). In contrast to a traditional ethnography (Van Maanen, 1988), a pseudo-ethnography is an abbreviated version designed to create an initial working knowledge of a focal phenomenon that will guide future inquiry (Schmidt, 2015). While a pseudo-ethnography may be abbreviated and limited in scope, it must still rely on the same rigors of ethnographic research. Therefore, we followed Klein and Myers’s (1999) principles for interpretive research to guide our data collection, analysis, and interpretation, in line with prior research (e.g., Califf et al., 2020). Appendix C outlines how this study aligns with these principles and the key outcomes of the qualitative study. As part of this process, we used two open-ended questionnaires with thematic analysis to validate our observations.

## 5.1 Context

MTurk is one of the most popular platforms for workers seeking to complete short-term, well-defined tasks for payment online without physical proximity to the task requester (De Groen & Masselli, 2016; Kalleberg & Dunn, 2016). MTurk requesters solicit workers to complete microtasks (referred to as Human Intelligence Tasks or HITs) at a predetermined price (Irani, 2015a, 2015b). Several MTurk-related communities have emerged to mitigate the design limitations of MTurk, which prevents workers from sharing information about requestors and microtasks (Cini, 2023; Fieseler et al., 2019; Panteli et al., 2020). For example, Turkopticon is designed to alleviate the invisibility and inequity of MTurk workers by focusing on requester reputation (e.g., requester pay equity) (Irani & Silberman, 2013, 2014). Each requester is presented with a community thread so that workers can review a chronological history of requester behavior and summary ratings focused on requester behaviors. In contrast, Mturk Forum and TurkerView provide more traditional online community designs. Mturk Forum maintains several discussion threads to engage MTurk workers. TurkerView takes a more granular approach with the direct evaluation of microtasks and requesters, including tracking performance data. Therefore, the first author became a member of Turkopticon, TurkerView, and Mturk Forum for 60 days to observe workers’ interactions and develop a working knowledge of keywords, acronyms, and “turking” cultural norms.

## 5.2 Data Collection and Analysis

Following the guidance of Schmidt (2015), we engaged in pseudo-ethnography to familiarize ourselves with microtask workers’ perspectives and work environments. The first author used political skill as a sensitizing lens to become an active participant on MTurk and a participant-observer in the three MTurkrelated communities, refraining from engaging directly in social interactions to avoid altering social discourse within the community. The author earned US\$28.65 with an approval rate of 100%.<sup>4</sup> To ensure that a full breadth of microtask work was observed, the author completed a range of microtasks, including image translations, audio transcription, academic/market surveys, narrative or review summaries, and real-time experiments. Microtasks ranged in completion time from a few seconds to several minutes, with compensation ranging from US\$0.02 to US\$3.00. This approach allowed the author to experience and confirm the MTurk platform’s limitations in terms of information transparency (e.g., Irani & Silberman, 2013, 2014; Silberman & Irani, 2016). Additionally, the author focused on how workers utilized their social skills to engage in information gathering, evaluation, and assimilation processes to identify and access the resources and capabilities needed to ensure successful outcomes (Di Gangi et al., 2022; Ferris et al., 2005a; Ferris et al., 2020; McAllister et al., 2018).

Self-reflection helped the first author develop insights into how workers identify and use community capabilities to achieve their work objectives. These interpretations were shared with the second author for critical reflection and refinement. This process resulted in observations that could be abstracted and then generalized to the prevailing online community literature for translation into constructs for use in the quantitative study (Gioia et al., 2012). The interface design elements of each community were also examined to identify insights into how these communities facilitated the information gathering, evaluation, and assimilation processes critical to microtasking work practices. As a passive observer, the author examined discussion threads to gain an appreciation for communication style and discourse norms within the communities. The first author engaged in an iterative process with the second author to consider the interdependencies of the parts and the whole as they formed regarding community capabilities and worker objectives (Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1995).

Two open-ended questionnaires were collected and analyzed using thematic analysis to validate our pseudo-ethnography observations. Thematic analysis related “our data to our ideas about these data” (Coffey & Atkinson, 1996, p. 27) and established relevant data interpretations or themes (Boyatzis, 1998; Guest et al., 2012; Teigland et al., 2014). Theoretical relationships were then derived through an iterative process between coding raw information and interpretation by the researchers, with a reliability assessment to ensure consistency in the application of the coding process (Boyatzis, 1998; Teigland et al., 2014). The authors focused on understanding the implicit and explicit patterns, or “themes,” and situated them within the context (Guest et al., 2012).

## 5.3 Pseudo-Ethnography Observations and Qualitative Inferences

## 5.3.1 Author Immersion in Study Context

The first author became an MTurk worker using his personal Amazon account to obtain an MTurk Worker ID. His initial reaction to the MTurk interface was that it had an appealing minimalist design style. The main interface lists a series of microtasks available for completion, including a task and requester name, brief description, pay (“reward” in the MTurk parlance), time allotted, and creation time. MTurk provides a filtering feature so that workers can opt to see only those tasks for which they are eligible based on requester requirements. Tasks do not display requester pay or approval history, making it difficult for workers to estimate whether the pay is reasonable. A dashboard tracks completed microtasks that have been approved, rejected, or are pending approval. The dashboard shows earnings segmented by approved task, bonus, and total earnings.

The first author completed a few microtasks without using the communities to gain a worker’s perspective of MTurk’s functionality. Most of these microtasks focused on text entry or survey completion. For example, one microtask asked the author to transcribe a retail store receipt, including the item, quantity, and price. Another microtask asked the author to provide a brief keyword description of several images (e.g., yellow bird, red apple). The author completed this task using his mobile device with text prediction to speed up the entry process. Each task required relatively little effort, taking five seconds to two minutes to complete, depending on the number of text entries required. Requesters approved/rejected the HIT for payment to the author anywhere from a few minutes to several days later.

## 5.3.2 Defining MTurk Workers’ Performance Objectives

Once the first author became comfortable with the MTurk platform, he joined the three microtasking communities to learn what resources were available to support workers’ objectives. This included installing tools/scripts (e.g., MTurk Suite, TurkerViewJS, Turkopticon userscript) that extended the interface of the MTurk earnings dashboard to focus more on financial productivity. The TurkerViewJS script highlights the hourly wage and aligns all activities with this metric. This represents a critical artifact of microtask workers’ ideological beliefs, which represents the shared narrative among workers about microtasking and the importance of the communities and their role in shaping their work. These scripts align with workers’ central arguments regarding their beliefs about the inequities in microtasking work, characterized as pay fairness (Deng & Joshi, 2016). This belief shapes how workers define their financial goals and provides context about the relevant community capabilities workers can utilize to support their performance objectives.

Figure 1 presents an overview of the TurkerViewJS application programming interface (API), which highlights how TurkerView ties to financial productivity in its presentation of information to workers (i.e., hourly wage). A popup box over the requester details the historic hourly wage from all microtasks the requester has offered, along with qualitative ratings about pay sentiment, approval times, and communication with workers. Specific HIT information shows the real-time hourly wage average that workers completing the HIT earn, including the best and worst hourly wages earned by workers. On MTurk, requesters do not estimate the time needed to complete the microtask. TurkerViewJS aggregates and displays information reported by fellow workers on the average time to complete a task so that a worker can estimate the expected risk/reward for a microtask based on the effort required. Using this script helped the first author gain confidence in attempting microtasks that required longer time commitments (e.g., market research surveys).

It is important to note that not all communities initially focused on interface design elements that support financial productivity. Figure 2 highlights Turkopticon’s current interface, where pay information has evolved to align with an hourly wage model. Turkopticon initially used a five-point scale similar to the fairness, fast approval, and communication with requester ratings shown below.<sup>5</sup> This demonstrates the evolution of financial performance to productivity as a performance outcome used to evaluate microtasks. The interface change allows workers to evaluate microtasks by connecting pay with the effort expended, thereby helping them maximize hourly wages.

## 5.3.3 Identifying Community Capabilities

To improve their financial productivity, workers create complementary social resources within each community that organize information about a requester’s historical behavior. Workers are motivated to honestly represent the quality of a microtask to fellow workers in order to (1) minimize information asymmetry issues, (2) ensure continued support from fellow workers in contributing new microtask and requester information, and (3) ensure that “good” requesters have not been unfairly maligned, leading to fewer opportunities or a less desirable MTurk platform. Figure 3 shows the first author’s TurkerViews requester page, where workers can see how the author’s average hourly wage compares to US federal and California minimum wage standards, as well as several summary characteristics about the requester’s behaviors (e.g., no blocking of workers, approval of HIT submissions in a timely fashion, generosity of pay).<sup>6</sup>

Underneath this wage data is an area for workers to share the pros and cons of individual microtasks offered by the requester. On the page presented in Figure 3, workers shared comments to build rich narratives about a microtask or interactions with a requester to identify microtasks that align with their experience and financial productivity goals. Embedded within these narratives are their personal hourly wage and their completion time for the specific task. This allowed the author to see real-time statistics regarding the “true” pay value and time required for a microtask aggregated from workers sharing their experiences with the community. Positive comments often focused on the HIT being enjoyable or a personal experience dealing with the requester when an issue arose. Often, workers expressed positive statements about effort relative to pay (e.g., “good pay,” “worth the time”). Negative comments focused on HIT design or requester behavior (e.g., small font requiring browser zooming, rude requester replies, payment delays, rejection of work performed).

Workers provided details about their microtask experience to enhance the limited task description on the MTurk platform. For example, workers reported completing long surveys with “lots of bubbles” and “AC” (i.e., attention checks) to alert workers to practices used by academics to ensure data quality. Most workers did not disclose the AC itself but referred only to its presence to maintain the integrity of the microtask as a sign of respect to the requesters. Interestingly, workers engaged in self-governing behaviors and chastised workers who violated these social norms. Additionally, posting negative comments due to a misunderstanding between the worker and the microtask description or its requirements was viewed negatively by community members. The summary information and rich narratives enabled the author to identify workers who provided valuable information and “honest” evaluations about potential microtasks. The author also found that some workers engaged in “turking” during similar periods or shared a similar interest in particular types of microtasks (e.g., less repetitive and more opinion/content-oriented microtasks), making their comments more valuable because of the limited time availability of microtasks. Some workers’ comments may be more valuable than others; however, some communities have included features that allowed the author to Block or Follow workers to identify other valuable microtasks to complete.<sup>7</sup>

The pros/cons commentaries tied to a specific microtask were not the only discussion channels available during the study period for workers to socially interact. Several communities also maintained discussion boards to engage in both work-related and non-work-related conversations. For instance, Mturk Forum maintained discussion boards about general MTurk issues, requester introductions, investing advice, and goals that motivate workers to continue microtasking. Workers posted to discussion boards about their “turking” goals for a specific work session or an overall goal and advised new workers on effective microtasking strategies. Information about how to complete microtasks quickly and avoid bad requesters to maintain a high approval rate was critical to the author’s success. The approval rate history becomes more important once a worker achieves minimum completion thresholds because workers’ needs shift as they mature in their microtasking efforts. The first author was able to use this information to avoid disreputable requesters and maintain a 100% approval rating across the 100 microtasks that were completed over the 60 days.

Workers also engaged in non-work-related discussions. Typically, workers mentioned a financial goal to facilitate life interests/motivations, such as purchasing presents or a new car part or other personal endeavors. Such discussions highlight how “turking” supports workers’ lifestyle choices, aligning with an ideological belief about being an MTurk worker. The community supported these needs by providing a social layer to this goal-attainment process. Figure 4 shows the Mturk Forum goals discussion board, where a worker shares why they became a “turker,” with other members offering to help them navigate the more tedious aspects of microtasking. While the first author was passively engaged, several workers actively shared their troubles and experiences. These discussion boards provided workers with social support. The insights provided by the examples were bolstered by the numerous comments covering various topics.

The social interactions among workers also made it apparent that their needs changed over time as they became more invested in microtasking. While information utility was frequently the primary driver for workers to utilize these communities, many workers were also motivated by social support. For these workers, the discussion threads became critical as their MTurk “careers” matured. Through reading comments about goals and non-work-related topics, it became clear to the author that microtasking is similar to traditional work in that it requires social breaks to maintain optimal productivity. While the author did not engage with workers directly, passively reading this material was impactful when microtask opportunities were limited. Taken together, this shows that the evolving needs of the workers were fulfilled by community features that assisted workers beyond their informational needs.

## 5.3.4 Emergence of Microtasking Ideology

Over time, an underlying belief structure that motivated how workers utilized the microtasking communities and defined their work objectives became apparent. We refer to this underlying construct as the microtasking ideology, which represents the set of beliefs or values attributed to worker interests in terms of autonomy and fair treatment (Deng & Joshi, 2016; Irani, 2015a; Martin et al., 2014). The author observed that these beliefs are important because the worker’s ideology affects how they define their objectives for engaging on MTurk and the microtasking communities (e.g., pay fairness represented through hourly wages). This highlights which community capabilities are relevant to achieving a worker’s objectives. Whereas some workers focused on the communities’ information utility solely to improve their hourly wages, others became more socially involved in the community and sought support to maintain their wellbeing and establish an identity with microtasking. This social identity represented another aspect of some workers’ beliefs about microtasking, enabling them to maintain more freedom in how, when, and where they worked. Such beliefs align with what Deng and Joshi (2016) refer to as the “work value outcome.” Microtasking fulfills important values relating to a worker’s lifestyle and preference for freedom and choice and supports an ideology that broadens the notion of job autonomy beyond the specifics of a single microtask to one where workers independently choose which tasks they complete based on their own objectives.<sup>8</sup> Consequently, ideological strength varied based on workers’ focus on pay fairness, job autonomy, and/or the MTurk “lifestyle.” While most workers exhibited strong beliefs in pay fairness and treatment by requesters, not all saw microtasking as part of their lifestyle. On several occasions, the author was presented with multiple microtasks to complete and the information from like-minded workers provided guidance on which task to prioritize.

## 5.3.5 Role of Political Skill for Accessing Community Capabilities

Based on the first author’s 60 days of microtask work and observations from the microtasking communities, political skill emerged as an important factor that helped workers recognize and obtain the resources needed to facilitate their microtasking work. Identifying and leveraging important connections within the community (e.g., using the Follow feature), recognizing hidden agendas embedded within worker complaints (e.g., evaluating HIT commentaries), and displaying a sincere interest in fellow workers’ goals (e.g., engaging within the asynchronous non-workrelated discussion boards) were critical to accessing the information utility and social support needed to facilitate microtask selection and improve performance. Perceived system evolvability was a more nuanced observation in that it drove perceptions about information utility and social support based on the worker’s current needs or desires when accessing the community. Thus, we identified evolvability as a worker’s perception of the value of community features/tools that impact their productivity based on their current goals (e.g., hourly wage productivity). ## 6 Building and Hypothesizing the Research Model

The qualitative phase of this study addressed the ambiguity found in the microtask literature regarding financial performance. The results suggest that hourly wage performance is the primary financial metric used by microtask workers to optimize their microtask work performance. Political skill was used as a sensitizing lens to uncover the community capabilities that facilitate worker hourly wage performance. The pseudoethnography identified perceived system evolvability, social support, and information utility as critical capabilities that support microtask workers. Perceived system evolvability reflects the flexibility of the technical components of the community to align with a worker’s present needs, social support focuses on addressing worker well-being by mitigating stress and burnout, and information utility acts as an aggregation resource to facilitate the recognition of opportunities based on both microtask and requester characteristics. Thematic analysis validated these capabilities and the role of political skill in accessing them.

Based on our observation that microtasking is a goaloriented activity, we concluded that political skill is a suitable theoretical lens for understanding the differences in workers’ financial performance. The pseudo-ethnography revealed an additional moderating influence, microtasking ideology, which reflects various workers’ beliefs about how microtasking fulfills important values related to workers’ lifestyles and preferences—fairness of pay, job autonomy, and alignment with their “lifestyle.” We developed a research model to resolve contradictions within the microtask literature and to clarify how politically skilled workers use microtasking communities and their capabilities for financial gain. Figure 5 presents the research model, which was empirically tested in the quantitative phase to confirm our qualitative findings.

## 6.1 Political Skill in Microtasking Communities

Workers in labor markets that rely on open calls for participation face significant challenges in managing the quality of information needed to evaluate microtasks (Di Gangi et al., 2023). Similar challenges have been found in the online community literature regarding how to manage information flow to best utilize these communities as information resources (Fadel et al., 2015). Politically skilled workers have proven adept at navigating ambiguous work environments (Ferris et al., 2005a; Ferris et al., 2020; McAllister et al., 2018). This is important because the uncertainty associated with ambiguity often engenders frustration and anxiety in people seeking to achieve work outcomes (Chang et al., 2009; Hochwarter et al., 2020).

In our pseudo-ethnography, we observed MTurk workers using online communities to improve their microtask evaluation and selection process. While the tools/scripts (e.g., TurkerViewJS API script) played a critical role in meeting the efficiency interests of workers (e.g., Respondent 12 below), the social aspects of the community were also relevant to this filtering process (see Respondent 94 below). Workers used the pros/cons commentaries to evaluate feedback from fellow workers completing microtasks in near real time. Di Gangi et al. (2022) found that politically skilled workers developed opportunity recognition skills through microtasking communities to facilitate their HIT completion and bonus earnings. The commentaries provided politically skilled workers with the ability to identify and follow important connections through the Follow feature, which allowed them to understand the underlying social context of the community and helped them mitigate information overload and identify lucrative microtask opportunities. The data-driven thematic analysis also revealed the role of political skill in accessing community capabilities:

Through my relationships, I have been able to gain insight into the different types of tasks available, the best practices for completing tasks, and the different strategies for maximizing earnings. Additionally, I have been able to gain access to resources such as online forums, message boards, and Facebook groups which have been essential for obtaining timely information and feedback. Moreover, the community has enabled me to gain access to a variety of helpful tools, such as scripts and scripts/programs to automate work, that have been essential for improving my efficiency and maximizing my earnings. (Respondent 12; emphasis added for networking ability)

Understanding how the [redacted] community works enable me to accomplish my goals because it helps me wade through the vast amount of information on there. It helps me quickly identify which users are helpful and which information I should use and trust. (Respondent 94; emphasis added for social astuteness)

Political skill explains why some workers benefit more than others when using community capabilities to increase their hourly wages. For instance, one worker noted that the community provides community capabilities that meet their current needs, such as “new tools that can be used and just general status updates about the [MTurk] service” (Respondent 54). Perceived system evolvability is the degree to which the technical features of the microtasking community are flexible enough to meet workers’ current needs and interests (Di Gangi & Wasko, 2016). Politically skilled individuals view the community’s flexibility as meeting their diverse needs (e.g., asynchronous discussion boards for socializing and the MTurk Suite browser extension to alert workers to microtasks matching their interests). Some workers develop relationships via the discussion boards to avoid social isolation due to working independently or working in geographically dispersed locations (Martin et al., 2016; Wood et al., 2023; see Respondent 30). However, most workers leverage these communities to mitigate information asymmetry issues embedded within the microlabor market platform (i.e., scripts that aggregate information about requesters) (Irani, 2015a; Silberman & Irani, 2016; see Respondent 26).

Maintaining relationships with other Turkers facilitates information exchange and quick help when I encounter problems with my MTurk work. Also, these relationships and discussions about MTurk work help me feel that I am less alone when working on MTurk, which helps to build my morale and motivation for pursuing my MTurk work. (Respondent 30; emphasis added for networking ability used to access information utility and socia support based on a worker’s current needs)

I’m only on the forum to read and very occasionally share information related to the work. I don’t have any ongoing relationships with other MTurk workers, nor would I ever want any. I’m just there to make some cash and that sometimes involves getting or sharing info with other workers. We’re not buddies or coworkers. (Respondent 26; emphasis added to demonstrate lack of political skill with a focus on information utility needs only)

As discussed earlier, political skill has been modeled as a higher-order construct with underlying dimensions that work collectively toward achieving a specific personal objective (Ferris et al., 2005a; Ferris et al., 2020; Ferris et al., 2012; Munyon et al., 2015). In the present study, political skill defines a worker’s current needs and assesses the community’s ability to meet those needs. Thus, as a second-order construct, we hypothesize that political skill positively affects perceived system evolvability.

H1: Political skill is positively associated with perceived system evolvability.

Social support provides emotional benefits by provisioning resources within the community’s network (Herrero & Gracia, 2007; Johnston et al., 2013). Politically skilled individuals excel at forging friendships and other relationships (Munyon et al., 2015) and are able to expertly create and maintain networks capable of providing valuable resources built on trust (see Respondent 94 below). These benefits of political skill are expected to manifest not only in faceto-face interactions but in microtasking communities as well. Because the skills necessary to build and maintain networks are equally relevant to microtasking communities, politically skilled individuals develop strong networks to commiserate about bad microtasks, share motivation and kindness when needed, and build camaraderie with their peers (see Respondent 81 below) to tackle shared problems on discussion boards (see Respondent 127 below).

My ability to convey messages authentically and honestly allows me to connect to other people and feel a bond. We understand each other’s experience better, allowing us to help each other when needed or to provide information to each other. (Respondent 81; emphasis added for apparent sincerity)

It [The community] helps me to find/share hits with fellow members, and when problems arise, we can all come to an arrangement of what to do to resolve problems we detect with the platform. (Respondent 127; emphasis added for interpersonal influence)

Unlike in face-to-face environments, in microtasking communities prior conversations and comments are retained, creating an oversaturation of content that potentially decreases the site’s information quality (Fadel et al., 2015). Politically skilled individuals separate signals from noise and can better assess the utility of the information embedded within the community to identify lucrative microtasks to pursue (Di Gangi et al., 2022). Information utility represents a worker’s satisfaction with the usability of an information source within a microtasking community in terms of its relevance, uniqueness, reliability, and comprehensiveness for realizing their microtasking objectives (Johnston et al., 2013; Moenaert & Souder, 1996). Workers with greater political skill recognize relevant information and create networks capable of supporting their objectives (see Respondent 55 below). Within microtasking communities, workers utilize the wage history tracker accessible via TurkerViewJS and on the TurkerView requester pages to identify trends in pay and approval rates. They also share helpful information with their networks to maximize future benefits via each microtask’s pros/cons commentaries (see Respondents 8 and 96 below):

Understanding the community members and their experiences helps me to relate to specific situations that I may find myself in and what I should do to function well within them. (Respondent 55; emphasis added for social astuteness)

When I was new they helped me learn the basics. I also still learn tips and tricks to get better, but also help with letting each other [know] when good hits drop.” (Respondent 8; emphasis added for interpersonal influence)

By understanding the culture of the [redacted] community and adapting my behavior accordingly, I am able to achieve better results when interacting with other workers. For example, I am able to communicate more effectively by ensuring that my language is respectful and that I am providing helpful information. Additionally, I am able to build strong relationships by being respectful of others’ opinions, being open to listening to their advice and suggestions, and offering support and encouragement when needed. (Respondent 96; emphasis added for networking ability, social astuteness, and apparent sincerity)

Based on our pseudo-ethnography, social support and informational utility are important community capabilities that workers rely on to mitigate burnout and reduce information asymmetries. McAllister et al. (2018) theorized that networking ability and social astuteness create social embeddedness, enabling workers to recognize opportunities within their environment. In contrast, interpersonal influence and apparent sincerity facilitate opportunity capitalization to improve workers’ odds of achieving their work goals. In line with McAllister et al.’s (2018) theorizing, we suggest that politically skilled workers recognize the value of social support and information utility and access these capabilities to accomplish their work objectives. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2: Political skill is positively associated with (a) social support and (b) information utility.

## 6.2 Leveraging Capability Perceptions

We observed that workers’ current needs range from social support to combat social isolation due to the nature of microtask work to informational benefits obtained by providing critical contextual information about microtasks and requesters. Di Gangi and Wasko (2016) found that the perceived system evolvability of a social media platform positively influences perceptions of its relevance to fulfilling workers’ needs. On the social support side, workers construct relationships with workers to meet their information needs and engage in non-work-related conversations via asynchronous discussion boards that can be used to motivate and commiserate with each other. Ma et al. (2018) found that active participation in microtasking communities is positively related to affective commitment to microtasking, suggesting that socializing instills a sense of belonging and togetherness. We found a similar observation within our data-driven thematic analysis. One worker noted how they constructed their identity as an MTurk worker based on these relationships and explained how socializing within a microtasking community “provides me with a sense of community, and a communal identity (i.e., being an ‘MTurker’).”

In our study, social support was present, but information utility needs were the primary driving factor behind the use of microtasking communities. The separation between microtasking communities and the digital platform provider creates the possibility of dynamic change within the microtasking ecosystem. For these communities to remain useful, they must continue reducing workers’ coordination costs and information asymmetries by remaining constantly updated on which microtasks are available and which requesters treat workers fairly. Workers can do so by utilizing scripts such as MTurk Suite that allow workers to filter available microtasks and receive alerts about HITs that align with their work preferences (e.g., minimum reward, search terms relating to a HIT description, and eligibility requirements). However, a change in the underlying microlabor market platform may render the communities’ tools and resources obsolete because of incompatibilities (e.g., scripts embedded in MTurk’s API—see Respondent 137 below).

## …helps me keep up to date on the going on in the MTurk world. Finding some hits and qualifications. Helping with troubleshooting of hits and scripts. Finding scripts to help my [sic] turk better. (Respondent 137; emphasis added)

Community members may need to adjust which tools/scripts to use to account for new discussion topics (e.g., the introduction of training materials for onboarding new workers). For instance, in our study, completion volume was critical for new workers because requesters can limit microtask availability based on worker attributes (e.g., only workers with over 100 HITs approved and/or an approval rate greater than 90%). Maintaining a high approval rating becomes more important when a worker surpasses the approved microtask minimums. Thus, the technical platform underlying microtasking communities and community discussion board features must evolve to meet members’ changing informational needs.

Perceived system evolvability is tightly correlated with beliefs about system feature quality because it captures how well a system adapts to meet the needs of its users (Forsgren et al., 2016; Popovič et al., 2014; Wixom & Todd, 2005). Recently, Di Gangi et al. (2023) described microtask information quality as the ability of the community to reduce worker uncertainty about microtasks by enabling them to learn about available microtasks in a timely and accessible manner. Petter et al. (2008) support the view that perceived system evolvability correlates with system use outcomes such as informational benefits that impact worker performance. Thus, we expect the perceived system evolvability of the microtasking community to relate to social support and information utility. Thus, we hypothesize:

H3: Perceived system evolvability is positively associated with (a) social support and (b) information utility.

## 6.3 Community Capabilities Influencing Hourly Wage Performance

Social support and information utility mitigate invisibility concerns, feelings of social isolation, and marginalization through unfair requester labor practices. Each of these concerns (Irani, 2015a, 2015b; Martin et al., 2014; Martin et al., 2016), if not mitigated, can inhibit the ability of a worker to pursue opportunities effectively. Worker performance requires information relevant to completing one’s work. As one worker noted: “The community shares links to available tasks and indicates whether the task offers fair hourly compensation.” Workers use information aggregators, enhanced worker dashboards (including real-time hourly wage tracker), and scripts (e.g., MTurk Suite and TurkerViewJS to filter and find suitable HITs) in the communities to reduce ambiguity concerning task requirements and requester reputation for worker treatment (Gupta et al., 2014; Martin et al., 2016; Savage et al., 2020) and to maximize their productivity.

Social support and information utility play critical roles in shaping the ability of individuals to use online communities to become empowered to take action toward their personal objectives through goal-directed behaviors (Johnston et al., 2013). These actions involve exchanging information and engaging in social interactions that bind workers in the community together, helping them to overcome the struggles and stress inherent in the microtasking labor market (Deng et al., 2016; see Respondents 126 and 28 below). Such actions demonstrate that socializing produces emotional benefits to reduce frustration while maximizing one’s financial benefits.

It’s good to see what other people are working on and what issues they’ve run into. It’s also helpful to know what tasks are good to work (easier, higher pay, interesting, etc.) versus not. Last, it’s fun to blow off steam and just chit-chat. (Respondent 126; emphasis added for information utility)

Honestly, my connections on this platform help cheer me up, and they also give me lots of good advice about working on Mechanical Turk. These relationships let me keep moving forward and help me make good decisions. (Respondent 28) (emphasis added for social support)

Prior microtask research has produced contradictory results in some of these areas. Ma et al. (2021) found that social posting negatively impacts microtask worker performance. Similarly, research on technostress has demonstrated that social isolation and work speed caused by technological advances negatively affect worker performance (Califf et al., 2020; Tarafdar et al., 2015). However, since Ma et al. (2021) focused on the volume of postings, they may not have accounted for the qualitative impact of social support on workers, such as mitigating stress (Tarafdar et al., 2015) to combat the effects of social isolation on microtask workers (Deng et al., 2016; Panteli et al., 2020). Thus, community capabilities in the form of social support—rather than social posting—and information utility would be expected to influence workers’ hourly wages. Hourly wages assess the ability of workers to earn an equitable wage based on the effort needed to complete tasks and their pay. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H4: Social support is positively associated with microtask worker hourly wages.

H5: Information utility is positively associated with microtask worker hourly wages.

## 6.4 Microtasking Ideology as a Moderator

Recent research suggests that political skill may have contingent properties in terms of its effectiveness in achieving a worker’s objectives (Frieder et al., 2023). Microtasking ideology emerges to represent workers’ shared beliefs about the nature of their work and its potential to shape their goals. These beliefs are consistent with what Choi et al. (2015) describe as a set of shared values and narratives (i.e., ideology) that emerge within an online community (Bagozzi & Dholakia, 2006). Ideology significantly influences workers’ perceptions and behaviors (Choi et al., 2015; Stewart & Gosain, 2006). Our pseudo-ethnography identified three core values—pay fairness, job autonomy, and microtasking lifestyle—that influence how workers define their objectives. However, these values may not always align with work objectives.

Job autonomy and lifestyle integration may contradict pay fairness—workers may prioritize the flexibility to choose when and how to work over financial considerations (Strunk & Strich, 2023). We found that some workers elected to work during intervals of personal downtime (see Respondent 31 below). Jiang et al. (2021) suggested that workers will engage in less enjoyable microtasks that do not align with their financial objectives when their situational boredom is higher, choosing to complete microtasks to alleviate their boredom. For example, one respondent highlighted how the job autonomy of microtasking offers workers the ability to integrate microtasking into their daily lives, suggesting that financial productivity may be secondary to convenience during downtime.

The reason I complete tasks on MTurk is because they are a good way to waste time while making little money in the process.

For example, whenever I am at the library waiting for sports practices and have some time to spare, I just log onto mturk.com and make a little bit of cash. (Respondent 31; emphasis added for job autonomy and lifestyle integration)

Pay fairness was the most prominent shared value in the qualitative study, indicating that financial productivity is a critical objective for many MTurk workers. We observed that workers share information about microtasks and requesters because it combats unethical work practices and benefits individuals and the community (Irani, 2015a; Irani & Silberman, 2013; Silberman & Irani, 2016). Such beliefs also manifested in the design elements of the communities, such as the TurkerView display showing real-time hourly wage data for available microtasks. Therefore, our research model focused on the shared goal of financial productivity (i.e., hourly wages).

Political skill research has mainly addressed performance-based outcomes, finding that workers with more political skill outperform those with less (Ferris et al., 2005a; Ferris et al., 2020; Maher et al., 2022; Munyon et al., 2021). For instance, Munyon et al. (2021) found that realtors’ political skill directly influenced their mean monthly sales. Bolander et al. (2015) found that the political skill of home technology sales professionals positively influenced monthly units sold. More recently, Di Gangi et al. (2022) found that political skill affected microtask opportunity recognition, positively affecting workers’ monthly earnings. However, workers with high microtasking ideology, particularly those concerned about job autonomy and lifestyle integration, may contradict the financial productivity perspective. For instance, a worker may choose a personally rewarding microtask over a financially rewarding one or may be limited to available microtasks based on their lifestyle preferences (e.g., Respondent 31 choosing to engage during periods of downtime). Therefore, we hypothesize:

H6: Microtasking ideology moderates the influence of political skill on community capabilities and community capabilities on performance such that higher microtasking ideology weakens these relationships.

## 7 Phase 2: Quantitative Component

We tested our research model by surveying MTurk workers who participate in MTurk-related microtasking communities. A microtask was created on MTurk asking US workers who utilize MTurkrelated communities to complete a survey. US workers represent the largest proportion of MTurk workers (Gupta et al., 2014), and this population is accustomed to hourly wages as a measure of financial productivity.

Respondents were paid US\$3.50 to complete the survey based on their most frequently used MTurk community. A power analysis was conducted to identify a minimum sample size based on the research model parameters. Due to MTurk work occurring externally to the MTurk-related community, we assumed a small effect size would emerge for performance data. We utilized G\*Power 3.1 to estimate a required sample for our model with an effect size value of 0.1 and alpha of 0.05 for four predictor variables (Faul et al., 2009; Faul et al., 2007). A minimum sample of 125 respondents was required to achieve 80% statistical power.

## 7.1 Survey Development

All constructs were measured using seven-point Likert scales. We measured political skill using the 18-item instrument developed by Ferris et al. (2005b). Prior use of the political skill instrument did not include specific reference to an organization or community because the instrument has historically focused on individuals’ innate political skill. During the qualitative phase, the openended questionnaire included a pilot study of the political skill instrument to assess its suitability for the present study’s context.<sup>9</sup> Since we focused on the respondent’s most frequently used community, the survey items included the name of the community. Minor revisions were made to two items from social astuteness and one item from interpersonal influence to improve discriminant validity. Political skill was modeled as a first-order reflective, second-order formative construct to reflect its dimensionality.

Perceived system evolvability was adapted from Di Gangi and Wasko (2016), who explored the evolutionary efforts by social media platforms to meet user needs and interests. This instrument is based on the flexibility construct from Wixom and Todd (2005) and focuses on the technical aspects of the microtasking community. We created two items based on the data-driven thematic analysis, referencing the technical resources (e.g., APIs, scripts, programs) or community features that help workers meet their current microtasking needs. Information utility was adapted from Moenaert and Souder (1996). Four items captured information embedded within the community about HITs and requesters. These items align with our data-driven thematic analysis results (i.e., microtask quality and requester reputation), where workers focused on information about “good” HITs and requesters. We adapted social support from Johnston et al. (2013), with six items reflecting the ability of the community to provide support in managing stress and emotional support. This measure also aligns with our data-driven thematic analysis, where workers stated interest in using these communities to reduce stress and feelings of belonging.

We developed a three-item formative measure to represent microtasking ideology, with items capturing pay fairness, job autonomy, and lifestyle integration perceptions derived from prior MTurk literature (Deng & Joshi, 2016; Deng et al., 2016) and observations from the pseudo-ethnography. The overarching concept of ideology requires measuring disparate beliefs about the nature of microtasking work and its broader lifestyle, which did not necessarily covary. H6 argues that microtasking ideology would weaken the relationships within our research model. Therefore, a sample split based on the mean of microtasking ideology—high (n = 138) and low (n = 115)—created roughly equal groups for the moderation analysis.

Workers’ financial performance was measured as the hourly wage based on worker interest in financial productivity. The hourly wage indicates whether microtask workers are maximizing their productivity and achieving fair compensation based on a combination of time commitment and pay. We asked respondents to selfreport their hourly wage based on an estimate from their daily work activity on MTurk. Additionally, respondents self-reported their MTurk performance data and uploaded an image of their MTurk dashboard, which included their objective performance data (e.g., total earnings, approved HITs). By utilizing the image capture approach, we were able to conduct a robustness check of the research model using total earnings. We captured control variables on worker demographics and MTurk-specific attributes, including age, education, ethnicity, gender, MTurkrelated community name (multicategorical), the number of communities a worker participates in, community tenure (months), MTurk tenure (months), qualifications (page count), master worker (dummy coded), approved HITs, and total earnings.

## 8.2 Robustness Check

We conducted a robustness check using MTurk total earnings as a dependent variable to determine whether community capabilities retained their influence on worker financial performance. The robustness check model indicated excellent fit (CMIN/DF = 1.58, CFI = 0.97, RMSEA = 0.05 (p-close = 0.54)). ## 8.3 Post Hoc Analyses

We conducted post hoc analyses to identify other relationships that might better explain the data. We first tested the relationship between political skill and hourly wage, with insignificant results regardless of workers’ microtasking ideology level. Perceived system evolvability on hourly wage was also insignificant. Our analysis did not find a significant relationship between social support and hourly wage. Ma et al. (2021, (p. 14) argued that “posting off-task, nonexpertise topics is an effective way to build social relationships with other members (Maloney-Krichmar & Preece, 2005).” Such a perspective could suggest an interpersonal influence strategy to strengthen social ties with members that provide informational benefits through commiserating about MTurk work conditions. Therefore, we examined whether social support impacts information utility directly. We found a significant, positive relationship between social support and information utility for individuals with low microtasking ideology (β = 0.307, p = 0.002), which slightly increased the explained variance of information utility to 44.2% compared to the research model (39.7%). This relationship was insignificant for high microtasking ideology workers (p = 0.380). When adding the relationship between social support and information utility, the relationship between political skill and information utility became insignificant, regardless of microtasking ideology. A review of the AIC value of the revised model shows a slight decrease relative to the research model (1.37), indicating that the revised model was slightly better suited to the data.

The revised model suggests that political skill may only affect information utility indirectly via perceived system evolvability and social support. ## 9 Discussion

This study aimed to address contradictions within the qualitative and empirical microtask literature concerning the operationalization of financial performance and improve the understanding of differences in worker performance among microtasking community users. Our pseudo-ethnography used a sequential mixed methods approach (Venkatesh et al., 2013; Venkatesh et al., 2016) to identify the community capabilities that provide information about microtask work and avenues for socializing to facilitate workers’ financial productivity as their needs evolve. The pseudoethnography also uncovered an underlying belief structure (i.e., microtasking ideology) that influences how workers access community capabilities and define their objectives. The qualitative component of our sequential mixed methods approach enhanced our understanding of the microtasking context to ensure that our research model included all relevant constructs and control variables. We then empirically examined how political skill, community capabilities, and microtasking ideology explain why some workers achieve better financial performance than others.

Due to our mixed methods design, this study makes several contributions. Although qualitative research has consistently noted the positive impact of microworker communities on worker performance (e.g., Martin et al., 2014; Wood et al., 2018), few studies have empirically linked workers’ cognitive traits or abilities to financial performance (Di Gangi et al., 2023; Di Gangi et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2021; Mourelatos et al., 2022). Our qualitative study advances the microtask literature in three ways. First, the empirical literature and workers real-world financial interests are misaligned. Prior research uses financial metrics that do not consider the trade-off between pay and work effort, masking unpaid coordination costs (e.g., Hara et al., 2018). Second, workers develop a microtask ideology centered around pay fairness, job autonomy, and lifestyle integration. We observed these ideological values within the underlying design elements of the tools/scripts (e.g., aggregating average time to complete and real-time hourly wages of prior workers completing a microtask) and features of the microtasking communities and the social discourse among its members (e.g., pros/cons commentaries and asynchronous non-work-related discussion). Lastly, we found that political skill is a viable theoretical lens to understand how workers leverage community capabilities and focus on financial productivity.

We grounded our theorizing in three assumptions: (1) political skill can influence perceptions about social and technological artifacts, (2) microtasking is a dynamic activity requiring community capabilities that meet workers’ current needs/interests, and (3) political skill is goal oriented. Our quantitative study subsequently confirmed our qualitative observations, showing that political skill directly influences perceived system evolvability (H1). This finding provides insights into how politically skilled workers select capabilities to utilize in a community based on their current needs/interests. The present study adds depth to the understanding of this relationship by demonstrating that politically skilled individuals benefit from the transparency of microtasking communities, including the social support (H2a) and information utility (H2b) these communities provide.

This study builds upon prior research by empirically linking political skill to the ability to leverage community capabilities to increase hourly wages. While Di Gangi et al. (2022) identified a positive relationship between political skill and opportunity recognition, they assumed that workers develop this intangible, personal skill from the resources embedded within the community. In contrast, Di Gangi et al. (2023) used social capital to explain the development of microtask information quality, arguing that community capabilities improve worker performance. This study closes the loop by simultaneously examining political skill and community capabilities to explain financial performance differences among workers utilizing microtasking communities.

In the present study, perceived system evolvability impacted perceptions of social support (H3a) and information utility (H3b). As microtask workers become better connected, they keep the stress of microtasking in check (e.g., Respondent 30 discussing social isolation). Informational benefits allowed community members to gain work efficiencies, enabling them to select requestors that maximize hourly wage performance (H5). Interestingly, social support did not influence hourly wage (H4). The datadriven thematic analysis found that information utility was mentioned more frequently than social support, suggesting that social support may be relevant only to a subset of community members (see Appendix D, Table D1). In the post hoc analyses, the relationship between political skill and information utility was fully mediated for low microtasking ideology workers via perceived system evolvability and social support. This suggests that politically skilled workers use social posting to strengthen their network as an interpersonal influence strategy to access informational benefits.

This conclusion finds support in prior research. Ma et al. (2021) argued that social posting enables workers to build strong social relationships (Maloney-Krichmar & Preece, 2005; Yan & Tan, 2014). The results indicate that politically skilled workers engage in such activities as an influence tactic to strengthen their information network by socially identifying with fellow members’ microtasking struggles. Shared emotional distress builds empathy within a community, which the social capital literature has found increases members’ willingness to contribute knowledge (Zhao et al., 2013). Politically skilled workers may liken such activities to a common chess strategy to sacrifice a pawn to achieve a strategic objective. Such an argument aligns with Ihl et al.’s (2020) instrumental social support perspective, where social support enables workers to gather direct, workrelated advice to accomplish specific goals leading to optimal outcomes. Interestingly, the relationship between social support and information utility was only relevant for politically skilled workers with low microtasking ideology.

The qualitative study identified microtasking ideology as an influential concept that defines workers’ goals for engaging within a microlabor market. This study theorized microtasking ideology as a moderator because workers can have multiple goals—pay fairness, job autonomy, and lifestyle integration. Job autonomy and lifestyle integration may counter pay fairness because some workers may sacrifice productivity for flexibility. Our research model focused on a single aspect of microtasking ideology (i.e., pay fairness), operationalized as hourly wage performance. Since politically skilled workers are goal oriented (Ferris et al., 2020; McAllister et al., 2018; McAllister et al., 2015; Munyon et al., 2015), we theorized that high ideology would weaken political skill’s impact on workers’ financial productivity. Our quantitative results support this argument (H6). Political skill requires a contextual understanding of the dependent variable, regardless of whether direct relationships are hypothesized.

## 9.1 Implications for Research

Our findings have several implications for research. This study demonstrates the utility of political skill as a social effectiveness concept for explaining an individual’s ability to navigate online communities and obtain the resources necessary to achieve their work objectives. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the first to empirically support political skill at the construct level in an online community or microtasking context (Di Gangi et al., 2022; Matook et al., 2015). Future research should further compare political skill at the dimensional and construct levels to identify further implications for IS researchers.

While Di Gangi et al. (2022) focused their efforts on demonstrating a conceptual link between political skill’s dimensions and average monthly earnings performance, the present study takes a pay fairness perspective, a key concern raised among MTurk workers (Brawley & Pury, 2016; Irani, 2015a). This study aligns the empirical microtask literature with the real-world worker financial interests highlighted in our qualitative study and confirmed in our quantitative study. Whereas Ma et al. (2021) found a negative relationship between social posting and financial performance, our study found that no such relationship exists when the focus is on hourly wages. However, in our study, social support did impact hourly wages via information utility for low microtask ideology workers. Further research is needed to understand differences in these financial metrics based on members’ ideological views and community capabilities.

Moreover, the present study extends the understanding of political skill in a microtasking context and offers implications for both empirical and theoretical development. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents one of the first to empirically examine ideological beliefs and their moderating effects on political skill and community capabilities. Microtasking ideology was developed as a formative measure based on our qualitative insights and Deng and Joshi’s (2016) work value outcome concept. Political skill is goal oriented and has historically focused on financial productivity (e.g., realtors and home technology sales professionals) (Bolander et al., 2015; Munyon et al., 2021). Alternative contexts may exist where ideology could act as a divergent or convergent factor. Researchers could use our formative measure to control for workers’ ideological beliefs in future studies on microtasking performance or to further investigate the role of microtasking ideology on worker behaviors.

This study may be the first to theorize how ideology moderates political skill, regardless of its context. Prior research suggests that political skill possesses contingent properties, calling into question its effectiveness in achieving a worker’s objectives (Frieder et al., 2023). Frieder et al. (2023) note that the extent to which a work environment is politicized varies extensively (Pfeffer, 1981) and that such contexts may exist that constrain or enhance the effectiveness of worker behaviors (Johns, 2006, 2018). Microtasking communities operate with a cooperative dynamic— workers must collaborate to share relevant information but compete due to the limited availability of microtasks (Di Gangi et al., 2023). The present study adds credence to Frieder et al.’s perspective by finding that microtasking ideology moderates political skill.

Future research should further explore the relationship between political skill and ideology. For example, open source software communities may operate similarly when entrepreneurs or corporate developers cooperate with a community to enhance their product or service offerings (Daniel et al., 2018; Yetis-Larsson et al., 2019; Yetis-Larsson et al., 2015), suggesting that politically skilled developers may enjoy different benefits. Daniel et al. (2018) found that a developer’s ideological fit has implications for both the community and the developer’s organization, suggesting that further exploration of this concept within the political skill literature and IT-enabled work contexts may prove valuable. Therefore, research should examine whether ideology operates similarly when ideological beliefs align with an organization’s values/mission.

Our study also draws an empirical connection to the role of community capabilities as mediators between political skill and worker performance. Prior research has shown that political skill is similar to social capital (Di Gangi et al., 2023) in that it does not appear to not extend its influence beyond the virtual boundaries of the community at the dimensional or construct levels (Di Gangi et al., 2022). Research has also demonstrated a modest correlation between political skill and work performance (Munyon et al., 2015); however, the present study found that this relationship was mediated by perceived system evolvability and information utility. Further, while a prior study showed that worker characteristics affect performance, it did not examine workers who utilize microtasking communities (Mourelatos et al., 2022). In looking at these points from prior research as a whole, it would seem that the relationship between political skill and productivity may benefit from an understanding of how politically skilled workers become more productive through better utilization of their resources and, subsequently, how the evolution of their need for resources further impacts their productivity. As mentioned earlier, political skill exterts intrapsychical impacts, shaping how individuals view their environment and its resources to attain their goals. Future research should explore how other social effectiveness constructs shape perceptions and objectives and how political skill interacts with communities to increase work productivity.

The present study offers guidance for scholars interested in examining microtasking and the broader microlabor market. Our qualitative inferences are based on our pseudo-ethnography and the microtask literature that examined how workers organize and leverage resources to perform their work activities (Di Gangi et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2021; Martin et al., 2016). Combining the first author’s experiences with the literature provided insights into the community capabilities critical for workers to function efficiently and effectively in a microlabor marketplace. Our analysis surfaced information utility, perceived system evolvability, and social support as constructs relevant to understanding microtasking communities that may be useful to future researchers interested in microtask worker behavior. We found evidence that workers perceive information asymmetries within the microtasking labor market and resolve these issues by leveraging emergent resources (i.e., communities that are flexible to a worker’s needs while remaining separate from the labor market platform).

Prior microtask research has focused on the explicit community capabilities of social and informational support. Our study goes beyond prior work by including the perceived system evolvability of a community. By considering this evolution, future research could reveal whether these capabilities aid microtasking communities in resolving the worker invisibility problem. Specifically, the literature suggests that workers can shape their microtask work conditions—for example, through the Turkers’ Bill of Rights (Irani & Silberman, 2014; Silberman & Irani, 2016). Recent research has shed light on worker behaviors that can engage others in work-related issues (Deng et al., 2023). Network governance models could be used to examine whether these efforts impact microlabor markets (e.g., Jones et al., 1997). Johnston et al. (2013) found that information utility positively impacts patient empowerment for members of online health communities. Future research could explore the role of community capabilities in empowering workers to sanction requesters through network effects.

## 9.2 Implications for Practice

Our research has practical implications for microtask workers, community designers, and platform providers. First, workers can realize value by recognizing the different ways that microtasking communities offer access to informational and social resources that facilitate their work. We found that politically skilled workers within these communities were more perceptive of their evolving needs. Workers leveraged this knowledge to ensure that information resources improved their hourly wage while providing social support for their emotional well-being. Prior research has shown that political skill is not an innate skill; instead, it can be acquired through participation in a social environment (Di Gangi et al., 2022; McAllister et al., 2018; McAllister et al., 2015; Oerder et al., 2014). Therefore, workers should focus on developing their political skill in microtasking communities to mitigate unfair labor practices and improve their hourly wage performance.

Second, this study highlights the importance of perceived system evolvability for community designers. By maintaining a strong awareness of the underlying needs of community members, designers could modify functionalities as they gain or lose relevance to workers’ needs, particularly concerning the delivery of relevant microtask information (i.e., information utility) that facilitates financial performance. Designers could integrate functionalities that support these capabilities—such as discussion boards, requester dashboards highlighting microtask details and payment responsiveness, and training materials for technical resources—to improve the chances of a community’s survival. In particular, it is important to help new members develop their skills (Cini, 2023; Joyce & Stuart, 2021). Further reflection on the thematic analysis responses indicated the importance of onboarding new MTurk workers to introduce them to critical resources, similar to how employee orientations introduce newly hired employees to an organization and its processes. Community designers could offer training materials and links to technical resources to facilitate this learning process. Moreover, community designers should be aware of members’ ideological views, given that microtasking ideology moderates several key relationships among community capabilities and their impact on hourly wage performance.

Lastly, this study underscores that microtasking communities are important for resolving information asymmetries between microtask workers, requestors, and digital platforms. Such communities present an interesting issue for microlabor platforms that derive income from requester fees (Kalleberg & Dunn, 2016). Microlabor platforms need to limit worker disengagement to remain an attractive source of labor for requesters. If platform designers fail to remain mindful of the role of microtasking communities in introducing efficiencies to participate on their platform, this could create a potential market entry point for competitors (e.g., platforms that are better at resolving information asymmetries between workers and requestors). For platform designers, encouraging microtasking communities—or at least not creating barriers to them—could help build and sustain worker participation on their microlabor platforms.

## 9.3 Limitations and Future Directions

This study has limitations that also afford avenues for future research. First, to ensure internal validity, we purposely chose US MTurk respondents. Generalizing our results to additional populations (e.g., India is the second largest MTurk worker population) will require further research. Future research could examine non-US workers in the MTurk communities that we examined and communities outside those discussed in the present study and evaluate whether non-US MTurk workers employ different productivity metrics. Second, while we purposefully studied MTurk because of its popularity and the availability of several microtasking communities, other microlabor market platforms (e.g., Uber, TaskRabbit) may offer different opportunities to workers or reward other aspects of their work behaviors. Future research could examine these platforms and their communities to determine the generalizability of the present study’s findings. Such research may prove helpful if technical features are available in these communities beyond those identified in the present study (e.g., information aggregating scripts or browser extensions, asynchronous discussion boards, pros/cons commentaries, worker Block/Follow features).

All methodological approaches come with limitations. The pseudo-ethnography we employed limited interactions with subjects to avoid altering social discourse, potentially allowing a community capability to be overlooked. While our thematic analysis should have mitigated this concern, the potential for such an event remains. Furthermore, our survey primarily relies on self-reported measures, which raises common method bias concerns. While our study design mitigated some concerns about bias (e.g., gathering field data, obtaining objective validation of performance data, attention checks, and a marker variable), evenly distributed bias was detected. Our marker variable mitigated this bias by controlling for it during data imputation. Finally, our study’s survey items included the worker’s most frequently used community. While the community name was insignificant, the number of communities a worker was a member of positively impacted their hourly wages, but only for workers with low microtasking ideology. This implies an opportunity for future research to examine how community-specific features and the collective impact of these communities affect performance outcomes.

## 10 Conclusion

This study demonstrates that political skill at the construct level serves as a useful theoretical lens for IS scholars to study worker performance in microlabor markets. The results show that political skill influences worker perceptions about community capabilities for microtask work in terms of perceived system evolvability, the utility of information shared, and the creation of a supportive environment. These capabilities can then influence workers’ hourly wages. Microtasking ideology, a construct that emerged from our qualitative study and was confirmed by our quantitative study, was a critical moderator that weakened the effects of political skill and community capabilities on financial productivity. Through studying microlabor markets, IS scholars can contribute to the understanding of labor environments that rely on digital platforms to connect employers with on-demand workers and explain how such workers use online communities to mitigate unfair treatment. Future research should continue to explore how workers leverage online communities and other collaborative technologies to achieve their objectives.

## Appendix E: Phase—Validity Assessment

Screening techniques were used to ensure respondents were attentive while completing the survey (Desimone et al., 2015). Since not all MTurk workers utilize microtasking communities, respondents were first asked if they used an MTurk community as part of their microtasking activities and to identify the name of a valid MTurk community that they used most frequently. If workers did not indicate they used an MTurk community or failed to identify a valid community, they were dropped from the sample. Respondents completed attention check questions at two different points within the survey, and duration was captured for each survey page to identify speed response strategies. Following current guidance on utilizing MTurk for data collection (Aguinis et al., 2021; Lowry et al., 2016), any respondent who failed an attention check was dropped from the sample. Any respondent who completed a survey page with an average of less than one second per item was labeled inattentive and dropped from the sample. We also compared the objective performance image captures against the self-reported data. If the data did not match, the response was discarded from the sample. Lastly, all survey items were randomized to reduce common method bias.

We conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using AMOS version 25 to assess discriminant and convergent validity based on the recommendations of Straub et al. (2004) and Hu and Bentler (1999). The reliability of each construct was measured using composite reliability with a threshold value greater than 0.70, indicating sufficient reliability. We assessed convergent validity by examining the significance of the loadings for the indicators of all reflective items on their respective latent constructs and by examining the average variance extracted (AVE) value, as suggested by Fornell and Larcker (1981), with a minimum value of 0.50 to indicate sufficient validity. All indicators were significant, and all AVEs were above the 0.50 threshold. We assessed discriminant validity by comparing the correlations among constructs against each construct’s square root of the AVE (Straub et al., 2004). All were greater than the respective correlations, indicating suitable discriminant validity.

Political skill was measured as a first-order reflective, second-order formative measure. An initial CFA identified two items from social astuteness that should be dropped from the analysis. These items were the same items removed during the pilot study. Political skill has been theorized to contain four dimensions (i.e., networking ability, social astuteness, interpersonal influence, and apparent sincerity). Political skill was validated by examining item t-statistics significance, outer loadings, and variance inflation factor (VIF) values (less than 3.0) (Hair et al., 2020). The correlations between social astuteness and interpersonal influence and interpersonal influence and apparent sincerity are consistent with the pilot study and prior research on political skill, supporting its operationalization as a second-order construct. Table E1 shows the results of this analysis, indicating that the measure meets formative measure validity requirements. As expected, outer loadings were all above 0.50 and statistically significant. Some VIF values were above 3.0 when each indicator was not considered part of a higher-order construct. Once modeled as a second-order construct based on political skill’s dimensions, all VIF values are below the recommended threshold (i.e., networking ability = 1.53, social astuteness = 1.63, interpersonal influence = 2.28, and apparent sincerity = 1.55).

## Appendix F: Survey Instrument

1. Please indicate the name of the community you use most frequently when working in MTurk? (text entry)

2. How old are you (in years) (e.g., 18, 40, 55…)

3. What is your current education level?

4. What is your employment status?

5. What is your gender?

6. What is your race?

7. How many MTurk communities do you participate in as a member?

8. How frequently do you use the \_\_\_ community? (Never to Frequently)

9. How long (in months) have you been a member of the \_\_\_ community?

10. How long (in months) have you been a member of MTurk?

11. Are you a MTurk Master Worker? (Yes or No)

12. How many pages of MTurk Qualifications do you currently have in your account?

13. What percentage of your overall/total annual income is from MTurk?

14. On average, what is your hourly wage when working in MTurk?

15. Please upload a copy of your worker dashboard. (upload image) (captures MTurk Total Earnings and Approved HITs)

## Political Skill (adapted from Ferris et al., 2005)

## Networking Ability Dimension

1. I spend a lot of time networking with others in the \_\_\_ community.

2. In the \_\_\_ community, I know a lot of important people and am well connected.

3. I am good at using my connections and networks to make things happen in the \_\_\_ community.

4. I have developed a large network in the \_\_\_ community who I can call on for support when I really need to get things done.

5. I spend a lot of time developing connections within the \_\_\_ community.

6. I am good at building relationships with influential people in the \_\_\_ community.

## Social Astuteness Dimension

7. I always seem to instinctively know the right thing to say or do to influence \_\_\_ community members.

8. I have good intuition or savvy about how to present myself within the \_\_\_ community.

9. I am particularly good at sensing the motivations and hidden agendas of others in the \_\_\_ community.

10. I pay close attention to \_\_\_ community member’s words and expressions. (dropped)

11. I understand people in the \_\_\_ community very well. (dropped)

## Interpersonal Influence Dimension

12. It is easy to make most \_\_\_ community members feel comfortable and at ease with me.

13. It is easy for me to develop good rapport with most people in the \_\_\_ community.

14. I am able to communicate easily and effectively within the \_\_\_ community.

15. I am good at getting \_\_\_ community members to like me.

## Apparent Sincerity Dimension

16. It is important that people in the \_\_\_ community believe I am sincere in what I say and do.

17. When communicating within the \_\_\_ community, I try to be genuine in what I say or do.

18. I try to show a genuine interest in others in the \_\_\_ community.

## Perceived System Evolvability (adapted from Di Gangi & Wasko, 2016; Wixom & Todd, 2005)

1. As a platform, the \_\_\_ community shapes its programs and features to my needs and preferences.

2. \_\_\_ ‘s platform produces scripts and other tools that meet my current needs and interests.

## Information Utility (adapted from Moenart & Souder, 1996)

1. I am likely to use the information about requesters obtained from the \_\_\_ community.

2. I am satisfied with the information about good HITs obtained within the \_\_\_ community.

3. I am satisfied with the form and presentation of HIT information obtained from the \_\_\_ community.

4. I believe the information obtained about requesters from the \_\_\_ community will be useful to my MTurk work.

## Social Support (adapted from Johnston et al., 2013)

1. I can count on the \_\_\_ community to distract me from my worries when I feel under stress from my MTurk work.

2. I can really count on the \_\_\_ community to help me feel more relaxed when completing my MTurk work. (dropped)

3. The \_\_\_ community accepts me totally, including at my worst and best moments while doing my MTurk work.

4. I can really count on the \_\_\_ community to care about me regardless of what is happening to me while completing my MTurk work. (dropped)

5. I can really count on the \_\_\_ community to help me feel better when I am feeling generally down-in-the-dumps about my MTurk work.

6. I can count on the \_\_\_ community to console me when I am very upset about my MTurk work.

## Microtasking Ideology (adapted from Deng & Joshi, 2016 work value outcome construct)

1. The \_\_\_ community facilitates being paid the amount I deserve for my MTurk work.

2. The \_\_\_ community enables the freedom and control to make my own decisions about what MTurk work to complete.

3. As a member of the \_\_\_ community, I value the MTurk “lifestyle.”

## Marker Variable-Privacy Concern Control (adapted from Malhotra et al., 2004)

1. Consumer online privacy is really a matter of consumers’ right to exercise control and autonomy over decisions about how their information is collected, used, and shared.

2. Consumer control of personal information lies at the heart of consumer privacy.

3. I believe that online privacy is invaded when control is lost or unwillingly reduced as a result of a marketing transaction.
