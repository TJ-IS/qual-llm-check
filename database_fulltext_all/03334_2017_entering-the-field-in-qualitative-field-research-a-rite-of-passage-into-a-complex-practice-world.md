# Entering the field in qualitative field research: a rite of passage into a complex practice world

Hameed Chughtai\*<sup>,†</sup> & Michael D. Myers<sup>†</sup>

\*Faculty of Business, Law and Art, Southampton Business School, Southampton, SO17 1BJ, UK, email: H.Chughtai@soton.ac.uk, and <sup>†</sup>Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, University of Auckland Business School, Private Bag 92019, Auckland 1142, New Zealand

Abstract. The concept of ‘the <sup>fi</sup>eld’ is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in ethnographic research as well as qualitative research methods more generally. However, how a <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher enters the <sup>fi</sup>eld is usually taken for granted after gaining access to the <sup>fi</sup>eld. We suggest that entrance is a distinct phase of <sup>fi</sup>eldwork that differs from negotiating access. Entrance is not a trivial event; rather, it is a rite of passage into a complex practice world and marks a critical <sup>fi</sup>eld moment. Drawing on our ethnography and insights from hermeneutics and anthropology, we show that a practical understanding of the <sup>fi</sup>eld represents a fusion of horizons where a <sup>fi</sup>eldworker is thrown. The concept of thrownness highlights the fact that the <sup>fi</sup>eldworkers’ own historicity and prejudices affect their entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld; hence, entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld orientates an ethnographer in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and in<sup>fl</sup>uences the entire period of <sup>fi</sup>eldwork that follows. Our theorizing is intended as a contribution towards advancing the discussion of qualitative research methods.

Keywords: <sup>fi</sup>eldwork, entrance, access, ethnography, hermeneutics, interpretive <sup>fi</sup>eld research

## INTRODUCTION

Ethnographic research in information systems (IS) is often used in interpretive and critical studies of social, organizational and technological phenomena (e.g. Lee & Myers, 2004; Bell et al., 2005; Jones, 2014; Oreglia & Srinivasan, 2016). It is an anthropological method with strong philosophical and theoretical foundations (Geertz, 1973, 2000). The ethnographic corpus in IS research contains signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions to methodology such as guidelines of <sup>fi</sup>eld research (Klein & Myers, 1999), dramaturgical analysis of qualitative interviews (Myers & Newman, 2007), spatiality in the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork (Dourish & Bell, 2007), confessional and re<sup>fl</sup>exive techniques (Schultze, 2000a, 2000b) and most recently, a toolkit for design ethnography (Baskerville & Myers, 2015).

One of the essential features of any ethnographic study is <sup>fi</sup>eldwork. An ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher, or simply a <sup>fi</sup>eldworker, enters the <sup>fi</sup>eld in order to learn about social and cultural practices and their meanings. In a sense, a <sup>fi</sup>eldworker is like a child who learns by observing and participating in a new world (Van Maanen, 2011a, p. 75; Van Maanen, 2011b, p. 220). After gaining the access to the <sup>fi</sup>eld, an ethnographer attempts to develop ‘an intimate familiarity with the dilemmas, frustrations, routines, relationships and risks that are part of everyday life’ in order to understand the practice world of the participants (Myers, 1999, p. 5).

The concept of the <sup>fi</sup>eld is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant not just in ethnographic research but also in qualitative <sup>fi</sup>eld research methods more generally. In qualitative research and ethnographic research in particular, how a qualitative researcher enters the <sup>fi</sup>eld is usually taken for granted. In the liter ature, entrance is often seen as synonymous with negotiating access to the <sup>fi</sup>eld, that is, as an administrative issue. The focus of discussions of qualitative methods usually centres on later issues in the <sup>fi</sup>eld concerning data collection or analyses (e.g. Nandhakumar & Jones, 1997, p. 117; Schultze, 2000b, pp. 92–3; cf. Myers, 2013, pp. 80ff, 139ff). Entering the <sup>fi</sup>eld thus tends to be seen as a rather trivial event in the course of the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork, the only issue being one of negotiating access to the research site.

We propose that entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld marks a critical <sup>fi</sup>eld moment that affects the entire course of <sup>fi</sup>eldwork that follows (Turner, 1997; Trigger et al., 2012, p. 514). Turner (1997) says that ‘critical moments’ in the <sup>fi</sup>eld are periods of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance, sense- and meaning-making (for a seminal ethnographic example, see Geertz, 1973, pp. 413–416). For <sup>fi</sup>eldworkers, a critical <sup>fi</sup>eld moment is thus not a <sup>fi</sup>xed temporal point that can simply be grasped instantaneously after securing access to the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Rather, <sup>fi</sup>eldworkers need to be vulnerable to what is going on in the <sup>fi</sup>eld practices in order to make sense of the critical <sup>fi</sup>eld moments and, in so doing, enter the practice world of the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Goffman, 1989). Entrance thus marks an important milepost in the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork and is just as important as access, acceptance (‘living with’ the participants), immersion (‘living like’ the participants) and exiting the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Michailova et al., 2014; Van Maanen, 2011a, 2011b, p. 2, 80). The entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld orients the ethnographer in a new practice world (Geertz, 1973, pp. 10–28) and is in effect a rite of passage (Van Gennep, 1977), providing a deeper understanding of <sup>fi</sup>eld practices. Turner (1974, 1997) says that <sup>fi</sup>eldworkers need to develop new interpretations of rites of passage as critical <sup>fi</sup>eld moments. To that end, we extend the traditional notion of rite of passage as one that applies to <sup>fi</sup>eldworkers themselves and adopt an interpretation that requires ongoing engagement with practices.

In this paper, we provide a critical interpretive theorizing of a <sup>fi</sup>eldworker’s entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld. We follow Heidegger (2008) and, in particular, his being-in-the-world analysis, to develop an engaged interpretation of entering the <sup>fi</sup>eld. We understand a <sup>fi</sup>eldworker’s entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld as being thrown into a world where one makes sense of it by using one’s historical position across space and time, amidst already existing structures, things and practices (Heidegger, 2011, p. 26; cf. Schatzki, 2006, p. 171; Van Maanen, 2011a). Thus, we acknowledge that we always already have a horizon of understanding before we enter the <sup>fi</sup>eld. The concept of thrownness suggests that we interpret and understand the world from the horizon of our prior background practices and experience (Heidegger, 2008, p. 36ff; cf. Rabinow & Stavrianakis, 2013). Our horizon of understanding provides us with our spatiotemporal orientation in the <sup>fi</sup>eld (cf. Geertz, 1973). Such horizons of understandings include, but are not limited to, ou historicity, temporality, spatiality, everyday language and presuppositions of the phenomena (Gadamer, 1989). From these horizons, we make sense of the various situations in the <sup>fi</sup>eld that we <sup>fi</sup>nd ourselves in. Accordingly, entrance can thus be examined and understood through an interplay of multiple horizons of understanding.

Our empirical evidence comes from an ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eld study at a reasonably large-scale information technology (IT) service organization in the Asia-Paci<sup>fi</sup>c region. We offer an interpre tation of entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld as a rite of passage into a practice world where an ethnographe is thrown (cf. Marcus, 2009, p. 12; Sluka & Robben, 2012, p. 17).

The structure of this paper is as follows: We <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss the need for the theorization of entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld. We then present some hermeneutic principles to lay the groundwork for our interpretation. Next, we discuss entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld in terms of Heidegger’s concept of thrownness. This is followed by a presentation of some empirical evidence from the <sup>fi</sup>eld. The <sup>fi</sup>nal section provides a critical discussion on the methodological implications for IS scholars and <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers.

## INTERPRETING THE FIELD IN FIELDWORK

The concept of ‘the <sup>fi</sup>eld’ is central to qualitative inquiry, particularly ethnography and interpre tive <sup>fi</sup>eld research (Goffman, 1989; Klein & Myers, 1999; Ybema et al., 2009; Van Maanen, 2011a; Jones, 2014). However, what is the <sup>fi</sup>eld? Particularly, what does it mean to enter the <sup>fi</sup>eld? Is the <sup>fi</sup>eld simply a locality out there, waiting to be discovered? If that is the case, then entering the <sup>fi</sup>eld is a rather trivial exercise; it is a mere stepping stone after gaining access to a physical, symbolic or virtual venue.

Organizational <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers often see their physical entrance to a research site as an entrance into a practice world (Jones, 2014, p. 116; Pole & Hillyard, 2016). That is, entrance is understood as a physical activity achieved by the virtue of negotiating access to the research site. The politics of negotiation and interpersonal challenges of gaining sustainable access to the <sup>fi</sup>eld have already been discussed elsewhere (on complex access strategies, see Van Maanen, 2011b, pp. 84ff; also Reeves, 2010). We acknowledge that gaining access is an important issue, and access needs to be maintained by developing relationships in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and sustained during the course of the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork (Harrington, 2003).

However, it is one thing to gain physical access to the <sup>fi</sup>eld site, and another to enter the practice world, be accepted and be there along with the participants, living with them in their everyday situations (Kunda, 2013). That is, before a <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher attempts to immerse herself in the <sup>fi</sup>eld practices, it is required to enter the practice world where she wants to be immersed. It is thus possible for a naïve <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher, if all they are concerned about is access, to appear to the participants as a stranger trying to break in to their practice world, somewhat akin to being a ‘burglar–researcher’ (Alvesson, 2009, p. 162). As Jones (2014) points out, while gaining access requires following the rules and norms of the research site, such administrative strategies are not always suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to enter the lives of people in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Jones uses Goffman (1989) to broaden the distinction between getting in (access) and getting on (entrance and acceptance) to illustrate the difference between approaching the <sup>fi</sup>eld and being in the <sup>fi</sup>eld.

We take this argument further and suggest that entrance marks a critical <sup>fi</sup>eld moment, whereby <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers can gain rich local knowledge from the inside of the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Geertz, 2000).

All <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers begin their journey as strangers trying to make sense of a new unfamiliar world (Kunda, 2013; Fine & Hallett, 2014). But after gaining access, entrance is when a researcher’s status changes from that of an outsider to being a member (albeit only temporarily) of the practice world (Moeran, 2009). We see entrance as a rite of passage into a practice world (cf. Clifford, 1983). One’s entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld marks a status-changing event that is pivotal in forging a plausible ethnography. Entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld orientates an ethnographer in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and thus in<sup>fl</sup>uences the entire period of <sup>fi</sup>eldwork that follows (Harrington, 2003, p. 594; Schwartz-Shea & Yanow, 2009, pp. 65–6). In this perspective, entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld is disclosed as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant rite of passage from a theoretical as well as a practical point of view (cf. Turner, 1974, pp. 56ff). For instance, entrance can determine the <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers’ position within the circle of understanding in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, where they <sup>fi</sup>t and where they <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves in the existing everyday world of the participants (Geertz, 1973; Rabinow, 2007). Hence, how one enters the <sup>fi</sup>eld can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the dynamics of the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork and to a large extent determine what one is going to <sup>fi</sup>nd out (Rabinow & Stavrianakis, 2013, pp. 31–46). The way a <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher enters the <sup>fi</sup>eld determines the way the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork will be conducted; a problematic entrance might lead to problems of authenticity and plausibility of the research later on.

While access to the <sup>fi</sup>eld is mostly an administrative or structural issue, entrance is an exercise in meaning- and sense-making in the <sup>fi</sup>eld in which a <sup>fi</sup>eldworker attempts to become part of a larger whole. Perhaps the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of entrance can be further brought forth by understanding the <sup>fi</sup>eld where one enters. The <sup>fi</sup>eld is the home of the phenomena and, as Dreyfus (1991, p. 162) explicates. ‘we can only describe the phenomena as they show themselves and show how they <sup>fi</sup>t with the rest of human existence’. Here, the task of an ethnographer is argued to coherently document the <sup>fi</sup>eld practices in order to tell a plausible account of how the <sup>fi</sup>eld phenomena are entwined with practices through dialogical reasoning while preserving and disclosing multiple horizons of understandings (Clifford, 1988, p. 63). The entrance is thus an initial encounter with a complex practice world (Van Maanen, 2011a; cf. Heidegger, 2011, pp. 210ff) where the participants are already entwined in a nexus of historical practices (Schatzki, 2010). A <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher encounters, lives through and makes sense of this world by engaging in the everyday practices in the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Anteby, 2013; cf. Goffman, 1989, p. 125).

In order to achieve entrance, the <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher needs to develop an intimate familiarity with the everyday affairs of the people (see Geertz, 1973, pp. 432ff). Such an enterprise calls fo absorbing oneself in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and involves skilful coping in everyday practices to reach the ‘epistemic core’ of the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Fine & Hallett, 2014, p. 191; cf. Dreyfus, 1991, pp. 60ff). In this way, gradually, the <sup>fi</sup>eldworker’s comprehension of the <sup>fi</sup>eld phenomena becomes automatic and her responses transparent in practice. While access is often a negotiated process, entrance is an engaged practice that is often achieved by being vulnerable and engaged with the field situations (Geertz. 1973, pp, 413–7: Goffman, 1989), Thus, insofar as a fieldworker steadily becomes one with the world of participants, the <sup>fi</sup>eld practices slowly become intelligi ble; the <sup>fi</sup>eld tends to become a familiar place rather than an alien world. A <sup>fi</sup>eldworker <sup>fi</sup>nds he place in the <sup>fi</sup>eld apropos a part belonging to a whole (Rabinow, 2007; Anteby, 2013). As this transparency is achieved in practice, the <sup>fi</sup>eld becomes a world where one belongs, where everything makes sense simply by virtue of being there (Geertz, 2000, p. 69; cf. Van Maanen, 2011a). Being there, living with people in their everyday situations, is what gives the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork its signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. We now further elaborate on the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the dialectical nature of being there, a part belonging to a whole, using the concept of the hermeneutic circle (for a related discussion in IS theory, see Klein & Myers, 1999, pp. 71ff; Cole & Avison, 2007, pp. 822ff).

## The <sup>fi</sup>eld as a hermeneutic circle

While we acknowledge that there might be other points of departure to grasp entrance to the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Gupta & Ferguson, 1997), we draw on insights from phenomenological hermeneutics to theorize entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld, consistent with our critical interpretive lens (Geertz, 2000; Heidegger, 2008). We, <sup>fi</sup>rst of all, problematize the concept of the <sup>fi</sup>eld by mapping it to the hermeneutic circle insofar as it deals with the ‘dialectic between the understanding of the text as a whole and the interpretation of its parts, in which descriptions are guided by anticipated expla nations’ (Myers, 2004, p. 107). Using the hermeneutic circle to interpret the logic of everyday practices, we understand that we always already <sup>fi</sup>nd ourselves in a practical situation where our meaning- and sense-making of a phenomenon pivot on our apposite prior experiences, pre-understandings and presuppositions (Gadamer, 1989, pp. 268ff; Heidegger, 2008, pp. 188ff). Thus, by being inside of practices, we make sense of the world and the phenomena concerned.

Geertz (2000, pp. 5ff) applies the concept of the hermeneutic circle to <sup>fi</sup>eldwork by discussing how <sup>fi</sup>eldworkers develop a practical understanding of the <sup>fi</sup>eld phenomena. For example, if we are studying a particular culture, in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, the part (e.g. sacred symbol) can only be understood in the context of the whole (e.g. a culture and a belief system). Similarly, when a <sup>fi</sup>eldworker accesses the <sup>fi</sup>eld, he or she might not be aware of the whole context, and thus has to enter the practice world in order to try to grasp its practical logic (cf. Van Maanen, 2011a, p. 220).

In a sense, <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers are also in a hermeneutic circle (cf. Schultze, 2000a, pp. 25ff) as they continually engage with many interpretations of their self-understanding and their understanding of the world, namely the <sup>fi</sup>eld where they <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves. Thus, acknowledging Geertz (2000), we can say that all interpretations of the <sup>fi</sup>eld need to begin by taking an engaged immersive view, i.e. of being already absorbed in the practices of the <sup>fi</sup>eld. As Dreyfus (1991, p. 4) succinctly points out, ‘one must always do hermeneutics from within a hermeneutic circle’. Entrance, too, can only be partially understood by a conceptually detached theorizing; rather, it requires one to be practically engaged in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. We now make the logic of the entrance into <sup>fi</sup>eld explicit by discussing the structure of understanding in a hermeneutic circle (the <sup>fi</sup>eld).

## The structure of understanding in a hermeneutic circle

The hermeneutic circle can be seen as a dialectic of many horizons of understanding. In this dialectic, Heidegger (2008, pp. 191ff) points to a trinity of minute horizons within every interpre tation which he refers to as fore-structure that consists of

1 fore-having (our taken for granted background);

2 fore-sight (our assumptions concerning the interpretations); and

3 fore-conception (our expectations; something we already grasp).

The hermeneutic circle thus spells out the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance and intricacies of our presuppositions. While fore-having can be understood as what the <sup>fi</sup>eldworker takes for granted about the <sup>fi</sup>eld site, fore-sight is the horizon from where one observes and participates in the site. From the perspective of one horizon, one observes the other; taken together, it is our fore-conception which enables us to make sense of the phenomena in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Thus, our background, our historicity, plays a crucial role in our everyday sense-making. In this way, before one develops a practical understanding of the <sup>fi</sup>eld practices, it is essential to grasp the <sup>fi</sup>eld phenomena appropriately. Heidegger (2008, p. 195) puts emphasis on one’s entrance into the hermeneutic circle as follows:

What is decisive is not to get out of the [hermeneutic] circle but to come into it in the right way…. To be sure, we genuinely take hold of this possibility only when, in our interpretation, we have understood that our <sup>fi</sup>rst, last, and constant task is never to allow our fore-having, foresight, and fore-conception to be presented to us by fancies and popular conceptions, but rather to make the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theme secure by working out these fore-structures in terms of the things themselves.

Hence, Heidegger stresses that all understandings are connected in a series of forestructures – including one’s historicity – that cannot be eliminated. He says that we cannot truly set aside our prior knowledge before interpreting a phenomenon. If we apply this to <sup>fi</sup>eldwork, this means that the fieldworker needs to become as aware as possible of these fore-structures and take account of these interpretive in<sup>fl</sup>uences in their entrance to the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Being aware of fore-having, fore-sight and fore-conception serves as the foundation for understanding the <sup>fi</sup>eld phenomena. We can say that the authenticity of the interpretation of any observation hinges on a <sup>fi</sup>eldworker becoming aware of and transparently engaging with the phenomenon in the <sup>fi</sup>eld; a critical understanding of fore-structure enables a <sup>fi</sup>eldworker to go deep into participants practice worlds.

Dreyfus (1991, p. 199) advances the discussion and explicates the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the forestructure in an interpretation and redirects our attention to the circular relationship caused by our expectations (fore-conception) when making sense of a phenomenon. An interpretation occurs against a backdrop of assumptions in which the latter itself is conditioned upon the former through understanding (Figure 1).

The circular relationship as illustrated in Figure 1 problematizes our understandings of every day situations in which we encounter the phenomena. For instance, we develop understanding by interpreting a situation, but our interpretation is based on our assumptions concerning the phenomenon. Thus, we cannot truly set aside our presuppositions; any new understanding is always mediated through our historicity (Geertz, 2000). Further, Gadamer (1989, pp. 271–3 305) points out that our many horizons of understanding within the fore-structure seamlessly work together or ‘fuse’ with each other. The <sup>fi</sup>eld is a ‘fusion of horizons’ in which an ethnogra pher utilizes various perspectives through their prior and historical knowledge, their horizons of understanding (Gadamer, 1989, p. 337: Boland & Tenkasi, 1995), We will now take this idea fur ther by discussing the hermeneutic concept of prejudice within an interpretation.

## The role of prejudice

In critical hermeneutics, prejudice is not necessarily negative (as in say, racial prejudice). According to Gadamer (1989, pp. 269ff), prejudice is our prior awareness or understanding of something. Our prejudices, our likes and dislikes, are based on our previous experience and in a way sum up where we are coming from. In our <sup>fi</sup>eldwork, we cannot just detach ourselves from our history and pretend that we are a <sup>fl</sup>y on the wall. Gadamer points out that without any prejudices (i.e. our pre-understanding), we cannot develop a new understanding of anything. However, this does not mean that all our prejudices are good. Bad prejudices are when we leap to conclusions, ignoring any evidence that might contradict our pre-understanding. In this way, Gadamer (1989, pp. 268–9) explicates that prejudices are necessary for any new critical understanding to take place insofar as one is open to new meanings and understandings:

All that is asked is that we remain open to the meaning of the other person or text. But this openness always includes our situating the other meaning in relation to the whole of our own meanings or ourselves in relation to it ... This kind of sensitivity involves neither 'neutrality’ with respect to content nor the extinction of one’s self, but the foregrounding and appropriation of one’s own fore-meanings and prejudices. The important thing is to be aware of one’s own bias, so that the text can present itself in all its otherness and thus assert its own truth against one’s own fore-meanings.

Accordingly, by paying critical attention to one’s own prejudices in the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork, we can say that a <sup>fi</sup>eldworker enters a practice world with her own background practices. She has to remain open to the meaning of this new world while foregrounding and appropriating her own fore-meanings and prejudices. It is important for her to be aware of her own biases so that she can allow the participants to assert their own truth against hers. Despite our prejudices, however, we have to allow the otherness of those in the <sup>fi</sup>eld to speak to us such that we can attain an understanding of their own truth.

## GRASPING THE LOGIC OF ENTRANCE

The <sup>fi</sup>eld is a complex world where ethnography occurs and where the ethnographer <sup>fi</sup>nds herself thrown (Geertz, 1973; Goffman, 1989). In our thrownness, following Heidegger (2011, pp. 26, 44), the entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld can be critically grasped as ‘the [spatial] state one <sup>fi</sup>nds oneself in’. When an ethnographer attempts to enter the <sup>fi</sup>eld, the entrance is grounded in and entwined with her own fore-meanings and prejudices along with the fore-meanings and prejudices of the participants. An ethnographer is thus thrown into a situation, but it is precisely this thrownness that enables her to respond and make sense of the <sup>fi</sup>eld as a practice world (Heidegger, 2008, pp. 219–224).

The concept of thrownness suggests that the authenticity of an ethnographer hinges on engaging with the <sup>fi</sup>eld phenomena, becoming involved in and doing the activities in the <sup>fi</sup>eld insofar as ‘one is what one does’ (Heidegger, 1985, p. 244; Heidegger, 2008, p. 283). Of course, it is neither possible nor desirable to truly gain the native point of view; rather, the hope is that through the appropriate entrance, the gap between ‘living like’ and ‘living with’ the participants can be intimately closed (Van Maanen, 2011b). In this vein, authentic ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eldwork requires the <sup>fi</sup>eldworker to be practically doing the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork by being actively engaged with practices in the <sup>fi</sup>eld (cf. Goffman, 1989). This point of engagement is crucial for any authentic <sup>fi</sup>eldwork (Golden-Biddle & Locke, 1993, p. 599): A <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher needs to be intimately involved with the participants’ practices in the <sup>fi</sup>eld as opposed to becoming a mere spectator of them (Klein & Myers, 1999, p. 74; cf. Van Maanen, 2011a).

Hence, we can see that the <sup>fi</sup>eld is more than just a venue and the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork is more than the simple collection of data. Rather, the practice of <sup>fi</sup>eldwork requires critically grasping what one does in it by understanding where one <sup>fi</sup>nds oneself and how one <sup>fi</sup>nds oneself in it. The extent to which we engage is delimited by our thrownness and the extent to which we engage with the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork by doing the same activities as the participants. The concept of thrownness provides a tangible way for the <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers to make sense of the <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## Being thrown in the <sup>fi</sup>eld

Geertz (1973, p. 13) says that ‘<sup>fi</sup>nding [one’s] feet’ is an important ‘personal experience’ for ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eldworkers. When we are thrown into an unfamiliar situation, we need to <sup>fi</sup>nd a <sup>fi</sup>rm place to stand on, but how do we start? If standing on our feet is something that we need to do in order to gain entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld, where do we begin? The answer lies in the practically engaged logic of practices: Heidegger (2008, pp. 458ff) says that we are thrown and <sup>fi</sup>nd the world already there before us. Thus, in our everyday practices, by the virtue of our being-in-the-world, we are already in the world involved (Sandberg & Tsoukas, 2011, p. 350; cf. Schatzki, 2010); in other words, we are always already thrown in our everyday situations. As <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers, we are thrown from one practice world to another. Rather than taking a de tached conceptual position, we need to make sense of entrance through our engagement in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. In thrownness, it is our horizons of understandings which, as shown earlier, help us make sense of the <sup>fi</sup>eld and help us <sup>fi</sup>nd our feet.

We encounter the <sup>fi</sup>eld on the basis of our preconceptions, i.e. our prejudice horizon. However, we are thrown not just into an environment but also into a historical moment in a practice world; thus, our prejudice horizon is fused with the historicity of the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Therefore, when we <sup>fi</sup>rst approach the <sup>fi</sup>eld, we have but a factical understanding of the <sup>fi</sup>eld phenomena. One can grasp the difference between factual and factical as follows: While the latter is our ways to be (researcher and mother), the former is its properties (male and female). Dreyfus (1991, pp. 20–5) stresses that we can never be sure of our factical ways of understanding. In thrownness, our factical understanding of ourselves is always bound with others’ factical understanding of the <sup>fi</sup>eld – a nexus of practices, equipment and entities – which we encounter: Our interpretation of phenomena in the <sup>fi</sup>eld is their interpretation and vice versa. Heidegger (2008, p. 400) explains,

[We get] dragged along in thrownness; that is to say, as something which has been thrown into the world, it loses itself in the ‘world’ in its factical submission to that with which it is to concern itself.

Haar (1993) develops this idea further and points out that our standard response to being thrown is to become busy and engage ourselves in activities with one another. That is to say, we <sup>fl</sup>ee from anxiety by seeking shelter in the normativity of everyday practices. That is why it is problematic to start making sense of the <sup>fi</sup>eld as soon as one gains access. Rather, <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers are required to do things as the participants do. Dreyfus (1991, p. 236) says that we are thrown straight into the routine affairs of the <sup>fi</sup>eld where the factical interpretation of practices matters more than a conceptual understanding. In a similar way, Ciborra & Willcocks (2006, p. 135) discuss thrownness in terms of facticity and raise the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of practices; they say that the ‘concern for factical life expresses the re-balancing of activities’; that is, an everyday understanding of activities takes precedence in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. We can then say that it is our thrownness that enables us to interpret the meaning of our engagement with the participants in the <sup>fi</sup>eld.

Further, when we <sup>fi</sup>nd ourselves thrown into a practice world, it often requires engagement with equipment, things, places, people and practices. An appropriate initial response would be rather normative insofar as to follow established practices such as social or organizational norms before making sense of practices and, is so doing, before moving on to engage with phenomena in a meaningful way (cf. Heidegger, 2008, p. 190). Engagement with everyday practices hinges on a holistic understanding. Geertz (1973, p. 12) sheds light on the subtle intricacies of engagement and provides a practical example illustrating how a holisticall engaged approach works:

© 2016 Blackwell Publishing Ltd, Information Systems Journal

To play the violin it is necessary to possess certain skills, habits, knowledge, and talents, to be in the mood to play, and (as the old joke goes) to have a violin. But violin playing is neither the habits, skills, knowledge, and so on, nor the mood, nor (the notion believers in ‘material culture’ apparently embrace) the violin.

Geertz succinctly points out that playing the violin requires all of these things to come together at once; the whole performance is greater than the sum of the parts, and all need to be understood in a holistic harmony by interpreting phenomena against a background of associated practices. He applies this point to <sup>fi</sup>eldwork: Upon encountering an unfamiliar practice, the <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers need to take an engaged holistic view of the <sup>fi</sup>eld where the practice belongs before interpreting it. Further, a <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher needs to understand the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of entwined background practices insofar as often these remain invisible and require one to engage with practices in order to make sense of them. These ‘nexuses’ of background prac tices contain many things, purposes, norms and other sets of practices (Schatzki, 2010). In thrownness, the participant observation, thus, is not ‘at them’ but ‘with them’, as Boland (1985, p. 343). Boland says that in this way, the ‘understanding comes step by step, layer by layer, as preconceptions, prejudices and assumptions are recognized and seen through (p. 343). Klein & Myers (1999, p. 74) go one step further and say that the participants, as well as the researchers, are interpreters and analysts insofar as ‘they alter their horizons by the appropriation of concepts used by [<sup>fi</sup>eldworkers] interacting with them, and they are analysts in so far as their actions are altered by their changed horizons’.

Hence, as <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers, we <sup>fi</sup>nd ourselves thrown in a <sup>fi</sup>eld amidst intricate nexuses of practices of the participants and equipment. Although we might be only ‘temporary participants in the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Sandberg & Tsoukas, 2011, p. 350), in thrownness, the horizons of understandings of both the <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers and the participants are entwined in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Next, we present some evidence from our ethnographic research to illustrate how entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld can be understood as a rite of passage and interpreted as being thrown in a practice world.

## RESEARCH METHOD

## The nature of the <sup>fi</sup>eld and access to the <sup>fi</sup>eld

We used ethnography to study the everyday technological practices of young professionals in an organizational setting. The site of the ethnography was a large technology service organization in Asia-Paci<sup>fi</sup>c, from here onwards called FieldTech. The <sup>fi</sup>eldwork lasted just over 18 months. The <sup>fi</sup>rst author, from here onwards called the ‘ethnographer’, was in the <sup>fi</sup>eld from August 2013 to March 2015, at least 3 days a week. In accordance with the scope of the study, only the newest and youngest IT staff were considered. They were primarily newly hired graduates or people who were in their <sup>fi</sup>rst year of employment at FieldTech.

The initial contact was made in July 2013. After the access negotiations, FieldTech agreed that ethnographic research might be of some value to their organization. In the thrownness per spective, our access had more than an element of good luck insofar as our research and the research site’s organizational strategy were aligned (Van Maanen, 2011b. p. 84). In a sense, the horizons of understanding of the researchers and the researched were found to be fused together in a meaningful way. The ethnographer was then invited to join FieldTech’s IT department as a software engineer to work part-time on a key ongoing project with one of the software development teams (here onwards called FieldTeam), while on the other days, he was free to focus on his research. Good access was thus granted by securing a working role in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, being an active project member and being at the coalface of the organization; it also meant that a fully sustained access was also granted to the organization’s IT systems, spaces and staff. Further, IT infrastructural access was granted simply by the virtue of being a software enginee working on the project.

The move from access to entrance took a slow gradual course. Initially, the ethnographer was placed in FieldTech’s <sup>fl</sup>agship software development team, FieldTeam, to work on the ongoing IT project design issues. With a longstanding software engineering practitioner history, we found that the ethnographer was more often than not approached to work on various software design issues despite his temporary status in the organization. Thus, from the ethnographic immersion perspective, we can say that we were fortunate to obtain the opportunity to be at the heart of the fusion of practices in the <sup>fi</sup>eld from the very beginning. Further, being a working em ployee also established the ethnographer’s position as an engaged interpreter (a team member) and not just a detached observer (Anteby, 2013; Fine & Hallett, 2014). Being a working employee allowed us to make sense of the practices from the inside. just like the participants

Our access highlights two things: From a thrownness perspective, we can say that access involved interpretation of horizons of understanding, but it only paved the way towards entry that did not guarantee entrance. Second, as we shall further elaborate, it established the role of the researcher in the field and determined the subseguent progress of the fieldwork

## Data collection and analysis

Engaged participant observation was the primary source of data. The daily <sup>fi</sup>eld notes included ‘thick description[s]’ (Geertz, 1973, pp. 3–30) of the events and practices in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. The <sup>fi</sup>eld notes also included notes of weekly meetings, informal chats as well as audio notes, videos, photographs, personal logs and of<sup>fi</sup>cial electronic records (e.g. project communications, video-conferencing, instant messaging, emails, graphic notes and illustrations). The ethnographer also participated in two different recurring user group meetings. In addition to these sources, 10 qualitative interviews were conducted with select participants. These interviews were conducted according to the guidelines provided by Myers & Newman (2007). The interviews were informal, candid and rather confessional in nature and lasted between 1 and 2 h. All interviews were digitally recorded, except one (on the request of the interviewee). The unrecorded interview was methodically reconstructed immediately after the session by using interview notes and observation notes. The data were interpreted by using hermeneutic analysis (Myers, 2004) and followed the hermeneutic principles for interpretive <sup>fi</sup>eld studies (Klein & Myers, 1999).

In the following discussion, we present and engage with the ethnographic evidence primarily from the initial 270 h of immersive observational data. This is the most relevant evidence pertaining to the entrance phase of the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork.

## ENTERING THE FIELD

As discussed earlier, there are many horizons of understanding in the <sup>fi</sup>eld which determine one’s thrownness. Some of the examples are everyday language, historicity, prejudice, tempo rality and spatiality among others. Here, we present the evidence for just two horizons, namely historicity and prejudice, to illustrate how the <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers’ entrance can be seen as being thrown in the ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## Horizon of historicity

We begin by expounding the historicity of the researcher. In our case, the ethnographer is also a seasoned programmer and has worked internationally in the corporate sector. During the initial contact with FieldTech, it was evident that the technical skills of the ethnographer were in line with the workplace IT practices of FieldTeam. Likewise, as stated earlier, our long-term research project objectives (i.e. studying of technological practices in organizations) were also somewhat closely aligned to FieldTech’s organizational strategy. The horizon of historicity discloses that the <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher was thrown in a somewhat familiar world and had some pre-understanding of the horizons of the participants. In line with the hermeneutic of thrownness, entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld and integrating into FieldTeam quickly became transparent as the <sup>fi</sup>eld notes from the <sup>fi</sup>rst day re<sup>fl</sup>ect:

[one of the managers] commented on my query on the team’s working hours: ‘You know how they [developers] work…come on, you are one of us, you should know [laughs]’. [Excerpt from the <sup>fi</sup>eld notes]

Being a researcher in an unfamiliar organization with a different culture can be challenging. Even the <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers who once belonged to the larger practice world of the <sup>fi</sup>eld face dif <sup>fi</sup>culties re-entering a rather familiar world (the Chicago school of <sup>fi</sup>eldwork is full of such exam ples; for a general discussion, see Anteby, 2013, p. 1285). But in this case, the ethnographer was described as ‘one of us’ on the very <sup>fi</sup>rst day of <sup>fi</sup>eldwork, ostensibly because of prior work experience and his historical background. Being called ‘one of us’ also meant that it was much easier than expected to become intimately in touch with the participants’ horizons of understandings. This point further manifests itself in the candid and confessional nature of many informal chats and interviews. Consider the <sup>fi</sup>rst encounter with a young programmer:

In my <sup>fi</sup>rst coffee area talk, I was asked: ‘You are a programmer?’ I saw her eyes widen with curiosity. I replied yes, but when I started to give my background, I was promptly and continuously interrupted: ‘Which [programming] languages?’… ‘Which [software development] framework? She completely ignored the fact that I was introduced as a researcher just a day ago. […] Her curiosity increased, as I provided details and was topped by a geeky compliment: ‘Ooh, C++ [programming language], very hard-core’, she smiled, almost turning into a giggle.

[Later] when I asked her about […] other team members, to my amazement I was told: ‘oh, you’ll like them; they are like us, you know, programmers!’ [Excerpt from the <sup>fi</sup>eld notes]

What is interesting is the seemingly immediate change of perspectives of both the young programmer and the ethnographer. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of this change in practice is twofold. First, following Anteby (2013), we can see it as an instance of upholding ethnographic distance while remaining involved in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. One minute the researcher is a complete stranger doing research and just starting to explain what his research project is about (ethnographic distance); the next minute he is ‘like us’ and counted as being one of the team (ethnographic involvement). Without understanding the fusion of horizon, without critically understanding our thrownness, it is rather dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to be involved while keeping distance in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Second, we acknowledge the everyday aspect of this critical dialogue. Of course, this could be just a simple matter of luck or courtesy. Nonetheless, it does seem to indicate a fusion of horizons of both the ethnographer and the participants. In this case, it is the historical fact that both parties have a background in software development that is itself grounded in a certain set of practices, and it is that which establishes the dialogue.

Although the role as a researcher was clearly mentioned in the of<sup>fi</sup>cial introduction to the team, the young programmer seemed to forget about this and from then on simply treated the researcher as a fellow software engineer (because the ethnographer was working part-time as one while also conducting the <sup>fi</sup>eld research). The other programmers reacted in the same way as the <sup>fi</sup>rst one: None brought up the subject of research but seemed more interested in knowing about the ethnographer’s computer science background. We can say that the horizon of historicity persists in the participants’ perspective. This historicity facilitated a prompt integra tion into the team as ‘one of us’ and paved the way towards engaged participation. It was no coincidence that we found that the participants tended to became rather comfortable despite the presence of the researcher in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. During project meetings and gatherings, as early as in the <sup>fi</sup>rst week, it allowed us to capture the evidence from within – up close and persona (Figure 2). The participants’ understanding of the ethnographer was formed from the very <sup>fi</sup>rst day of the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork and in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the entire period of <sup>fi</sup>eldwork that followed.

In the light of the preceding texts, using the idea of historicity in thrownness, we can say that the ethnographer in this case quickly found his feet, but this was only because of the shared background practices. Conversely, we suggest that it could be much more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for a <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher to <sup>fi</sup>nd their feet if there were little or no tangible link between the participants and researcher’s histories; a fusion of horizons in the <sup>fi</sup>eld would take longer to manifest and be more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to grasp. From the outset, this might appear as a trivial observation, but the point is, precisely, to highlight the criticality of the everyday aspects of the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork such as historicity. Such details are often bracketed out as the focus is set on the phenomena in the foreground. However, for a <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher, neglecting to pay attention to the fusion of horizons could mean that she becomes lost in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and loses her orientation.

Using our phenomenological hermeneutic lens, we can say that the historicity determines the thrownness and this thrownness is based on the fore-structure of understanding. Table 1 summarizes the movement of understanding that took place when the researcher entered the field First, the participants already belonged to a project team (FieldTeam) and thus, the software development background of all the participants, the norms and culture of being a software engineer and so forth are simply taken for granted (fore-having). The ethnographer too had belonged to many different software development teams before joining FieldTeam and thus his background was also connoted. Second, the fact that all the participants and the ethnogra pher had this shared background meant that the people immediately made certain assumptions about the researcher from the very <sup>fi</sup>rst encounter (fore-sight). They simply assumed that he al ready knew about the nature of work, work practices and work roles in a software developmen environment – there was apparently no need to explain how the team worked. Third. the forehaving and the fore-sight led to the participants now having certain expectations about what the <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher would do (fore-conception). Because the ethnographer had previous orga nizational work experience in software development, the participants simply expected him to be already familiar with software coding practices and hence it was seen as a critical <sup>fi</sup>eld moment which allowed him to integrate smoothly into the local team. Of course, these expectations of the <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher then needed to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled (Coffey, 1999, pp. 88–9). The actualization of expectation is achieved through engagement with everyday practices and demonstrating tha the researcher is indeed ‘one of them’ by becoming involved in the work during the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork and engaged with the phenomena as expected.

By spelling out the components of the fore-structure of the horizon of historicity, we observe how a researcher’s engagement with the participants in the <sup>fi</sup>eld is grounded deeply in the historicity of everyday practices. Further, it shows how the <sup>fi</sup>eld is entwined with the practices of both the researcher and the participants. As a researcher begins to engage with the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork, it is the dialectical interactions within this horizon that enable understanding. The preunderstandings of the participants and the researchers are part of an ongoing dialectic.

Having presented some evidence for the horizon of historicity, we now engage with evidence for the horizon of prejudice, to illustrate further how the <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers’ entrance can be seen as being thrown in the ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## Horizon of prejudice

As a working member of FieldTeam, the ethnographer was asked to shadow and then to proceed to manage an ongoing technological change; this was simply due to an unforeseen human resource issue. However, this task assignment is a practical example of a ‘good prejudice’ insofar as the managers had a positive pre-understanding of the researcher’s skills. Part of the work required the ethnographer to attend meetings with IT stakeholders from other involved teams (in this case, FieldGroup). In one such meeting, there was a brief discussion about what advice the ethnographer might be able to give to counter arguments from FieldGroup. Here, we observe the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of horizon of prejudice: The ethnographer is asked to support a social group in the <sup>fi</sup>eld (FieldTeam) based on assumptions (prejudice) about the researcher’s current role and previous practical experience (horizon of historicity). In this way, it was possible to disclose how subtly the two horizons fused transparently in practice.

Regarding the preceding texts, consider this excerpt from the follow-up meeting with a senior manager, which occurred soon after the intradepartmental meeting with FieldGroup representatives:

[the manager] said that he is really glad that <sup>fi</sup>nally ‘a real dialog’ has started between FieldTeam and other teams [i.e. FieldGroup]. When I asked him to unpack what he means, he explained that sometimes the developers [in FieldTeam] are so quiet in saying their mind [or immersed in their everyday practices], ‘they’ll ignore everything as long as their code is compiling’. He continued to say that in such situations it is impossible to let others know what their point of view is… since no one says ‘what the problem is’. He then thanked me politely and said that he really needs ‘someone who knows the developers’ perspective, someone who speaks for the developers’. (Excerpt from the meeting notes)

We observe how good prejudice discloses the developing intimacy with <sup>fi</sup>eld practices vis-à- vis an entrusting expectation (fore-conception) in the <sup>fi</sup>eld inasmuch as the ethnographer is seen as someone who might be able to ‘speak for’ the participants, facilitate dialogue and, in effect, present a plausible account of their perspective. Indeed, the practice of <sup>fi</sup>eldwork can be seen as an ongoing dialogue between the ethnographers and the participants (Clifford, 1983). A dialogue that is engaged polyvocality insofar as the ethnographic voice is an entwinement of many voices in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, of participants as well as the <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers (Clifford, 1988). In the same vein, towards the end of the meeting, the same subject came to the fore again as the manager disclosed by,

…referring to the [earlier] argument with FieldGroup, [he says] it’s good to have ‘this debate and how ‘no one dares says anything, no one says their point of view’, [and] that after such discussions, only then, ‘we can <sup>fi</sup>nd a ‘common ground’ which will help us… building better IT systems’.

…When I was about to leave, he revealed that ‘Jim is the quiet one, I know his personality, that’s why I put you with him, so you have to do a lot of talking’, I told him that I understand and will make sure we are all on the same page all the time, he nodded back and I moved back to my desk. (Excerpt from the meeting notes)

At <sup>fi</sup>rst glance, this conversation seems a matter of workplace openness. However, applying the thrownness lens, we see that the ethnographer is thrown in the world (1) against the horizon of prejudice and (2) the prejudice reveals others’ (participants) positions in the horizon. That is, <sup>fi</sup>rst, the prejudice discloses the work habits of the participants, which is not a negative thing but simply their absorption in practices. Along the same lines, the researcher’s counsel is sought, by the virtue of good prejudice (from our point of view), as it is grounded in rituals concerning standard technology practices which are otherwise overlooked in developers’ everyday technological habits. For instance, here, the ethnographer is already familiar with software practices rules and regulations which the developers usually taken for granted. Thus, the prejudice-laden decision of a manager led to a larger fusion of horizons, that is, an intra-team dialogue between FieldTeam and FieldGroup.

We note that the ethnographer is thrown head <sup>fi</sup>rst into the <sup>fi</sup>eld inasmuch as he is freely asked to support FieldTeam in an ongoing dialogue with another team, despite the acknowl edged status of <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher as independent and, to some extent, as Sandberg & Tsoukas (2011) put it, a ‘temporary participant’ in the <sup>fi</sup>eld practices. The upshot, nonetheless, is that the local horizons of participants fuse with the horizons of understanding of the <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher, and both are driven by their corresponding prejudices. This fusion of horizons facilitates a change of status of ethnographer in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and can be seen as a rite of passage in a practice world. The ethnographer moves from being a participant observer to a meaningful part of the holistic whole that is the practice world of the participants.

Consistent with the concept of thrownness, the intricacies of the fore-structure of the horizons of understanding presented here suggest that (1) there is a dialectical interaction in the <sup>fi</sup>eld practices which <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers encounter in their entrance and (2) in order to interpret this dialectic, both the researchers’ and participants’ perspectives need to be taken into account.

## DISCUSSION

The purpose of this paper has been to shed some light on a <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher’s entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld and how it can be theorized. We have used the concept of thrownness from Heidegger along with the hermeneutic concepts of historicity and prejudice to illuminate the practice of en trance as a rite of passage into a complex practice world. As we have seen, entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld in<sup>fl</sup>uences how an ethnographer initially engages with the participants in the research site; precisely, the entrance orientates the ethnographers in the <sup>fi</sup>eld that then holds sway during the course of the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork.

## Entrance vs. access

Many qualitative researchers have discussed the topic of gaining access to a <sup>fi</sup>eld site; insofar as gaining access can be a challenge, various suggestions have been made as to how the problem of access can be overcome (e.g. Walsham, 2006; Gobo, 2008; Myers, 2013). However, as we have demonstrated, access to a <sup>fi</sup>eld site is not the same as gaining entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Whereas ‘access’ involves gaining permission to conduct research in a particular <sup>fi</sup>eld setting, entrance is a rather engaged practice of what you see (and how you are seen) and what you do (and are asked or allowed to do) when you arrive there after gaining access. In the same vein, while good access still requires ‘co-operation’ from the participants (Pole & Hillyard, 2016, p. 27), entrance is about stepping into the practice world of participants. Hence, access and en trance into an ethnographic space are two different things. While gaining access can be best seen as an administrative concern (Gobo, 2008, p. 118–126: Neyland, 2008, pp, 63–65), en: trance is a complex phenomenological enterprise that involves engaging with the practice world of participants, requiring researchers to be sensitive to <sup>fi</sup>eld practices in the <sup>fi</sup>eld.

Entrance is also shown to be entwined with the historicity of the researcher. For example, it would be possible for a <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher to gain access to a research site (in our case,

FieldTech), but then a researcher without some practical experience of the <sup>fi</sup>eld practices (in our case, software development practices) would not be regarded as ‘one of us’ from early on in the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork. Also, it is unlikely that such a person would be asked to give advice in order to coun ter the arguments of another group within the <sup>fi</sup>eld (e.g. another project team); this is not to say that being regarded as an outsider or as not being ‘one of them’ is necessarily a bad thing. Nonetheless, it does suggest that entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld largely determines what the <sup>fi</sup>eld re searchers see and <sup>fi</sup>nd out.

Hence, the <sup>fi</sup>rst contribution of this paper is to suggest that entrance marks a critical <sup>fi</sup>eld mo ment in qualitative research methods where <sup>fi</sup>eld research is conducted. We have shown that there is not one, but two distinct initial phases of qualitative <sup>fi</sup>eld research, namely access and entrance. These phases broadly relate to getting in (access) and being there (entrance) in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Both phases are equally important in enabling the <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers to immerse themselves in the world of participants, but the one should not be confused with the other.

## Rite of passage

The second contribution of this paper is to show that entrance marks the transition not only from one phase to another but also from one status to another. That is, entrance is a rite of passage that determines what the <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers see and do and how they are seen by the others. In our case, entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld marked a change of status from being an outsider to being ‘like us’ and being ‘one of us’; from that moment onwards, the <sup>fi</sup>eld was seen as a world to be in. Of course, the notion of the entire period of ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eldwork being a rite of passage has long been an important theme in anthropology (Hammersley & Atkinson, 2007), but the idea that en trance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld can also be seen as a rite of passage is new to the IS research literature on qualitative research methods. Through this rite of passage, a <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher aims to be one with the participants rather than embarking on the quest to become one of the participants (Geertz, 2000). Here, it is the thrownness of the <sup>fi</sup>eldworkers, by orientating them in the <sup>fi</sup>eld in order to achieve the entrance, which determines how immersive a researcher can be with the participants in their practice world.

## Thrownness in the <sup>fi</sup>eld

Our third contribution is theoretical: We have used Heidegger’s concept of thrownness to provide a way of conceptually grasping the entrance as a rite of passage. The thrownness lens pro vides the possibility to understand the <sup>fi</sup>eld as a complex world where a <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher needs to place herself in a way that the world makes sense to her.

Using thrownness as a conceptual lens, <sup>fi</sup>eldworkers can also become aware of their own historicity and prejudices. As they engage in a dialogue with the participants, being aware of their own prejudices and acquiring a disposition of becoming attuned to the various voices in the <sup>fi</sup>eld should help them to be open to new meanings and understandings. Ethnographic awareness in the <sup>fi</sup>eld lets <sup>fi</sup>eldworkers know their position in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, and from that position, they can begin to approach and make sense of <sup>fi</sup>eld practices. Thrownness suggests that awareness is an engaged practice rather than a detached state that one achieves simply by observing raw facts, data or events in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Entering the practice world of the participants allows <sup>fi</sup>eldworkers to ‘be in the situations’ in the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Goffman, 1989). Grasping entrance through thrownness empowers the <sup>fi</sup>eldworkers to be there in the <sup>fi</sup>eld rather comfortably, and hence, in effect, be at home in the world of the participants (cf. Van Maanen, 2011a). By acknowledging and understanding one’s thrownness in a situation, an ethnographer can critically engage with the <sup>fi</sup>eld practices ‘along with’ the participants instead of reporting on them as a mere spectator in the <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## Research instrument calibration

In ethnographic research, the researcher is the primary data collection instrument; hence, this ‘instrument’ needs to be ‘calibrated’ (Myers, 2013, pp. 139, 187; Conquergood, 1991, p. 180). Accordingly, a plausible ethnography is one where the readers know how the research instru ment was calibrated: whom did the <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher talk to, what did they do, where were they, how did they arrive there and how long were they in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and so forth.

Hence, the fourth contribution of this paper is to make ‘instrument calibration’ explicit in qualitative <sup>fi</sup>eld research methods. This aspect tends to be ignored as <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers simply presume that the researcher is already ‘calibrated’ by virtue of their academic training. However, as Fine & Hallett (2014) point out, entering the ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eld from academia is like being thrown from one <sup>fi</sup>eld to another <sup>fi</sup>eld. While academic training is essential, it only helps to a certain extent (Fine & Hallett, 2014, p. 89). A <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher still needs the practical experience of being there in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and to become engaged with everyday practices. The concept of thrownness provides one such methodological tool to become intimately closer to phenomena in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Further, it can help researchers identify and acknowledge distinct horizons of understanding and, in so doing, become aware of and ‘calibrate’ their position in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Only an ethnographic research instrument that is appropriately calibrated in the <sup>fi</sup>eld can authentically conduct the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork (Golden-Biddle & Locke, 1993).

Entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld entails engagement with a series of critical <sup>fi</sup>eld moments during which whatever, calibration' takes place influences the entire period of fieldwork On the one hand, as we have said earlier, the researchers need to become aware of their own historicity and prejudices. Prior to beginning a study of the others, the researcher must <sup>fi</sup>rst study herself. On the other hand, the researcher is not just studying herself; she is there to learn about the social, historical and cultural practices of the participants. Hence, the ethnography is not merely a confessional account of one’s own personal experiences (which could be based on ‘bad prejudices’) but nor is it an objective account of the <sup>fi</sup>eld ‘out there’. Rather, the completed ethnography represents the written account of the dialogue between the <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers and the participants; a dialogue that is always polyphonic insofar as the ethnographic voice is an entwinement of voices, of participants as well as that of the <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher (Clifford, 1983, 1986). A plausible ethnography is one where the nature of this dialogue is explored and instrument calibration made explicit. This is also consistent with the principle of interaction between the researchers and the subjects in interpretive <sup>fi</sup>eld research, as suggested by Klein & Myers (1999).

## Practical implications

We suggest that the concept of thrownness, as applied to entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld, has some practical implications for <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers. First, the thrownness lens enables <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers to acknowledge and make explicit their own prejudices and assumptions; this in turn should help them to critically understand some of the other horizons in the <sup>fi</sup>eld such as everyday language, spatiality, temporality and so forth (Gadamer, 1989). It is by fusing the horizons of understanding that the nature of practices comes to the fore and these, then, become available for interpretation and inquiry. Also, acknowledging historicity enables the researcher to do the <sup>fi</sup>eld work along with the participants in the practice world instead of, as Miettinen et al. (2009, p. 1315) put it, becoming a ‘mere observant’ of the <sup>fi</sup>eld practices.

Second, the hermeneutics of thrownness enables the <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers, when entering the <sup>fi</sup>eld, to <sup>fi</sup>nd their feet as quickly as possible. It also encourages them to track and address anomalies by

1 moving away from the literal meaning of phenomena;

2 deferring judgement until the interpretation is clear; and

3 opening up avenues for further analysis.

Third, using the concept of the hermeneutic circle in one’s entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld paves the way to practically incorporate metaphor and narrative in an ethnographer’s account of their <sup>fi</sup>eldwork (Dreyfus, 1991, p. 36; cf. Ricœur, 1991). The best way to explain the <sup>fi</sup>eldworker’s thrownness is by providing a polyphonic narrative of their entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Clifford, 1986). For example, using the horizon of prejudice, we have shown that, in the hermeneutic circle, there are always more voices than one entwined in a meaningful whole. Field researchers can also use the vocal horizon of understanding to highlight the participants’ voices, identify their own voice, give voice to ‘the others’ and, in effect, attempt to bring ethnographic polyphony in the <sup>fi</sup>eld evidence to the fore (Clifford, 1988). The ethnographic narrative is sometimes reduced to a simple reproduction of evidence or direct quotes, but we suggest that <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers need to go beyond this and critically engage with the polyphonic nature of their ethnographic evidence.

## Limitations and future research

We acknowledge some limitations of our study. One obvious limitation is that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are from a single period of <sup>fi</sup>eldwork in one ethnographic research project only. Another is that there was only one researcher on-site. Despite this, we believe that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may be relevant to all kinds of <sup>fi</sup>eldwork inasmuch as the concept of the <sup>fi</sup>eld is central to many qualitative research methods.

One possible avenue for future research would be to study exit from the <sup>fi</sup>eld by using the thrownness lens. Like entrance, exiting the <sup>fi</sup>eld is an important phase in ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eld re search (Michailova et al., 2014). In particular, as <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers attempt to return to the aca demic world, using the concept of rite of passage can help explain how the change in a researcher’s status in<sup>fl</sup>uences interpretations, data analyses and compilation of the ethno graphic account.

Another fruitful avenue of research could be to apply and extend our work to consider entrance into symbolic, digital and virtual spaces. Scholars might also want to explore the problems of entrance where automatic access is usually given such as in autoethnography or at-home ethnography (Alvesson, 2009). Automatic access might not necessarily guarantee automatic entrance into the epistemic core of the research phenomena. There are complex power structures and con<sup>fl</sup>icting horizons of understanding that can threaten the plausibility of the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork (Karra & Phillip, 2008). New conceptualizations of entrance in autoethnography might be required.

## CONCLUSION

In this paper, we have discussed entering the <sup>fi</sup>eld as a rite of passage that represents a transition in the life of a <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher. Much more than access, entrance into the <sup>fi</sup>eld determines how the researcher is able to engage with the participants and <sup>fi</sup>eld practices. The entrance largely determines what the <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers see and do and how they are seen and understood by the others in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. One implication of our theorizing is that, before a <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher enters the <sup>fi</sup>eld, it is crucial to develop some understanding of the world where she is to be thrown. The <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers also need to become aware of their own historicity and prejudices as they engage in a dialogue with the participants. Another way of saying this is that the plausibility of an ethnographic account is improved if we know how the ‘research instrument (the <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher) was ‘calibrated’. Further, in addition to learning the social and cultural practices of the participants, <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers also need to remain open to new meanings and understandings. Our hope is that this paper will enable <sup>fi</sup>eld researchers conducting <sup>fi</sup>eldwork to <sup>fi</sup>nd their feet quickly, to remain open to new interpretations, and above all, to fee at home in the practice world that is their research site.
