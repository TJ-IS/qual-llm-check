## a b s t r a c t

Decision support systems (DSSs) aim to enhance the performance of decision makers, but to do so DSSs have to be adopted and used. Technology acceptance research shows that user evaluations (i.e., beliefs, perceptions, and attitudes) are key drivers of adoption and use. This article <sup>fi</sup>rst presents evidence from the literature suggesting that the link between user evaluations of DSSs and actual performance may be weak, or sometimes even negative. The authors then present two empirical studies in which they found a serious disconnect between user evaluations and actual performance. If user evaluations do not accurately re<sup>fl</sup>ect performance, then this may lead to harmful neglect of performance-enhancing DSSs. The article concludes with a discussion of interventions that may alleviate this problem.

## 1. Introduction

Decision support systems (DSSs) are IT-enabled tools that aim to enhance the effectiveness and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of managerial and professional decision making for ill-structured or weakly-structured problems [33]. There is a wide variety of decision support systems (see [25,26]), including passive DSSs that provide the user with compiled information only and active DSSs that provide speci<sup>fi</sup>c solutions or recommendations. In order to enhance the decision-maker's performance, DSSs have to be adopted and used [56]. According to generally accepted models of technology acceptance [10,11], technology diffusion [53] and information system success [13,14], user evaluations (i.e., perceptions, beliefs and attitudes) are key drivers of DSS adoption and use. However, evidence in the literature suggests that what users achieve with DSSs, i.e., their actual performance, does not always correspond with what users perceive, i.e., their evaluations of the DSSs. When performanceenhancing DSSs are not used because the intended users do not recognize the added value or objective quality of the system, we have a situation of harmful neglect. The disconnect between user evaluations of DSSs and actual performance may partly explain the low adoption and usage rate of DSSs in practice [8,38].

To facilitate systematic research into DSS evaluation, Rhee and Rao [52] recently proposed a general framework that is applicable to a wide variety of DSSs. In this framework, they explicitly distinguish between DSS performance as perceived by the user, on the one hand, and their actual performance with the DSS, on the other hand. In the present article, we investigate the link between user evaluations of DSSs and actual performance. The prevalent assumption in the DSS literature is that “if users give a system ‘high marks’, then it must be improving their performance” [22: p. 1827]. However, many studies in psychology and other domains challenge this assumption because they have found evidence that human perception and judgment are subject to biases. The hypothesized connection between user evaluations of DSSs and actual performance has not been researched extensively [22], but there is (indirect) evidence that there is a potential disconnect.

For example, in an experimental study, Lilien et al. [39] found that participants who had access to a database-oriented decision support system made objectively better decisions than those with access to an Excel spreadsheet only, but their subjective evaluations of both the decision outcomes and the decision process were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different. As Lilien et al. [39: p. 233] note, “we <sup>fi</sup>nd a surprising disconnect between objective performance measures that are favorable and subjective evaluation measures that are mixed or unfavorable”. Van Bruggen et al. [61] reported similar <sup>fi</sup>ndings. In their simulation study, users of a high-quality DSS (with an error between the DSS outcomes and the actual outcomes that was set to 3%) performed much better than users of a medium-quality DSS (with an error that was set to 23%), but they were not more con<sup>fi</sup>dent about the quality of their decisions. As user evaluations of DSSs are frequently used in research and practice, this apparent lack of connection with objective performance is of great concern, and motivates our research.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss possible reasons for a disconnect between user evaluations of DSSs and actual performance and then present existing evidence from the literature. Next, we present our research framework and discuss two empirical studies in which we investigated the link between user evaluations of DSSs and actual performance. Both studies show a clear discrepancy between what users perceive and what they actually achieve with DSSs. This should be a reason for concern to both researchers and practitioners, as it may lead to wrong decisions regarding the adoption and use of DSSs. A neglect of performanceenhancing DSSs, for example, may hamper a company's competitive position and should be avoided. The article concludes with a discussion of interventions that can help users form evaluative judgments of DSSs that are more accurate.

## 2. The connection between user evaluations of DSSs and actual performance

Extant research on the adoption and use of DSSs predominantly uses the well-known and extensively validated Technology Acceptance Model (TAM: [10,11]) as a theoretical lens. This model is based on Ajzen and Fishbein's [2] theory of reasoned action (TRA) and describes how people come to accept and use new technologies. According to the model, user evaluations are important drivers of behavioral intentions and actual behavior with regard to the adoption and use of computer-based systems in general. User evaluations are elicited perceptions, beliefs and attitudes towards the use of DSSs [22]. In the TAM model, the key driver of use intentions and actual use is perceived usefulness. Perceived usefulness is “the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance” [10: p. 320]. The dominant role of perceived usefulness has been con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in numerous empirical studies (see, for example, [1,2,35]).

The TAM model, as well as more general theories on the diffusion of technological innovations (e.g., [53]), assumes that users are sensitive to differences in decision outcomes (accuracy, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, etc.) as a result of technology use and are therefore able to accurately evaluate their performance [23]. Another well-known model, i.e., DeLone and McLean's model of IS success [13,14], explicitly links objective system quality with user evaluations and system use, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence individual and organizational performance. Here too, the implicit assumption is that, based on their use of the system, users are able to accurately gauge its objective quality. However, the connection between user evaluations of DSSs and actual performance is dependent on the user's ability to (1) recognize improvements in performance and (2) attribute (part of) it to the use of the DSS [22].

It is not always easy for users to evaluate the contribution of a DSS to their decision-making performance. First, the understanding of what constitutes a good decision and which factors exactly contribute to the quality of the decision may be limited because of incomplete information and/or a lack of accurate and timely performance feedback from the market [16]. When information is incomplete or when performance feedback is delayed, managers may have to rely on inferences and exploratory reasoning to assess their performance, which increases the risk of perceptual biases or misperceptions [32]. As weakly-structured or ill-structured problems, by their nature, come with incomplete information, misperceptions of the quality of a decision and the contribution of a DSS to decision-making performance are quite likely to occur.

Second, performance expectations and how people attribute success (i.e., to themselves or to the system) also play a role when forming evaluative judgments of DSSs (cf. [34]). For example, when users think that they did not perform well on the task, they may blame the DSS, but when they expect high performance, they may give themselves the credits rather than to attribute (part of) their high performance to the DSS. The connection between user evaluations of DSSs and actual performance, or the lack thereof, has received limited attention [22,23,41,47]. Evidence from the literature, which we will present in the next section, suggests that the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from using high-quality decision support systems may often be substantially more than users think [39].

## 3. Evidence on the disconnect between user evaluations of DSSs and actual performance

A DSS either enhances performance or it does not, and users recognize this or they do not. This leads to four possible situations (see Fig. 1). “Rightful Conviction” (upper right quadrant) is a situation in which the DSS enhances performance and users recognize this. “Harmful Neglect” (lower right quadrant) occurs when the DSS enhances performance, but users do not recognize this. “Seductive Illusion” (upper left quadrant) means that the DSS does not enhance performance, but users think it does. “Wise Abstention” (lower left quadrant) occurs when the DSS does not enhance performance and users recognize this. We searched the literature for DSS studies that could be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the four quadrants of Fig. 1. Eligible DSS articles were those that provided information on (i) the actual contribution of the DSS to decision-making performance, and (ii) the evaluation of the DSS by the user. We began our search in the premier outlet for DSS research in the domain of management, viz. “Decision Support Systems”. We searched all issues from 1995 to 2010 and found eight relevant studies (key words used: DSS, evaluation, performance, effectiveness, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, objective, subjective). We also used Google Scholar and the databases of JSTOR, Science Direct and EBSCO to search for additional evidence in other journals that publish DSS research, which resulted in eight additional studies (published in, for example, Management Science, Information Systems Research, Information and Management, and Decision Sciences).

Seven of the sixteen articles we found can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the “Rightful Conviction” quadrant, six in the “Harmful Neglect” quadrant, two in the “Seductive Illusion” quadrant, and one in the “Wise Abstention” quadrant of Fig. 1. Most of the presented evidence is indirect, meaning that the article reported both user evaluations and actual performance at the aggregate level without directly investigating their relationship. Only two studies, viz. Goodhue et al. [23] and Marsden and Mathiyalakan [41], explicitly examined this relationship, that is, they related the actual performance of individual users to their subjective evaluations of the DSS.

## 3.1. Rightful conviction

The ideal situation is when the effects of a DSS on actual performance and user evaluations are both positive. For example, Goodhue et al. [23] studied the effectiveness of an information system that facilitated the integration of different databases. The goal was to help managers <sup>fi</sup>nd answers to simple, structured managerial questions (such as: what are the year-to-date shipping expenses for the different types of carriers that we use?). They found that managers who used the system were more effective (i.e., they gave more accurate answers) and more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient (i.e., they needed less time to come up with an answer). They also found that user evaluations were positively related to the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the process, i.e., the less time they needed to come up with an answer, the more favorably they evaluated the system. The upper right quadrant of Fig. 1 presents six other DSS studies [5,27,29,30,45,47] that reported positive effects on both user evaluations and actual performance.

## 3.2. Harmful neglect

When the effect of a DSS on actual performance is positive, but user evaluations are negative (or neutral), users are not likely to adopt and use the DSS. In the introduction, we discussed the studies by Lilien et al. [39] and Van Bruggen et al. [61] that represent clear cases of harmful neglect. In both experimental studies, the DSSs showed a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive effect on the actual performance of the participants, but their evaluations of the DSSs were mixed or unfavorable. In the lower right quadrant of Fig. 1, we present four other DSS [15,31,44,57] studies that reported a positive effect on actual performance, but a negative effect (or no effect) on user evaluations, suggesting a lack of connection between actual performance and user evaluations.

## 3.3. Seductive illusion

When the effect of a DSS on performance is negative (or neutral), but user evaluations are positive, users are likely to adopt and use a dysfunctional DSS. Kottemann et al. [36] reported positive user evaluations of a DSS that facilitated “what-if” analyses for a productionscheduling task, despite its negative impact on decision-making effectiveness (i.e., minimizing long-run costs). The authors argued that DSSs might give users the illusion of control over (ill-structured) problems that have inherently uncertain outcomes [36]. We are not aware of other published DSS studies that reported similar effects. A study by Hung [28] showed no difference between two DSSs in terms of decision-making ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. However, one DSS was clearly favored in terms of perceived usefulness, which also suggests a misperception of the actual contribution of the DSSs.

## 3.4. Wise abstention

When the effects of a DSS on actual performance and user evaluations are both negative (or neutral), users are likely to abstain from adopting and using the DSS. Marsden and Mathiyalakan [41] reported no effect of a DSS that allowed users to perform “what-if” analyses for making commercial lending decisions (i.e., approving or rejecting loan applicants) on decision-making effectiveness (i.e., accumulated net pro<sup>fi</sup>ts). However, they found a strong, positive correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>- cient of .79 (pb.001) between the perceived usefulness of the what-if facility and the users' actual performance. This suggests that the users recognized that the DSS was not helpful for improving their performance. We did not <sup>fi</sup>nd DSS studies that reported a negative effect on actual performance along with unfavorable user evaluations, which may be due to a publication bias against negative results.

Not surprisingly, the majority of the studies presented in this section reported positive effects of the DSS on decision-making performance (13 out of 16). Nonetheless, in 46% of the cases (6 out of 13) where DSSs were actually found to be effective and for which we know how users evaluated them, users did not recognize the positive contribution of the DSSs. This high incidence of harmful neglect of performanceenhancing DSSs is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient reason for concern and a motivation to investigate the relationship between objective and subjective evaluations of DSSs more closely in two empirical studies. We discuss these studies in the next sections. Before doing so, we <sup>fi</sup>rst describe the conceptual framework that guided our empirical investigations.

## 4. Research framework

Fig. 2 depicts our research framework. The framework is based on Rhee and Rao's [52] general framework of DSS evaluation mentioned earlier, but we added a link between the effects of the DSS on actual performance and the effects of the DSS on user evaluations (see the bold arrow in Fig. 2). This link is the focus of our empirical investigations. Following Rhee and Rao [52], actual performance includes the effects of the DSS on the quality of the decision outcome and the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the decision process. Managers may be inclined to use a DSS mainly to improve their decision-making ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency or productivity rather than to enhance their effectiveness (i.e., the well-known effort-accuracy trade-off, see [48]). Ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency or productivity gains are also easier to assess for users than improvements in decision quality [39], especially for ill-structured problems that come with incomplete information and often have unclear goals [62]. Thus, it is important to take both decision-making effectiveness and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency into account when assessing the effects of the DSS on actual performance. Based on the TAM model, we assume that user evaluations in<sup>fl</sup>uence DSS adoption and use (hence, the arrow feeding back into the DSS use construct in Fig. 2).

In the remainder of this article, we discuss the results of two experimental studies in which we investigated the link between user evaluations of DSSs and objective performance. We conducted two controlled laboratory experiments with a single-factor design (i.e., type of DSS) in order to have the simplest and cleanest possible test of the performance-enhancing capacity of the DSSs. The two studies differ with regard to the complexity of the task, i.e., generating ideas for solving a business problem (Study 1) versus designing a creative marketing campaign (Study 2). The help offered by a DSS is more likely to be appreciated in a more complex task, such as in Study 2. DSSs that aim to enhance the creative output of individuals or teams are also known as Creativity Support Systems (CSSs, see [9,20]). This is an emerging type of DSS that is not widely used yet. In practice, managers are more likely to resort to popular self-help books, workshops, and (ad-hoc) training sessions to stimulate their creativity rather than to use DSSs. In Study 1, we selected representative DSSs from three broad categories of commercially available systems that aim to help people generate creative ideas, namely mind mappers, process guides, and stimulus providers (see Section 5.3). In Study 2, we developed two types of DSSs that aim to help users in designing creative marketing campaigns, namely an analogy-based DSS and a rule-based DSS (see Section 6.3).

In both experimental studies, we <sup>fi</sup>rst assessed the actual performance of the DSSs by comparing the users' creative performance to a baseline situation in which no DSS was available. Since the DSSs intend to enhance creativity, we expect their users to demonstrate higher levels of creative performance. Since managerial problems that require creativity lie towards the ill-structured end of the problem continuum (with many possible solutions), it may be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for users to assess performance improvements due to the use of a DSS, resulting in a likely disconnect between user evaluations and actual performance. The key user evaluation measure in our studies is the perceived usefulness of the DSS. We also included a few additional user evaluation measures speci<sup>fi</sup>c to each study. The main differences between the two studies are summarized in Table 1. These differences in the type of task, types of DSSs, and types of performance and user evaluation measures aim to enhance the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 5. Study 1: DSSs for generating creative ideas

In this <sup>fi</sup>rst study, the task was to come up with creative ideas in response to a business problem. For this purpose, we selected representatives of three broad types of commercially available DSSs for generating creative ideas, viz. mind mappers, process guides, and stimulus providers (see Section 5.3). These three types were identi-<sup>fi</sup>ed after a review of secondary data (articles, books, and websites). For each group, we selected a representative software application that was professionally designed and easy to use. Participants were given a general business problem that has been used in a study by MacCrimmon and Wagner [40], namely “improving an ailing donut franchise”. The task had to be performed individually in order to avoid group confounds. Since the task was to generate creative ideas, we measured actual performance by the quality of the generated ideas (effectiveness) as well as the number of generated ideas (productivity) (e.g., [20,40,42,54]) (see Study 1 in Table 1).

## 5.1. Subjects and procedure

The participants were students of undergraduate business classes, who are likely to use such DSSs in their future jobs, for example, as manager or business consultant. In total, 144 students participated in the experiment, which was announced as a creative problem solving study. They were informed that they would be paid €10 for about 90 min plus a €20 bonus if their performance was among the top 10%. Participants were between 18 and 49 years old (96% were in their twenties), their average age was 22.2 years (SD= 3.5 years), and 56% were female.

In the university's behavioral laboratory, participants <sup>fi</sup>rst completed a booklet with a number of individual difference measures (see Section 5.4.3). Next, after a brief introduction of the available DSS, participants had 5 min to familiarize themselves with the software and to ask questions. They were then told that they would have 25 min to produce as many solutions as possible in response to a business problem for which there were no right or wrong answers. They were asked to be as creative as possible. Next, the cards that contained the problem description were handed out. After 25 min, all participants were asked to save their solutions on the hard disk and to <sup>fi</sup>ll out a post-experimental questionnaire with the user evaluation measures. After handing in the questionnaire, the participants were paid and dismissed.

## 5.2. The creative task

We slightly modi<sup>fi</sup>ed the “improving an ailing donut franchise” problem that was used previously by MacCrimmon and Wagner [40]. That is, we used sandwiches instead of donuts, since sandwiches are more common in the country where the experiment was conducted Participants were asked to generate as many creative ideas as possible for supporting the advertised claim of “fresh sandwiches” (i.e., less than three hours old) while keeping the display case — in which the sandwiches were presented to consumers — full at all times. But, at the same time, their aim was to increase the company's revenues by <sup>fi</sup>nding ways to minimize the cost of disposing sandwiches during off-peak hours.

## 5.3. Experimental conditions

The <sup>fi</sup>rst condition (n=40) involved a mind-mapper DSS that facilitates the creation of mind maps that help people unfold their existing knowledge structures related to the target problem. As a typical representative, the MindMapper 3.4 Professional Edition software was chosen. This software allows for the easy creation of maps that help to better visualize the problem by linking it to associated concepts in the mind, which is claimed to enhance creativity [7]. New <sup>fi</sup>les in MindMapper show a central node, and branches with new nodes can be added simply by pressing the enter key.

The second condition (n=37) involved a process-guide DSS that systematically guides the user through all the steps of the creative problem-solving process. The creative process roughly comprises the following phases: problem presentation, preparation, idea generation, idea evaluation, and idea selection [3]. As a typical representative, the Thoughtpath™ software was chosen. Thoughtpath organizes each step of the creative process in one window that contains instructions. Clicking a button opens the window for the next step in the process. Thoughtpath includes the following steps: Describe (clearly describing the problem), Wish (making wishes about the problem and the situation after solving it), Idea (<sup>fi</sup>nding ideas to make the wishes come true), and Solution (producing complete solutions by working out the details of the selected candidate solutions).

The third condition (n=38) involved a stimulus-provider DSS that provides the user with (random) stimuli, such as words, sentences, and pictures, which are intended to serve as stimuli for generating creative solutions. The user is supposed to detect, explore and use associative connections between the provided stimuli and the problem at hand. As a typical representative, we chose the IdeaFisher Writer's Edge software (see [42]). The core of this program is a dictionary of associations; a huge database of associatively linked and categorized words. The 10,924 words associated with “sandwich” and “display case” were included as stimuli for idea generation.

The fourth condition (n=29) was a control condition in which the participants did not receive a DSS. This allowed us to assess performance improvements due to the use of a DSS. Participants in the control condition only had a word processor at their disposal.

## 5.4. Measures

## 5.4.1. Actual performance

Actual performance was measured by decision-making effectiveness (i.e., the quality of the generated solutions) and productivity (i.e., the number of generated solutions). For problems that require creativity, there is no single, correct solution, but multiple solutions are possible that, at best, can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as better or worse. Hence, expert judgment is normally used to assess solution quality in such situations [3,46]. As mentioned, the task was about fresh sandwiches. Therefore, we asked expert judges from two large food-service companies (one department head and one area manager) to independently rate the quality of the generated solutions. They rated all generated solutions on two commonly used dimensions of creativity [50], viz. novelty and usefulness (single-item, ten-point scales ranging from 1=not at all novel/useful to 10=very novel/useful). To prepare the solution documents for scoring, they were <sup>fi</sup>rst given an identical layout.

The intraclass correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients (ICCs, see [58]) for novelty and usefulness were .90 and .92, respectively.<sup>3</sup> Hence, we averaged the scores across the two judges. We then took the average of the novelty and usefulness scores [42] to obtain a single creativity score for each generated solution. We then computed an overall creativity score by averaging the creativity scores of all solutions generated by the participant. An example of a creative solution is the following: “Arrangement with tourist agency: tourist busses are brought to the sandwich shop for a snack during off-peak hours”. An example of non-creative solution is the following: “Clear the display case every three hours and <sup>fi</sup>ll it with new sandwiches”.

## 5.4.2. User evaluations

Commercially available DSSs are professionally designed and should be easy to use in order to entice potential buyers and users. Next to perceived usefulness (2 items: “helped to enhance productivity” and “increased quality”; α=.86), we therefore included two additional TAM concepts, viz. perceived ease of use, which refers to “the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free from effort” [10: p. 320] (3 items: “troublesome” (reversed), “convenient” and “fun to use”; α=.78), and use intentions (1 item: “conceivability of future use”). We also measured whether participants enjoyed performing the task (which in three conditions included the use of a DSS) (2 items: “interest in the task” and “task enjoyment”; α=.65) (see [12]). All items were measured on a <sup>fi</sup>ve-point scale (1=completely disagree to 5=completely agree).

## 5.4.3. Individual differences

Prior to the experimental task, participants <sup>fi</sup>lled out a booklet that contained measures for a number of likely covariates. Since one of our actual performance measures is the number of generated ideas, we measured the participants' ability to think divergently, i.e., to generate diverse ideas in response to an open-ended task. Divergent thinking ability was measured by means of the subtest “Identical Features” of a commonly used Verbal Creativity Test [55]. This subtest asks participants to list things that have similar semantic or functional characteristics, e.g., things that are bendable. In addition, we measured the participants' openness to experience by means of the corresponding subtest of the NEO-FFI [6], which is a widely used assessment tool for the “Big Five” personality traits. One of these traits, openness to experience, has been shown to play a role in both creativity [18] and the adoption and use of new technologies [43].

## 6. Study 2: DSSs for designing creative marketing campaigns

In the second study, the task was to design a creative marketing campaign, which is arguably a more complex task than the one in Study 1. We compared the performance of two prototypical DSSs for supporting the design of creative marketing campaigns (see Section 6.3) to a baseline condition in which no DSS was available. We again asked participants to evaluate the DSS that they had used. As in Study 1, the purpose here was to examine whether users evaluate more effective DSSs more favorably. However, in this study we also addressed a second question: if user evaluations of a DSS are not related to actual performance, what then drives these evaluations?

## 6.1. Participants and procedure

The participants were marketing students, who are comparable to the recently graduated assistant brand managers typically in charge of designing marketing campaigns in companies. The students were informed that they would be paid €30 for about 3 h. Sixty students (33% female) between 19 and 30 years old $( \mathrm { M } = 2 3 . 4 , \mathrm { S D } = 2 . 0 )$ participated in the experiment. Of the 60 participants, 73% were master students specializing in marketing and the remaining 27% were bachelor students who had taken basic marketing courses. Age, gender and education level were proportionally distributed over the three experimental conditions $( \mathrm { F _ { a g e } } ~ ( 2 , ~ 5 7 ) = . 5 9 0 , ~ \mathrm { p = . 5 6 ; ~ \chi _ { g e n d e r } ^ { 2 } ( 2 ) = }$ .150, $\mathtt { p } = . 9 3 ; \chi _ { \mathrm { e d u l e v e l } } ^ { 2 } ( 2 ) = . 6 \tilde { 8 } 2 , \mathtt { p } = . 7 1 ) .$

Upon arrival in the university's behavioral laboratory, participants were randomly assigned to one of the three conditions: an analogy-based DSS, a rule-based DSS, or no DSS (see Section 6.3. for more details). In the lab, the participants <sup>fi</sup>rst collectively took a 15-min creative ability test (viz. the Abbreviated Torrance Test for Adults, [21]). Next, they were asked to read the assignment, including the campaign brief and a two-page manual on how to use the DSS. The participants were given 3 h to work on the task individually. After completing the task, they <sup>fi</sup>lled out a survey containing a measure of perceived usefulness and two additional subjective evaluation measures, namely the perceived impact of the DSS and the participants' general attitude towards DSS use. We also included two process measures: DSS usage time and intensity (see Study 2 in Table 1).

## 6.2. The creative task

The task was to design a creative marketing campaign for a wellknown Dutch beer brand. The aim of the campaign was to boost loyalty among heavy users and to consolidate the brand's core values (e.g., active in life, supreme quality, and passionate). The campaign also had to adhere to the brand's connection with (live) music. The campaign proposal had to include a brief description of the idea, the sales promotion technique(s), supporting activities, and a rough estimation of the costs. The participants were instructed to be creative and not to copy one of the brand's previous campaigns. They were also told that their proposals were to be judged by experts.

## 6.3. Experimental conditions

The <sup>fi</sup>rst condition (n=20) involved an analogy-based DSS. That is, this DSS comprised a collection of cases from the domain under study (i.e., previous, successful marketing campaigns) and a set of search criteria to retrieve cases for situations similar (or analogous) to the problem at hand (cf. [37]). Analogical reasoning has been shown to enhance creativity (see, for example, [24]). Participants were told that they could use, adapt, or combine the retrieved cases that they found useful for constructing a solution.

The second condition (n=20) involved a rule-based DSS. This DSS was a module taken from the BRANDFRAME software [63]. Users were asked to specify the problem situation by de<sup>fi</sup>ning (1) the goals of the campaign, (2) the constraints of the campaign, and (3) the relative importance of these goals and constraints. The output was a ranking of sales promotion techniques (in descending order of appropriateness) plus a brief description of each technique, including its advantages and disadvantages. The knowledge incorporated in this rule-based DSS was derived from marketing textbooks and experts.

The third condition (n=20) involved no DSS but a paper document that comprised an inventory of sales promotion techniques plus a brief description of each technique, including its advantages and disadvantages. The content of this paper document was taken from the rule-based DSS. The paper document was also given to the participants in the two DSS conditions. Contrary to the rule-based DSS, the paper document did not provide advice on which technique would be most appropriate given the problem situation.

## 6.4. Measures

## 6.4.1. Actual performance

Actual performance was measured by decision-making effectiveness and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. As in Study 1, we asked expert judges to rate the quality of the solutions produced by the participants. We sent the 60 campaign proposals for evaluation to three experts (two judges were creative directors of a marketing agency and the other judge was the manager of the beer brand mentioned in the task description). The proposals were sent in randomized batches of 20 proposals each.<sup>4</sup> All identifying information with respect to the participant and the experimental condition had been removed.

The expert judges rated all campaign proposals on seven items related to novelty (three items: uniqueness, originality, surprise) and usefulness (four items: effectiveness, <sup>fi</sup>t with the brand, appeal, usefulness). The items were measured on a scale from 0 (= very poor) to 10 (= excellent). Non-orthogonal factor analyses and reliability analyses within judges revealed two related, but distinct dimensions, namely a novelty and usefulness dimension, which were both internally consistent (all Cronbach's alphas> .85). Hence, we averaged the individual item scores to create a single novelty score and a single usefulness score per campaign per judge. As a measure of inter-rater reliability, we calculated the intraclass correlation coef-<sup>fi</sup>cients (ICCs). The coef<sup>fi</sup>cients were .62 and .72 for the novelty and usefulness scores, respectively. Hence, we averaged the novelty and usefulness scores across experts. We <sup>fi</sup>nally averaged these aggregated novelty and usefulness scores [42] to obtain a single creative performance score.

We used Parikh et al.'s [47] measure of “decision time” as a measure of decision-making ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. That is, we subtracted the time worked with the available means of support from the total time that the participant took to complete the task in order to obtain the net “decision time”, i.e., the time needed to work out a solution after using the support tool. We did this because the time needed to get acquainted with the available means of support clearly differed across the three experimental conditions (i.e., the analogy-based DSS, rule-based DSS, and paper document only). We measured total time and system usage time by means of self-reports and computer log <sup>fi</sup>les. Due to technical problems, the log-<sup>fi</sup>les registered total time for 90% of the participants, but DSS usage time for only 53% of the participants. The correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients between self-reported and computer-recorded times were nonetheless very high (r=.83, pb.001 for DSS usage time, and r=.90, pb.001 for the total time). Because of these high correlations and the absence of computerrecorded usage time for the paper document in the no DSS condition, we proceeded with the self-reported times.

## 6.4.2. User evaluations

Besides measuring perceived usefulness, for which we used six items from the measurement instrument developed and validated by Davis [10], we measured an additional concept of the TAM model in this study, viz. the general attitude towards DSS use [11] for which we used eight items from a measurement instrument developed and validated by Van Bruggen [60]. Examples of items for perceived usefulness are: “using the system increased the quality of the solution” and “using the system made it easier to come up with a solution” (ranging from 1=completely disagree to 7=completely agree) (α=.93). A sample item for the general attitude towards DSS use is the following: “I think that marketing decisions made using a system are better” (ranging from 1=completely disagree to 7=completely agree) (α=.88). The attitude towards DSS use was measured after the participant had worked with the DSS. We also included the perceived impact of the DSS on the solution as a subjective evaluation measure of the DSS (on single-item scale ranging from 0% to 100%).

## 6.4.3. DSS use and individual differences

Next to DSS usage time, as discussed in the previous section, we also assessed the intensity of DSS use. For the analogy-based DSS, we therefore asked participants to report the cases that they had read and used. “Case read” means that the participant had read a case that was stored in the DSS. “Case used” means that the participant had actually used a case, or elements of the case, for constructing a solution. For the rule-based DSS, we asked participants which sales promotion technique the DSS had recommended and which technique they had eventually used.

Finally, we took into account a number of likely covariates, such as the participant's creative ability, age, gender, and education level, which might in<sup>fl</sup>uence both actual performance and user evaluations of the DSSs. We also controlled for the length of the campaign proposal as the sheer number of words may in<sup>fl</sup>uence experts' quality ratings of the solution (see, for example, [39]).

## 7. Conclusions and discussion

## 7.1. Substantive findings

User evaluations of decision support systems are commonly used in research and practice, but it is not clear whether, and when, these evaluations accurately re<sup>fl</sup>ect objective performance. In this article, we <sup>fi</sup>rst examined the literature looking for evidence on the connection between user evaluations of DSSs and actual performance. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed sixteen DSS studies that reported both user evaluation measures and actual performance measures and categorized them into four different categories: “Rightful Conviction” (7 studies), “Harmful Neglect” (6 studies), “Seductive Illusion” (2 studies) and “Wise Abstention” (1 study). Of the studies that reported a positive effect of the DSS on actual performance, almost half reported neutral or negative user evaluations. This is alarming as it suggests that users often fail to recognize the performance-enhancing potential of DSSs. The results of the two empirical studies presented in this article add to this bleak picture. We failed to <sup>fi</sup>nd signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive correlations between user evaluations of the DSSs and actual performance in either of the studies. We even found signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly negative correlations, meaning that improvements in actual performance were associated with less favorable evaluations of the DSS in question.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that if users were to follow their own perceptions, effective DSSs may not be adopted and used (harmful neglect) or ineffective DSSs may be adopted and used (seductive illusion). In our studies, the mind-mapper DSS (Study 1) and the rule-based DSS (Study 2) would be adopted and used based on their favorable user evaluations, even though these DSSs neither increased the quality of the solution nor did they speed up the decision-making process or improve productivity. In Study 1, the stimulus-provider DSS that enhanced performance most was the least likely to be used in the future. In Study 2, the analogy-based DSS enhanced actual performance, but it was perceived as less useful than just a paper document. Based on the process data of Study 2, it seems that readily observable indicators, such as usage time and usage intensity, drive user evaluations more than actual performance. Neglecting performance-enhancing DSSs or using dysfunctional DSSs may eventually hurt the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of an organization.

From a research perspective, the observed disconnect between user evaluations of DSSs and actual performance calls for great care when using user evaluations as indicators or proxies of actual performance. As demonstrated in this article, what users actually achieve with a DSS may not always correspond with what they perceive. In other words, subjective and objective measures of DSS performance may produce very different results (cf. [4]). Although including different measures of the same construct may enhance content validity in meta-analytical studies of the TAM or similar models [14,59], it may deteriorate statistical validity when the measures are disconnected. For example, in their meta-analysis, Petter and McLean [49] reported a weak link between the constructs “system use” and “user satisfaction”, which they argued might be due to the fact that “use was measured as actual use, self-reported use, depth of use, and importance of use” [49: p. 164].

## 7.2. Limitations and further research

The participants in our studies were business school students. Students have been shown to be good surrogates for real managers in experimental studies [51]. Moreover, these students are prospective users of DSSs in their future roles as consultants and product, brand or general managers. Nonetheless, it would be worthwhile to replicate our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in a <sup>fi</sup>eld setting with practitioners. The moderate inter-rater reliability coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for the creativity ratings in Study 2 may be another limitation. However, according to Finke at al. [19: p. 41], inter-rater agreements for creativity ratings are typically lower than in other <sup>fi</sup>elds, because for creativity “judges should disagree to some extent, as a re<sup>fl</sup>ection of their varying backgrounds and expertise”. In their creativity studies, Finke et al. [19: p. 41] found inter-rater reliability coef<sup>fi</sup>cients that “typically ranged from .5 to .6”. Using hard, objective performance measures (e.g., sales) would, of course, be better (see [56]), but that would require the actual implementation of all generated campaign proposals, which is not feasible.

An interesting question for further research is whether the observed disconnect between user evaluations of DSS and actual performance is dependent on the area of management. Are areas with less structured decision problems, such as marketing, strategy and new product development, more prone to this phenomenon? Several studies listed in the “Harmful Neglect” quadrant of Fig. 1 deal with marketing problems and our empirical studies deal with problems that require creative solutions. In such domains, it is not easy to judge the objective quality of a solution. It would be interesting to study whether a disconnect also exists in domains with more structured problems, such as inventory, transportation and scheduling problems.

## 7.3. Interventions

Now that we know that the user's recognition of the performanceenhancing potential of a DSS does not always come naturally, an interesting question for further research is: what kind of interventions or strategies can we deploy in such situations? In our studies, user evaluations of the performance-enhancing DSSs appeared to be insuf-<sup>fi</sup>cient to stimulate their adoption and (continued) use. What kind of interventions could alleviate this problem?

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide some clues for devising interventions that could stimulate the rate of adoption and use of the studied DSSs and, hence, would help to better exploit their potential for enhancing the creative output of employees. Two potentially effective strategies come to mind. (1) Tell success stories. To alleviate the “lack of connection” problem, stories based on (in-company) experiments or <sup>fi</sup>eld studies (see, for example, [17]) that demonstrate the positive effects of DSSs on creative performance could be used. But it also seems important to warn users that such performance improvements may be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to assess immediately and that the contribution of a DSS may only become evident after a period of extended use. (2) Use ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciency gains as bait. As mentioned earlier, it is generally easier to assess ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains (i.e., reduced cognitive effort or time saved) than to assess improvements in decision quality [39]. In Study 2, users tended to evaluate the DSS more positively when they were able to construct a solution more quickly after using the DSS. Such ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains could be emphasized to stimulate use, which may eventually enhance decision quality (through DSS use).

Accurate assessments of DSS performance are essential for the contribution of DSSs to managerial decision-making. In terms of further research, it is therefore important to study the conditions that facilitate or hinder users in forming accurate evaluative judgments of DSS performance. This research agenda should also include a systematic investigation of potential moderators of the relationship between user evaluations and actual performance, such as the type of problems and the experience of the user. This will help to design effective interventions that facilitate the adoption and use of performanceenhancing DSSs in practice.

Niek Althuizen is Assistant Professor at the Department of Marketing, ESSEC Business School Paris. He received his Master degree in Agricultural Economics from Wageningen University (Netherlands) and his Doctoral degree in Marketing from the Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University. His research focuses on marketing decision making and decision support, with a special interest in creativity support systems.

Astrid Reichel is Assistant Professor at the Department of Management, Vienna Universit of Economics and Business. She received her Master and Doctoral degrees in Business Administration from the University of Vienna. Her dissertation work involved a comparative analysis of creativity support systems. Her current research focuses on international and comparative human resource management, professionalization of HRM, the HRMperformance-link and careers.

Berend Wierenga is Professor of Marketing at Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University. His research interests focus on marketing models, marketing decision making and marketing management support systems. He has published widely on these topics, in journals such as Journal of Marketing and Management Science. He has also written several books most recently: Marketing Management Support Systems: Principles Tools and Implementation. He is the editor of the Handbook of Marketing Decision Models, which appeared in the International Series in Operational Research and Management Science. Before joining RSM, he served as a faculty member at Wageningen University (Netherlands), where he also obtained his PhD and he has held visiting positions at Stanford University, INSEAD and The Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania.
