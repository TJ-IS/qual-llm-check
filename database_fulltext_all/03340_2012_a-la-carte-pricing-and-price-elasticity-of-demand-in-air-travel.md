## a b s t r a c t

Global distribution systems (GDSs) and online travel agencies (OTAs) have been providing critical intermediation services for the air travel services industry. Their systems services have eroded the airlines' revenue by encouraging consumers to do price comparison-based purchases though, which has caused the commoditization of air travel services. The airlines have begun to adopt à la carte pricing mechanisms in their direct distribution channel, especially through their Web sites. This way, they can provide travelers with an array of information for different airline product bundles and the flexibility to customize different bundles. This mechanism design may allow airlines to decommoditize their services in the minds of consumers. We analyze two years of data from a large international airline that sold airline tickets via several different direct and indirect distribution channels. Our results suggest that demand in the à la carte channel as a whole is less priceelastic. The results for price elasticity for different market segments and bundle types across channels were not altogether consistent with our predictions though. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, for some branded bundles of services, price elasticity is lower in the à la carte channel, and for others it is higher. We discuss the implications for managers and the design of à la carte selling mechanisms.

## 1. Introduction

Travel agents and global distribution systems (GDSs) have played important roles as intermediaries for air travel distribution since the 1970s [12]. Sabre (www.sabre.com), Amadeus (www.amadeus.com), Galileo (www.travelport.com/lob/gds/galileo.aspx) and Worldspan (www.travelport.com/lob/gds/Worldspan.aspx) have provided computer search and <sup>fl</sup>ight booking services for nearly every travel agent in the U.S. As early as 1985, for example, 86% of all tickets were issued by intermediaries [13].

Although previous studies and forward-looking industry observers predicted that the Internet would enable the airlines to disintermediate the middlemen in the structure of air travel services distribution [31], in 2008 the GDSs still processed more than twothirds of airline bookings in the U.S., and one-half in Europe [34]. That is in part because GDSs successfully penetrated the Internet channel by powering some of the early leading online travel agencies (OTAs), such as Expedia (www.expedia. com) and Travelocity (www. travelocity. com). Meanwhile, the airlines fought back by introducing their own online travel sites. During 2009, over 105 million American adults used the Internet to make their air travel arrangements. In that year, 38% of travelers preferred to book via OTAs while 34% preferred airline Web sites. This has created a situation in which the digital intermediaries have been able to exert more of control over online distribution and selling at the expense of the airlines [7]. Airline suppliers such as American Airlines and Delta Air Lines only sell 20% to 30% of their tickets through their own Web sites [32].

The intermediation of the GDSs and OTAs thus comes at a cost for the airlines. First, the oligopolistic market structure present in the intermediated market permits the GDSs and OTAs to charge higher booking fees to the airlines. There are industry estimates that GDS booking fees account for about 3.4% of the cost of the average ticket sold for the carrier, while combined reservation and sales expenses represent about 10.1% of total operating expenses for major U.S. airlines [22]. Second, for the airlines, <sup>fi</sup>nding ways to differentiate their products amid competition for online distribution has become increasingly hard. This is because the selling mechanism design employed by OTAs is focused on price discovery. Most OTAs sort search results based on ticket prices and schedules, diminishing the ability of suppliers to communicate their value propositions to consumers. (See Fig. 1.) Consumers tend to pay more attention to prices than other attributes of the travel services as a result. This has increased price competition. Anthony and Christensen [1] refer to this as the commoditization of products. It occurs when sellers cannot differentiate their products and services in the minds of consumers, even though they are different.

With easy search and comparison for consumers, suppliers that provide identical product and service offerings will earn very little. Since much of the industry sales are intermediated, the airlines have no real strategic pricing <sup>fl</sup>exibility and must honor all of the sales they make, even if some lead to losses [33]. As a result, the airlines have attempted to decommoditize their offerings to obtain more power in their essential distribution channels. This involves restoring the differentiation of their services relative to other airlines, so consumers will not solely focus on product prices. The objective is to regain a superior market position, which then improves pro<sup>fi</sup>tability [6]. One of the approaches airlines are increasingly employing in their own travel Web sites is à la carte pricing. This approach has also been adopted by hotels and hospitality services, cruise vacation and tourism services, and rental car and public transportation services.

À la carte means “from the menu.” In air travel, it is a mixed bundling pricing mechanism in which all the products and services offered to consumers are sold in bundles, but also individually listed, described, and priced. Its use is intended to offer greater shopping <sup>fl</sup>exibility by enabling travelers to select the services and air ticket bundles that maximize their utility based on their needs and tastes. United Airlines (www.united.com) was one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst service providers in 2005 to offer à la carte pricing through its Economy Plus Access program. Other examples include Southwest Airlines' (www. southwest.com) and American Airlines' (www.aa.com) ticket bundles. In line with current airlines' efforts to shift the burden of pro<sup>fi</sup>tability to fee-based services, passengers now must purchase services that provide higher quality services and more attractive product features for a fee.

Table 1 compares the à la carte pricing channel with the traditional, non-à la carte pricing intermediated distribution channel in the air travel industry.<sup>1</sup> We will refer to the latter as the traditional channel. The information processing capabilities of the GDSs were developed in mainframe computing systems during the 1970s. They made it possible for travel agents to search for low prices for airline tickets. Building on the GDSs' capabilities, later the OTAs began to offer GDS-based information to help consumers compare the tickets of different airlines. The OTAs have updated and expanded their systems capabilities to handle the current requirements for air travel services ticket booking and distribution. Still, however, they don't have the capability to support the unique requirements of individual airlines to implement à la carte pricing and offer customized bundles. The OTAs are not able to offer much more than the pre-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed standard bundles that the airlines have been offering for decades.

The à la carte pricing mechanism creates several opportunities for the airlines to decommoditize their products and services, so air travelers no longer will focus on comparing prices. Passenger demand is likely to be more price-inelastic as a result. First, à la carte pricing enables airlines to offer several variations of their branded products. The different features of branded bundles, such as AA's Economy Super Saver and First Flexible bundles, allow them to try to differentiate themselves. The expectation is that this design based on branded bundles will lessen price competition [35]. See Fig. 2.

Second, à la carte pricing allows airlines to engage in hyperdifferentiation and resonance marketing, enabling them to offer services to more closely match what travelers want to purchase. Some airlines allow customers to customize the branded bundles that appear upon a search request. Thus, travelers will be encouraged to select offerings based on what they believe is value-maximizing, and again, the expectation is that this design will reduce the salience of price in the purchase process [10].

Third, the higher level of product information (i.e., product features and quality) permits passengers to scrutinize detailed information about offerings in the transaction process. This information is likely to mitigate uncertainty about the product characteristics and the need for consumers to compromise on their purchase based on a lack of perceived <sup>fi</sup>t [10]. The product transparency of the à la carte design can make demand less price-elastic than in the more opaque traditional distribution channel.

The introduction of the à la carte selling mechanism suggests that the suppliers' Web sites are in transition, from offering a limited set of <sup>fi</sup>xed-price bundles, similar to what they make available through the GDSs and OTAs, to a collection of products and services that can be mixed and matched according to the needs of individual travelers [33]. The airlines believe this will increase their pro<sup>fi</sup>tability through diverting consumer attention away from airline ticket prices, but the impact on travel demand is not clear.

We will analyze industry data to assess whether à la carte pricing can decommoditize air travel services in the minds of consumers to enhance airline revenue. We will assess price elasticity of demand for two pricing mechanisms, the direct-to-the-customer and indirect, intermediated sales channels. We will leverage prior studies that model consumer demand, and examine changes for different distribution channels, using data on tickets sold by an international air carrier. The price elasticity comparisons across channels provide insights about the possible decommoditization effects of à la carte pricing, and allow us to empirically evaluate the claim that passengers value the <sup>fl</sup>exibility of an à la carte offering.

Our research questions are: What is the difference in price elasticity of demand between the à la carte pricing and traditional pricing channels? What are the implications for the current battle over air travel services distribution? To answer these, Section 2 <sup>fi</sup>rst presents the theoretical background for this research. Section 3 then describes the research context and data for this study. Section 4 develops a model for air travel demand to represent channel strategies in air travel services. Section 5 presents the results of a baseline model, and extensions to it. Section 6 interprets the results, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Theory

We next will discuss the relevant theoretical background related to the direct and indirect distribution channels, price elasticity, and empirical models of price elasticity in the air travel industry.

## 2.1. Information technology and intermediation

Chircu and Kauffman [9] proposed an intermediation/ disintermediation/reintermediation cycle that describes changes in the market distribution over time in light of the development of information technologies. Traditionally, suppliers have relied on intermediaries for value-added functions that are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for them to implement or for which they have insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient expertise to offer cost-effectively. For instance, intermediaries provide services for managing inventory, breaking up bulk shipments, supplying information, marketing, and coordinating transactions [27]. Airline tickets illustrate such reliance on intermediaries: it typically has been costly for airlines to transact with passengers directly [12]. The GDSs that connect airline and passengers have played a critical role as middlemen in the air travel industry.

ITs have created novel electronic distribution channels, which have changed traditional distribution and decreased supplier costs to capture information and communicate with consumers. The airlines have tried to disintermediate the GDSs because they now are able to contact travelers directly via their own direct “Supplier.com” Web sites. The GDSbased OTAs, such as Expedia and Travelocity, have been very successful in intermediating online air travel bookings though.

The Internet is a virtual shopping mall in which consumers can easily move among supplier sites. Competition often occurs between collocated stores. Suppliers, thus, must protect their brands and margins, especially when intermediaries obtain a high share of the market or when they begin to exert control over the preferred points of consumer contact. Direct sales are able to attract the highest value consumers from intermediaries [27]. So the direct channel reduces intermediaries' market power, resulting in lower mark-ups and pro<sup>fi</sup>t margins for them. Not surprisingly, the airlines are investing and redesigning their own Web sites to redisintermediate GDSs and OTAs. One of the main strategies for this redesign is the use of à la carte pricing interfaces, and one way to assess whether this approach is working is by comparing price elasticity across the different channels.

## 2.2. Price elasticity

Price and demand are closely related. Demand is “the willingness … of buyers to purchase different quantities of a good at different prices during a speci<sup>fi</sup>c time period” [2, p. 54]. Based on the law of demand from microeconomics, as the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded of the good falls. This law doesn't reveal anything about how quantity demanded changes as price changes. So economists use price elasticity of demand, the ratio of percent change in the quantity demanded for a 1% change in price.

Understanding the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence price elasticity is essential [5], in view of changes in fuel costs, landing rights, and operational costs. Re<sup>fi</sup>nements to the estimation models and related factors for consumer price sensitivity have been proposed. Brons et al. [6] list several determinants of demand for passenger air travel. First, more product substitutes lead to a higher degree of price elasticity, whereas a lack of them leads to lower price elasticity. In air travel, multiple levels of substitution can be observed. Consumers may choose among different airlines for different routes, select among different transportation modes for the same destination, and consider destinations with similar but not identical characteristics.

Three other drivers are relevant. Income affects demand. As income increases, expenditures on air travel become a smaller share of a consumer's disposable income, which encourages more demand [4]. Flight length is another factor: long-distance <sup>fl</sup>ights have lower demand than short-distance <sup>fl</sup>ights. Substitutes for long <sup>fl</sup>ights are fewer than for short <sup>fl</sup>ights though, which implies an inverse relationship between distance and price sensitivity. Thus, it's not clear in advance which effect will prevail.

Trip purpose or market segment can also affect price elasticity. Dresner [16] notes that leisure and business travelers value their time differently. Leisure travelers are more price-sensitive and inclined to seek lower fares due to income constraints. Business travelers prefer more frequent <sup>fl</sup>ights, less transit time, and shorter waits in the airport. Airfares are a smaller part of their total travel costs. So an increase in airfare affects their all-in travel costs less. Business travelers are concerned about effectiveness, so they will pay for <sup>fl</sup>exibility, comfort and time-savings.

## 2.3. À la carte pricing and price elasticity

We believe that à la carte pricing can make demand less priceelastic through bundle customization, hyperdifferentiation, branded variants, and higher transparency.

## 2.3.1. Bundle customization

The intermediated channel offers consumers airline tickets in the form of bundles. This is a pure bundling strategy: the services are sold only in packages and never separately [36]. Although pure bundling enables sellers to sell a larger number of products in one single transaction [17], they are less able to differentiate themselves from competitors, leading to more price comparison and competition. À la carte pricing enables a shift from pure bundling strategies to mixed bundling strategies. Consumers can modify bundles by adding or dropping services.

## 2.3.2. Hyperdifferentiation

Mixed bundling makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for a competitor to imitate speci<sup>fi</sup>c bundle customizations, and enhances product variety, which may reduce price sensitivity [36]. Thus, à la carte pricing enables hyperdifferentiation, the ability of a <sup>fi</sup>rm to offer almost anything that any potential customer might want, increasing product <sup>fi</sup>t and willingness-to-pay [10]

## 2.3.3. Branded variants

Branded variants refer to variations of branded products of suppliers [3]. Their variety enables consumers to choose different options to satisfy their needs [29]. They typically prefer sellers who can provide variety in a salient manner, by branding the variants of a product. This may discourage consumers from shopping across stores, and their price elasticity of demand should decrease [3].

## 2.3.4. Product transparency and resonance marketing

The higher level of product transparency in à la carte pricing gives the airlines the opportunity to practice resonance marketing. Consumers are able to select goods and services based on what they truly want to purchase, which reduces the salience of price [11]. À la carte pricing can improve product <sup>fi</sup>t by providing tailored offerings and satisfying traveler needs through the <sup>fl</sup>exible creation of bundles. It aims to increase consumer trading out behavior, where consumers seek to <sup>fi</sup>nd non-traditional alternatives with the best <sup>fi</sup>t for their preferences, and decrease their trading down behavior, which involves the search for less expensive offerings.

## 3. Context and data

We next discuss the empirical data on booking records that we have been able to obtain through a large international airline <sup>fi</sup>rm. This carrier wishes to remain anonymous. We obtained about two million individual booking records for forty city-pairs for travel in February and March 2009 and February to April 2010. The off-peak season bookings in our data set are more appropriate because they re-<sup>fl</sup>ect demand patterns, as opposed to the supply patterns observed in peak periods due to capacity constraints. Our data also contain a booking window of up to fourteen weeks prior to a <sup>fl</sup>ight's departure. Each record has tickets sold for a given origin–destination (OD), travel and booking dates, inventory class, agency name, bundle sold, and traveler frequent <sup>fl</sup>yer member status. We aggregated tickets sold by channel (à la carte, traditional), travel purpose (business, leisure), branded bundle sold, advance purchase week, OD city-pair, and booking year. This aggregation produced 17,920 unique records.

Two pricing mechanisms are included: the à la carte and traditional channels. The à la carte pricing channel includes the airline's own Web site and other travel agencies that sell in à la carte mode. Other Web sites with full à la carte content have the same interface. This pricing mechanism is not available in the channels intermediated by the GDSs, including the OTAs. The traditional channel is used by travel agents that sell airline tickets in the conventional way, in fixed bundles that cannot be altered.

Though branded OTAs have different characteristics and levels of information transparency [24], their user interfaces offer essentially the same support for search. They sort and compare ticket bundles on the basis of a set of limited criteria: price and schedule. The search logic across all these websites is the same. In addition, travel agencies typically use the GDSs to serve customers on the phone or face-to-face, with a similar interface that GDS-powered OTAs have. Thus, it is justi<sup>fi</sup>able to aggregate the bookings of the

GDSs and the OTAs, even though their Web interfaces may differ in other ways.<sup>2</sup>

We categorized the travelers who purchase airline tickets in the business or leisure segment, depending on the inventory class, departure date, frequent <sup>fl</sup>yer status, and length-of-stay, and whether the stay included a Saturday night. Four different branded bundles (with their true names disguised) are offered: Deeply-Discounted, Discounted, Flexible, and Premium. The bundles include different services, and are priced differently according to the services that they contain. The Deeply-Discounted bundle has the lowest price and fewest features; it excludes services such as advance seat assignment and extra mileage accrual. The Premium bundle is the highest-feature bundle with the highest price; it includes seating in Business Class and extra mileage accrual. In the à la carte channel, travelers are explicitly informed about the name of the ticket bundle and the services included in a bundle. By clicking on one of the branded bundle names, the user can see product features and fare rules. They can also add or drop services to a branded bundle. For example, the customer can add seat assignment to the Deeply-Discounted bundle.

## 4. The model

Based on the previous literature, we consider the general model, DEMAND=f (PRICE, CHANNEL, Controls). DEMAND is estimated as a proxy variable, QUANTITY, for airline tickets sold for a given city-pair route. PRICE is the average price in dollars of the airline tickets sold for a given city-pair route. And Controls represents other factors known to in-<sup>fl</sup>uence price elasticity of demand. Since our purpose is to study how à la carte pricing mechanisms affect airline sales, we include CHANNEL to represent the traditional and à la carte channels. See Table 2 for de<sup>fi</sup>nitions.

## 4.1. Main effects

## 4.1.1. Price

Price is an essential element for predicting consumer demand. PRICE captures market prices for one-way trips across channels, segments, frequent <sup>fl</sup>yer membership, bundle types, and OD city-pairs. The airlines set different advance purchase fares to adjust consumer demand. PRICE represents posted prices for each week before a <sup>fl</sup>ight's departure, that is, across booking periods.

## 4.1.2. Channel

The CHANNEL dummy stands for two distribution channels. It allows us to consider channel substitution to model changes in consumer demand. The à la carte channel contains the airline's own Web site. Authorized travel agents also have access to the à la carte content. Travel agents use the traditional channel to sell <sup>fi</sup>xed-feature and <sup>fi</sup>xed-price bundles that cannot be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed by air travelers. Included in this category are sales via GDS-based of<sup>fl</sup>ine travel agencies and GDS-based OTAs.

## 4.2. Control variables

## 4.2.1. Advance purchase

ADVPURCH measures time of purchase in weeks before departure. A traveler's urgency to book is not controlled for by PRICE. ADVPURCH captures the urgency of consumer demand as the <sup>fl</sup>ight date approaches. We expect a negative relationship between ADVPURCH and QUANTITY.

## 4.2.2. Market segment

The SEGMENT dummy corresponds to the market segments of business and leisure travelers, who have different levels of price sensitivity. This variable has proven to be valuable in models that predict consumer demand, since it captures unique variance in the dependent variable.

## 4.2.3. OD city-pair dummies

The OD dummies are used to represent the OD city-pairs in our data set. They control for macroeconomic or regional drivers (regional economy, population, income level, etc.) that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence demand across the different cities. They act as a proxy for the relevant catchment area, the region populated by travelers that use a city's airports [38]. They capture the <sup>fi</sup>xed effects of regional variables such as income and business activity. We worked with other variables that permitted us to capture information about the hub cities of the airline, but they added no value.

## 4.2.4. Branded bundle dummies

The airline's four standard bundles are branded and offered upon a search request. The services in each bundle affect consumer demand. The BUNDLE dummies capture the branded bundle characteristics and <sup>fi</sup>xed effects on demand, and unobservable but relevant attributes.

## 4.2.5. Booking year

The data in this study cover airline ticket bookings in 2009 and 2010. The YEAR dummy variable represents the <sup>fi</sup>xed effects on demand, with 2009 as the base case.

## 5.1. Model diagnostics and assessment

## 5.1.1. Correlation, multicollinearity and heteroskedasticity

We <sup>fi</sup>rst ran pair-wise correlations to detect potential data problems prior to estimating our model. See Table 3. The correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients are not as high as the traditional tolerance thresholds for collinearity at 0.80. We also calculated the variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factors (VIFs) to gauge whether there were any remaining multicollinearity problems. The largest VIF among the main effects and control variables was 5.43, which is below the applicable threshold value of 10 for identifying problems. Overall, the tests suggest no multicollinearity problems in the reduced model. Table 4 includes descriptive statistics of the sample. We also conducted multiple tests for heteroskedasticity, and found evidence that it was present. Based on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we will report the results of the model estimation using White's heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors.<sup>3</sup>

## 5.1.2. Endogeneity

When there is correlation between an independent variable and the error term, the independent variable may be viewed as an endogenous variable that is correlated with omitted variables. This can lead to biased estimates of the model's parameters. Also, price often is endogenously determined, leading to model misspeci<sup>fi</sup>cation. To evaluate whether endogeneity was a problem, we conducted a generalized Hausman test. This procedure involves separate estimations of the model with ordinary least squares (OLS) and instrumental variables (IV) regression. It is followed by a comparison to evaluate whether there is a difference between the variances of the ef<sup>fi</sup>cient but potentially inconsistent OLS estimator and the proposed consistent IV estimator for the independent variable that is possibly endogenous.

To support the Hausman test, we evaluated what instrumental variable for PRICE was appropriate to perform a two-stage least squares (2SLS) regression.<sup>4</sup> In the air travel industry context, an appropriate instrumental variable for price is stage length, which represents the distance in air miles between the OD city-pair. It summarizes information about an airline's costs of operation on the route that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence a revenue manager's choices about how to set the price.<sup>5</sup> We could not reject the null hypothesis though, which suggests the absence of endogeneity due to the PRICE variable, and that estimating the log-linear model with OLS regression is appropriate.<sup>6</sup>

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {QUANTITY} = e ^ {\beta_ {1}} \cdot P R I C E ^ {\eta + \lambda C H A N N E L} \cdot A D V P U R C H ^ {\beta_ {2}} \cdot S E G M E N T ^ {\beta_ {3}} \cdot Y E A R ^ {\beta_ {4}} \\ \quad \cdot \prod_ {i} O D _ {i} ^ {\delta_ {i}} \cdot \prod_ {j} B U N D L E _ {j} ^ {\theta_ {j}} \cdot e ^ {\varepsilon} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

$$

## 5.3.3. Price elasticity across channels by branded bundle

The airlines have designed lower-feature, lower-priced bundles for price-elastic leisure travelers. They target higher-feature, higherpriced bundles to more price-inelastic passengers. We expect price elasticity to decrease as bundle features increase. We also expect to see lower price elasticity in the à la carte channel for each branded bundle.

To estimate price elasticity differences across bundles in the distribution channels, we adjusted the model speci<sup>fi</sup>cation across the selling mechanisms as in Eqs. (3) and (4). We included additional dummies for the interaction effects between distribution channels and branded bundles. In this model, η is the price elasticity of the Deeply-Discounted bundle sold in the traditional channel, and it is the base elasticity. Also $\lambda _ { i } ( \mathrm { w i t h } i = 1 \mathrm { t o } 7 )$ is the price elasticity differential of the Deeply-Discounted bundle with respect to other bundle types in the distribution channels. See Table 8.

The estimation of price elasticity of the Deeply-Discounted bundle in the traditional channel was negative and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( \eta = - 0 . 5 4 ,$ $\mathtt { R S E } = 0 . 0 4 , p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ . The estimates of the price elasticity differences for other bundles in both channels were positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Consumers showed the lowest price elasticity when they purchased Deeply-Discounted bundles in the à la carte channel. One explanation for this counter-intuitive result is that the airline created some constraints on the booking of the Deeply-Discounted bundle in some of months that our data cover. The bundle was not available in the traditional channel during a portion of that time. So travelers who preferred the bundle could only purchase it through the à la carte channel for a portion of the period of our study, resulting in lessthan-perfect measurement and estimation.

Demand for the Premium bundle also was found to be less priceelastic $( \eta + \lambda _ { 7 } = - 0 . 5 4 + 0 . 0 9 = - 0 . 4 5 )$ in the à la carte channel than in the traditional channel $( \eta + \lambda _ { 7 } = - 0 . 5 4 + 0 . 0 7 = - 0 . 4 7 )$ However, it was higher for the Discounted and Flexible bundles. We interpret this result further in the discussion section.

## 5.3.4. Price elasticity across channels by segment and bundle

We drilled down further with our demand estimation by examining the interaction of bundle type and market segment. Again, we modi<sup>fi</sup>ed the baseline model. The Deeply-Discounted bundle purchased by business travelers in the traditional channel was the base case in the analysis, which is represented by η in the estimation results. The parameter λ (with i = 1 to 15) is the price elasticity differential of the base case with respect to the interactions between bundle type and market segment in the two distribution channels. See Table 9.

The pattern of changes in price elasticity of bundles across channels between the traveler types persists. The price elasticity of Deeply-Discounted bundle in the à la carte channel was lowest, and consistently lower for the Premium bundle in the à la carte channel in both market segments. Also in both segments, the Discounted and Flexible bundles had a higher price elasticity in the à la carte channel.

In sum, travelers showed lower overall price elasticity of demand in the à la carte channel. The differences of price elasticity across distribution channels varied depending on bundle types. Consumers who purchased Deeply-Discounted bundles in the à la carte channel were much less price-elastic than those who bought the same bundle in the traditional channel. Travelers were also less price-sensitive for Premium bundles in the à la carte channel. Demand is more priceelastic in the Discounted and Flexible bundles. These patterns were also present for business and leisure travelers.

## 6. Discussion

Although price elasticity of demand in air travel has been studied, the issues that surround the new approaches to pricing and bundling amid high levels of distribution channel con<sup>fl</sup>ict between suppliers and intermediaries are truly new. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, business travelers have higher price elasticity of demand in the à la carte channel, while leisure travelers exhibited lower price elasticity of demand. This contradicts our argument that à la carte pricing will make both types of travelers less price-elastic. Yet it is somewhat consistent with Granados et al. [26], who found that the business segment was more price-elastic in a more transparent environment.

Comparing the price elasticities of different bundles across channels shows that only the Premium and Deeply-Discounted bundles were less price-elastic in the à la carte channel. See Fig. 4b. This contradicts our argument that each bundle will be less price-elastic in the à la carte channel, and that bundles with more features will have less price elasticity. Our cross-channel analysis for bundles suggests the presence of similar patterns for the business and leisure segments. See Fig. 4c and 4d. For both segments, only the Premium and Deeply-Discounted bundles were less price-elastic in the à la carte channel.

Although these <sup>fi</sup>ndings don't match all our theoretical arguments, they are consistent with Granados et al. [26]. Based on the estimation of bookings across channels, bundles, and segments, they found that business travelers and frequent <sup>fl</sup>yers tend to book more in the à la carte channel. They may be attracted to the transparent and <sup>fl</sup>exible features of the à la carte channel. We <sup>fi</sup>nd a similar pattern. The high-end segment of the market, both business and leisure travelers who purchase a Premium bundle, are less price-elastic. That is not the case, however, for the lower end of the market, that is, travelers who purchase Flexible and Discounted bundles. The implication for the design of à la carte pricing is that airlines should focus on branded bundles with higher features. This demand is more likely to be able to be decommoditized. The lower-end segment bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from higher product transparency, but tends to focus more on price.

In summary, à la carte pricing seems to be helping the airline in our study to win back the ownership of its customers, but it has failed to address the problem in the manner we expected at the outset. Perhaps more consideration needs to be given to the design of the bundles and customization options. In general, à la carte pricing mechanism design should address: (1) the design of branded bundles and their variants; and (2) the design of services that can be added or dropped by the consumer. Both cover content and price design issues. Consumers evaluate their purchases based on acquisition utility, the perceived value of the product or service, and transaction utility, the difference between the consumer's reference price and the price that must be paid to the seller for the product or service [37]. They will impact the consumer's evaluation and purchase behavior. This logic applies when the consumer can add or drop features from a bundle. Unless the perceived value of the product is appropriate, consumers will not choose to purchase it. Also, if they <sup>fi</sup>nd a bundle feature that is not worthwhile, they will drop it.

The higher level of information transparency empowers informed consumers in the à la carte pricing channel. Most consumers expect to see product information before committing to make purchases. So the rich information about the full range of available offers and high <sup>fl</sup>exibility of modifying the standard <sup>fi</sup>xed-feature, <sup>fi</sup>xed-price bundles in à la carte pricing should help them to ascertain their needs better. A consumer may be willing to pay a price premium, and be attracted to the à la carte pricing channel as a consequence. But she may also be deterred if the right customization options are not offered, or if they are offered at a higher price than her valuation. The challenge for the airlines is to design à la carte pricing in a way that it offers the right features and customization options at the right price.

With effective design of an à la carte pricing mechanism, the higher levels of information transparency that are created will diminish discounts in willingness-to-pay due to uncertainty or lack of <sup>fi</sup>t. If the mechanism is not implemented well though, then higher levels of information transparency could make things worse. Why? Because consumers may focus on cheaper alternatives when branded bundles with unattractive features or customization options are offered. In fact, some consumers, it turns out, are already utilizing à la carte pricing as a way of trading down, by leveraging Internet search to make purchases based on the least expensive alternatives they can <sup>fi</sup>nd. Quinby and Offutt [34] pointed out that six of ten U.S. air travelers indicated that <sup>fi</sup>nding better prices was their main motivation for choosing a particular Web site when they book travel online. Consumers trade down because they seek to minimize regret from choosing alternatives that may be of uncertain value; so trading down essentially is satis<sup>fi</sup>cing behavior. They will bene<sup>fi</sup>t from information transparency and <sup>fl</sup>exible choices.

Our theory and empirical evidence suggest that à la carte pricing makes demand less price-elastic, although the results varied by market segment and branded bundle. Higher sales of à la carte ticket bundles allow the airlines to save commission fees and to build more intimate customer relationships, but the airlines have to carefully design à la carte pricing mechanisms to create greater consumer willingness-to-pay.

## 7. Conclusion

Although Internet technologies were predicted to enable the airlines to connect to consumers more directly and lead to disintermediation, the GDSs and GDS-based OTAs still process a large number of bookings. Their systems services have eroded the airline's revenues by encouraging consumers to do price comparison-based shopping, which has caused the commoditization of air travel services. In response, the airlines have begun to adopt à la carte pricing for direct distribution to decommoditize their services. We performed an empirical assessment of whether à la carte pricing has a bene<sup>fi</sup>cial effect on the decommoditization of air travel services. To do this, we analyzed and compared price elasticity of demand across channels, market segments, branded bundles, and the multiple interactions between them.

Demand tends to be less price-elastic in the à la carte channel, based on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our data. This matches the expectations of senior managers at the airline <sup>fi</sup>rm whose customers we studied. However, the results were not consistent across some of the bundles and market segments. In fact, in some cases, price elasticity was lower in the à la carte channel.

The above <sup>fi</sup>ndings have one fundamental implication for airline managers and other industries where à la carte pricing is being adopted. Even though we only have evidence of the differences in price elasticity across channels on an aggregate level, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings that price elasticity is not consistently lower in the à la carte channels suggests that airlines should pay close attention to the Web-based design approach they use for à la carte selling. The challenge of implementing an à la carte pricing mechanism is how to design the content and prices of the bundles, and what features should be added or dropped. Whether the higher level of information transparency of à la carte pricing will be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial depends on its design. Poor design choices, as we discussed, may make the airline worse off. Airlines may be able to analyze microdata on consumer behavior to improve their designs, and to perform panel surveys and <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments with frequent <sup>fl</sup>yers to further re<sup>fi</sup>ne the branded bundles offered and the customization options.

This study makes several contributions. First, it is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to offer a full-scale, model-based empirical analysis of price elasticity of demand in the à la carte and traditional distribution channels. Although the airlines have utilized à la carte pricing since 2005, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study that examines its impact on price elasticity empirically. Second, this research investigates the impact of à la carte pricing on air travel services demand through the estimation of price elasticity across many OD city-pairs. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may be helpful for other industries that have channel con<sup>fl</sup>icts between suppliers and intermediaries. Such problems occur in the hospitality, online video entertainment, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial services industries.

We note several limitations. First, this research used a very large data set only for one airline to estimate the results, so generalizability is limited. Data for several airlines are preferable, and would make comparisons and consistency checks across them possible. Second, the industry setting is a quasi-monopoly market. So price elasticity of demand may not be the same as other markets. Third, the airline's restriction on the sales of speci<sup>fi</sup>c bundles in the traditional channel during some months of our study also may bias our estimation results and the comparisons that we have made across channels for this bundle.

## Appendix B. Bootstrap analysis

Two important problems in statistical estimation are the determination of an estimator for a parameter of interest and evaluation of the accuracy of that estimator through estimates of its standard errors and related con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals for the parameter [8]. Measuring accuracy and constructing con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals require knowledge of the estimators' sampling distributions. This knowledge is often unavailable though. The assumptions for the distribution of the error term may be false, and calculating the characteristics of a sampling distribution may be hard also.

Bootstrapping analysis addresses this issue. The bootstrap resamples data from the original data set. It is a data-based simulation method for statistical inference, which evaluates the accuracy of the statistical estimators [18]. The formal de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the bootstrap follows [15]. We let $X { = } ( X _ { 1 } , \ X _ { 2 } , \ . . . , \ X _ { \mathrm { { n } } } )$ be a vector of n independent and identically-distributed (i.i.d.) random variables, and ${ \boldsymbol { x } } = ( x _ { 1 } , \ x _ { 2 } , . . . , \ x _ { \mathrm { { n } } } )$ be their observed realizations. The effects of x denoted by <sup>^</sup>θ are used to make inferences about the characteristics of the parameter of interest θ. A procedure to assess the accuracy of <sup>^</sup>θ is de<sup>fi</sup>ned in terms of the empirical distribution function $F _ { n } .$ The function $F _ { n }$ assigns probability mass 1/n to each observation $x _ { \mathrm { i } } .$ The empirical distribution function is the maximum likelihood estimator of the distribution for the observations when no parametric assumptions are made. The bootstrap distribution for <sup>^</sup>θ - θ is obtained by generating ${ \hat { \theta } } ^ { \prime } s$ through independent sampling $F _ { n }$ with replacement. The bootstrap estimate of the standard error of <sup>^</sup>θ is the standard deviation of the bootstrap distribution for <sup>^</sup>θ −θ. Practical application involves these steps: (1) Generate a bootstrap, as a sample with replacement from the empirical distribution. (2) Compute <sup>^</sup>θ obtained through using the bootstrap sample in place of the original sample. (3) Repeat Steps 1 and 2 k times, as appropriate.

An assumption of the bootstrap is i.i.d. errors. Our model was diagnosed to have heteroskedastic errors. The wild bootstrap and the pairs bootstrap have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed to deal with heteroskedastic errors [30]. The idea is to estimate the variances from individual residuals. The bootstrap error terms are a transformation of the ordinary least squares (OLS) restricted residuals multiplied by a random variable with mean 0 and variance 1. The pairs bootstrap [21] involves resampling the data instead of the residuals. It resamples the regressand and regressor together from the matrix [y, X]. Each observation of the bootstrap sample is $[ y ^ { * } , X ^ { * } ]$ , a randomly chosen draw from [y, X]. This method's dependent and the independent variables are always selected in pairs.

Although the pairs bootstrap is readily implemented, it has two de-<sup>fi</sup>ciencies [30]. First, it doesn't impose any restrictions on $\beta ,$ the estimator of independent variables. If we want to test such restrictions, as opposed to estimating standard errors or forming con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals, we need to modify the related test statistic so it is testing something that is true in the bootstrap data-generating process. Second, compared to the wild bootstrap, the pairs bootstrap generally does not yield very accurate results, because it is not conditioned on the X matrix. The wild bootstrap performs better than the pairs bootstrap when heteroskedasticity is present [20,28], and so this was our choice.

The wild bootstrap data-generating process is based on ${ \boldsymbol { y } } _ { t } ^ { * } { = } { \boldsymbol { X } } _ { t }$ $\hat { \beta } + f \left( \hat { u } _ { t } \right) \nu _ { t } ^ { * } ,$ where $\hat { \beta }$ is a vector of parameter estimates, $f \left( \hat { u } _ { t } \right)$ is a transformation of the tth residual $\hat { u } _ { t }$ , and $\nu _ { t } ^ { * }$ is a random variable with mean 0 and variance 1. There are two ways to specify the distribution of the $\nu _ { t } ^ { * }$ depending on the skewness of the error term [14]. The simplest one is to use Rademacher's distribution: $\nu _ { t } ^ { * } = 1$ with probability 0.5; and $\boldsymbol { v } _ { t } ^ { * } = - 1$ with probability 0.5.

The wild bootstrap usually performs better than other distributions when the distribution of the error term is approximately symmetric [14].
