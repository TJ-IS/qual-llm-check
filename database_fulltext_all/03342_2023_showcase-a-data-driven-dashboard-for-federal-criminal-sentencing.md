## Abstract

The main purpose of the Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 was to provide more uniformity in sentencing and reduce interjudge disparity. Subsequently, the act created the federal sentencing guidelines to offer judges a possible sentencing range for offenses. However, since these recommendations were based on historical data, the guidelines amplified existing biases and increased inequality and the disproportionate sentencing of minorities. To address this problem, we developed an artifact called “ShowCase”—a data-driven dashboard—that is grounded in penal theory, organizational context theory, social bonds theory, and triangulation notion in design theory. The artifact helps judges make fairer and more objective decisions by integrating a variety of data points. We used a design science research methodology and mixed methods to guide the development and evaluation of the proposed dashboard. Our research inquiry revealed the legal and extralegal factors that contribute to more equitable judicial decisions. We also found support for integrating data science and more diverse viewpoints in the sentencing process. Our study shows that a validated data-driven dashboard can be used to promote fairness, objectivity, and transparency in the criminal justice system.

Keywords: Data Science, Criminal Justice, Federal Sentencing, Fairness, Transparency, Decision Support Systems, Triangulation, Design Science Research

Roger Chiang, Ahmed Abbasi, and Jennifer Xu were the accepting senior editors. This research article was submitted on April 13, 2021, and went through two revisions. This paper is part of the Special Issue on Data Science for Social Good.

## 1 Introduction

The US criminal justice system is complex and difficult to navigate. There have been numerous debates and efforts to improve it, culminating in the Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 to ensure fair and consistent sentencing (Wilkins et al., 1991). Consequently, the US Sentencing Commission was established to reduce disparities and promote transparency and proportionality in sentencing. The Commission reviewed over 40,000 federal convictions and used a subsample of about 10,000 presentencing reports to develop the original federal sentencing guidelines (see Appendix A for the most up-to-date version). While this led to more uniformity and reduced interjudge disparity (Breyer, 1988; Hofer et al., 1999), there were also unintended consequences, such as mandatory minimums, prison overcrowding, and racial profiling (Forer, 1994; Mooney et al., 2018). As a result, the guidelines were recently downgraded from mandatory to advisory, and when a single judge is presiding over a case, they have vast discretion over the sentencing decision. One of the biggest challenges is how to leverage the current guidelines and ensure that the system treats everyone fairly and objectively.

Data-driven decision support systems (DSS), which rely primarily on data science to make sentencing recommendations, have been introduced as a solution to that problem, but this approach has also sparked a debate with diverging conclusions. One of the biggest concerns is how to train DSS to make ethical and fair decisions without reinforcing existing biases in the dataset (Osoba & Welser IV, 2017) or amplifying inherent biases in future decisions (Wiens et al., 2019), which would further erode the lack of trust in the outcomes (Linegang et al., 2006; Stubbs et al., 2007). We argue that while data science has a place in the criminal justice system, there is also a greater need to demonstrate how it can be used to promote fairness, objectivity, and transparency in order to increase citizens’ trust in the outcomes. In the current study, we demonstrate that making data more accessible to judges is a fundamental part of this process.

We developed an artifact called “ShowCase,” aimed at offering judges a holistic view of the criminal case during the sentencing process. We used a design science research (DSR) approach (Hevner & Chatterjee, 2010; Hevner et al., 2004) to lay the foundations of our work. Our goal was to identify key factors relevant to sentencing decisions and determine how to best represent them on the “ShowCase” interface. We reviewed prior studies and conducted initial quantitative and qualitative inquiries to solidify the need to develop the artifact and identify its pertinent features. “ShowCase” is grounded in several kernel theories—penal theory (McFatter, 1982), organizational context theory (Dixon, 1995), and social bonds theory (Hirschi, 1969). We applied design concepts such as a 2×2 matrix, triangulation (Wijnhoven & Brinkhuis, 2015), and human-computer interaction (HCI) principles (Dix, 2009) to develop the interface prototype. To evaluate the artifact, we employed legal experts who assessed its key components and their pertinence to the sentencing process. We found that “ShowCase” addresses both the problems of data availability and data comprehensiveness (Hadley et al., 1994), allowing sentencing decisions to be more objective and fair. Our data-driven approach promotes transparency in the criminal justice system, it is centered on the notion of equity and balances objectivity and fairness.

In the rest of the paper, we discuss prior literature on federal sentencing guidelines and their challenges, followed by our delineation of how data science alone cannot effectively resolve these issues, thus illuminating the need for a more comprehensive approach. We establish the theoretical foundation and discuss the quantitative and qualitative inquiries and results that furthered our research investigations and provided us with instructive guidelines for the artifact. We then describe our design approach, followed by the presentation of the “ShowCase” interface prototype and our evaluation strategy. Finally, we discuss the limitations and implications of our work.

## 2 Background

## 2.1 Federal Criminal Sentencing Issues

Despite their flaws, since their inception in 1984, the federal sentencing guidelines have been a major force in criminal sentencing. Undoubtedly, they have made a positive impact, specifically in providing more uniformity in sentencing among similar offenders (Anderson et al., 1999; Kahan & Posner, 1999). Even though the reform had some success in reducing interjudge disparity (Hofer et al., 1999), its success has been limited (Anderson & Spohn, 2010). Many researchers have demonstrated the negative effects of biases related to demographic characteristics (Rehavi & Starr, 2014; Steffensmeier et al., 2017), guilty pleas (Albonetti, 1997), rehabilitated drug offenders (Shapiro, 1991), and the lack of institutional balance in federal sentencing (Bowman III, 2005). As a result of these issues, in 2005, the guidelines were marginalized from mandatory to advisory, giving judges even more discretion (Byrne & Turner, 2010). However, studies have shown that judges are susceptible to biases (Kang et al., 2011) and extraneous factors such as the order in which cases are presented or whether they are exhausted or hungry (Glöckner, 2016). This is especially problematic in district courts, where a single judge is presiding over a case and serves as the ultimate decision maker. According to the Administrative Office of the United States Courts (2020), the majority of federal criminal lawsuits start and end in the district courts, which highlights the need to further study the inputs used for sentencing decisions to ensure fair and transparent trials for all defendants.

When a single judge is presiding over a case, the judge’s personal interpretations of the law, attitudes, opinions, and experiences are the sole basis for determining the punishment. The judge must consider the likelihood and the seriousness of the offense, and their sentence should minimize potential harm in the future (Berk & Hyatt, 2015). Such forecasts must be highly accurate to achieve a balance between the severity of the crime and the punishment. For a long time, subjective judgment, based on intuition and guided by experience, was the main driver for sentencing decisions. Inadvertently, this led to some issues such as the disproportionate imprisonment of minorities and those of lower socioeconomic status (Pettit & Western, 2004). A potential solution to mitigate the problems associated with having a single decision maker would be to devise a panel of judges, similar to the appellate courts or the Supreme Court structure. However, since the current judicial workload is overburdened (Habel & Scott, 2014), having multiple judges presiding over a case at the district court level is unfeasible. A better solution, we propose, would be to supply judges with pertinent data about the case at hand in order to facilitate more effective decision-making.

## 2.2 Data-Driven Criminal Sentencing

Recent studies have shown that a data-driven, or evidence-based, approach can be beneficial to judges during sentencing (Divine, 2017; Hyatt et al., 2011), but it may also introduce new problems such as causing judges to ignore the influence of racial and economic disparities in imprisonment (Monahan & Skeem, 2016). We argue that a data-driven approach can be utilized in the criminal justice system but it needs to be carefully examined to reduce potential errors. The growing impact of “machine justice” (Peeters & Schuilenburg, 2018) also brings up numerous concerns about civil rights and liberties, such as relying on data from jurisdictions with an extensive history of unlawful police practices (Ferguson, 2016). Thus, any kind of system used for predicting criminal activity should integrate input from the public to ensure transparency and legitimacy. This is consistent with recommendations from Peeters and Schuilenburg (2018), who argue for empowering citizens and integrating their opinions in the decision-making process. We acknowledge such concerns and used them for guidance when developing our proposed solution: a data-driven interface to promote transparency in the criminal justice system. We emphasize that while our approach can be useful, its role is to complement judges’ work, not solely drive decisions.

Using findings from prior literature to aid our design thinking, we continued our research inquiries by exploring potential factors that can be used to promote transparency and fairness in the courtroom. According to von Hirsch (1983), the primary purpose of sentencing is to predict future criminality. However, the reality of ever-increasing caseloads (Habel & Scott, 2014) makes it difficult for judges to allocate enough time to thoroughly explore each case. We argue that utilizing a data-driven approach can have a positive impact on the decision-making process by integrating multiple perspectives and data points. To successfully address the problems identified in the studies discussed above, we need to better understand the role of technology and what features it must possess to be effective. Therefore, the research question guiding this study is: What are the pertinent components of a datadriven artifact that promotes transparency, fairness, and objectivity in federal criminal sentencing? In the next section, we delineate the design process needed to build such an artifact based on the research question.

## 3 Design Process

## 3.1 Design Science Research

We created our artifact “ShowCase” through the use of DSR, as proposed by Hevner and Chatterjee (2010) and Hevner et al. (2004). Our design process is twofold: first, we developed an analytical plan (Figure 1) to operationalize DSR for the current study by perusing the literature and coming up with an initial design. Then, guided by inductive reasoning, we conducted quantitative and qualitative inquiries to gather empirical data on the need for the artifact and the potential features to be included. We conducted several rounds of artifact iterations, following the rigor, relevance, and design cycles in DSR (Hevner, 2007). Once we satisfactorily included all feedback in our design, we asked attorneys, prosecutors, legal experts, and criminologists to evaluate “ShowCase,” following the methodology outlined by Venable et al. (2016). Figure 1 depicts an overview of our design process.

## 3.2 Kernel Theories

The theoretical foundations of “ShowCase” stem from several criminological theories—namely, penal theory and organizational context theory, emerging from our literature review, and social bonds theory, which we identified in the empirical data gathering. We leveraged these theories to establish the components relevant to our artifact.

Since the problem we are addressing is situated in the criminal justice domain, we used penal theory and organizational context theory to inform our artifact. Determining exactly how much punishment an offender deserves is impossible (Tonry, 2007). However, an accurate representation of the legal and extralegal factors can offer judges the necessary guidance to craft sentences that meet the ideological goals of sentencing while still delivering sanctions that most appropriately reduce the risk of reoffending. Modern penal theory (McFatter, 1982) defines these ideological goals as follows: retribution, rehabilitation, deterrence, and incapacitation. We apply the theory by integrating a variety of viewpoints to balance these different perspectives. For example, (1) the public may be more lenient and may seek rehabilitation for a low-level offense, (2) the presiding judge may have a deterrence approach and a history of harsher punishments in the past, and (3) the guidelines may suggest a retributive sentence guided by data points relative to the crime and excluding any extralegal factors such as family ties or employment history. Our interface prototype captures these different approaches and highlights the potential imbalance if only one of these inputs were to serve as the basis of a criminal sentence.

We supplemented penal theory with organizational context theory to further inform our design. Organizational context theory emphasizes the need for more diversity in the criminal justice system and explains variances in sentencing (Dixon, 1995). Improving the criminal justice system hinges on having a diverse racial composition of the judicial workforce and the sentencing policies of courts.

Currently, judges are predominantly White males (Feagin & Ducey, 2017), though a well-balanced and diverse criminal justice workforce is more likely to adhere to the guidelines and make consistent decisions (Farrell & Ward, 2011).

While restructuring the judicial force is unequivocally necessary, it requires long-term planning, education, and training efforts, which are out of the scope of our study. Utilizing organizational context theory, our artifact includes insights and decisions from a wide pool of peer judges from diverse backgrounds and jurisdictions. This approach can help the presiding judge recognize potential unconscious biases and make a more informed decision. If judges have access to a database of their peers’ rulings on similar cases, they can more easily determine an optimal sentence that is aligned with society’s notion of justice (Baum & Kamas, 1995). Therefore, in our development of “ShowCase,” we focused on diversifying the organizational context of the presiding judges without increasing their workload.

In addition to the two theories that emerged from the literature, our empirical findings pointed to the need to incorporate social bonds theory into our artifact. According to Hirschi (1969), socialization and social learning lead to greater self-control, reducing one’s inclination to partake in antisocial behavior and commit crime. Various aspects of socialization and social support such as family ties, friendship networks, societies, cities, and neighborhoods can significantly decrease one’s likelihood of engaging in deviant activities (Makarios & Sams, 2013). Participants in our study mentioned the importance of these social bonds and their impact during sentencing. This argument is supported by prior literature and explains reduced recidivism rates among offenders who are released on probation after being incarcerated (Hepburn & Griffin, 2004; Smith, 2017). However, “social bonds” is a broad term, and the specific details may vary across different crimes. To operationalize this theory for our artifact, it was necessary to conduct further empirical data gathering and investigate the unique social support components relevant to each crime.

While penal theory, organizational context theory, and social bonds theory are not new to the field of criminal justice, their intersection in a single study has not been previously examined. Our work addresses this research gap and shows how these theories can be integrated in the context of information systems (IS). The artifact we produced is a direct result of extending current knowledge to a new domain and leveraging advances in DSS to promote fairness and transparency in criminal sentencing. To develop our initial interface prototype, we elicited specific requirements through quantitative and qualitative data-gathering procedures.

## 3.3 Requirements Gathering

## 3.3.1 Quantitative Procedures and Results

We began the requirements-gathering process by soliciting inputs from nonexperts to identify factors that may be of significance to the community. Typically, juries of peers are used to decide whether someone is guilty or not, and it is usually the judge’s responsibility to determine the appropriate sentence. Our goal was to extend this concept and provide additional feedback to judges. Community input in the form of victim impact statements is already part of the sentencing process (Long, 1995) but is not used consistently in the courtroom (Myers & Goddard, 2014). We address this issue by taking a crowdsourcing approach and presenting judges with public opinions without taking away their control over the final decisions.

To gather community input, we took a quantitative approach and created two fictitious scenarios, one about drug possession and the other about financial fraud, both based on the United States Code (Appendix B). Our goal was to identify extralegal factors that could influence the sentencing process. The scenarios were intentionally vague and lacked specificity so that participants could independently identify the characteristics relevant to each crime. The survey instruments underwent several rounds of pilot testing and editing. For the actual surveys, we recruited participants via Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (Mturk) platform and compensated them with a payment of \$10/hour, on average, for each completed survey. We followed survey procedures established by Young and Young (2019). To ensure quality, we measured survey attentiveness via multiple control questions following the best practices suggested by Berinsky et al. (2014). On average, participants took six minutes to complete the first survey (Min = 1, Max = 119, SD = 708.55) and six minutes to complete the second one (Min = 1, Max $= 3 7 , S D = 4 . 8 9 \rangle$ . We removed any incomplete surveys, responses completed in less than a minute, and any obvious outliers in the dataset based on our pilot test results. This narrowed our sample from 743 to 612 participants (308 in the first survey and 304 in the second). No participant was allowed to complete both surveys. About 30% of participants were minorities, and although Hispanics/Latinos comprised only 3.2% of participants, this sample provided interesting insights into the general public’s thoughts on fair and objective sentencing. A detailed breakdown of the participants’ demographic information is available in Appendix C. Following is an explanation of how we gathered the extralegal factors for our interface.

After showing the participants the two general crime scenarios, we asked them if they would need any additional information to issue sentences. We showed them a list of various characteristics about the offender and the victim from prior literature (Ulmer et al., 2016; van Wingerden et al., 2016) and asked them to identify how relevant each one was using a 5-point Likert scale, with 1 being least relevant and 5 being most relevant. Any items that pass the threshold mean of 2.5 were visualized on the interface, with the top five factors displayed more prominently. For example, in the drugrelated scenario, the offender’s age, drug history, motivation for committing the crime, showing remorse, and being a primary caregiver were the main offender characteristics our participants identified. Regarding the victim characteristics, only age and prior drug history were considered relevant to the crime. For the financial fraud scenario, only the offender’s motivation and remorse were identified as relevant factors for sentencing (specific results for each factor are available in Appendix D). We used the results from this quantitative method to empirically validate our literature review findings and to inform the design of our artifact.

## 3.3.2 Qualitative Procedures and Results

After gathering the initial input for the artifact from the literature and the quantitative surveys, our next step was to collect more information from legal, technical, and HCI professionals. The purpose was to understand whether the interface prototype contained relevant data points that a judge could use to make a decision and to solicit additional features before we finalized our artifact for evaluation. We used semi-structured interviews and asked five experts for their input on the features and design elements of “ShowCase.” Following is a summary of the participants’ feedback.

When first presented with the idea of a data-driven approach to sentencing, many of the interview participants were hesitant due to concerns about ethics and allowing technology to make decisions. However, all eventually agreed that an ideal decision would include a panel of judges with some input from technology. Their rationale was that “when working together, judges have to carefully think and craft their arguments to convince their colleagues on the panel until a unanimous decision can be made.” This approach is currently practiced only in appellate courts and the US Supreme Court, leaving most judges solely responsible for sentencing decisions. In this context, the participants agreed that “ShowCase” can offer a glimpse of others’ opinions without necessarily imposing them.

Another interesting point that one of the participants made was differentiating between objective and fair sentencing. The participant described “objective” as “consistent with the law or applying the law in the same way, regardless of individual circumstances” and “fair” as “based on the individual needs of the offender.” We found this to be an intriguing distinction, and the participant stated that it was among the most significant challenges of the criminal justice system. When it came to the specific characteristics of the offender, most of the experts highlighted the need to consider existing social bonds and ties to the community. Some additional factors they proposed were marital status, education, employment history or employability, gang activity or affiliation, and domestic violence. These observations are consistent with social bonds theory (Hirschi, 1969). While some of these factors may lead to bias it was important to include them in the interface to highlight potential disparities. Furthermore, adding information on programs and support during incarceration is key to reducing recidivism and reoffending (Tripodi, 2010). These factors provide more specificity to the prototype and ensure that justice is served and that individual needs are considered.

Transparency was another key theme that came up in the interviews. When we presented the interface prototype to the participants seeking feedback, most of them wanted to know where the data points came from and how credible the sources were. They emphasized the Importance of transparency when implementing any new tools and technologies in the courtroom due to the high stakes involved. These concerns confirmed our literature review findings, and the participants were pleased when we showed them how additional information about each value can be easily obtained with a single click.

Initially, we developed “ShowCase” with the goal of assisting judges during sentencing. However, several participants suggested that we expand this notion and open it to defense attorneys and prosecutors as well. Over 90% of federal criminal cases result in conviction due to guilty pleas (Subramanian et al., 2020). Thus, our participants explained that if all involved parties had access to the same data as the judge, they would be able to make more objective and fair plea bargains. Access to this data would be very helpful, especially to minorities and individuals of low socioeconomic status, who are often marginalized and treated unfairly by the criminal justice system (Albonetti, 1997). This recommendation supports the notion of transparency that “ShowCase” aims to provide. Overall, the participants were satisfied with what we showed them and expressed their support for pilot testing of the system in the future.

After we analyzed the results from our quantitative and qualitative data, we made some changes to “ShowCase” by including some new data points and clarifying some visual components. Such iterations are common practice in the DSR methodology and show the importance of finding balance between the artifact and theory (Baskerville et al., 2018). More specifically, our improved interface included replacing a more general risk of recidivism with an individualized score based on the Pennsylvania Sentence Risk Assessment Instrument (Monahan & Skeem, 2016). We also added any individual needs of offenders to ensure they receive proper support while serving their sentence and that they will not pose a threat to society once released from prison. This is consistent with the risk-need-responsivity model for guiding offender assessment and treatment and indicates the importance of reducing recidivism through proper program support (Basanta et al., 2018). Another thing we changed on the interface was replacing the term “crowdsourcing” with “public opinion.” Finally, the participants identified additional factors that judges often consider, such as employability, education, and gang affiliation. We included these factors in the design of the dashboard and also reinforced the concept of transparency by adding more details on the methods of data-point collection.

## 4 Artifact

## 4.1 Artifact Structure

We integrated our theoretical foundations and empirical findings to guide the development and structure of “ShowCase.” In addition to encompassing the kernel theories, the design and the subsequent artifact must satisfy the overarching principle of transparency, which is fundamental to objective and fair sentencing. We instituted a 2×2 matrix (Table 1), loosely inspired by the Eisenhower matrix (Covey, 1989), to create the structure of our dashboard and to divide the presented information into four quadrants. We followed recommendations from Anders (2016) and chose to compartmentalize the information presented to judges to avoid possible cognitive overload.

Following is an overview of the four quadrants identified above. The first theme in the matrix is general versus individual, depicted as rows in the “ShowCase” structure. The general aspect ties into the objectivity of the law, according to which everyone should be treated equally. The individual aspect concerns fairness and consideration of the individuality of the case and the offender. The second theme is triangulation vs. specification, which is presented as columns in the structure. While triangulation is concerned with balancing a variety of data points, specification focuses only on the information relevant to the defendant.

Triangulation is integral for mitigating existing biases within the criminal justice system without increasing judges’ caseloads. It is considered to be a systematic way of combining multiple viewpoints to arrive at the ultimate “truth” and confirming it by comparing various data points from multiple sources and perspectives (Denzin, 2009). This approach has been successfully applied to reduce bias when processing diverging sources of information (Metzger, 2007; Wijnhoven & Brinkhuis, 2015). Triangulation is fundamental for our artifact and we incorporated its principles to offer judges a variety of viewpoints before they make the final sentencing decision. More specifically, we took a Leibnizian approach (Wijnhoven & Brinkhuis, 2015), applying its three main postulations: (1) to present comparable data points that aid in decision-making, (2) to present the comprehensiveness in the relevant variables for criminal sentencing, and (3) to highlight the potential causal relationships between different variables. Via this school of thought, we established what information is relevant and how to present it meaningfully to judges so they can make the most objective and fair decisions.

## 4.2 Artifact Description and Use Case

We created the “ShowCase” artifact based on the structure above by cross-pollinating between the themes and creating quadrants. Following is an overview of each quadrant along with the appropriate data it contains. Figure 2 displays a use case of the artifact.

Quadrant 1, “Ranges to Consider,” is where triangulation and generality meet. From a design standpoint, this is the most important part of the interface (Dix, 2009); therefore, it was placed at the top left of the interface. In this quadrant, we provide data points regarding the generality of the case and showcase different sentencing outcomes using triangulation. Тhe four circles in Quadrant 1 depict suggested ranges of sentencing from four different sources, ranging from the most important (the guidelines) to the least (public opinion). If a user needs additional information about the construction of these ranges, they can click on the “i” (information) button. The “i” button provides an extra layer of transparency, explaining how the information was collected and displayed. For example, a user can see recommendations based on the guidelines and the prior decisions they and their peers have made in similar instances. If a judge clicks on peer recommendations, they can narrow the results based on demographic characteristics or geographic location. The same concept can be applied to view public opinion scores. As case rulings sometimes differ from one jurisdiction to another (Vigorita, 2003), it may be helpful for the judge to view information from their own constituents only, especially in areas where judges are elected rather than appointed.

In Quadrant 2, “Related Cases,” we display a case reference tool, which allows users to scroll and peruse the similarities and differences between current and past related cases. Here, we combine generality (objectivity of sentencing) with specificity (providing one specific case at a time for inspection). This information is helpful to judges, who are obliged to make their rulings as consistent as reasonably possible with previous judicial decisions on the same topic (Waluchow, 2007). Allowing judges to quickly view relevant information from other cases can help them achieve a certain level of reasonable uniformity. This design element is also intended to reduce implicit bias because looking at different cases can help judges identify patterns and reflect on possible reasons for systematically deviating from the guidelines. Even if a judge is more traditional and does not trust other opinions, this data point can motivate them to compensate for the influence of any potential biases (Rachlinski et al., 2008). Such case information is currently available but it is difficult to sort and go through quickly. Thus, “ShowCase” optimizes the search process and allows the judge to examine and reflect on how decisions have been made in the past.

Quadrant 3, “Additional Factors to Consider about This Case,” integrates triangulation by offering a myriad of information about the individual aspects of the current case to ensure fairness. While the guidelines already take into consideration some mitigating and aggravating factors such as criminal history or use of a weapon, judges often rely on other extralegal factors that are not directly included in the guidelines. Each factor in Quadrant 3 is relevant to the crime and the offender and can dynamically change. Consistent with social bonds theory (Hirschi, 1969), our empirical findings point out that the following factors are relevant for the drug possession scenario: the age of the offender, their drug history, whether they are a repeat offender, whether they show remorse, and whether they are a caregiver.

Quadrant 4, “Defendant Risk Score” and “Defendant Needs,” is where specificity and the individual are integrated to display information that pertains to the defendant. The idea of a defendant risk score is currently used in various processes within the criminal justice system. For example, pretrial risk assessment is a score to determine whether someone should be released on bail or await trial behind bars (VanNostrand & Keebler, 2009). Here, the risk score represents the post-sentencing proclivity of the defendant. As a result, the defendant risk score is a viable choice: it depicts the probability of recidivism based on a score from 0 to 18. A score between 0-4 is low risk, 5-11 represents medium risk, and 12-18 is high risk. This idea has been signed into law and implemented in Pennsylvania since 2010 (Monahan & Skeem, 2016). Subsequent experiment analysis confirmed the utility of the risk score as a graph and as an additional piece of information that can be used during sentencing (Ruback et al., 2016). Actuarial risk information can be very beneficial when assessing future offender risk (Bonta, 2007), and we incorporate that as one of our data points in the interface.

Finally, the other purpose of Quadrant 4 is to present the various needs of the defendant. This information is designed to serve as a counterweight to the risk score and provides relevant post-sentencing information about each defendant. Information like rehab treatments, mental health counseling, vocational training, or other needs is presented here to help judges understand the defendant as a person and ensure that they receive proper support while incarcerated to reduce their risk of recidivism. This approach is consistent with the risk-need-responsivity model for guiding offender assessment and treatment and indicates the importance of reducing recidivism through proper program support (Basanta et al., 2018), which is why we included it in the final version of the dashboard.

In this fictitious scenario, a 21-year-old man named Mark Smith who has no prior criminal history is on trial for violating US 21§841—possession of 50 grams of methamphetamine. The defendant has a history of using drugs. He showed remorse during the trial by taking responsibility for his actions, and he is the sole caregiver for his elderly grandmother. No weapon was involved in the crime and there were no other aggravating circumstances such as gang affiliation or use of violence. Mark Smith never finished high school and is currently unemployed. The presiding judge, the Honorable Anna Reed, would be provided with the information displayed on the dashboard to help her make a sentencing decision in Mr. Smith’s case. ## 4.3 Artifact Evaluation

To evaluate “ShowCase,” we followed recommendations and processes by Venable et al. (2012) and Venable et al. (2016) because they offer relevant guidance on assessing DSR artifacts. More specifically, we (1) explicated the design goals, (2) chose an evaluation strategy, (3) determined the properties to evaluate, and (4) designed the individual evaluation episodes. Our first goal was to demonstrate the value of “ShowCase” to the intended users: legal professionals, defense attorneys, prosecutors, and criminal justice experts. To achieve this, we conducted semi-structured interviews, focusing exclusively on the artifact evaluation. The average length was 34 minutes (Min = 18 minutes, Max = 48 minutes), and we reached data saturation after 11 interviews. We recorded and transcribed the interviews using videoconferencing software such as Zoom and Microsoft Teams. After each interview, two researchers individually reviewed the transcripts, coded the data, and identified themes and patterns. Then, they compared notes and discussed their observations to create common concepts. For our evaluation strategy, we mimicked the human risk and effectiveness strategy (Venable et al., 2016) to assess whether our design could fulfill the original design purpose. Although this approach is not specifically concerned with measuring the transparency, fairness, and objectivity of sentencing decisions, it is appropriate for our study because it addresses the main characteristics of a DSR artifact. Such knowledge is relevant to answering our research question and identifying the pertinent components of a data-driven artifact for federal criminal sentencing.

The evaluation strategy we employed consists of five criteria proposed by Checkland (1999) and adapted to DSR by Venable et al. (2016): effectiveness, efficacy, efficiency, ethics, and elegance. Together, effectiveness and efficacy form the rigor of the artifact evaluation; whereas effectiveness addresses whether the instantiation would be able to fulfill its original design purpose, efficacy concerns the assessment of how the instantiation could improve the problem space. Efficiency refers to the efficient use of resources within the instantiation. Ethics is another crucial component of our instantiation, as it is important to be prudent in providing the appropriate data to lead to the most equitable decision. Finally, elegance concerns the aesthetics of the artifact. Following is a discussion of how each of the five elements is connected to our design, and how we used them to assess our artifact and answer the research question driving this study.

We combined effectiveness and efficacy, as they complement each other in terms of measuring whether “ShowCase” fulfilled its stated design purpose—to promote transparency, objectivity, and fairness in criminal sentencing. Most of the interview participants confirmed that the dashboard was thorough, rigorous, and accurate by highlighting its usefulness and practicality along several avenues. Below are some of the main takeaways from the qualitative evaluation on effectiveness and efficacy as they pertain to the objectives of the study.

First, from a design perspective, the participants were quite receptive to “ShowCase.” They described our interface as “easy to use” and “easy to navigate.” The components were characterized as “intuitive” and the presented information as “understandable” and “simple to operate.” The participants further indicated that our artifact could potentially lead to fairer and more objective decisions and, in that sense, would be aI improvement over the existing sentencing approach in the US.

Second, the participants appreciated the overall availability of the necessary data and the accompanying display of the source alongside the data. This evaluation jived with the original purpose of “ShowCase”: making data more accessible and transparent. They praised the comprehensiveness of the interface and its ability to give a high-level summary of the case.

Third, the participants thought that providing several ranges would broaden the viewpoints of what an appropriate sentence could be. This information can be found in Quadrant 1 of our dashboard. The participants also considered the ability to view similar cases in Quadrant 2 as a good reference point to better understand what an appropriate decision should be.

Finally, given that individualized information on prior cases also bolsters confidence in making a fair decision, one of the participants praised the variety of information in Quadrant 3 of the artifact, stating that while different judges would want different factors presented, factors like these are crucial for judges to consider during the sentencing process. Quadrant 4, where fairness and specificity meet, was subject to the fewest comments. Several participants appreciated that the offender’s needs (healthcare, education, employability, etc.) were highlighted prior to sentencing; however, some participants were concerned about the effect of the risk score. We explained that this score was modeled after the Pennsylvania Sentence Risk Assessment Instrument (Monahan & Skeem, 2016), and when “ShowCase” is adopted at the federal level a similar instrument would be devised to better reflect the specifics of federal offenses. After this clarification, the participants agreed that having such information displayed would be useful during sentencing.

To measure the efficiency of our interface, we asked participants whether it captured sufficient information pertinent to the case, allowing the judge to make a fair and objective decision. All participants agreed. Furthermore, they stated that the interface can save time and resources because, while most of this information is currently available to judges, it is often difficult and time-consuming to go through it—especially in rural areas, where much of the information may still be paperbased. In addition to the participants’ feedback, we also compared “ShowCase” to some existing IS used in courts today. Specifically, we looked into the Judicial Inquiry System used in Florida (Office of the State Courts Administrator, 2018) and the Automated Criminal/Infractions System in North Carolina (North Carolina Administrative Office of the Courts, 2017). We compared these systems to “ShowCase,” following criteria established by Palmius (2007). The participants stated that they believed that our artifact improved upon existing systems due to its better organization, consideration of individual needs and circumstances, integration of a variety of information, platform independence, and the application of best practices in user-centered design and systems development. During the interviews, participants needed no more than five minutes to orient themselves and understand the various data points without relying on a manual or additional support. We contend that efficiency can be achieved through simplicity and offering users improved interface and navigation features.

Since our literature review and empirical findings highlighted the need to ensure integrity when using a data-driven approach, our next evaluation component concerned the ethics of the presented interface. Initially, almost all participants expressed some hesitation about the ethics of utilizing a DSS in the legal system but were hopeful when we demonstrated the transparency of the design process. They found it novel and valuable that “ShowCase” featured multiple viewpoints to help judges make decisions. Participants agreed that the triangulation of viewpoints could offset some existing biases and could ultimately lead to better-informed judges. The legal experts we interviewed confirmed that age, drug history, prior offenses, remorse, and caregiver status (as indicated by the MTurk workers) were generally considered during sentencing, but stated that some of these factors were not viewed consistently and could have different weights depending on the judge’s discretion. The participants approved of the individual needs and risk factors and considered their inclusion to be much more equitable and fair to the defendants.

Finally, we measured the elegance of our design using the definition provided by Venable et al. (2016). While the focus of the study was to elicit relevant input for more objective and fair sentencing, we wanted to incorporate the notion of simplicity in our design.

Participants were pleased with the intuitiveness of the interface and how easy it was to find the relevant data points. “ShowCase” incorporates HCI best practices (Dix, 2009) and received positive feedback from the participants regarding the color scheme and presentation of relevant information. Following this evaluation of the artifact, we now reflect on our findings and draw some conclusions from the lessons learned.

## 5 Discussion

In this research, we combined theories from criminology, empirical data findings, and design principles to identify the pertinent components of a data-driven artifact that promotes fairness and equality in federal criminal sentencing. To answer our complex research question, we developed the “ShowCase” interface prototype, which demonstrated in practice how various legal and extralegal factors could be integrated into the sentencing process. Our artifact highlights the importance of transparency and balancing individual and general needs to ensure fair and objective sentencing.

“ShowCase” is an improvement over existing approaches, such as the 2021 Guidelines Manual<sup>1</sup> and the US Federal Sentencing Guidelines Calculator <sup>2</sup> since it captures a multitude of legal and extralegal factors for each case. We established the overall need for the development of such an artifact and demonstrated that it could serve as a tool that judges and other actors in the criminal justice system could use to make more informed sentencing decisions. Our work reveals how integrating kernel theories from criminology can positively influence the design process. Through our framing of the artifact structure via the 2×2 matrix, we concretely demonstrated where each theory was operationalized within the interface. This step is an original approach to artifact design within the DSR knowledge base. Consistent with Leidner’s (2020) definition of novelty, we incorporated three mature but new-to-IS theories (penal theory, organizational context theory, and social bonding theory) and applied them to the emerging phenomenon under investigation, namely promoting social justice through more fair and objective datadriven sentencing procedures.

In the context of the knowledge innovation matrix (KIM) created by Gregor and Hevner (2014), “ShowCase” and the current work fall under the improvement quadrant because we developed a new solution (artifact) for a known problem (lack of trust and transparency in the criminal justice system). We also explain how the notion of triangulation in design theory could be applied to new contexts. The current study demonstrates that the Leibnizian system of inquiry is a good candidate for providing a multitude of viewpoints, especially for a phenomenon that contains no universal “truth.” In our case, each data point not only represents a case on the dashboard but also a human life with a complicated history. Providing more pertinent information can lead to increasing equity in the decision-making process and an overall improvement in sentencing outcomes.

Our findings reveal that data-driven DSS are viewed rather negatively in the criminal justice system based on the argument that a decision that could dramatically impact a person’s life should not be left to computers and algorithms. While this principle is indisputable, incorporating a data-driven approach into the decisionmaking process nevertheless has its own utility in helping judges make better sentencing decisions. Judges are hesitant to relinquish control, and rightfully so. Since current law dictates the direct relationship between judges and the criminal cases over which they preside over, any deviation from that law is a violation in and of itself. In addition, implementing DSS, which have repeatedly demonstrated flaws and ambiguity, creates more friction than assistance. The way to combat such reluctance in adoption is to highlight the transparency of the data and how it is derived and used. Our research shows that transparency and triangulation are fundamental for any data-driven approach to the criminal justice system. Without the ability to peek “under the hood” and present data from various sources, any implementation would fail (Chiao, 2019). Such concerns were central to the development of “ShowCase,” and we leveraged existing knowledge and empirical findings to address them throughout our study.

In terms of practical implications, our work provides an alternative to the current criminal sentencing approach in the US. The basic principle of sentencing is proportionality, but when these decisions are left solely to the discretion of a single judge, concerns about bias and arbitrariness arise (Chiao, 2018). Thus, our work responds to calls to predict future sentences more accurately by addressing existing biases in the dataset (Ball, 2014; Berk & Hyatt, 2015; Grgic-Hlaca et al., 2018). Our findings demonstrate that an empirically developed and validated artifact such as “ShowCase” can offer one possible solution to this problem. We lay the foundation for building a complex system that can provide transparency, fairness, and objectivity by identifying the relevant inputs that are necessary for making reliable data-driven decisions.

We take a novel approach to empowering citizens by integrating their opinions into the sentencing process. The experts we interviewed were ambivalent about this strategy. On one hand, they liked the comprehensiveness of the viewpoints that were offered. On the other hand, they expressed reservations about the makeup of the data samples, the ignorance of the public regarding the law, and the potential of data to become static once collected, thus becoming incommensurate with current public sentiment (Greenstein & Zhu, 2018; Le Mens et al., 2018). However, it is worthwhile to implement such viewpoints because they can highlight potential disconnects between what the layperson and the law consider to be a fair sentence. Such viewpoints can also reveal existing implicit biases against minorities because public opinion viewpoints are based on people from diverse backgrounds and thus represent community sentiment. If static data reveals itself to be a problem, periodic data collection could be established and carried out as a federal program, similar to the American Community Survey, an annual survey about population and demographics from the US Census Bureau.<sup>3</sup>

Finally, “ShowCase” represents a renewed effort in terms of transparency within the criminal justice system. We not only incorporate transparency as a main design principle but also take it a step further and open our system to all parties involved in the sentencing process, such as judges, clerks, prosecutors, and defense attorneys. Such transparency implicitly provides the checks and balances needed to ensure that sentencing decisions are fairer and more objective, which would ultimately increase trust in the outcomes and legitimacy of the criminal justice system. Our study responds to calls for more transparency and equity in using data-driven technology (Carlson, 2017; Simmons, 2018). We focused on establishing the relevant components of “ShowCase” because this is crucial for producing legitimate and trustworthy results once the system is implemented.

## 6 Limitations And Future Research

We acknowledge that there are certain limitations and challenges associated with this study. First, our research represents the first step of many in the long journey toward more objective and fair court rulings. While the idea of what is fair has shifted over the years (Bradford & Myhill, 2015), the law still lags behind conventional ideas. Any changes in the justice system will inherently face opposition from multiple stakeholders. Our artifact paves the way for a carefully crafted, data-driven sentencing system. This proof of concept also raises awareness of both the benefits and drawbacks of implementing such a solution in a concrete way. To be completely clear, our purpose is not to change the law but rather to demonstrate how a data-driven approach can be used for social good.

One lingering question from the research is: “Would a real implementation of this artifact work?” We admit that “ShowCase” has not yet been field-tested. Any implementation, no matter how small, requires tactful navigation of the political landscape of the federal government. We have begun the process of designing a pilot program: gathering buy-ins from local district courts, identifying a champion for the program, securing a test site, developing the system, and evaluating its preliminary results. We envision “ShowCase” as a webbased open source cloud platform. We also surmise that while, in principle, “ShowCase” could bring about positive changes, the long-term effect is unknown, and a longitudinal study would be needed to expound upon the implementation consequences.

Currently, our artifact only focuses on the sentencing aspect of US federal district courts. Rehabilitation and probation, despite their importance, are outside the scope of this study. Also, we did not explore sentencing guidelines at the state level. Future research could examine the differences and extend our work from federal to state courts and even compare similar sentences across various states. The interview participants stated that adoption would likely be our biggest challenge. While reception has been positive so far, navigating the political landscape to obtain approval from the Department of Justice to implement “ShowCase” would admittedly be a monumental task. Participants also warned us about potential resistance, especially from more traditional judges who are not as technologically savvy. However, they were optimistic about obtaining buy-in from younger and more progressive judges seeking to win the trust of their constituents and ensure the legitimacy of the sentencing process. We are committed to continuing our efforts to demonstrate the value of taking a datadriven approach to criminal sentencing and we plan to engage experts from the Department of Justice for future iterations of the artifact.

## 7 Conclusion

Recent civil rights movements in the US, such as Black Lives Matter and Stop Asian Hate, have amplified the need to rethink our criminal justice system and strive for more equality and fairness. Our study demonstrates that a data-driven approach can help judges make fair and objective sentencing decisions while reducing their workload. We developed and evaluated a system interface prototype—“ShowCase”—that identifies relevant inputs for judges to consider when making a decision. This artifact is not only grounded in theory but is also empirically validated; we believe it can promote trust in the sentencing outcomes by building a culture of fairness and transparency. Our study reveals how the data-driven approach can be used for social good and the key role that IS can play in rebuilding confidence in the criminal justice system through empowering citizens and engaging them in the decision-making process.

## Appendix B: Survey Scenarios

The first scenario presents study participants with the following hypothetical case: “A person is found guilty of distributing 50 grams (about 1.8 oz) of methamphetamine (meth). This was their first offense, and there was no death or serious bodily injury resulting from the substance they were distributing.” This scenario was developed based on US 21§841. Per the US Code, the punishment for the offender should be no less than 10 years in prison and a fine of no more than \$10,000,000.

The second scenario involves the following case: “A Chief Executive Officer (CEO) of a corporation was found guilty of knowingly certifying a falsified financial report.” In this case, the punishment should be up to \$1,000,000 and/or imprisonment for no more than 10 years based on US 18§1350.

For each scenario, we asked the participants several questions concerning whether the offender should go to prison and, if so, for how long, and whether the offenders should pay a fine and, if so, how much should the fine be. We also asked whether they needed any additional information to make a fair decision. We presented participants with multiple victim and offender characteristics (e.g., age, gender, education, socioeconomic status, etc.) to choose from and also offered them an open-ended question to provide other factors we did not include. Both scenarios presented to the participants were left intentionally vague, without any additional context of the crime, the offender, or the victim(s), because we wanted to explore in more detail the participants’ mindsets and perceptions of whether current punishments were aligned with their notion of fairness and justice. This information is instrumental for the development of our “ShowCase” artifact because it gives us a broader perspective on important issues like criminal justice and social good.
