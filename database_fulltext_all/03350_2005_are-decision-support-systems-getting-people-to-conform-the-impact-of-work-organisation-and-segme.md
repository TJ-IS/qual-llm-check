# Are decision support systems getting people to conform? the impact of work organisation and segmentation on user behaviour in a French bank

## Abstract

The longitudinal study of the most sophisticated decision support system (DSS) for the management of debit accounts provides new answers to the question of conformity in French banking. In 2003, the analysis of 45 observations and qualitative interviews showed that the advisor maintains his free appreciation of risk. However, even if conformity does not exist, the results on the modification range show that the DSS does exert an influence on user behaviour. In addition, the interpretation and acceptance of DSS recommendation are different according to the type of portfolio managed and how the work is organised. The less the financial advisor knows the client, the greater the influence of the DSS. Recent decisions regarding the division of labour for the management of lower segments heighten the risk that DSS used without knowing the client leads to more conformity, or at least to what we conceptualise as strategic conformity, and a taylorisation of services.

## Introduction

D espite the misleading appearances, there is often a fine line dividing automatically taken decisions and help in decision-making. The question can indeed be asked whether, albeit justified from a company’s point of view, the employees who use decision-making tools on an everyday basis are not acting like robots, and not using their ability to think or their own knowledge. Faced with judgement information (Iselin, 1988) such as scores, do they operate as simple recording chambers? Do they go around the system? Or do they really engage in some reflective activity after taking into account the decision aid and its judgement information? When we refer to ‘decision support systems (DSS)’, we should question the kind of user behaviour that the system in a certain environment is developing. In other words, we consider three basic effects:

(1) the system facilitates the reflections and the decision process of the users but does not lead to the systematic approval of the judgement information (this information helps but does not dominate – it informates (Zuboff, 1988)),

(2) the users resist using the system; even if the choice of a class of risk is mandatory they may not pay much attention to the judgement information and do not take their decisions on the basis of the additional information provided by the system, which they do not even consult,

(3) the system so strongly influences the users’ behaviour that they conform to the judgement information proposed by the system.

Conformity to the judgement information such as a score is an important problem for an organisation and particularly for a bank. The core competency (Hamel and Prahalad, 1990) is risk assessment in commercial banking. In fact, it is a key success factor for several activities. Credit risk management has a very high social and economic impact on various stakeholders (Emel et al., 2003). If all the employees systematically approve the scores, this behaviour can be as dangerous for the bank as when control is deliberately avoided on financial markets (Drummond, 2003). The banking industry is developing decision aids and automation to increase its productivity (Rowe, 1994). However, banks should equally realise they are facing knowledge erosion and this might induce far too standardised behaviours when implementing expert systems (Benders and Manders, 1993). These questions are not new but are very important (Zuboff, 1988). Dan Remenyi opinion is that decision-making will increase, with advances in the knowledge system, the centralisation for more control and the automation of business (2002). If we wish to acquire a better insight into the society we are building with IT, we have to know more about behaviours and attitudes that are developing with the use of the technology. Furthermore, we need to know more about the environment, which allows both businesses to benefit from it and employees to fully consider the situation of the clients.

Whereas management discourse stresses employees’ empowerment, organisations are increasingly dependent on DSS. Facing the risk of information fragmentation and information overload, information workers might welcome decision aids (Hammer and Champy, 1992) offering all available information integrated into one application, as well as judgement information that synthesises the situation and provides decision and action guidance. If, as Bromiley and Curley (1992) argue, risk-related behaviours vary by both population and situation, we need to examine several combinations of situations and individual characteristics to develop a better understanding of risk-behaviour with DSS. Depending on the organisation, this may lead to a situation where employees conform to the decision aid. The goal of this study is to shed light on how and why users decide to accept or reject the judgement information provided by the DSS. We will show that there is a high probability of conformity only in certain types of environments for semistructured tasks. The focus of the paper is the extent to which conformity occurs in an organisational setting and how it can best be characterised.

Over the last 10 years, we have had the opportunity to directly and indirectly study the most sophisticated system in French retail banking for the management of debit accounts. The case study at Bank B provides a very worthwhile opportunity to understand the effects of work organisation, DSS capabilities and configuration on conformity, given that these characteristics have changed throughout the decade in that bank. Through the study of debtor management procedures in 10 other French banks, we were able to see to what point non-automated processes are cumbersome in their application and hierarchical follow-through. Some banks are inevitably trying to imitate Bank B (Rowe, 1994), but no other French bank offers the same DSS capabilities for this task. In this respect, the case described here offers unique insights into the situation in France.

The paper begins with a literature review on conformity, and more briefly on DSS, so as to be able to produce a better qualification of the phenomenon we observe. We then introduce and justify our case study methodology. The empirical findings are presented in the next section. The paper subsequently concludes with implications for both theory and practice.

## Literature review

## On conformity

‘IS must conform to the business and technical environment they operate in, otherwise they fail to provide the functionality that is expected of them’ write Garner and Raban (1999: 958) about enterprise-wide information systems solution. In this sense, the tool must comply with a set of requirements within different contexts. In this paper, we do not focus on this requirements engineering problem that we shall call process conformity or compliance. Instead, we study conformity as an attitude and a behavioural problem in the use of a system.

Socio-economists define conformity as ‘copying the most observed behaviour in a population’ (Carpenter, 2004: 395). Based on laboratory experiments, they show that the size of the majority (id.) or the proportion of models (Coultas, 2004) is a significant predictor of conformity. Conformity can be used to model technology diffusion phenomena or cooperative behaviour. Conformity qualifies a behaviour. It can even become an attitude when it is internalised. Zuboff (1988: 351) showed that managers expected that workers’ motivations would ensure their anticipatory conformity in order not be singled out due to the unnormative use of a system.

However, conformity is essentially a psychological notion bridging behaviour (following another behaviour) and a belief about such behaviour. Conformity occurs when there is a difference between individual thought and public norms, and when an individual declares himself or herself of the same opinion as the group (Levine et al., 1993). As for this strict and seminal sense in human sciences, Sherif (1935, 1947) was the first to demonstrate the human beings’ motive in conforming in ambiguous situations. The group members’ personal consequences of having participated in a collective decision may result in post-decisional conflict, i.e., a situation in which a person has committed himself or herself to a decision that seems incorrect to them (Janis and Mann, 1977). This happens when there is public conformity without private acceptance. This phenomenon sometimes prevents groups from learning and taking the best decisions and Sengupta and Te’eni (1993) suggested that incorporating into GDSS the cognitive feedback that describes the members’ decision strategies should therefore reduce such process losses.

In psychology and human–computer interface studies, most conformity research is based on laboratory experiments. It generally involves several confederates trying to influence real participant(s). The technological and cultural contexts between all participants seem to play a major role in conforming to the norm. For instance, Crede´ and Sniezek (2003) studied group judgement processes and outcomes in video-conferencing vs face-to-face groups. To their minds, video-mediated decision-making favours creative decisionmaking in organisations and environments characterised by high status differences and norms and expectations of conformity to authority. On the other hand, this entails having lesser applicability in situations requiring quick decisions and an implementation by all mutually trusting individuals.

Although this result was obtained for an intellective task, Tan et al. (1998) have shown that majority influence is stronger for a preference task than for an intellective one. In addition, the majority influence was stronger in the unsupported setting in an individualistic culture than the face-to-face computer-mediated communication (CMC) and dispersed CMC setting. In the collectivistic culture there was no corresponding difference. The findings of Crede´ and Sniezek may therefore only apply to individualistic environments. In a more collectivistic environment, the need to conform to the majority is so strong that the medium does not make a difference. Nevertheless, in the capitalist world also such a need exists in certain circumstances, even in individualistic cultures. For example, individuals, such as fund managers, may emphasise conformity even when they have potential beneficial private information. ‘By mimicking the decisions of other highprofile managers, they reduce the likelihood that their decisions stand out from the crowd when they do not yield expected returns’ (Konana and Balasubramanian, 2005). This phenomenon also looks very much like that of reputational herding where CIOs follow the majority on adopting technology regardless of evidence that a nonconventional solution might be better for their company (Kauffman and Li, 2003). Therefore, we can extend conformity to the situation involving the influence of a credible source of information that a person can trust as being accurate.

In cognitive psychology, it is well known that misinformation can be incorporated into memories recalled by eyewitnesses. Several experiments have shown that participants who watched videotapes of criminal activity demonstrated memory conformity to misinformation presented by one other person (Mudd and Govern, 2004). Conformity starts here with an individual leading one individual in the wrong direction.

Following this continuum, conformity has been extended to the influence on individual users of scores or information contained in DSS (Be´al and Rowe, 1998). Indeed, judgement information provided by the model and controlled by the hierarchy can be considered as a public norm, at least when it concerns lower segments, given the choices made on work organization and because other employees can then see it. Moreover, some people treat computers as a ‘team member’ and show many of the same in-group favouritism effects for computers that they show with people (Nass et al., 1996). A diffusion of responsibility often emerges in human–computer interaction and has also been depicted as bringing about an automation bias (Skitka et al., 1999). In particular, ‘errors of commission occur when decision-makers follow automated information or directives, even in the face of more valid or reliable indicators, suggesting that the automated aid is not recommending a proper course of action’ (ibid.: 993). Providing evidence of conformity and of automation biases in real settings is a difficult task because of the risks borne by the social actors. However, user behaviour can suggest the possibility or probability of conformity. As a matter of fact, knowing the actors’ behaviours, and more specifically whether actors modify or approve the decision proposed by the DSS, is an important albeit first step for investigating conformity. Indeed, DSS users can:

\- approve but not fully agree with $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ conform) the DSS recommendation,

\- approve and agree with the DSS recommendation, - reject the DSS recommendation and modify the decision proposed.

If they approve it, they may not really believe the judgment proposed and may conform to the system. Consequently, the issue is as follows: do they exert their own judgement and how do they do so? Do they have time to make their own judgement? In practice, systems like enterprise systems (ES) provide near perfect visibility over business processes and increase both control and empowerment and can lead to reflective conformity (Elmes et al., 2005). Reflective conformity is an oxymoron, that is, a contradiction in itself. In fact, as Elmes, Strong and Volkoff pointed out in their literature, conformity is associated with reduced reflection. However, their observation of an ES in use leads them to conclude that there were workers who followed prescribed procedures and were able to increase inventory control through their reflections, whereas those who only followed did not control the inventory.

If they do modify the decision proposed, we cannot strictly claim that conformity exists, but this does not prove that the user exerts his or her own judgement on the client risk level or takes all the relevant information about the case into account. Many reasons can explain a change with respect to the decision proposed. They may not wish to be monitored and may prefer to maintain a minimum degree of leeway, or as Crozier and Friedberg (1977) would say, they maximise their uncertainty zone. In others words, they want to minimise their own risk and this risk is related to that of their own company regarding their actions, but also to the risk of their relationship with certain specific actors, for example, their clients (Mac Namara and Bromiley, 1997) or their hierarchy (Honore´, 1998).

For all these reasons, conformity cannot be directly observed but only deduced from user behaviour and user discourse about their beliefs.

## On DSS

While the managerial risk-return literature is relevant for examining risky decisions (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974; Kahneman and Lovallo, 1993; Mac Namara and Bromiley, 1999), it considers a limited rational behaviour with respect to expected returns (the utility theory, the prospect theory). And it mainly focuses on the performance issue. In fact, this literature appears less attractive if we lay emphasis on the behaviour itself and on its contingencies. New insights from psycho-sociology and organisation theory (Banker and Kauffman, 2004), taking into account the relationships and the organisational context could be added to the analysis of DSS use phenomenon (Eom, 2004). As regards the internal (work organisation) environment of the organisation and the way it enacts the external environment through segmentation, we contend that they play an important role on user behaviour and on the possibility of conformity even when they are mostly used by an individual,.

The literature on DSS suggests a general framework using eight elements: environment, task, implementation strategy, DSS capability, DSS configuration, user, user behaviour and performance (Eierman et al., 1995; Eom, 2004). This research is concerned with all these dimensions except for implementation strategy. It is mainly based on a case study of a semi-structured task and first provides new insights into the evolution of a previously studied DSS in terms of DSS capability and DSS configuration. However, the main goal of this research is to show that, according to the type of user, work organisation is considered to be an important aspect of the environment dimension, and plays a major role in influencing user behaviour. Regarding behaviour, Todd and Benbasat provide a model based on effort theory (Johnson and Payne, 1985) where the behaviour can be characterised by effort, accuracy and decision strategy. Most experts consider that environment and its relationship to user behaviour deserves more attention from researchers (Eierman et al., 1995; Todd and Benbasat, 2000). This is not surprising since most research relies on laboratory experiments. Eierman et al. (1995) could only cite three studies on this relationship, but none of them considered the work organisation as a dimension of the environment. Instead, the latter studied the impact of the incentive system, the information available, the variability of the decision-making environment and of top management support upon the amount of use of the system. From their literature review on the environment (ibid.), it appears that only Roland had proposed a model for including the organisational structure and the division of labour per se. In addition, the need for research on this relationship was emphasised by Todd and Benbasat: ‘Environmental factors such as information load, interruptions and technological limitations need to be examined to help understand the boundaries of the model and how well it translates into more realistic environments’ (2000: 13).

Research framework, research questions and methodology We elicited a research framework from this review to guide our empirical investigation (cf. Figure 1).

The study addressed the following research questions: Do the bankers using the system conform in a very frequent manner? How best can we characterise their behaviour? Has their behaviour changed as the DSS has developed and do the organisation of work and segmentation bear an influence on the conformity effect?

Our case study (Benbasat et al., 1987) featured two aspects:

\- The daily observation of advisors to derive certain statistics about the behaviour (rather than simple database collection with no simultaneous observation as in the previous studies at Bank B), and to understand to what extent the decision reveals a conformation to that proposed by the program.

\- An interview with the advisors in order to understand their way of thinking during analysis and to judge their independence vis-a\`-vis the program studied and the extent, if any, of conformity behaviour.

Our goal could only be achieved by a case study with enough observation and interviews to allow a better interpretation of the observed behaviour. A case study is an empirical inquiry that

\- investigates a contemporary phenomenon within its real life context, when

\- the boundaries between phenomenon and context are not clearly evident, and in which

\- multiple sources of evidence are used (Yin, 1990).

The research thrust was a second exploration, with new interviewing, observing and systems conditions that provided a better understanding of the phenomenon and further tested the effect of both the work organisation and the segmentation on conformity.

The research was made possible thanks to our partnership with the bank, which trained its new employees at the Institute of Banking and Finance in our University. The study was carried out at sites where one of the author’s students was doing his internship. He had already had half a year of experience with the bank and was in charge of collecting the data and conducting the subsequent interviews. This context was suitable not only for accessing the data but also for interviewing the employees in a relationship of mutual trust. In this way there was no sign of fear that the observer would not be trustworthy or had been sent by the hierarchy to check up on the employees. The author who designed the research would have encountered much difficulty in attaining this atmosphere of trust in order to carry out his observations. The task was very short, and collecting the same data would have required spending a month at the bank for only several minutes of observation per day. This might not have been enough for gaining the trust of the employees to proceed with unbiased interviews. The author had previously overseen a Ph.D. in the same bank and this student had spent 4 years doing his Ph.D. and working almost full time in this bank. The student had also been involved in this project for one year from 1994 to 1995 as he had been part of the reengineering team in the IS Division (Be´al and Rowe, 1998). While this position allowed him to gain access to the recordings of many more behavioural data, he visited branches but could not be considered as a neutral observer by the branch employees nor could he make subsequent interviews. In 1997, we together met the Risk Manager of the bank who was directing the design of the new version of the system. Just before it was released in 2001, the author visited the design team, and viewed and discussed the new capabilities of the DSS. The data presented in this paper were collected in the spring of 2003 after the new version of the system had been in operation for more than one year.

The observation led to several variables being collected in order to test a possible conformity. The starting risk level provided by the program was noted as was the final decision taken by the advisor. This method also made it possible to examine in what propensity the advisor approves the risk level, but also the difference that may exist between the proposed risk and the final risk. However, the advisors had been warned of the purpose of the observation and could have modified their behaviour. To eliminate this bias, we would have had to question the advisors whilst dissimulating the risk proposed by the program and compelling them to make a decision. This solution would have been more pertinent from a methodological point of view. However, it was unfeasible. We did not wish to change the advisors’ work tool and get in the way of their work. An additional study was set up at a later date to make up for this. This study consisted of gauging the time spent by advisors dealing with a debtor, in order to measure the analysis effort (Johnson and Payne, 1985; Todd and Benbasat, 1991, 1999). The indication of the analysis effort is precious. It gives a view of the extent to which conformity is correlated to the level of risk suggested by the DSS.

The analysis was deepened by completing the information collected with the advisors’ personal thoughts about the system (feelings, ideas, criticisms and wishes). In order to get their perception of DSS, discussions were held about the elements taken into account in their analyses, and the perceived independence of the setting up of the DSS. It was considered of interest to extend our study to another branch by giving our interview guidelines to a financial advisor, to a financial assistant, as well as to the three other advisors who had already been surveyed for the daily study, and also to the branch manager. The interview guidelines comprised six semi-structured questions, which were administered after a set of observations had been made for each banker.

## Context of the case study: task, DSS evolution, environment and sample

Managing debit accounts is one of the many risks borne by the banking sector. This risk continues to increase due to the growth in the number of banks used by economic agents and their growing indebtedness. The permanent checks on the level of litigation is therefore one of the key elements of risk management. This is carried out on a daily basis on thousands of clients, and even if the individual amounts are not high compared to the sums involved in the case of a loan, the overall amount can be relatively high.

However, the increase in the number of debtors makes the management of this phenomenon an increasingly harder task and costs the bank more. The management of debtors takes up a large amount of time that is expensive and not very productive. For this reason, several banks have decided to computerise this task in order to free-up sales time for their advisors, but also to make decisionmaking easier (Lebraty, 2000). Certain banks have opted for a debtor management system accompanied by a system that aids decision-making. Since 1995, Bank B has been implementing the most comprehensive system for this task among French banks, and has implemented more than 1200 rules. This DSS, called SAGED, was set up in their network to standardise decisions, improve productivity (by reducing time spent on this task) and optimise administrative followthrough. The final objective is to reduce the risks linked to this daily task and the resulting cost.

The SAGED program proposes a risk for the indebted client to the advisor and a series of measures aimed at reducing the risk the bank was running. To do this, the DSS studied calculates the balance of the client accounts. If the balance is in the black, the process stops here. Conversely, if the overall balance is in the red then the program analyses the situation according to the decision algorithm (Be´al and Rowe, 1998) in order to propose a level of risk and to systematise it.

From Table 1 we can see that the DSS does not just propose a risk level, but links each risk to a list of actions and behaviour suited to the situation. These different recommended actions aim to make the advisors work easier in terms of the choice of letters as well as the movements they reject. Since its installation in 1995 it has been a system that helps decision-making and action taking (ibid.).

Generally, advisors wish to take actions that comply with the estimated risk level. Therefore, in his/her analytical process, an advisor will graduate the actions he/she must take when facing a high or very high risk:

\- First of all, revoking overdraft facilities

\- Then taking away credit card facilities

\- Blocking standing orders

\- Lastly, refusing to honour cheques.

This graduation is meant to suitably follow the client and to meet the bank’s needs (in terms of risk management).

Correlative to these different needs, the program seeks to curtail an awkward situation as quickly as possible in order to stop the situation persisting and turning the recovery of funds into a serious problem. The DSS therefore proposes actions linked to the perceived level of risk.

The observation of 1996 showed a certain number of problems that restricted its use by advisors, which are characterised by a lack of flexibility and incomplete information. These problems affected the behaviour of the users and the efficiency of the system. The lack of flexibility of the DSS mainly resulted in the advisors receiving incomplete information. This influenced the perception of the advisors, encumbering the perceived efficiency of the system, and making researchers think that the system had intentionally been designed to limit the degree of freedom and to favour conformity to judgement information. We can no longer say today that designers wanted to standardise behaviour to this extent. Indeed, many measures were introduced to increase the flexibility of the system that resolved certain previously existing problems. The navigation between applications and the coordination of tasks were now taken into account in the design of the new program, which also worked with more complete information. We had also shown that the task was subject to interruptions nearly half of the time and that the ongoing work was consequently lost. The new configuration allowed the banker to preserve the ongoing work. With these changes in DSS capabilities and configuration, the conditions in the Be´al and Rowe proposition no longer apply:

The approval of a proposed score does not signify the ultimate confirmation of its pertinence. The risk of inducing a behaviour of conformity (Levine et al., 1993) is high (ibid.: 68) when a DSS is incomplete (nonexhaustive and not-current) and makes the completion of the information structure difficult without wasting time and spoiling work already completed, but offers in due time judgement information adapted to the circumstances.

In their article the authors gave numerous elements supporting potential conformity because, even if they showed that risk was only approved in half the cases, they added that the modifications were often marginal and highly consistent if not influenced by the 3 months evolution indicator. With the characteristics of the new version making it easier to access extra information, employees may no longer conform to the score. This is one of the motivations and reasons for undertaking this second exploration into our case study.

Concerning the environment, segmentation played a paramount role. The segmentation of the clientele at Bank B was carried out for functional, organisational and marketing purposes. It had not been created to manage debtor risk, although having studied the operating system of this management, it must be said that there is a real relation between the management of debtors and segmentation. Nevertheless, internal work organisation was not fully taken into account in the management of the debtor. A banker would handle his or her own client portfolio for higher segments and the clients would be served normally only by this person. Contrary to this, the relationship was not so ‘personalised’ for lower segments and the number of people consecutively involved in managing the risk of one client could be fairly high. We had reported very ordinary cases with relatively low risk where five people would manage the account over a 3-week period. In 1997, it was realised (Rowe, 1998) that ‘the higher in the segmentation the lower the rate of approval of very level risk’, the advisor is largely influenced by the segmentation of his clients. Furthermore, (ibid.) it was noticed that ‘the more employees that were involved in successively managing the same client, the higher the rate of approval’. This second result is consistent with the findings that people tend to expend less effort when working collectively than when working individually (Karau and Williams, 1993). We tried to verify the first result in 2003, when the DSS configuration and capability problems were solved to a large extent and when segmentation was less linked to work organisation.

Up until 1998 there existed a segmentation based on the same criteria, that is, socio-professional group, bank credits and financial flow, but it was much broader because it only comprised 4 categories, differentiated by a number of stars: from 1\* for the basic client, to 4\* to the top of the range client (Rowe, 1998). To favour its marketing strategy and its organisation, the banking group B chose a more detailed segmentation of its clientele.

The three criteria allow the definition of typical profiles of clients as well as their needs. We can consider that each sub-group has the same wants and demands, thus allowing a common and homogenous supply to be brought to each category.

Today, the market of non-business customers is divided into six segments (designated by an action or main goal of the bank with respect to the client):

T1: ‘To estimate’ the general member of the public with limited potential,

T2: ‘To discover’ clients with potential at the beginning of their life cycle,

T3: ‘To develop’,

T4: ‘To maintain a hold on ‘ clients with high bank credits and limited development potential,

T5: ‘To promote’ clients with high bank credits and high development potential,

T6: ‘To privilege’ the top of the range client.

Today, each advisor obtains a portfolio made up of a group of clients of the same category according to his professional situation. In this way, each advisor has a specific role to play adapted to his segment, advising only his clients, and his clients only dealing with him, except in the case of T1 and T2 clients, who are increasingly dealt with by the ‘Service de Negociation Amiable’ (SNA) by the AFAs.

## Example:

\- Assistant Financial Advisor (AFA) for segments T1 and T2,

\- Financial Advisor (FA) for segments T2 and T3, - Executive Financial Advisor (EFA) mainly for segments T4 (sometimes T3 and T5),

\- Senior Financial Advisor (SFA) for segments T5 and T6.

For each segment, there is an associated group of specific products (e.g. top of the range section – life assurance for a large sum with high returns but a large amount of risk.) Clients having tailor-made banking, where products are pre-selected and the advisors adapted to their financial and socio-professional situation. Thanks to this system, the bank can offer clients products that suit them and allows networking. This also allows for greater productivity and a better monitoring of clients. Unfortunately, however, this structure has certain limits, that is, there is a contradiction between wanting to standardise service provision whilst personalising it. By organising sub-groups of clients from three common and pre-defined criteria, the service provision is being standardised, but the more personal expectations of the clients are more or less hidden. Although part of the same segment, clients have their own identity and different expectations.

The sample was selected in order to make a possible correlation between the methods of use of the program and the different categories of portfolio. For this purpose, it was decided to study advisors working with different segments.

From the same branch (in Lannion), the following were studied:

\- The newly appointed branch manager, a woman, 28 years old, 3 years at Bank B (segments concerned – mainly T4 and T5 and hierarchical follow-up), using the program on a daily basis. To simplify analysis we only studied the analysis of her T4 and T5 clients.

\- An EFA, a woman, 43 years old, 25 years with Bank B (segments concerned – T4/T5), having a mainly upmarket clientele.

\- Two FA (segments concerned – T2/T3) with a middle of the range clientele: a woman, 49 years old, 15 years with Bank B and a man 47 years old, 22 years with Bank B.

All of them are familiar with information technologies, but their general technical expertise does not exceed that of any ordinary user in banking. They can be considered as having the same experience with SAGED. However, they mostly differ in terms of banking experience and in client knowledge (the branch manager vs the others).

## Results

## Post-observation quantitative analysis

The study allowed the analysis of the cases of 45 clients in debit, mainly from two types of portfolio: FA (segments T2/ T3) for the man (FAM) and the woman (FAW), EFA (E) and branch manager (B) (segments T4/T5). The replies of the advisor were inventoried case by case, as were the solutions proposed by the program in order to search for possible conformity. Table 2 describes the results of this enquiry.

Overall, we could say that the number of debtors is relatively low compared with the duration of the study. In all, 3 weeks were required to collect these 45 cases. It was especially difficult to pool 13 case studies for the EFA and branch manager segment (lines 33–45) given that no debtor case occurred on certain days.

## General analysis

We can see that 42% of the decisions approved the propositions made by the program. Therefore, in 58% of cases the advisor did not choose the proposed risk. Based on these statistics, it is difficult to say whether there is conformity or not, given that the results are relatively close and that the advisor changes the proposed risk in the majority of cases.

This was increased in 54% of the cases where the risk level changed. Advisors never hesitated to increase the risk level despite the consequences that this could have had on the client or themselves. However, the general analysis is not appropriate due to the diversity of the portfolios considered. The sample studied is heterogeneous and is made up mainly of two types of clients whose diverse characteristics compel the advisors to react differently when faced with a similar situation. For this reason, it seems to be more relevant to study the behaviour of advisors according to the type of portfolio. This will allow us to highlight any appropriation depending on the segment concerned.

## Analysis of the FA portfolios: segments T2/T3 (line no. 1 to line no. 32)

It can be seen that the approval of risk level concerned 47% of cases and at this stage of the analysis, we cannot make precise conclusions about the existence of conformity of advisors dealing with middle of the range clients.

On the other hand, the modifications resulted in an increase in 65% of cases where the risk level was changed. It can therefore be stated that advisors with this type of client have no qualms about being more rigorous when dealing with the risks they are faced with. This comes as no surprise given that the type of client they deal with is considered more of a risk by the banking establishments. In these cases, a great deal of frequently subjective elements are taken into account during analysis by various advisors when choosing the risk level. However, it should also be noted that of the six proposed R6 cases only one was approved by the advisor, although she did not hesitate to change an R5 to an R6, despite the consequences that this implied for the client.

## Analysis of the EFA portfolios

At the same time as analysing the FA portfolios, the decisions made by an EFA advisor (segments T4 and T5) and the branch manager were equally considered (last 13 lines of the table).

Of the 13 cases studied, the advisor approved the risk proposed in 31% of them (4 times). In 69% of the cases their risk was not validated. It can therefore be said that conformity was not high, and that despite the program, advisors remain in control of their decisions and do not hesitate to modify the risk factor initially proposed.

In the instances where they did modify the risk level, it decreased 67% of the time. It can therefore be noted that the advisors who deal with a higher grade of client dislike approving a high risk for a top of the range client, preferring to grant him a lower risk level. This is confirmed by the fact that no R6 factor was approved, despite being proposed in two of the thirteen cases.

Main interview results

What were the elements considered in order to make your own decision?

Almost all the participants replied as follows: ‘ Knowledge of the client is the most important.

The decision taken by the advisor is principally linked to this knowledge. This comes from the various interviews held with the client, knowing about his/her personal situation, and his/her needs expressed during various meetings and conversations that influence the advisor on the choice of risk level.

Let us take the example of an executive financial advisor. For her, ‘it’s client knowledge that is the most important. They may come out R6 because there are few movements coming in, but the client has plenty in credit in other banks.’

Client knowledge therefore has a large influence on the approval of the risk level proposed by SAGED.

One of the male financial advisors adds, ‘If I know that the client has fixed income and that this situation is temporary, or that the client is making an effort to save, I will reduce the risk.’ The female financial advisor however adds, ‘If the proposed risk level is justified I will approve it even if the knowledge of the client could lead to variations.’ As their profiles are very alike, we interpret this as a higher risk aversion that of the man.

Finally, in the words of the newly appointed branch manager (who has therefore little knowledge of the clients), ‘for an R5 or R6 risk level with little short-term visibility, I have no reservations about approving it. Not having specific knowledge of the client to go on, I place my trust in the program.’

## What screens do you look at to make your decision?

They all look at the client summary screen. (This gives an overall view and shows movements in and out of the account.) In addition, four advisors out of the five interviewees look at the SAGED history screen. Both these screens are the most commonly used by the advisors for managing debtors. There does not seem to be a lot of variation in terms of decision strategy.

The female financial advisor underlines this point:

‘Above all it is the SAGED background and previously sent letters that are important, and I add previously written notes to this.

How do you measure your independence against the risk level proposed by SAGED?

With a reply between one and 10, one being maximum dependence, and 10 total freedom, without the slightest hesitation, all of them replied with a ten!

However, the branch manager added,

‘The decision must be coherent however, if in doubt, it is best to approve it because if the risk increases our superiors cannot blame us.’

How do you see your freedom in the final decision and the choice of letters that are sent?

As far as R1, R2 and R3 risks are concerned, according to the financial advisor with higher risk aversion, ‘I always approve the risk as well as the sending of letters in order to try and make the client react.’

The branch manager insisted, ‘I don’t reject outgoing payments if the client hasn’t been warned as the client can reproach us for this’ (MURCEF law).

The opinion of the executive financial advisor was that, ‘the letters are well-written but some clients prefer to receive a phone call rather than a letter, that they feel is too impersonal and formal.

## How do you write your notes?

One of the FA underlines the fact that, ‘notes make future analysis easier’ (linked with the visualised screens) ‘and allow us to justify our decision to our superiors. They are a summary and an explanation of our actions.’

What criticism would you make of the system and the changes you’d like to see?

Overall, no real criticism was made and no future evolutions were needed.

However, it was said that, ‘the clients monitored by the SNA service are not monitored enough and the problems regarding these clients get passed onto the branches.’

We concluded lastly that out of the four bankers we had observed, the female financial advisor was the most risk averse. When hesitating between the proposed score and knowledge information about the client, she would select the proposed score. The branch manager was also more obviously aware of the importance of justifying any decision, but especially when changing the score.

However, we felt it was very difficult to derive any correlations between the behaviour observed (cf. Table 2) and the factors that consciously influence user behaviour, that is,

\- the decision proposed,

\- the possibility of getting additional information through the system such as historical data,

\- client knowledge and user risk with respect to the customer relationship, and

\- user risk with respect to the bank.

For instance, we could not say that the more risk averse financial advisor really displayed a different behaviour to that of the man dealing with the same middle of the range segments.

## Discussion

The link between the qualitative and quantitative results shows the general trends concerning the appropriation of a DSS by advisors and the potential risk of conformity.

## Risk approval and strategic conformity

As has been seen, an advisor does not automatically approve the risk level proposed by the program (58% of cases). The use of their own analysis allows the advisor to make a decision about the risk being run by the bank. The interviews showed that advisors felt independent in their decision-making. Some went as far as to say that they were entirely autonomous from the program. However, this independence should be looked at in perspective, given that in 42% cases the risk level proposed by the program is approved, climbing to 77% in the case of middle of the range clients. Our field study showed that when the advisors approve the risk level proposed, they do so also for corresponding actions. The majority of advisors (whatever their clientele) agree that the actions are adequate and correspond to the situation. This is new compared to the previous studies. Moreover, a careful appraisal of the replies shows that most advisors’ expressions reflect the importance of a number of constraints, especially for action. It looks like their ‘declaration of independence’ mirrors the discourse on taking responsibility and empowerment at the upper management level in the bank rather than an objective reality.

Observation and interviews allowed a link to be made between client knowledge and the final decision. This essential factor motivates the choice of an advisor in the approval of a risk level. The advisor himself or herself proposes a risk level given the personal situation of the client. However, can this create a problem of too much subjectivity in the analysis?

To counter this, it can be said that advisors do not fundamentally change the risk level proposed and rarely shift from R6 to R0 even if they know the client. On the other hand, they will go from an R6 to R5. The hierarchy of risk is therefore respected. The objective process of the advisor encompasses the wish for good risk management. Furthermore, the advisor keeps a risk level range that is restricted to 1 or 2 degrees (according to the quantitative analysis: 25 cases out of 26).

It can therefore be said that if an effect of conformity has not been brought to light in its own right, the advisor does take into account the judgement information of the DSS, as was observed in 1996 (Be´al and Rowe, 1998). Whether it be to keep good relations with clients (Mac Namara and Bromiley, 1997), to avoid making mistakes, or for strategic reasons of justification to superiors (Sutcliffe and Mac Namara, 2001), the advisor chooses a risk level that approaches the one proposed. The advisor therefore builds this into his/her decision-making process, wishing to be able to justify the risk level chosen by writing a few notes. As in an ES process, conformity to risk management is enforced through built-in error checks and role-specific algorithms to perform transactions. The choice of a judgement information constrains the possible set of prescribed actions. This systemic set of constraints creates a regime of truth (Elmes et al., 2005). This technology creates a certain discipline among the employees (Foucault, 1977; Willcocks, 2004). However, the approval rate is not high enough for us to state that bankers conform to the system. They place their trust more in the knowledge of their client than in the score itself. Nevertheless, their range of modifications never reach further than the next class of risk. And in particular, because their subsequent action is constrained by the risk they chose, when the risk is very high, they just lower it marginally. This situation, recurring with what had already been measured (Be´al and Rowe, 1998), can conceptually be best captured as strategic conformity. In this case, reflection does not come first and these bankers have to act very fast. Situations that could be characterised as reflective conformity are exceptional. They decide on the basis of the consequences of their actions for the bank, but also for themselves and for the client. In particular they act strategically with respect to the other actors, maximising their leeway (Crozier and Friedberg, 1977; Besson and Rowe, 2001). They do not take time to look at all the information and rarely check the optional screens in the application except the past transaction screen. Their decision is made quickly and approximately. The risk score and the evolution pointer will cover them in case of trouble. On the other hand, at least they know they will not see the client losing his temper or totally breaking off the relationship. Rather than taking a cognitive stance, they base their choice on the idea of maintaining the relationship (Mac Namara and Bromiley, 1997), but they do it without breaking the rules of good behaviour. In this way they conform to the process and indication (Garner and Raban, 1999), but they do not especially trust the judgement information ‘that they change it or not’. Their decision strategy is guided more by their relationship (Adams, 1976) and the organisational objectives and control, than by a cognitive attitude of ambiguity avoidance (Ellsberg, 1961).

The second study measuring the analysis time showed a link between the risk level and the validation time. The analysis time for R0, R1 and R3 was generally lower than the time spent on high risk (R5 and R6). It was also noted that the analysis time increases considerably when the client concerned is unknown, whatever the risk level. As a result, it can be said that conformity may very well exist for low risks that do not have heavy consequences for the client or the advisor, while strategic conformity occurs for very high risks. In this case, even if they act strategically, the employees have to check the credit history and see what others have done. This result could be moderated by user type (experience, cognitive style), but it is paramount according to the segment and work organisation which, in this case study, conditions the employee’s confidence in his or her client’s knowledge and the probability that other colleagues will look at his or her decisions.

## Strategic conformity according to segment type and work organisation

We showed that higher approval rate of the score for very high risks was much higher for lower segments and thought this result was partly caused by work organisation since we had a similar result on work organisation (cf. supra) (Rowe, 1998). Our interpretation was that it could be caused by a difficulty in assuming one’s responsibility when one was not very well informed directly by the client due to a collective form of organisation. Within the environmental context of another segment definition, the comparison between segments T2/T3 and T4/T5, with no difference in work organisation between these two segments confirms the first result of this previous study performed on 400 cases, but not our interpretation. As we could see during an interview with an EFA, their decisions will not comply with that of the program in the majority of cases, and will not be based solely on the consideration of the proposed risk level. For this segment of clients and according to them, knowledge of the client (or as we interpret it in some instances, simply to the type of client, since there is not much communication with the client) is the most important factor. The advisor prefers to attribute a lower risk level than the one proposed and to therefore avoid conflicting relations. This strategic onformity is conceptually directed towards all stakeholders, but it especially concerns well-off clients. This choice is all the more selfevident when the validation of the maximum risk level leads to heavy consequences. Indeed, the quantitative analysis showed that the EFA decreases the risk level proposed in 67% of modified cases (vs 35% for FAs).

As far as segments T2 and T3 are concerned, the interviews and the observation both show that client knowledge and personal analysis by the advisor influence the final decision. However, this needs to be put into perspective as they have the tendency to approve a risk level in order to cover themselves in respect to their superiors. As for the less wealthy segments, knowing the client does not bring as much good news regarding the potential risk as with the other segments (cf. interviews). Their behaviour can therefore be considered as a hybrid of covering themselves (Honore´, 1998) and conformity, a kind of strategic conformity intended to protect both the bank and themselves.

The difference in apprehension according to the segment is all the more visible when quantitative data is analysed. The approval rate ranges from 47% for an FA to 31% for an EFA. Given this large difference, it can be said that the appropriation of the program is different according to the types of client. In addition, FAs have a tendency to modify risk levels by increasing them (65%), whereas an EFA does so in only 33% of modified cases.

As regards T1 and T2 segments managed by the SNA service, it was impossible to make observations. According to the advisors at Lannion, conformity is high. They believe that the people who evaluate these risks have very little information on the personal situation of the clients. This would also confirm that work organisation has a strong impact on conformity, a result already obtained and correctly interpreted (Rowe, 1998) by the impact of the number of persons consecutively intervening in the decision in the previous work organisation for lower segments and the resistance to assume responsibility leading to a conformity effect. The transactive collective memory (Brandon and Hollingshead, 2004) does not work well.

Overall, if our interpretations are right, this means that the work organisation effect and the segment effect on conformity are less interdependent than we had hypothesised in 1998 and than what would result from an over hasty reading of Table 4 summarising the new observations of 2003. Thus, this second exploratory research helps in preparing for a field study aiming at an explanatory model of conformity.

## Conclusion

Before drawing conclusions let us point out one of the main limitations of this study. Firstly, we cannot claim to have tested conformity in depth, because we did not match each case with each belief, but one person’s belief and his/her cases. Our methodology could only provide certain facts about behaviour and derive a few hints about the potential of actual conformity by combining interviews and expressions. Indeed, the beliefs that make it possible to diagnose conformity on each case are very difficult to systematically collect in an environment where users are constantly striving and interacting with the system for productivity. Is not this yet another case where they would feel that ‘Big Brother’ is watching them? Nevertheless, it is our firm belief that our interpretation of the combination of interviews and observations is valid. Indeed the interviews were clear enough for the author to interpret the combination. Apart from these difficulties, the main theoretical contribution of this case study is to put forward the strategic conformity concept which attempts to capture both strategic behaviour with respect to various stakeholders and a willingness to keep close to the norm even if one does not abide by it.

In the future, we would like to more systematically collect time and decision strategies and improve the description on each case of the relationship with the client, including possible interactions. This could help evaluate the variations around conformity, draw a better empirical distinction between reflective conformity and strategic conformity and move towards a more explanatory study of conformity types.

In the case of traditional risk management in other banks, client knowledge is the most significant and appreciation of the risk is left entirely up to the advisor. As far as performance is concerned, it is obvious that the more experienced advisors, who have better knowledge of the client, are also very satisfied with this type of organisation. Conversely, beginners feel that the setting up of a DSS could only be beneficial and that their limited client knowledge means that any outside help would be useful.

In the case of assisted management, such as that used by Bank B, it has been revealed that all of the advisors appreciate the help provided by the program. Old hands and newcomers consider the DSS as a supplementary analysis tool to help their decision-making and has brought drastic productivity gains (Be´al and Rowe, 1998). With the new generation of the system, advisors no longer complain about navigation difficulties when searching for additional information. The likelihood of conformity and of errors of commission has therefore decreased. It would also be wrong to speak of conformity in the strictest sense of the term when examining the choices of approval by the advisors (except in the case of clients managed by the SNA service). Consequently, the DSS appears to be a complement without powers of persuasion in the final decision. The advisor keeps his or her free appreciation of risk. On the other hand, advisors do not really seem to take the time needed to benefit from this additional user friendliness and flexibility in the new system. Do they take time to really form their own judgements? It does not seem so. They tend to be ‘cognitive misers’ (Skitka et al., 1999), that is, most of them seem to choose a direction involving the least cognitive effort and use their client knowledge as a rule of thumb to be quicker (on heuristics see Fiske and Taylor, 1994). Their feeling of independence might just reveal their happiness at being considered fully responsible. Even if there is no approval, the results on the modification range show that the DSS does have an influence. It certainly has a greater influence for lower risk levels and the correlation between the risk level and analysis time is perceptible.

Moreover, the appropriation of the DSS by the advisors is directly correlated to the segment of the clientele managed by the advisor. Thus, the interpretation, rate of approval and validation of risk are different according to the type of portfolio managed.

The setting up of a department specialising in the management of serial debtors and for the bottom of the range segment (T1–T2) is a real change that aims to standardise and automate decision-making. When the division of tasks concerning the same client was introduced the tendency was for approval of risk level, or even conformity (Rowe, 1998). Although we conclude that in the general case conformity is now rare, automation biases and errors of commission are very likely in this work organisation and for these segments.

This change is the consequence of a modification in the banking establishment’s approach. The establishment is mainly concerned with seeking profits and managing risks. DSS-based debtor management is therefore promised a bright future, with the policies of the banks favouring a high turnover of staff. Advisors regularly change portfolios whilst still having to meet objectives. We can wonder how the bank – client situation will evolve in this context, the risk being an impersonal relationship between the client and the advisor, and having to admit that the advisor is nothing more than the spokesperson of a modern, highly productive and computerised bank. Clients do not get the deep support (Zuboff and Maxmin, 2003) they might expect when running into difficult situations. The spectre of Taylorism therefore still looms despite appearances and customer relationship management will merely serve the interests of the wealthy.

## About the author

Dr. Frantz Rowe has been Professor of Information Systems at the University of Nantes, France, since the fall of 1995. Before this he was a professor at Ecole Nationale Superieure des Te´le´communications, Paris, and an assistant professor at Ecole Nationale des Ponts et Chausse´es, Paris. He was trained as an engineer (Master in Engineering) and as an economist (Master of Arts). He graduated from UC Berkeley in 1985. His doctoral degree from the University of Paris was awarded in 1991. His major research areas pertain to information and communication systems use and their effects on organisations, and to IS project dynamics and change. He has recently directed two research projects: one on change management of ERP projects and one on the structure (including shared or virtual) of call centres and their performance.

He has published 32 articles in scientific journals including Database, Accounting Management and IT,

Journal of Global Information Management , Journal of Decision Systems, ISDN Networks and Computer Systems, Transportation Research, Technological Forecasting and Social Change and several books, including, as the editor, ‘Faire de la recherche en syste\`mes d’information (Doing research in information systems)’ published in 2002 by Vuibert: Paris. This book is the first effort by the Frenchspeaking community to deliver a state-of-the-art in IS (20 chapters). He is the Editor in Chief of Systemes d’Information et Management, http://revuesim.free.fr.

He has guided 16 Ph.D. students and was the first director of the graduate programme in IS at the Management Faculty of the University of Nantes. He is currently directing the Ph.D. programme in Management. He has been a member of the Board of the University of Nantes and in charge of the IS Policy. He has co-founded the Association Information et Management in 1991 and served as Vice President for Research till 1998.
