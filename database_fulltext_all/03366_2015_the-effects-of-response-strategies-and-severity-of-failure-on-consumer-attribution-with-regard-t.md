# The effects of response strategies and severity of failure on consumer attribution with regard to negative word-of-mouth

Hsin Hsin Chang ⁎, Yao-Chuan Tsai, Kit Hong Wong, Jian Wei Wang, Fang Ju Cho

Department of Business Administration, National Cheng Kung University, 1 University Rd., Tainan City 70101, Taiwan

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 27 January 2014 Received in revised form 3 January 2015 Accepted 18 January 2015 Available online 4 February 2015

Keywords: Attribution theory Response strategies Severity of failure Organizational reputation Negative word-of-mouth (NWOM)

## a b s t r a c t

The negative word-of-month (NWOM) contained in comments posted on online forums can have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on consumer purchase decisions. Despite this, the related corporate response strategies and their effects have not been investigated in depth. For this reason, this study examined the effects of different response strategies and the severity of failure on customer attribution in relation to NWOM. It also examined the relationships among customer attribution, organizational reputation, and NWOM. The results indicate that response strategies and severity of failure affect customer attribution, and that these have negative relationships with organizational reputation and positive relationships with NWOM. Severity of failure is likewise found to be an element in<sup>fl</sup>uencing customer attribution in addition to response strategies, and the negative relationship between customer attribution and organizational reputation is veri<sup>fi</sup>ed. This study suggests that managers in service industries can adopt accommodative strategies and attempt to prevent severe failures so as to improve customer perceptions of organizational reputation, thereby reducing their desire to engage in NWOM.

## 1. Introduction

Online forums, blogs, of<sup>fi</sup>cial websites, and the bulletin board system (BBS) have become popular online platforms by which consumers exchange information, and thus spread word-of-mouth (WOM). Of these, online forums are widely used by consumers to learn the opinions or experiences of others, and provide a relatively easy way for people to obtain information [21], thus sharing what is known as electronic wordof-mouth (e-WOM). InsightXplorer [25] reported that approximately 90% of people think e-WOM is important to them, and it can thus be seen as a viable marketing strategy by which to attract new customers and to retain existing ones. Moreover, a number of researchers have recognized the growing importance of e-WOM in the e-business environment (e.g., [54]).

WOM consists of positive (PWOM) and negative word-of-mouth (NWOM), and this study is primarily concerned with the latter as it has received much less attention in the literature. In addition, Wangenheim [44] noted that customers are more sensitive to negative information than positive information when they are evaluating a company. Similarly, Chang and Wu [5] pointed out that negative information has a greater and diagnostic effect on consumer purchase decisions than is the case with positive information. Xia [53] claimed that NWOM can have a very strong effect, with, for instance, a video of a Comcast technician sleeping on a customer's couch having been viewed more than 1 million times since it was posted online 2006, damaging the company's reputation for customer service. However, Qiu et al. [37] noted that the authenticity of online reviews may often be doubtful, thus making the issue of credibility a critical determinant as to whether or not the review will be accepted or rejected by readers. In addition, company response strategies have become another critical issue determining whether customers accept or reject NWOM. This is because a suitable and honest response strategy can gain customer trust and help repair a damaged reputation [53]. Response strategies are often based on studies of crisis management in order to enhance customer evaluations and organizational reputation [28]. Lee and Song [31] tested the effects of response strategies on consumer attributions and evaluations of companies in a study of online complaint behavior. Following on from this line of study, the question arises as to whether will consumers accept NWOM from an online forum after learning about the experiences of others with company response strategies?

The literature on NWOM has mainly focused on two issues: motivations and reasons for customer engagement in NWOM (e.g., [21,45,51]), describing the process by which a person becomes a complainer (e.g., [15,17]). The studies referred to above are all concerned with investigating the antecedents of NWOM from an individual perspective, which starts with customer cognitions and feelings (see Appendix A). However, Gregoire et al. [18] and Hennig-Thurau et al. [21] mentioned that there have been few studies that examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the interaction between consumers and companies on NWOM. Therefore, this study is designed to investigate the role of response strategies in NWOM.

Prior studies linking response strategies and NWOM have been few in number because such strategies are mostly studied in a service recovery context, rather than in the domain of NWOM, and they are often applied to every customer reaction [55]. Therefore, this study attempts to contribute to the NWOM literature by studying the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of response strategies on NWOM. On the other hand, while customers may be unlikely to go through a terrible consumption experience, it is very easy for them to read other customers' complaints on the Internet, and thus the behavioral intentions of people who read online reviews should be studied. Moreover, based on Lee and Song [31], this study goes further toward understanding whether the response strategies contained in complaint messages will affect customer attribution and NWOM. Based on studying this gap in current research, we hope to provide practitioners with a greater understanding with regard to choosing the right response strategies to reduce customer willingness to engage in NWOM. This work has a number of differences compared to Lee and Song [31], as explained below:

(1) This study incorporates severity of failure into the research model, and thus examines its in<sup>fl</sup>uence. The severity of a failure is often mentioned in complaint messages, and is also an important factor in<sup>fl</sup>uencing customer evaluations and their related emotions. Other factors are also considered when customers make a judgment about a complaint message, such as the quality of past service performance and the credibility of the complainers. However, this study does not focus on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c service or product, and no speci<sup>fi</sup>c company is mentioned either. The respondents thus cannot judge an incident from the past performance of a company they are familiar with, but can instead only see a complaint message in our study, so it is hard for them to judge whether the complainer is credible or not.

(2) Second, Lee and Song [31] examined the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of response strategies on attributions of failure and organizational reputation. The hypothesis of the current study is that the relationship between customer attribution and organizational reputation is based on attribution perceptions. Attribution theory is thus used to examine the relationship between a problem or failure and the subsequent outcomes, based on factors such as emotions, attitudes and behavior [48]. Therefore, this study aims to verify the relationship between attribution and organizational reputation.

(3) Lee and Song [31] only tested the effect of response strategies on customer attribution toward companies, and focused on the attribution of locus, which is just a part of attribution theory. In contrast, this study incorporates more constructs of attribution perceptions into its research model.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypothesis development

## 2.1. Negative word-of-mouth

Spreading negative WOM is an active customer reaction to inform others about a dissatisfactory experience; as such, it is not directed toward the company, but instead toward other individuals either within the buying <sup>fi</sup>rm or third-party recipients [12]. Lim and Chung [33] mentioned that NWOM is more effective in communicating information about credence attributes, because it is more congruent with regard to credence belief communication. In making a comparison with positive WOM (PWOM), Luo [34] pointed out that this contains favorable experiences with particular products, and buying recommendations for these are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial from the company perspective, while NWOM reports disappointing experiences about certain products, and thus can be harmful to companies.

Wangenheim [44] indicated that unhappy customers might spread NWOM about <sup>fi</sup>rms or products, and thus keep potential new customers from patronizing <sup>fi</sup>rms or increase the defection rates of current customers. According to Lim and Chung [33], negative information is commonly believed to have a greater in<sup>fl</sup>uence on decision makers, to be more credible and diagnostic, and to have stronger effects on consumer brand evaluations than PWOM. In a statistical report highlighted by the consulting company Oxford Metrica, it was found that companies now have an 82% chance of facing a corporate disaster within any <sup>fi</sup>ve-year period, compared to only 20% two decades ago. This is all as a result of the Internet and social media, which allow bad news to spread rapidly [53].

Prior studies have researched the motivational factors related to engaging in NWOM. For instance, Ward and Ostrom [45] referred to betrayal as a mechanism explaining the motivation underlying online NWOM. As for the goals of NWOM, different speci<sup>fi</sup>c negative emotions will entail different speci<sup>fi</sup>c goals related to engaging in NWOM [51]. Gregoire and Fisher [17] provided a model to explain why a loyal customer would take extreme actions to hurt a <sup>fi</sup>rm, and they found that customer-perceived betrayal was an important element that triggers NWOM. Gregoire et al. [18] developed a comprehensive model of customer revenge, and showed that customer desire for this to be a key driver of NWOM. Accordingly, the research outlined above describes various features of NWOM. However, most of these studies have looked at NWOM from an individual perspective, and thus neglected the interactions that may occur with companies. This is the reason why this study is an attempt to examine the relationship between response strategies and NWOM.

## 2.2. Response strategies

Response strategies are how a company responds to the complaints made by dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed customers about a service or product failure, and various strategies have been examined in different studies. Marcus and Goodman [35] classi<sup>fi</sup>ed response strategies into either accommodative or defensives strategies. However, Lee and Song [31] proposed three response strategies against NWOM: accommodative, no-action and defensive strategies. Accommodative strategies refer to corporate acknowledgment and acceptance of the existence of problems, as well as the action to be taken, including apologies, compensation and/or recovery actions [31,35]. No-action strategies refer to company attempts to keep away from problems by remaining silent, making meaningless comments or engaging in futile or short-sighted reactions [28]. Defensive strategies are developed through methods such as insisting there is no problem, claiming that the company has no responsibility for a problem, accusing the complainer, and even shifting the blame to others [31,35].

This study focuses on accommodative and defensive strategies. According to Lee and Song [31], defensive- and no-action strategies obtained similar means (M = 1.75 and 1.87, respectively) in a company evaluation, which reveals that non-action is similar to a defensive strategy. Another study [29] also pointed out that non-action to a complaint is the least effective strategy for preventing negative attitudes toward an organization. Therefore, we exclude the no-action strategy and consider it to be a kind of defensive strategy in this work.

Different response strategies will affect consumer evaluations of a <sup>fi</sup>rm. Lee [28] suggested that crisis responses will induce judgments related to impressions, sympathy and trust in a company. Recently, in online complaint behavior research, Lee and Song [31] pointed out that accommodative strategies are positively related to the reputation of a <sup>fi</sup>rm, while defensive strategies have a negative relationship with it. Lee and Cranage [29] proposed that organizational response strategies have a moderating effect on the relationship between NWOM consensus and external causal attribution and attitudinal change. Nevertheless, to date no studies have examined the relationship between response strategies and NWOM, and it is this gap in the literature that the current work addresses.

## 2.3. Severity of failure

The severity of failure is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the magnitude of loss that customers feel from a failure [22], and this can be either tangible or intangible. A tangible failure represents losses in monetary or service/ product value. On the other hand, an intangible failure includes anger, inconvenience, or time [39].

Severity of a failure is an individual perception. Bhandari et al. [2] mentioned that different service failures (graded according to the degree of severity) can provide service <sup>fi</sup>rms with insights into the determinants and consequences of customer responses. Previous studies on customer responses to service failures have shown that more severe service failures result in lower levels of customer satisfaction. Dunning et al. [11] found that a greater severity of failure increased the extent of emotional reactions, including upset, despair, frustration, and anger. Swanson and Hsu [40] indicated that the more serious the failure is perceived to be, the more likely the customer is to switch service providers and engage in NWOM. Accordingly, service failure has been discussed extensively with regard to its in<sup>fl</sup>uences on customers' perception and reaction. Therefore, this study incorporates it into the model in order to assess its effect on customer perceptions.

## 2.4. Attribution theory

Attribution theory deals with how people interpret incidents or behaviors in terms of their causal inferences, and their interpretations play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in determining reactions to these incidents or behaviors [26]. It has been used as foundation of inference for consumer behavior and product success or failure, and researchers have proposed different constructs when utilizing it (e.g., [26]). Weiner [47] classi<sup>fi</sup>ed causes into three constructs: attribution of locus, attribution of controllability and attribution of stability, which is the most common category of attributions adopted when investigating product or service failures.

Attribution of locus is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which the cause of a service failure is located in either the service provider or the customer [3,36]. For instance, a customer bought a set of new bookshelves from IKEA, but it then collapsed one day after assembly. If the consumer thought that he did not assemble it correctly, he would make a customer-related attribution. If, on the other hand, it occurred due to a defect in the bookshelves, the attribution would be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the manufacturer's responsibility [13]. Attribution of locus is related to consumers' desire to communicate about their bad experiences. When customer dissatisfaction arises from a seller or manufacturer rather than the customer, the customer tends to complain to the seller or manufacturer and engage in NWOM about the failure.

Attribution of controllability is the extent to which customers perceive the cause of failure as volitional by the service provider [3, 23]. Although environmental constraints on service providers may force a failure, sometimes they have opportunities to prevent failures from occurring. For example, <sup>fl</sup>ight delays during a blinding snowstorm are not controllable by the airline; however, a delay due to sluggish boarding procedures is controllable [48]. When customers <sup>fi</sup>nd out that a failure could have been avoided, they will become angry and be less willing to make a repeat purchase. What is more, the failure could provoke retaliatory behavior intended to hurt the company's business [14].

Attribution of stability means the extent to which causes are viewed as temporary (<sup>fl</sup>uctuating over time) or permanent [3]. When customers attribute causes of failure to stable reasons, they expect the problem will happen repeatedly and will warn their friends and acquaintances not to patronize the seller or manufacturer [8]. Browning et al. [3] stated that a customer is more likely to be dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with a service if the failure is attributed to a stable cause, such as ongoing and previous service failures.

Several researchers have examined the types of attributions leading to complaint intentions, complaint behavior, and the valence of WOM intentions and behavior (e.g., [29,31,40]). Anchored in attribution theory, the presence of a con<sup>fl</sup>icting aggregated rating decreases consumers' product-related attributions of a review, which in turn, negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uences their perceptions of review credibility and diagnosticity [37]. The transmission of NWOM involves both interpersonal and informal processes. Therefore, attribution theory appears to be particularly helpful with regard to understanding a receiver's interpretation of a writer's motives for sharing a message [5].

This study incorporates attribution of locus and attribution of controllability into the research model to investigate how corporate response strategies contained in other consumers' complaint messages in<sup>fl</sup>uence customer evaluations and NWOM intentions. There are two reasons why we only adopt attribution of locus and controllability. First of all, under situations of suf<sup>fi</sup>cient information, customers make inferences about the causes of events according to three constructs: attribution of locus (who is related to this occurrence?), attribution of controllability (was the failure controllable or uncontrollable?), and attribution of stability (does this happen frequently or temporarily?). However, in the context of this study, we create a research condition and assume that customers have accidently read a complaint message when sur<sup>fi</sup>ng the Internet. Therefore, it is not easy for them to tell whether the service failure is consistently unstable by reading a single complaint message unless they have read more of the same messages online from time to time. Furthermore, Swanson and Hsu [40] mentioned that customers are usually more emotional and care about whoever the <sup>fi</sup>rm or customer determines is to blame for a failure, as compared to a case of routine or <sup>fi</sup>rst time service failure. If customers attribute a failure to the <sup>fi</sup>rm and view it as controllable, more negative emotions (disappointment, regret, anger and sadness) will be aroused. This is expected to lead to increased dissatisfaction and generate NWOM; however, unstable service has been shown to result in low repurchase intention [40]. Therefore, we restrict our focus to attribution of locus and attribution of controllability.

## 2.5. Organizational reputation

A <sup>fi</sup>rm's reputation is an overall assessment of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's product and service expertise, social character, customer experiences, and credible communications about the <sup>fi</sup>rm's ability to serve customers [32]. According to Lee and Park [30], it is the evaluation of reputation based on the stakeholder's direct experiences with a company, as well as any form of communication and symbolism that provides information about the organization's actions. Organizational reputation is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as how an organization is perceived by customers who receive informational signals from companies, the media, and other people [31] that form their beliefs, attitudes, and impressions about the company. It has been shown to have a positive relation with some important customer-outcome variables, such as customer satisfaction, loyalty, trust and WOM [42].

In the marketing and management literature, organizational reputation is discussed primarily with regard to people who have a relationship with a company [42]. In the crisis communication <sup>fi</sup>eld, Coombs and Holladay [7] investigated how response conditions and crisis responsibility affects organizational reputation. Lee and Song [31] later studied the relationship between response strategies and organizational reputation in the online complaint context. Related studies have focused on reputation as it relates to measuring consumer perceptions of trustworthiness and the degree of good will toward information sources, and it has also been related to the interests of a third party (e.g., [7,31]). On this basis, this study further links organizational reputation to NWOM, and we attempt to <sup>fi</sup>nd better response strategies to protect organizational reputation and explore whether consumer evaluations of a company will give rise to NWOM.

## 2.6. Conceptual framework and hypotheses development

This study extends Lee and Song's [31] research model, which studied how response strategies affect the target of attribution and changes in evaluations, in order to examine whether people will engage in NWOM after seeing online complaint messages. The conceptual framework is shown in Fig. 1. All variable de<sup>fi</sup>nitions are adopted from previous studies, and detailed descriptions are provided in Appendix B.

## 2.6.1. Response strategies, attribution of locus and controllability

Response strategies to complainers may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the attribution of a service failure. Consumers who read complaint messages will infer the attributions of locus and controllability of the related failure based on the response strategies of the organization, including both accommodative and defensive strategies. An accommodative strategy means that a company admits the existence of a problem and tries to make amends with actions such as apologies, compensation and recovery actions, whereas defensive strategy means that a company denies the problem or fails to assume responsibility for the service failure.

When companies receive a complaint from a customer the use of an accommodative strategy can enable them to solve problems better and produce better outcomes, such as restoring the company's positive image and increasing consumer trust [27]. For instance, in December 1998 Sara Lee Corporation voluntarily recalled packaged meat products with a total value of US\$50–US\$70 million before of<sup>fi</sup>cial test results were announced that linked a recent listeriosis outbreak with Bil Mar Foods [38]. This response strategy not only immediately minimized the risk of infection, but also signaled to the public that Sara Lee takes the health and safety of its customers seriously, thus enhancing its reputation. This case indicates that when a company responds by using an accommodative strategy, consumers might try to <sup>fi</sup>nd some reasons to forgive the service providers and ascribe the failure to external and uncontrollable causes.

In contrast, a defensive strategy seems to be more likely to aggravate the problem and induce negative inferences about service providers. Lee [27] showed that denial of responsibility for negative events will elicit people's anger, make them aggressive and trigger negative perceptions of a company. For instance, in the corporate scandal that hit AWB Limited in 2006 [16], the company initially tried to deny that there was any illegal or unethical activity involving the company under the United Nations (UN) program, and even attempted to shift the blame onto others and implicate the UN and Australian Government. This strategy not only broke the con<sup>fi</sup>dence of stakeholders, but also seriously damaged the reputation of the company. Lee and Song [31] found that when a defensive response strategy is applied then customers will be inclined to infer that the company should take more responsibility for the causes of the focal problem. Therefore, adopting defensive strategies as a response to incidents will result in consumer anger and make them more likely to feel that the company is guilty. People will see such responses as an excuse, and regard the failure as an internal and controllable problem that the service providers ought to have prevented from happening in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place.

Some inferences on the relationship between response strategies and attribution perceptions, as discussed above, are from a <sup>fi</sup>rst person perspective rather than a third person one, because there are few studies investigating the latter. Even though the inferences of such relationships are based on some works that investigated then the <sup>fi</sup>rst person perspective, we still believe they are reliable. Because attribution theory is focused on universal concerns about the origin of an event or a problem [48], people can make causal explanations for a failure or problem regardless of whether they go through such dif<sup>fi</sup>culties themselves. In addition, we believe that when customers try to attribute a failure or problem that others have experienced, they put themselves in the place of the person who was involved in the situation. Therefore, inferences based on the literature written from the <sup>fi</sup>rst person perspective are considered to be reasonable and credible. Therefore, this study presents the following hypotheses:

H1a. Response strategies in<sup>fl</sup>uence the attribution of locus. The use of defensive strategies increases the attribution of locus to a greater extent than the use of accommodative strategies.

H1b. Response strategies in<sup>fl</sup>uence the attribution of controllability. The use of defensive strategies increases the attribution of controllability to a greater extent than the use of accommodative strategies.

## 2.6.2. Severity of failure and attribution of locus and controllability

Weun et al. [52] stated that the severity of failure in<sup>fl</sup>uences the evaluation of a service provider after a service failure, and that it is an important component affecting consumer emotions and behavior. Severity of failure refers to the customer's perceived intensity of loss from a failure, including both tangible and intangible losses [22]. Therefore, customers will increase the attribution of locus under severe failure conditions. In contrast, if the focal product or service failure is trivial, consumers may not ask themselves why the failure occurred, and not blame the service provider so harshly.

For instance, in the case of the Dell laptop battery explosions that happened in 2006, when Dell responded to customers who posted videos of battery defects with threats of legal action, customer resentment did not subside until the company apologized [53]. Although online viewers heavily criticized Dell's slow response to the incident, the company still did not take any immediate of<sup>fi</sup>cial action [19]. This can be seen as a serious problem that reveals that the company negligent in controlling the processes and quality related to its products. However, if Dell took action actively and responded to the online comments in a polite manner, people would have been more likely to think these were minor problems and this ascribes less blame to the company. Therefore, in the context of a customer complaint, an excuse does not minimize the severity of the problem, and customers may even be more committed in attributing the problem to the company.

In contrast, if a customer thinks the problem is minor and beyond the company's control, then the degree of attribute of locus will be lower.

Customers expect service providers to take more action with regard to severe failures compared to minor ones. Because the problems that customers perceive as severe are usually both clear and unacceptable, customers tend to believe that service providers should not overlook them in the production process. For example, in 2007 Toyota recalled its cars and trucks due to the risk of accelerator pedals becoming stuck and causing accidents [20]. Customers would think that this was quite a serious problem, because cars not only cost a lot of money, but such failures could lead to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant injuries or even deaths. This study thus infers that severity of failure will have a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the attribution of controllability, as stated in the following hypotheses:

H2a. Severity of failure in<sup>fl</sup>uences the attribution of locus. The attribution of locus is greater in a severe-failure condition, compared to a minor-failure condition.

H2b. Severity of failure in<sup>fl</sup>uences the attribution of controllability. The attribution of controllability is greater in a severe-failure condition, compared to a minor-failure condition

2.6.3. Attribution of locus and controllability and organizational reputation

This study applies the concepts of attribution of locus and controllability [3,40,49] to investigate their relationship with organizational reputation. DeCarlo and Leigh [9] assumed the relationship between attribution and evaluation to be consistent with a cognitive processing model of performance evaluation, and directly linked the performance attribution of an employee to his or her evaluation. In the Salmonella scare that happened at Cadbury's in the UK, the company had since 2002 failed to take appropriate action on product recalls, and this resulted in a Salmonella outbreak at the <sup>fi</sup>rm's Marlbrook plant, in Herefordshire, in 2006 [4]. This incident seriously harmed customer con<sup>fi</sup>dence in food safety, as well as the brand's reputation. This is because customers would think the incident is controllable, and thus attribute it to internal causes internally (e.g., poor SOP). Therefore, this study posits that the attribution of locus and attribution of controllability both have effects on organizational reputation.

In sum, service providers will be blamed when they fail to assume responsibility for the occurrence of a problem or when their ability to prevent an incident from occurring does not meet customer expectations. Therefore, this study hypothesizes the following:

H3. Attribution of locus is negatively related to organizational reputation.

H4. Attribution of controllability is negatively related to organizational reputation.

2.6.4. Attribution of locus and controllability and negative word-of-mouth

Hess [24], Lee and Cranage [29], and Swanson and Hsu [40] studied the effects of attribution on NWOM. Nevertheless, Lee and Song [31] did not investigate whether potential consumers, after reading a complaint message written in response to a certain response strategy, would ascribe the causes of service failure and take action using NWOM. Hess [24] indicated that customer attribution of a causal relationship is related to several important affective and behavioral outcomes, such as satisfaction, repurchase intention, and NWOM. Referring back to the case of Dell, its responses likely increased the level of anger and dissatisfaction, and ultimately resulted in more NWOM until the company apologized. The response strategy adopted by AWB Limited was even worse, as the company not only denied involvement in illegal or unethical activities, but also shifted the blame to the government, which was very likely to increase the negative feelings of customers and generate NWOM. As seen in these incidents, if consumers feel that an incident is controllable and caused by the company internally, more NWOM will be generated.

According to Verhagen et al. [41], consumers use NWOM for three reasons: 1) to draw attention to the cause of their dissatisfaction in order to get a solution, 2) to disclose unfavorable experiences to prevent others from going through similar bad experiences, and 3) to vent their feelings about a bad experience openly, as a way to encourage the company to improve its practices. Therefore, when customers attribute a service failure to the service providers, and the problems are perceived as controllable, they will have a greater propensity to engage in NWOM and tell others what they have gone through. Based on this, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H5. Attribution of locus is positively related to NWOM.

H6. Attribution of controllability is positively related to NWOM.

2.6.5. Organizational reputation and negative word-of-mouth

Hess [24] indicated that a <sup>fi</sup>rm's reputation will be directly related to both NWOM and organizational reputation outcomes after controlling for the effects of satisfaction. Customers who perceive a company to have a good reputation will be expected to be more willing to engage in PWOM than those who do not perceive it in this manner [43]. Referring to the Toyota example, although customers thought that the manufacturer should control its production process to prevent the problem, the approach that the company took to respond to the failure was generally considered acceptable. Therefore, customers may still feel that Toyota has a good reputation and so engage less in NWOM. With regard to the Dell example, on the other hand, the company responded defensively and aroused customers' negative emotions, thus harming its reputation. Consequently, customers could be expected to engage in more NWOM to vent their negative feelings openly in order to encourage the company to improve its practices [41].

A negative company reputation should thus be associated with more NWOM, because Anderson [1] suggested that customers like to talk about their experiences concerning service providers, especially when these are either extremely good or bad. Additionally, customer's NWOM behavior will tend to be initiated because of their desire to help others with their buying decisions, and their desire to save others from negative experiences [21]. Similarly, Walsh and Beatty [42] showed that <sup>fi</sup>rms with better reputations will receive PWOM. Therefore, the <sup>fi</sup>nal hypothesis is as follows:

H7. Organizational reputation is negatively related to negative WOM

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Experimental design and scenario development

To investigate the relationships examined in our model, participants were randomly assigned to one of four experimental conditions in a 2 (severity of failure) × 2 (response strategies) between-subjects design. The scenario for each condition described a service failure, delivering a secondhand product or a product with damaged wrapping paper from an online shopping mall, as well as a response strategy from the company after the consumer complained. The two manipulated variables were severity of failure (severe vs. minor) and response strategies (accommodative vs. defensive), and the measured variables were attribution of locus, attribution of controllability, organizational reputation and NWOM.

To develop the scenarios, this study referred to those used in past research [31], and a currently operating online forum (Mobile01) was used to gather some materials to develop these. In Lee and Song [31], an online complaint message depicted a service failure in which a secondhand espresso–cappuccino maker was delivered to a consumer. However, an espresso–cappuccino maker is not so common in daily life, and thus this may not have been an appropriate stimulus. We thus selected a digital camera as our stimulus, due to the popularity of these, as well as the frequency with which shoppers for such items search online for information. For example, a survey from InsightXplorer [25] showed that almost eighty <sup>fi</sup>ve percent of people believe the WOM from online forums and websites, and 3C products are the most popular category about which people seek suggestions from others before making a purchase decision.

In order to make it more likely that the respondents were familiar with the experience described in the scenarios, this study adopted actual incidents regarding the purchase of a camera from Mobile01, which is a popular online forum in Taiwan where many share their opinions and experiences, with an average of approximately three million daily visitors. Appendix C shows details of the manipulation of the severity of failure and response strategies.

## 3.2. Measurement development

The questionnaire for this study consisted of four constructs: attribution of locus, attribution of controllability, organizational reputation and NWOM. All scales were adopted from prior studies. For instance, attributions of locus and controllability were each measured with three-item scales adopted from Poon et al. [36] and Hess et al. [23], respectively. To measure organizational reputation, we adopted the <sup>fi</sup>ve-item scale from Coombs and Holladay [7], and added one reverse item to check the validity of the responses. The NWOM items were adapted from three items proposed by Gregoire et al. [18]. Furthermore, the items for manipulation checking and severity of failure were measured using a <sup>fi</sup>ve-item scale adapted from Hess et al. [22], and, based on Lee and Song [31], there was no manipulation check for the response strategies.

Age, gender and experience with online shopping were included as control variables, with the latter measured using a three-item scale adopted from Gelbrich [15]. According to Gelbrich [15] and Gregoire et al. [18], experience with online shopping is more in<sup>fl</sup>uential than the experience of reading complaint messages in a NWOM context.

All items were assessed using a seven-point Likert scale, which ranged from 1 “strongly disagree” to 7 “strongly agree”. People who had experience of browsing an online discussion forum related to consumer purchases were eligible to participate in the study. However, to ensure that the participants were in the role of a reader instead of a complainer when reading the complaint message, the following item was included in the section on the respondent's demographic details: “What role do you think you are playing when answering the questionnaire” Other items in this section asked about the respondent's gender, age, education, occupation, average time using the Internet per day, average time doing online shopping per month, and average expenditure on online purchases each time. The survey was completed at the end of August of 2013.

Before conducting the pretest, 14 consumers were invited to examine the instrument to ensure that all items were readable, understandable and corresponded with the related conceptual de<sup>fi</sup>nitions in order to achieve content validity. Some modi<sup>fi</sup>cations were then made to certain items based on the replies and suggestions received at this time so that they could better <sup>fi</sup>t the research context. Therefore, based on the aims of this study, no items were developed to check the manipulation of the response strategies.

## 3.3. Pilot test

The <sup>fi</sup>rst pilot test was performed with a paper-based survey. The participants were randomly recruited and assigned to one of the four treatments, which was a 2 (severity of failure) × 2 (response strategies) between-subjects design. A total of 57 questionnaires were collected, and 15 were eliminated due to invalid responses for the reversed item or the item on the role the respondent identified with (with the role of reader being the correct response). As a result, 42 questionnaires were complete and considered valid for analysis.

Cronbach's alpha (N0.7) and item-to-total (N0.3) were assessed. The results demonstrated that item OR5, “I think the online shopping mall was not concerned with the well-being of its customers”, was initially set as a reversed item in relation to OR1, “I think the online shopping mall was concerned with the rights and interests of its customers.” However, the item-to-total correlation of OR5 was .935, and the Cronbach's α for organizational reputation was relatively high, which revealed that OR5 was a very good reverse item to measure organizational reputation, and thus it was included as a measurement item. OR6 was thus created as follows: “I think the online shopping mall was not concerned with the rights and interests of its customers”.

To ensure the manipulation of severity of failure, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was adopted to assess the manipulation between the two conditions (severe and minor). The results suggested that the severity of failure manipulation was inappropriate, because the measures yielded insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant differences, $F = 0 . 2 3 3 , p = . 6 3 2 ,$ , for the severity of failure $( M = 5 . 7 6 2 , S D = 0 . 9 4 6$ for the severe condition; M = 5.627, SD = 0.842 for the minor condition). Therefore, we changed the delivered item from an unwanted brand-new product to an item with damaged wrapping paper in the minor severity condition. A second pilot test was then conducted.

The second pilot test was conducted by using an online questionnaire and following the same process as the <sup>fi</sup>rst pilot test. Sixty-<sup>fi</sup>ve questionnaires were retrieved, and 18 were invalid due to not passing the reversed or role identi<sup>fi</sup>cation items. A total of 47 were thus determined to be valid and used in the subsequent analysis. The results demonstrated that all the item-to-total correlations were above the threshold of 0.3, except that for OR2. However, the <sup>fi</sup>rst pilot test showed that OR2 had a high item-to-total (.712), and thus it was retained in the formal questionnaire. The Cronbach's α for all of the constructs was greater than 0.7, indicating that all the measures had high internal consistency. The manipulation check on the severity of failure in the second pilot test demonstrated a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between groups $( F = 1 6 . 6 9 7 , p = . 0 0 0 )$ . The results showed a greater mean value in the severe condition $( M = 5 . 9 9 9 , S D = 1 . 0 8 9 )$ than in the minor condition $( M = 4 . 4 4 2 7 , S D = 1 . 3 7 4 )$ . The manipulation of severity of failure was thus valid in this study, but we still made slight revisions to the wording of the minor condition scenario, as its mean was still greater than 3.5.

It was decided to eliminate the no-action strategy situation in the response strategies. However, in the pilot test, the no-action strategies were considered to be a kind of defensive strategy. This is similar to the results in Lee and Song [31], in which no-action and defensive strategies had similar means. We thus concluded that no-action strategies did not exist in the context of this study.

## 4. Results of data analysis

The formal survey was conducted from the end of July to the end of August in 2013, using a paper-based questionnaire. Participants were randomly recruited from a university in Taiwan and included both graduate and postgraduate students who had experience of browsing online discussion forums related to consumer purchases. One of the four treatments, which was a 2 (severity of failure) × 2 (response strategies) between-subjects design, was randomly assigned to the eligible participants. The questionnaire began with (1) an explanation of the aims of the study, (2) a statement reminding the participants that they were a reader of the online complaint message, rather than a complainer; (3) a complaint message was shown to the participants, and they were asked to read it carefully and asked to assume that they were seeing it on an online forum; and (4) they were then asked to answer the questions that followed. The scenario was presented in an online forum layout, which imitated the style of an existing website so as to make it more realistic. A detailed description of the scenario is provided in Appendix C.

According to the E-ICP data base 2012–2013, most people who visit online forum are aged between 20 and 29. It was thus assumed that university, graduate and postgraduate students, whose ages mostly ranged between 21 and 30 years old, could be a representative sample for use in this study. We collected 320 respondents, and after deleting the invalid data there were a total of 282 valid questionnaires. With regard to the demographic characteristics, there were 157 female respondents (56%) and 125 males (44%), and most were aged 21–30 (72%), with nearly all having a bachelor's degree or higher (98%). Seventy-four percent of the participants spent more than one hour per day online Internet, 48% of the them had made at least <sup>fi</sup>ve online shopping transactions within the previous three years, and 35% stated that they spent an average of US\$31–100 on each purchase. ## 5. Discussion and conclusions

## 5.1. Summary of findings and contributions

This study proposed a research model based on attribution theory and posited that response strategies and severity of failure affect customer attribution of locus and attribution of controllability, which then might affect organizational reputation and NWOM, and that, <sup>fi</sup>nally, organizational reputation would in<sup>fl</sup>uence NWOM. We endeavored to go further than prior studies (e.g., [31]) to explore the effects of response strategies and severity of failure on attribution of locus, attribution of controllability, organizational reputation and NWOM. A discussion of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and conclusions is presented in the following sections.

5.1.1. Response strategies, severity of failure and attribution of locus and controllability

With regard to attribution of locus, H1a was supported in this. This is consistent with Lee and Song [31], who indicated that adopting appropriate response strategies (i.e., accommodative strategies) could reduce customer perceptions of company responsibility for negative events. In contrast to accommodative strategies, defensive strategies make customers feel that service providers are at fault, and should be held responsible for negative incidents (i.e., there is a higher attribution of locus), because denial of responsibility provokes customer anger, triggers negative perceptions of the company, and makes customers <sup>fi</sup>nd it hard to believe that the service providers are innocent [27]. However, accommodative strategies help to restore a company's positive image, and provide a good reason for customers to feel sympathy for the <sup>fi</sup>rm and thus forgive it for any failures [50]. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that different response strategies lead to different degrees of attribution of locus, and that customers will tend to ascribe the responsibility for service failures to service providers when the latter adopt defensive strategies.

The results of this study also support previous research related to H2a [52]. According to our hypothesis, when customers experience a severe service failure, and thus feel a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant loss, they will be more determined to ascribe the cause to the service providers. In contrast, when failures are trivial, customers will not spend much time or effort inquiring into the causes [13,14], will be less inclined to think the service providers are responsible for such incidents, and thus ascribe less blame to them. This suggests that the more serious the customers perceive a failure to be, the higher the resulting attribution of locus.

The hypothesized effects of attribution of controllability on response strategies (H1b) and severity of failure (H2b) were not supported. This study proposed H1b based on an extension of Lee and Song [31], which only focused on the attribution of locus and said nothing about the attribution of controllability. It was assumed in this study that accommodative strategies encourage customers to attribute failures to uncontrollable causes, whereas defensive strategies make customers believe that failures are controllable and should have been prevented. To the best of our knowledge, we are the <sup>fi</sup>rst to incorporate the concept of attribution of controllability on response strategies, but its relationship was still unclear in the results.

Severity of failure did not cause any differences with regard to attribution of controllability in this study. Weun et al. [52] suggested that severity of failure is an important element in evaluating a service provider after a service failure. Hess et al. [23] demonstrated that, in an interactional service failure context, severity of failure positively in<sup>fl</sup>uenced attribution of controllability in Study 1, which involved different degrees of inattentiveness during check-in for an airline <sup>fl</sup>ight, but this was not the case in Study 2, which involved a hotel employee with an indifferent, unhelpful, and rude attitude. The results in Hess et al. [23] provided mixed support for their hypotheses. Therefore, the relationship between severity of failure and attribution of controllability might not be so clear, and may need to be investigated more carefully. In addition, another possibility for the insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between severity of failure and attribution of controllability found in this study might be the manipulation of the severity of failure, as explained below.

Because the means of both severe and minor conditions of severity of failure were greater than 3.5, this meant that the respondents perceived both conditions as severe. On the other hand, while the attribution of controllability was unclear, it was much easier for the respondents to decide who should be responsible for a problem.

5.1.2. Attribution of locus and controllability, organizational reputation, and NWOM

The results demonstrated that attribution of locus is negatively related to organizational reputation (H3), which is consistent with DeNisi et al. [10] and DeCarlo and Leigh [9] in the cognitive processing model of performance evaluation, which suggests that people's attributions will in<sup>fl</sup>uence their evaluations. That is, when customers regard the cause of a failure to be service provider-related, they will think that the provider is responsible for the incident, and thus give a negative appraisal.

The attribution of locus was found to have a positive effect on NWOM (H5). Previous studies have mainly investigated this relationship based on subjective experience (e.g., [8,13]), while this study examines it from a customer perspective. As predicted, the greater the degree to which customers see the problem as related to the service provider, the more likely they will tell others about what they saw from the complainer's perspective, even though they did not experience the failure personally.

The results of this study showed that customer evaluations of organizational reputation will negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uence their desire to engage in NWOM (H7). Organizational reputation is an alternative index by which to evaluate the service quality of a service provider [42], and, therefore, when customers perceive such companies to have a lower reputation, they will infer bad service quality from this service provider and want to take action using NWOM, so as to help others make better purchase decisions [21]. Walsh and Beatty [42] showed that reputation will in<sup>fl</sup>uence PWOM. However, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study suggest that when customers perceive a service provider to have a poor reputation, they will more willing to harm the company by spreading NWOM.

The results showed that H4 was not supported, and that the attribution of controllability is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and positively related to organizational reputation, contrary to the proposed hypothesis. This study thus further examined the correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient between the attribution of controllability and organizational reputation. The bi-variate Pearson correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient between these two constructs was −.127, indicating a negative relationship. Based on the correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient, the nature of the relationship and the direction of in<sup>fl</sup>uence conform to our hypothesis, which means that the attribution of controllability is negatively related to organizational reputation. The correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>- cient of the attribution of locus and attribution of controllability (.404\*\*\*) was then checked, and this revealed that these two constructs are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly correlated with each other.

When we tested the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the attribution of locus and attribution of controllability on organizational reputation simultaneously, most of the latter could be explained by attribution of locus, and, at the same time, an inaccurate estimation of the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of attribution of controllability on organizational reputation was induced due to the close relationship between the attributions of and controllability. Therefore, the nature of the relationship between attribution of controllability and organizational reputation may be distorted due to the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of attribution of locus. This implies that the attribution of controllability is negatively related to organizational reputation. However, when customers consider attribution of locus and attribution of controllability at the same time, the latter does not affect organizational reputation. This means that attribution of locus may play an important role in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing organizational reputation in our research context, which is not found for the attribution of controllability.

In this study it revealed that the judgment of the controllability of a failure is positively related to the desire to engage in NWOM (H6), and this result was unexpected and inconsistent with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of Curren and Folkes [8], Folkes [13], and Folkes et al. [14]. However, there might be several reasons for this outcome. According to our analytical results, response strategies and severity of failure do not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect the perception of attribution of controllability. In earlier discussions in this study, it was supposed that there might be few clues by which customers can judge the extent of attribution of controllability. Therefore, the respondents did not have enough evidence to understand the extent of controllability of failure, and thus it was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for them to decide whether or not to spread NWOM based on the cases they examined, and thus the relationship between attribution of controllability and NWOM was not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant.

Finally, the relationship between attribution of controllability and NWOM was not supported, and this might be a result of the strong effect of attribution of locus. The results showed that without the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of attribution of locus, H3 was still not supported, and it was negatively related to organizational reputation, while H5 was marginally supported. This implies that when customers assess the attribution of locus and attribution of controllability at the same time, the former has a stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence on NWOM and also makes the role of attribution of controllability more ambiguous. This result is consistent with Folkes [13], which showed that attribution of locus and attribution of controllability both in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers' anger and desire to hurt a <sup>fi</sup>rm, and that the effect of attribution of locus on NWOM is stronger than that of attribution of controllability. This suggests that when considering the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of attribution of locus and attribution of controllability on NWOM simultaneously, the former plays a more important role in determine whether consumers will engage in NWOM.

To date, there have been few studies that investigate response strategies in the marketing <sup>fi</sup>eld, although a notable exception is Lee and Song [31]. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, there have been no studies that have linked response strategies to NWOM. This study thus contributes to a further understanding of the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of responses strategies and severity of failure on attribution of locus, attribution of controllability and, in turn, NWOM. Furthermore, the results of this study provide several <sup>fi</sup>ndings that add additional insights to prior studies, as follows.

(1) Lee and Song [31] showed that perceptions of attribution of locus will be affected by different response strategies. However, severity of failure is always a key element that in<sup>fl</sup>uences people's evaluations, emotions and behaviors in a service failure context [52], and this was not considered in Lee and Song [31]. This study was thus carried out to examine the effect of severity of failure on customer attribution, and the results support our argument that this severity is a key factor that in<sup>fl</sup>uences customer judgments. However, it is notable that severity of failure mainly affects the extent to which customers attribute failures to service providers, and that it does not provide enough evidence for customers to assess the extent to which they perceive attribution of controllability.

(2) Attribution of failure did not relate to organizational reputation in Lee and Song [31]. Different response strategies induce different perceptions of attribution toward companies and company evaluations, respectively. However, attribution theory can be applied to <sup>fi</sup>gure out what causes a problem or failure, and what in<sup>fl</sup>uences the subsequent outcomes of a problem, as seen in consumer emotions, attitudes and behaviors [14,49]. Based on attribution theory, it is posited in this study that it is rational to assume organizational reputation should be placed after attribution, rather than in a parallel position to it. Consequently, this study posited that there is a negative relationship between attribution and organizational reputation, and the results supported our hypotheses and were consistent with those of past studies (e.g., [9,10]).

(3) This study extends attribution theory in our model to investigate evaluation and behavioral intention. Lee and Song [31] explored the effects of response strategies on customer attribution, and focused on the cognitions that customers generate to infer a company's responsibility for a failure. In other words, their work only considered attribution of locus, without incorporating attribution of controllability. Therefore, this study adopted two constructs — attribution of locus and attribution of controllability. The results with regard to attribution of controllability may serve as a reference for practitioners to help them better understand this construct, and thus respond more appropriately to service failures.

(4) Lee and Song [31] studied how a company's response strategies affect the perceptions of customers, as well as the company's reputation, and only examined the effect of response strategies on customer cognition. Nevertheless, a question arises as to whether seeing a complaint message containing response strategies will arouse further behavioral intention (e.g. the spreading of NWOM), and a conceptual model was developed in this study to explain how response strategies affect customer attributions, evaluations of the organization and, in turn, NWOM, from a customer perspective. The results of this study have several practical implications for marketers, and propose avenues for future research. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings may help managers and academics to better understand what factors lead customers to spread NWOM.

## 5.2. Managerial implications

As the Internet becomes more pervasive in our daily lives, it provides people with a fast and convenient way to search for the opinions and usage experiences of others. Indeed, many people do this by visiting online forums about certain products and services, but few studies have investigated whether the corporate response strategies detailed in the complaint messages posted on such sites will in-<sup>fl</sup>uence customer intentions with regard to NWOM. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study thus have some implications for public relations or marketing managers.

The incident of laptop battery explosions at Dell is used as an example to explain the managerial implications of different response strategies, as shown in Table 5. This suggests that when a company receives complaints from its customers, it is more appropriate to adopt an accommodative response strategy, as this would result in a low attribution of locus and low attribution of controllability. Furthermore, a low attribution of locus enhances organizational reputation, and thus less NWOM is generated. In contrast, a defensive response strategy is not always a good approach, as it may not solve the problem but simply increase negative emotions, such as dissatisfaction and anger, which could generate even more NWOM, which can deter potential customers from patronizing the company, or increase the defection rates of existing ones [44], thus reducing pro<sup>fi</sup>ts.

To conclude, response strategies and severity of failure are critical elements in NWOM. When a company adopts accommodative strategies to take care of a complaint, such as offering apologies or compensation, then this can help to restore the <sup>fi</sup>rm's positive image and increase customer trust. In contrast, defensive strategies, such as denial or shifting of blame to others, will hurt companies, because they provoke customers and inspire negative views [27]. Such response strategies can even strengthen customers desire to spread NWOM. Public relations or marketing managers should thus think carefully before deciding to adopt a particular response strategy.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

There are some limitations to this work and issues that need to be addressed in future studies. First, the respondents in this study were all university students. Generalization of our results thus requires further work on more varied populations, such as individuals in full-time employment or housewives. Moreover, an online forum was used as our experimental background, and future works could conduct experiments using other media where people most post details of their negative consumption, such as blogs or BBS.

Second, this study used an image of a <sup>fi</sup>ctitious product and only presented a complaint message, without any replies from the company. Therefore, it might not have disclosed enough information to help the respondents assess the entire situation. Furthermore, this study eliminated the attribution of stability, because the participants were only assigned one complaint message to read in order to answer the survey, which may not have enabled them to determine the attribution of stability. Future research could adopt a real example from an online forum to establish the simulation scenario in order to include various opinions expressed in replies from the companies involved, or different messages concerning the same company, so as to investigate this issue more deeply. Future research may also incorporate other critical factors, such as the quality of past service performance, credibility of complainers, message consensus, and individual propensity to complain, in order to test whether providing respondents with more information could help them judge the attributions of controllability and stability.

Lastly, this study did not have a manipulation check for the response strategies. We adopted a manipulation variable, namely response strategies, following the work of Lee and Song [31], and they did not provide detailed information or explanations about the manipulation check of response strategies. Although we believe that there was an obvious difference between the response strategies, this was still a limitation of this study. However, future research could develop manipulation check items for response strategies so as to carefully manipulate them. Moreover, future studies could amend the minor condition failure in order to make the mean lower than 3.5, so as to enable the respondents to identify the difference between the two conditions more easily when they manipulate the severity of failure.
