# A standards war waged by a developing country: Understanding international standard setting from the actor-network perspective

Heejin Lee <sup>a,\*</sup>, Sangjo Oh <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Systems, University of Melbourne, Vic. 61-3-9326-2201, Australia <sup>b</sup> Department of Internet Business, Dongyang Technical College, Seoul, Republic of Korea

Received 29 June 2005; revised 17 July 2005; accepted 31 October 2005 Available online 31 January 2006

## Abstract

Standards have become a basis of global competition among countries. Although there are many studies of standards and standardization, little is known about how international standards are set. Even less is known about how it occurs in the unprecedented case in which a developing country is actively involved in this process. In the past few years, China, leveraging the huge size of its domestic markets, has attempted to influence international technology standard setting. Standardization, especially at the international level, often revolves around building an alliance surrounding a partic ular technology. Actor-network theory (ANT) is a theory that helps analyze the ways in which actor form alliances and enroll other actors to strengthen such alliances surrounding a technology. There fore, we see a fit between the study of standard setting and ANT. In this paper, we use ANT to inves tigate the process of mobile standard setting in an international context where firms, industry consortia, and governments collaborate and compete in complex ways. It is found that China’s attempt to set WAPI as a national standard failed in enrolling other actors mainly due to the fac that WAPI was too closed a standard even for a de jure one; China did not release the WAPI security algorithm to the scrutiny of the international community. - 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Standards; Standardization; Actor-network theory; China; WAPI; Wi-Fi; Standards war

## 1. Introduction

Standards have become increasingly important in many industries, especially in IT and telecommunications where competitive advantages are often gained through owning and/ or controlling a standard. Standards often form the basis of international competition among countries; global competition in technology is transformed into competition in technical standards. Standards have been used by multinationals of developed countries to secure their market control.

In this landscape of international standard setting, an unprecedented phenomenon has recently been observed. Leveraging its huge market size, China, a developing country, has been attempting to play a role in this game of standard setting; that is, to set its own technological standards – that are diferent from international standards – within its own territory and/or worldwide. These attempts include Enhanced Versatile Disc (EVD), Intelligent Grouping and Resources Sharing (IGRS), Audio, Video Coding Standard (AVS), and Time Division Synchronous Code Division Multiple Access (TD-SCDMA), standards (Suttmeier and Yao, 2004). Wireless LAN Authentication and Privacy Infrastructure (WAPI) is the latest example of China’s endeavor not to simply follow the standards formulated by multinationals of developed countries.

In this paper, we examine where the WAPI case originated and how it developed. As presented in a later section, there are many studies of standards and standardization, but there is little research on standard setting at the international level, particularly when a standard is proposed by a developing country. In such an international case, more players/stakeholders (or ‘‘actors’’, the term we use throughout) are involved and unfolding processes are more complex. The patterns of interactions and dynamics among them surrounding standard setting are a key aspect of the question we aim to explore in this paper. That is: how do they make alliances with each other and compete against each other? In this process, how does a standard succeed in becoming, or fail to become, a national or international one? To answer these questions, we use actor-network theory (ANT). ANT is a theory that helps analyze the ways in which actors form alliances and involve other actors – including non-human actors (i.e., technology) – to strengthen such alliances and to secure their interests through the use of the technology. Therefore, we see a fit between the study of standard setting and ANT. Through the lens of ANT, we will investigate the process of mobile standard setting in the international context where firms, industry consortia, and governments collaborate and compete in complex ways. The process of translation, a pillar of ANT, is used as a framework through which we can analyze and explain the process by which an international standard is set.

For this study we collected documentary data, mostly from media and some archival sources (McCulloch, 2004). Initially we searched the Internet for related articles in both English and Chinese. The Chinese sources were identified by a researcher<sup>1</sup> who is bilingual in English and Chinese. In this way, we were able to establish a story line of the WAPI dispute. We then collected data from the ABI/Inform database, searching for the two keywords, ‘China’ and ‘WAPI’. This search yielded 47 articles, the first of which appeared in December 2003 and the last in March 2005. Then we analyzed the ABI/Inform articles using the four stages of translation from ANT: problematization, interessement, enrollment, and mobilization (see the following section). While analyzing, we paid special attention to the distinction between facts and commentaries (and/or interpretations), the latter particularly from feature articles. The distinction was important because commentators and media themselves play a role of actor in the evolution of the event by representing, implicitly and explicitly, national interests.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The following section describes the WAPI case against its political, economic, and technical backgrounds. Section 3 reviews studies of standards/standardization and ANT, and it is shown that a fit exists between standardization and ANT. In Section 4, we analyze the WAPI dispute through the four stages of translation and discuss how WAPI was not successful in becoming a national standard. The concluding section presents contributions and limitations of the paper and suggests a direction for future research.

## 2. WAPI vs. Wi-Fi

In this section, we first describe where the WAPI case came from. Then we present its background from both the technical, and the political and economic viewpoints: the technical origin of WAPI, and China’s accession to WTO. These viewpoints illuminate China’s motivations and justifications for WAPI.

## 2.1. How the WAPI dispute evolved

In November 2003, the Chinese government announced that its own wireless security protocol, known as WAPI, would be put into efect from 1 December 2003. Then, the 802.11 Wi-Fi<sup>2</sup> standard, developed by the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers Inc. (IEEE), was an internationally commonly used protocol for wireless equipment (Lemon, 2004). However, as it was a known fact that Wi-Fi had security holes, as described later, China wanted to set up WAPI as a standard for domestic markets (Hoo, 2004; Kanellos, 2004). WAPI was not compatible with chips based on Wi-Fi (For dahl, 2004). Mandatory compliance with the WAPI standard was required for both domestically produced and imported equipment such as Centrino notebooks, PDAs, and other wireless devices (Suttmeier and Yao, 2004).

Details of the encryption algorithm were given to only 24 Chinese companies, some of which were potential competitors with foreign firms (Suttmeier and Yao, 2004). Western chipmakers were required to pay these Chinese companies per-chip royalties for WAPI and/or cooperate with them on development (Kanellos, 2004) if they wanted to market their products in China. For the foreign chipmakers, it meant that they might have to provide technical product specifications to potential competitors, which led to responses of dissatisfaction and resistance. These foreign companies might have to build local facilities if they want to participate in the market, as the WAPI technology cannot leave the country (Kanellos, 2004). Complying with the WAPI standard would increase their manufacturing costs as they have to make a special type of device for China and another type for the rest of the world (Reuters, 2004 as cited in Suttmeier and Yao, 2004). Some feared that China would push WAPI as a global standard.

More importantly, it appears to be a violation of the national treatment commitment under the TBT (Technical Barriers to Trade) provisions of WTO<sup>3</sup> (Suttmeier and Yao, 2004). Foreign companies, especially US chipmakers, went to their governments for intervention, and the incident developed to the extent that the US Secretary of State, Colin Powell, got involved. At last, the Chinese government conceded that the WAPI implementation was to be delayed indefinitely (Perkins Coie, 2004b). ## 2.2. The technical background of WAPI: security holes in Wi-Fi

WAPI was approved by the Standardization Administration of China (SAC) in May 2003 and came into efect on December 1 2003. From a technical point of view, WAPI is China’s response to security holes found in IEEE’s 802.11 wireless networking standard, or Wi-Fi. The security problems were acknowledged by IEEE and Wi-Fi Alliance. WAPI is a diferent security protocol from that used in the 802.11 series (a, b, and g). The latter is called Wired Equivalent Privacy (WEP). As the name implies, it is intended to make wireless networks as resistant to snooping and intrusion as wired networks. The standards make encryption with a 64-bit key a mandatory capability and 128-bit encryption an option (Geier, 2004). Almost all vendors provided both 64- and 128-bit WEP encryption in their subsequent products. However, it was soon found that WEP could be easily cracked by intercepting and analyzing a suficiently large amount of encrypted trafic. WEP can keep a low-trafic network quite secure, but it is unsuitable for high-trafic corporate wireless LANs, where an attacker can quickly collect enough packets to extract the keys. Since WEP was found to have security holes, separate eforts had been made by both IEEE and the Wi-Fi Alliance to fix the problem.

In October 2002, the Wi-Fi Alliance announced WPA (Wi-Fi Protected Access), replacing WEP. It was an interim response to the delayed development of IEEE’s new security protocol 802.11i. When WPA was introduced, a task group of IEEE was still in the middle of developing 802.11i. Related firms needed a more immediate solution, even if it had restricted functionalities. As the Wi-Fi Alliance had been working closely with IEEE, the interim standard, WPA, was designed for forward-compatibility to become a subset of 802.11i. WPA is a standard agreed upon by the members of the consortium. The long awaited wireless LAN security standard 802.11i was ratified in June 2004. IEEE 802.11i is an amendment to the 802.11 standard specifying security mechanisms for wireless networks. The Wi-Fi Alliance refers to the new standard (802.11i) as WPA2.

## 2.3. China’s accession to the WTO and standards

In the previous section, WAPI is described as a Chinese response to security holes in Wi-Fi. Here we ask whether it is only a technical response.

China joined the WTO in 2001. After China had joined the WTO, Chinese domestic industries faced more challenges from globalization and foreign competition (Suttmeier and Yao, 2004). Under the WTO principle of free international trade (World Trade Organisation, 2004), traditional instruments for trade protection such as tarifs and quota are limited by WTO obligations. Instead, standards emerge as an efective means to protect and promote national industry within the WTO framework (Suttmeier and Yao, 2004). China’s post-WTO technology policy therefore emphasized the importance of standards. For example, the Standardization Administration of China published a report on ‘‘China’s Technology Standards Strategy Development’’ (Standardization Administration of China, 2004) which set out China’s technology standards implementation plan from 2005 to 2020, among other issues. National technology standards emerged as an increasingly important item on China’s development and foreign relations agenda. Cromer (2005) argues that WAPI is one of many Chinese technical barriers to trade (TBT) in the high-tech sector.

In the study of national technological innovation systems, two policy directions are identified: techno-nationalism and techno-globalism (TN and TG hereafter) (Monstresor, 2001). The policy goal of TN is to promote national interests by preventing or at least limiting globalization; TG aims to promote global interests by leveraging globalization. In the former, governments lead innovations; in the latter, global market forces do. TN is closed towards foreigners and tends to favor conflict; TG is open and tends to favor cooperation (Ostry and Nelson, 1995; Yamada, 2000).

When a country leans towards extreme TN, the result may be friction and resentment from trading partners and marginalization of its own industry. On the other hand, TG is insensitive to national economic interests. To tread the fine line between these two narrow approaches, neo-techno-nationalism has emerged (Yamada, 2000) where ‘‘expanded state commitments’’ to technological development are pursued (following TN assump tions), and at the same time ‘‘active public–private partnerships, a more welcoming openness towards foreigners in national technology programs, and greater commitment to international rule-making and policy coordination’’ are sought (Suttmeier and Yao, 2004, p. 17).

Discussions regarding TN and TG are often centered on standards. The standards policy is an important tool for the technology strategy of a country. Recently, particularly since the accession to WTO in 2001, China has been attempting to promote its own technical standards. Its recent approach to national technology standards is considered neotechno-nationalism (Suttmeier and Yao, 2004). China capitalizes on its market size and increasingly capable technical community to challenge the established technological archi tecture in the international economy. In so doing, China goes towards TG because policies solely based on TN are restrictive in the globalized world economy. China tries to set a standard of its own not only within its own territory, but also worldwide. This appears to be obvious in a series of Chinese standard setting attempts including TD-SCDMA (Time Division Synchronous Code Division Multiple Access) and the EVD (Enhanced Versatile Disc). China’s policy is considered neo-TN.

Suttmeier and Yao (2004) see WAPI as another policy aligned with neo-TN. However, we highlight here the diferences between WAPI and China’s previous standards challeng es. First of all, WAPI is a de jure standard. It was to be enforced as mandatory by the government, and it is a strictly closed standard. The encryption algorithm of WAPI was given to 24 Chinese companies only. Although previous standards were pushed by government agencies, they were based on voluntary and relatively open processes that encouraged foreign participation (Mannion and Clendenin, 2003). Therefore, we consider the WAPI case a return to TN in China’s standards policy, though it could be just a backward lurch in the mainstream trajectory towards neo-TN.

## 3. Theoretical perspectives: standard setting and actor-network theory

## 3.1. Standard setting

An industry standard refers to a set of specifications to which all elements of products, processes, formats, or procedures under its jurisdiction must conform (Wegberg, 2004). It is any written artifact that enables efective coordination of activities between independent developers, carriers, manufacturers or users of technologies. As such, a standard enables agreement on solutions of recurrent problems (Tassey, 2000). Standardization is a process in which conformity is pursued of all elements of products, processes, formats, or procedures that comprise an industry standard, with the objective of increasing the eficiency of economic activity (Tassey, 2000). Standards are increasingly important, particularly in ICT and telecommunications because they become the basis of increasing returns (Arthur, 1996) in which the more a product is produced, sold or used, the more valuable or profitable it becomes. Global competition in technology often means competition for establishing as a standard a particular technology that is favorable to its owner or supporters.

Standards have positive economic efects. They include promoting market eficiency and expansion, fostering international trade, encouraging competition, lower barriers to market entry and faster difusion of new technologies, protecting consumers from unsafe or unsubstantiated products, and enabling interoperability among products (Hemphill, 2005). However, standards can also cause negative consequences, one of which is possible ineficiency, which means the best solution will not necessarily become a standard (Hanseth, 2000). A well-known example is Microsoft Windows’ win over Macintosh. This phenomenon of possible ineficiency highlights the need to understand how a particular technology becomes a standard. This question shapes one stream of standard studies.

Existing studies of standards are largely grouped into two streams: corporate strategies and processes of standard setting (or standardization). As firms’ strategies to establish their own product as a standard influence the processes of standard setting, the two streams of studies are not exclusive, but closely related. However, because each stream tends to have its own disciplines, backgrounds, and motivations, we can still legitimately distinguish them. The studies of corporate strategies for standards mainly come from strategic management literature (e.g., Suarez, 2004). Due to the power of increasing returns and linked efects like network externalities, positive feedback, lock-in, and path-dependency (Arthur, 1996; Hanseth, 2000), standards are important for firms’ competition and therefore their survival. Establishing one or more attributes of a product (or service) as a standard can convey direct competitive advantage to the owner/controller of the technology producing those attributes (Tassey, 2000). Therefore, strategies for setting a standard are often sought by companies. Competitions for market dominance between incompatible technologies are fierce and often called ‘standards wars’ (Shapiro and Varian, 1999).

Review of the studies of standard setting begins with a well-known and basic classification of standards by the processes through which three types of standards are identified: de facto, formal, and de jure. In other words, standards may develop through market mechanisms, organizations that combine market participants, and government legislation (Greenstein, 1992). In the market selection mode of standards, the market decides which one of the available incompatible technologies will be adopted and become a standard (Wegberg, 2004). In this selection, there are no regulating institutional arrangements influencing the process. When entry to an increasing returns market is risky, firms can manage the vulnerability through alliance if the payof is suficiently high (Eisenhardt and Schoonhoven, 1996). Alliances emerge as a way for firms to hedge the risk of battling alone in an increasing returns market (Warner, 2003). De facto standards are often developed by industrial consortia or vendors (Hanseth and Monteiro, 1997; Weiss and Cargill, 1992).

Formal standards are worked out by standardization bodies, which are often voluntary organizations like ISO (International Organization for Standardization) and IEEE. They serve as a forum for discussion, development, and dissemination of information about standards. Previously, they largely codified standards determined by market processes; recently they are involved with anticipating technical change in standards and guiding their design (Greenstein, 1992). As ICT standards become far more complex and traditional leaders often lose their historical influence, a process of standardization gets more contentious (Weiss and Cargill, 1992). In response, standard development organizations increasingly choose to develop anticipatory standards, which are standards developed prior to the existence of markets for compatible products (Cargill, 1989; Weiss, 1991).

De jure standards are those imposed by law. Governments mandate some standards, especially where large economies of scale are present or when early market entry is considered an essential part of a national economic strategy (Tassey, 2000).

As seen above, there are many studies of standards and standardization. Most of them examine the corporate strategies and the processes at the corporate and/or consortium levels where de facto or formal standards are formed. They examine the market competition among firms and alliances in the selection of standards, and the development process of consortia (Weiss and Cargill, 1992; Keil, 2002). Standard setting involves a variety of stakeholders, and there are multiple interests at stake. However, existing studies of standards do not explain how the stakeholders interact with each other and compete against each other, and thereby identify the factors that may have made a diference between suc cessful and unsuccessful attempts.

Jakobs et al.’s (2001) approach is unique, in that it shows the political nature of standard setting committees in formal organizations. They investigated how standards actually emerge by looking inside standard setting committees. Due to the oficial procedures adopted by standard setting bodies, it is often assumed that the standard setting process is ‘democratic’ (Egyedi, 2003); that is, diferent stakeholders (vendors, service providers, governments, users, etc.) are equally represented. However, the reality is far from this ideal situation. The committee members interviewed in the study considered their committees as being dominated by representatives of vendors and service providers. This shows how a vendor’s vision is embodied (or inscribed) in a standard.

However, Jakobs et al. (2001) only show the internal dynamics of committees in formal organizations. Standards wars are taking place at the global level and the roles of governments are increasingly important in standard setting, not only in domestic markets, but also in global markets. Standards have become a basis of international competition among countries. According to Funk and Methe (2001), governments had a strong efect on the creation of global standards in the mobile communication industry. As firms can benefit from having their country’s standards become global standards (Funk, 1998), they often collaborate with governments and ask for political support. We believe that standardization is often a process of building an alliance surrounding a particular technology, especially at the international level. Therefore, research is needed to understand how standards are shaped at the global level.

The involvement of developing countries also needs to be examined. Developing countries have traditionally been regarded as requiring support in adopting international standards (ISO, 2005). The WAPI case is unique in that a standards war was initiated by a developing country. However, it will not be an isolated case; we can reasonably expect that more attempts will come not only from China but also from other countries with large markets, such as India. Existing studies of standard setting can help describe how the WAPI case evolved, but cannot explain the complex processes where actors compete and ally with each other to form and establish alliance networks. We suggest that ANT (Actor-Network Theory) is useful here, because it focuses on the ways in which a variety of actors, each with their own interests, work with and against each other in networks of alliances when innovating and difusing technology.

## 3.2. Actor-network theory

ANT was developed in the sociology of science and technology (Callon, 1986a,b, 1987; Callon and Latour, 1981). It originates in a belief that ‘‘the study of technology itself can be transformed into a sociological tool of analysis’’ (Callon, 1987, p. 83). ANT consists of two pivotal concepts: inscription and translation. Engineers who design, develop, and diffuse a technical artifact embody (‘inscribe’, in ANT terminology) into the artifact the way it is used, their intention, and their vision of the society and the world in which the artifact best fits. In this sense, they become sociologists, or to use Callon’s phrase, ‘engineer-sociologists’. The technical aspects of the engineer’s work are profoundly social, and it is therefore impossible to distinguish between the technical and the social during the process of innovation. When we accept that the technical is social, an artifact becomes an entity on which engineers inscribe the social that they want to see and realize.

ANT helps describe how actors form alliances and involve other actors and use non-human actors (artifacts) to strengthen such alliances and to secure their interests. This process is known as translation, which is defined as ‘‘the methods by which an actor enrolls others’’ (Callon, 1986b, p. xvii). Translation, when an actor-network is created, consists of four processes (Callon, 1986a): problematization (the focal actor defines interests that others may share, establishes itself as indispensable, and sets the obligatory passage point through which all the actors in an actor-network must pass); interessement (the focal actor convinces other actors); enrollment (other actors accept the interests as defined by the focal actor); and mobilization (the focal actor uses a set of methods to ensure that the other actors act according to their agreement and would not betray). To translate is to oblige an actor to consent to the passage point defined by the focal actor (Callon, 1986b). Translation is not always successful; it often fails and may halt at any stage. Callon (1986a) states that each entity enlisted by the problematization could choose to submit to being integrated into the initial plan or, alternatively, refuse the translation.

In information systems research, we often hear of calls for new theoretical perspectives to enhance our understanding of IS phenomena. ANT rates high on a list of those useful theories. It is because there is a fit between the ANT’s initial creed that the technical is essentially social on the one hand, and an underlying belief in IS research that information systems are social systems (Walsham et al., 1990; Waema and Walsham, 1990) and an interactive integration of technical and social systems (Lee, 2001) on the other. It is recognized that ANT has the potential to illuminate the complex social interactions associated with IT (Walsham, 1997).

ANT is increasingly popular as a powerful tool ‘‘to help us overcome the current poor understanding of the information technology (IT) artefact’’ (Hanseth et al., 2004) and has been applied in various IS contexts and problems (Adams and Berg, 2004; Allen, 2004; Atkinson and Stergioulas, 2004; Atkinson, 2002; Faraj et al., 2004; Frohmann, 1995; Klischewski, 2001; Linde et al., 2003; Mahring et al., 2004; Walsham and Sahay, 1999; Williams-Jones and Graham, 2003). ANT has also been used in standards research. Hanseth and Monteiro (1997) investigated how standards in a health information infrastructure in Norway inscribed behavior among related actors and suggested that the notion of inscriptions is a promising vehicle for understanding the complexity of information infrastructure and standardization processes. Fomin and Keil (2000) reviewed economic literature on standard setting and alliance formation as well as social network theories, including, but not limited to, ANT, to come up with a socio-economic theory of standardization. Yoo et al. (2005) analyzed the role of standards and their impact on the difusion of broadband mobile services in Korea. In the same study mentioned above, Egyedi (2003) used a loosely defined concept of actor-network and examined the ‘democratic rhetoric’ that standards consortia lack democratic processes. It was found that formal standards bodies like ISO, ITU, and IEEE on the one hand, and standards consortia on the other roughly worked in the same way; neither were democratic.

## 4. Discussion: analyzing the WAPI case through the ANT lens

Inscription and translation are the two axes of ANT. In this case of WAPI, it is rather easy to see how the inscription was made; WAPI was inscribed from its birth with the Chi na’s interests to set its national standard. Setting a standard often involves forming an alliance (Eisenhardt and Schoonhoven, 1996; Hanseth and Monteiro, 1997; Warner, 2003; Weiss and Cargill, 1992), particularly in a complex situation like an international standard setting where many stakeholders at various levels interact ranging from individual firms, industry associations, national governments, and international organizations. Translation is in essence concerned with how actors form alliances and involve other actors to strengthen such alliances. Therefore, there is a fit between standard setting and translation. In this section, we analyze the case of the China-US WAPI dispute through the four stages of translation (Callon, 1986a). Then we discuss how WAPI failed to become a standard even within the Chinese territory.

## 4.1. Problematization – China as the focal actor

The first stage of translation is ‘problematization’, during which the focal actor seeks to become indispensable to other actors by defining the nature of a problem and others’ problems and then suggesting that these would be resolved if the actors negotiated the ‘obligatory passage point’ (OPP). The SAC, representing the Chinese government, spotted the security holes in the Wi-Fi standards. They problematized this and proposed WAPI as a solution, at least within their own territory. This way WAPI emerged as an actor with which all actors wanting to participate in the Chinese marke must conform.

The first step in this problematization is the ‘interdefinition of the actors’ (Callon, 1986a). As seen in the political and economic background of the WAPI dispute, it was initiated by the Chinese government, which chose a policy direction towards techno-nationalism (TN) and wanted to set WAPI as a standard within its territory. Therefore, the focal actor of the WAPI network was the Chinese government, as represented by SAC. Establishing WAPI as the mobile security standard would require alliances with five major actors: (1) mobile chip manufacturers; (2) finished goods producers using mobile chips (e.g., laptops); (3) international standardization bodies like IEEE 802 Local and Metropolitan Area Networks Standards Committee (‘IEEE 802 Committee’) and ISO; (4) national governments like the US government and EU; and (5) WAPI itself. Before the emergence of WAPI, firms from (1) and (2) had formed the Wi-Fi Alliance, an industry standards consortium, which is defined as ‘‘an alliance of companies and organizations financed by membership fees, the aims of which include developing publicly available, multi-party industry standards or technical specifications’’ (Egyedi, 2003, p. 23). In prac tice, these consortia include most large companies.

The second step in the problematization involves defining OPP in relation to the focal actor making itself indispensable to the other actors and defining the latter’s interests and problems (Callon, 1986a). Mobile chip manufacturers, finished goods producers, and international standardization bodies had interests that were similar, if not identical, in solving the security problems in Wi-Fi. Therefore, they have close relationships with each other. All of them acknowledged the security problems inherent in the mobile network, and they were working together for a new and robust security standard. The IEEE 802 Committee developed an improved security standard (802.11i) that is backward compatible with the Wi-Fi Alliance’s WPA.

However, mobile chip manufacturers and finished goods producers have diferent interests in adopting WAPI and selling their products in the China market. Finished goods producers such as laptop manufacturers can be relatively more neutral to the mobile standard than mobile chip manufacturers because the former can readily adapt themselves to the change of standard; they can find alternative suppliers and source mobile chips based on the new standard. Notwithstanding, large companies in each group have worked closely for a long time in the Wi-Fi Alliance to reconcile their conflicts of interest. These longterm relationships have increased their interdependence, and the interdependence has, in turn, made it harder for them to exit from the relationships.

Despite their deep interdependent relationships, however, the market potential of China was suficient to attract interest from some mobile chip manufacturers and finished goods producers. Adoption of WAPI as a mobile security standard would mean a great opportunity to enter the rapidly growing Chinese mobile market. Firms took diferent stances in the course of interessement and enrollment; some decided to support WAPI, some resisted, and others remained lukewarm by not joining either side.

The US government was not involved in the beginning; it appeared as an actor when it was approached by US firms. They accused China of a violation of WTO obligation by setting up a technical barrier and asked for government intervention. If China goes ahead with WAPI, it could establish a precedent encouraging China to set more exclusive technical standards (Flynn, 2004). The US government shared this concern and decided to get involved.

## 4.2. Interessement

After problematization, when the focal actor defines other actors, comes interessement when the focal actor attempts to impose and stabilize the identity of the other actors (Callon, 1986a). If successful, interessement confirms (more or less completely) the validity of the problematization and the alliance that it implies (Callon, 1986a).

When the SAC announced the promulgation of the WAPI standard in May 2003 and its implementation in November 2003 (Fang et al., 2004), one could contend that it was an action of interessement. This is because regulations have legal binding force, and the potential acceptance of the legal force by various other actors would suggest their confirmation that WAPI is the OPP addressing the problems or realizing the interests of various actors that China defined for them.

After the promulgation, the Chinese government and the agencies continued to make announcements to stabilize the identity of other actors as follows. The SAC announced that China would continue to implement WAPI as planned in spite of a controversy over it (SinoCast China Business Daily News, 2004a). In a response to Intel’s announcement that it would not support WAPI, an oficial of WAPI announced that China would not allow Intel to sell its products if its products did not meet the WAPI standard established, due to national security concerns (SinoCast China Business Daily News, 2004e). The China Certification Center for Electromagnetic Capability (CEMC) would withdraw 3C (China Compulsory Certification) certifications from laptop computers, whose embedded wireless LAN modules do not support WAPI (SinoCast China Business Daily News, 2004f).

Despite China’s eforts for interessement, many actors, particularly mobile chip manu facturers and finished goods producers, remained lukewarm. While the majority of the firms kept silent, observing what others were doing, a rift emerged between those who were willingly enrolled in the WAPI actor-network and those who decided to defend the existing Wi-Fi actor-network.

## 4.3. Enrollment

Enrollment takes place when other actors accept the interests as defined by the focal actor (Callon, 1986a). Some chip makers and finished goods producers enrolled in the WAPI actor network. Texas Instruments was the first to publicly announce their intention to launch chips supporting WAPI before June 2004. The President of TI Asia was reported to say, ‘‘WTO member countries have rights to make their own standards, and the Chinese WLAN standard is a perfect technology’’ (SinoCast China Business Daily News, 2004b). Texas Instruments was followed by big wireless chip makers like Atheros and Realtek. Some European manufacturers, such as Phillips, expressed that they understood and would support WAPI. Furthermore, the enrollment involved more than thirty Taiwan manufacturers, who declared that they would cooperate with IT manufacturers in China to develop the wireless network products that supported WAPI (SinoCast China Business Daily News, 2004c). Finished goods producers such as Dell and BenQ also became enrolled in the WAPI actor network (Flynn, 2004; SinoCast China Business Daily News, 2004d).

According to Callon (1986a), however, each actor enlisted through problematization could choose either to submit to integration into the actor network defined or to refuse the translation. Intel, the largest chip maker, openly confronted the Chinese government and thereby refused to be enrolled in the WAPI actor network. Intel declared that it would not meet China’s June 1 deadline for adhering to WAPI (Flynn, 2004). ‘‘After a considerable amount of analysis we have decided not to support WAPI or produce any product that supports WAPI. We have concerns about its deployment and performance and the quality of user experience.’’ The company argued that WAPI was more than a generation behind current technologies (Foremski, 2004). Broadcom, a Wi-Fi chipmaker also opposed WAPI, arguing that the compliance with WAPI would force it to share proprietary technology with one of the 24 Chinese companies that control intellectual property pertaining to the WAPI standard (Yiguang, 2004).

When other actors choose to refuse the translation that the focal actor defines for them and not to submit to the latter during interessement, they could define their identity, goals, orientations, motivations or interests in another manner (Callon, 1986a). This is exactly what the Wi-Fi Alliance did with Intel. During its course of opposing China’s WAPI decision, the Wi-Fi Alliance strengthened its existing actor-network. In so doing, the Wi-Fi Alliance reinforced itself as the focal actor and involved other actors like the US government and international standardization bodies (e.g., IEEE and ISO). The Wi-Fi Alliance even negotiated with the SAC (representing the Chinese government).

## 4.4. An unfinished war: competing actor-networks of standards

The final stage of translation is mobilization where the focal actor ensures that the other actors behave according to their agreement and would not betray (Callon, 1986a). We cannot analyze this stage because WAPI has failed to reach mobilization. The following description is rather concerned with the mobilization of Wi-Fi’s existing actor-network.

In response to China’s WAPI, the Wi-Fi Alliance strengthened its actor-network to protect Wi-Fi. The Alliance was closely working with IEEE which had a working group for mobile security standards to produce a ‘formal’ standard for mobile security. The Alliance was developing its own standard, which was compatible with the IEEE 802.11. Defied by the Chinese WAPI, the Wi-Fi Alliance approached the IEEE. Because the Wi-Fi market did not grow enough for a standard to be established as a de facto standard, they needed a formal approach. The Alliance mobilized all the actors favorable to Wi-Fi by convincing them that Wi-Fi is for their interests, and that WAPI will be a threat to their interests.

Due to the opposition from most of the parties concerned, the Chinese government announced in April 2004 that it would postpone WAPI implementation indefinitely (Perkins Coie, 2004a,b). It was reported that there was an agreement between US and China that WAPI would be reviewed by IEEE. This means that WAPI may be made open and compatible with Wi-Fi and thereby recognized as an international standard. Intel said, ‘‘the company always supports open standards that are consistent with international ones, and supports the Chinese government to submit the amended WAPI standard proposal to ISO’’ (SinoCast China Business Daily News, 2005). Then the WAPI standard was listed as a formal proposal along with the IEEE 802.11i proposal at the 2004 annual meeting of ISO (SinoCast China Business Daily News, 2005). However, the Chinese delegation walked away from an ISO conference in February 2005 after it failed to fast-track WAPI through ISO (Clendenin, 2005). The delegation claimed that there was unfair treatment of WAPI against IEEE802.11i. It was supposed that the Wi-Fi Alliance would be able to make the Wi-Fi standard a de facto standard in the market during the extended time (Yan, 2005).

At the time of writing, it appears that China has failed, and the WAPI actor-network was defeated by the Wi-Fi actor-network. However, translation is a never completed accomplishment (Callon, 1986a), and, as one commentator says, ‘‘the WAPI struggle represents the beginning of the standards game in wireless technologies, not the end’’ (Weber et al., 2005).

## 4.5. How did WAPI fail in translation?

Current studies of standardization do not explain the process of standard setting. By using the process of translation, we are able to see how standard setting proceeds and where it fails. China’s attempt to set WAPI as a national standard faced fierce resistance from the Wi-Fi Alliance and began to rattle at the stage of enrollment. While some firms enrolled in the WAPI actor-network, the Wi-Fi alliance started its counter-attack. From the analysis, we suggest that WAPI failed in enrolling other actors mainly due to two factors, both of which are closely connected to each other: China’s overconfidence in its market power and WAPI’s closedness as a standard.

It seems that the Chinese government was initially confident of the mobilization of other actors, and thus of the success of WAPI, because its market was indispensable to the other actors for their interests. Equipped with such sheer confidence and given that China is the world’s largest telecommunication market (Chang et al., 2005), China’s ofi cial statements and media reports repeatedly emphasized ‘‘market’’ as the main, or almost the sole, criterion in determining a country’s power in setting international technology standards. During a meeting of the Chinese oficials in July 2003 in respect of WLAN national standards, they reached the consensus: ‘‘Standards form the foundation stone of the information industry, and market is the pillar of standards’’ (Fang et al., 2004). Some experienced technology professionals also asked, ‘‘Why can’t we use the huge market to make Chinese standards as international standards?’’ (Fang et al., 2004). In light of the Chinese perception of the ‘market’ as the primary criterion in granting China the power in setting international technology standards, it appeared that SAC thought it could make WAPI the OPP for various other actors; that is, they must accept WAPI in order to realize their respective interests. It seems that they did not consider Wi-Fi Alliance’s capa bility to mobilize political influences (e.g., bringing in US intervention and dominating international organizations like IEEE and ISO) in addition to the status of Wi-Fi in world markets which was approaching a de facto standard.

This overconfidence in market was also reflected in the nature of WAPI, that is, a closed standard. WAPI was set as a de jure standard and had some critical problems in acquiring a suficient number of firms from mobile chip manufacturers and finished goods producers to become a global standard. It is a closed standard, and that technology cannot leave China. China refused to release the WAPI security algorithm to the scrutiny of the international community.<sup>4</sup> Foreign firms had no choice but to cooperate with one of the 24 Chinese companies who were awarded the WAPI protocol to implement the specification, which may result in disclosing their intellectual property to their potential competitors. WAPI was not compatible with previous versions of Wi-Fi or 802.11 standards. This required firms to invest in new manufacturing equipment, but it is uncertain that related products could be manufactured economically under economies of scale if the world market is divided into two segments that require separate standards. The SAC from the beginning left no room for negotiation, and foreign firms had no choice but to resist at any cost.

## 5. Conclusion

Standards inscribe interests, which are those of nations, firms or individuals. Standardization is often concerned with forming a network of alliances with a variety of stakeholders with their own interests at stake, especially in international standard setting where it is harder for one dominant player (e.g., a country or a multinational) to take full control of the whole market. There is no jurisdiction on the basis of which a de jure standard can be enforced; it is simply too big and complex for one company to dominate.

Until recently, developed countries were the largest clients in the global technology market and played a dominant role in setting international technology standards. International standards were often formed by alliances of firms from developed countries, whether through industry consortia (e.g., Wi-Fi Alliance) or through voluntary standardization organizations (e.g., IEEE, ISO). These standards reflected their values and national interests. In this landscape of international standard setting, a significant unprecedented phenomenon is emerging. While standards or standardization are increasingly important in the national technology policy of a country and in the world economy, developing countries have been considered mere recipients of international standards formulated by devel oped countries. Now some developing countries, based on their already substantial and increasingly growing markets, want to participate in shaping international standards and thereby realize their interests as the developed countries did. WAPI is the latest and the most conspicuous example in this trend. The significance of the WAPI case is that it is not isolated, but just one that will come not only from China but also from other countries equipped with technological capabilities sophisticated enough to develop their own standards. One of the contributions made in this paper is that we highlight the importance of the WAPI dispute in the context of international standard setting where developing countries’ voices will be increasingly heard.

Another contribution is that we have built a framework through which we can analyze the process of standard setting, particularly at an international level. Existing studies of standards or standardization are mostly concerned with corporate strategies to gain dominance at standards wars or the development, and comparison, of industry consortia and voluntary standardization organizations. They fail to show key roles played by some actors (e.g., governments) and the complex dynamics of collaboration and confrontation among the actors involved. By drawing on the four stages of translation from ANT, we become able to analyze the development of the WAPI case and therefore explain the pro cess of international standard setting.

The data used in this study are mainly articles from newspapers and industry magazines and partly published reports from related organizations. Although primary data sources such as interviews would be desirable, given the nature of the problem (unfolding in the present tense) and the number and complexity of the actors involved, it was extremely difficult to access to primary sources while conducting the study. However, content analysis of secondary sources was suficient to reveal facts and a variety of standpoints taken by major actors surrounding the WAPI case.

This limitation is related to a direction for future research. Translation is a never completed accomplishment. As noted by one commentator, the current situation of the WAPI case is just a beginning of standards war in mobile communications at the global level. It is possible that the WAPI actor-network continues to develop to ally with, and compete against, other related actor-networks. Therefore, to enhance understanding of the process of standard setting, further research is required that follows the development of this case over time.
