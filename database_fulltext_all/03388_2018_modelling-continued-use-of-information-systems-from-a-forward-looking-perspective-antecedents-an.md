# Modelling continued use of information systems from a forward-looking perspective: Antecedents and consequences of hope and anticipated regret

Yi Ding

Department of Management Science and Engineering, School of Economics and Management, Southeast University, 2 Sipailou, Nanjing, 210096, PR China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Continued use Hope Anticipated regret Appraisal theory Discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation Mobile applications

## A B S T R A C T

This study focuses on two future-oriented emotions, hope and anticipated regret, to predict continued use of information systems. Empirical results based on two studies show that these emotions can exert independent and additive e<sup>f</sup>ects on continued use, controlling for previous use behaviour and satisfaction. Interestingly, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of hope on continued use is insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant during initial use but becomes signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the later stage. Furthermore, discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation and involvement are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as antecedents of hope and anticipated regret. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that incorporating forward-looking variables into models of continued use is necessary and that their in<sup>fl</sup>uence can be dynamic in nature.

## 1. Introduction

Continued use of information systems (IS) is a subject of lasting and extensive investigations in IS research. It is also highly critical to a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s sustained success and long-term pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. The issue of continued use is even more pressing for <sup>fi</sup>rms that provide services through mobile applications. Recent research shows that on average a mobile application (app) loses 77% of its daily active users within the <sup>fi</sup>rst three days after installation, and this loss quickly escalates to 90% within one month [1]. It appears that users become increasingly impatient as too many apps are competing for their attention. This raises an important question regarding the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant gap between initial adoption and long-term use that needs to be addressed urgently.

Many studies on continued IS use are traditionally based on the cognitive paradigm. Recently, the IS community shows increasing support for a more balanced view that considers both cognition and emotions [2,3]. These existing models can e<sup>f</sup>ectively explain continued use and are quite robust across di<sup>f</sup>erent settings. However, the variance accounted for by these models commonly ranges from 40% [4,5] to 60% [6]. The relatively large portion of variance not explained by current models indicates the existence of other potential determinants of continued IS use. The unexplained variance is also corroborated by our real experience that a user may continue to use an information system despite his/her low satisfaction with it. Indeed, in existing models the prediction of future user intent is predominantly based on a hindsight perspective. Thus, users may keep using an information system with occasional glitches because they expect impending improvements. In other situations, users may abandon a seemingly satisfactory information system as better alternatives are anticipated in the future. Therefore, we argue that the incorporation of a forwardlooking perspective in the prediction of continued IS use can be insightful, as individual decision-making commonly involves imaginary experiences [7] or future-oriented considerations [8]. In fact, IS researchers generally agree that users continue to discover and try new features after a system has been adopted [9]. Psychological studies also <sup>fi</sup>nd that a<sup>f</sup>ective states can be more intense during anticipation than during retrospection because of the greater uncertainty involved [10].

The forward-looking perspective is particularly relevant to products and services available in rapidly evolving technologies, e.g. services delivered by mobile apps. Some app reviews re<sup>fl</sup>ect this future-oriented mindset, as the following examples quoted from the iTunes App Store demonstrate:

‘Seriously?!! Can we not just get the app to open and display everything quickly?? I’ve about had it waiting update after update to see if the next one is better…’

‘I regret to inform you that your services and or online website are not up to par. I feel as though ever since I created my account years ago I have never had an experience with your services that has been considered satisfactory… I see an update that says bug fixes major improvements, etc. etc. and I download it hoping changes are actually made that will increase my experiences with your services…’

As shown by these examples, user reviews are not uncommon in the app market. Both reviewers appear to be active and long-term users, thereby highlighting the relevance and importance of the app, yet neither of them is fully satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with it. The gap between their desired experience and the current performance of the app gives rise to hope for an improved version in the future. This is consistent with the argument that one fundamental condition of hope is that the current situation is unsatisfactory [11]. However, the review content also reveals that users still believe that a better version is possible in the future. Intrigued by this example, we propose hope as the <sup>fi</sup>rst future-oriented emotion in this study. We argue that being hopeful about an information system can be essential to its sustained use. The other future-oriented emotion we examine is anticipated regret. In the technology domain, anticipated regret is extensively studied in relation to consumers’ product upgrade decisions [12], but rarely in relation to continued IS use. Furthermore, longitudinal surveys were used to capture the potential dynamic relationships between future-oriented emotions and continued use.

This study uses appraisal theory to understand these two emotions. As one of the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential current psychological approaches to emotions, it speci<sup>fi</sup>es the conditions for the rise of a particular emotion, usually known as appraisals, and predicts the di<sup>f</sup>erentiated consequences of distinct emotions. Goal congruence and importance are two appraisals common to most emotions. Based on their de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, goal congruence and importance can be linked to existing concepts in IS literature, i.e. discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation and involvement, and are posited as two important determinants of hope and anticipated regret.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempts to incorporate future-oriented emotions in models of continued IS use. It not only con<sup>fi</sup>rms the hypothesised e<sup>f</sup>ects of hope and anticipated regret on continued use of information systems but also identi<sup>fi</sup>es the time-variant feature of the relationship between hope and continued use through two studies. Among the three models tested, at least 50% of the variance in continued use is explained, which is comparable to most current studies. When hope is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant determinant of continued IS use, the variance explained can exceed 70%, which is higher than most models in the literature and thus justi<sup>fi</sup>es the inclusion of forwardlooking factors. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study have important practical implications. For example, the recognition of future-oriented emotions can present new opportunities for market communications that enhance user engagement. Therefore, <sup>fi</sup>rms working on understanding system users based on relatively static indicators and historical patterns should consider not only past and present satisfaction but also customers’ future considerations.

In the following sections, <sup>fi</sup>rst we conduct a literature review of the two main streams of research on continued IS use. Then, we develop the theoretical model with six hypotheses. After that, we present our research methods including the design of survey instrument and the process of data collection. Next, we explain the data analysis procedures and results. Finally, the paper concludes with a discussion of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings, theoretical and managerial implications, limitations and directions for future research.

## 2. Literature review

Many studies demonstrate the importance of continued use for the ‘long-term viability and eventual success’ ([13,pp. 351–352)) of information systems. Therefore,it is essential to better understand factors contributing to continued use to make more informed decisions and implement more e<sup>f</sup>ective measures. To this end,we <sup>fi</sup>rst synthesise the literature on continued IS use from two perspectives,i.e cognition-based models and emotion-based models. Then,we discuss a third perspective that considers future-oriented factors.

## 2.1. Cognition-based models

Early works on continued IS use maintain theoretical continuity with the body of knowledge on IT adoption and acceptance. Many models come from technology adoption studies,such as the technology acceptance model (TAM). Of the many antecedents examined,only the results for perceived usefulness are consistently found to a<sup>f</sup>ect continued use across various settings [14], whereas the results for perceived ease of use are ambiguous. Although some studies <sup>fi</sup>nd it signi<sup>fi</sup>cant [15,16], the prevailing view is that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceived ease of use subsides as a user gains more experience [13,17]. One critique of the use of adoption models for continued IS use is that they fail to account for important factors emerging from post-adoption experiences. As a result, alternative theories have been proposed, among which the most widely cited is the expectancy-discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory (EDT). In this theory, continued IS use is a result of expectation and satisfaction, the latter being a stronger predictor [13].

Generally, the EDT model has a greater theoretical appeal than those extended from traditional adoption models, such as TAM. EDT is widely used in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of information systems for diverse areas, such as disruptive technologies [18], ubiquitous media systems [19], electronic medical records [6] and word-processing programmes [20]. EDT accounts for the experience accumulated through repeated use after initial adoption and, consequently, for the potential psychological and behavioural changes that distinguish continued use from adoption. Bhattacherjee and Premkumar [21] demonstrate this process using a temporal model based on the EDT and <sup>fi</sup>nd that users adapt their beliefs and attitudes from their prior reference levels. However, such adapta tion tends to stabilise after initial <sup>fl</sup>uctuations as users develop a more accurate and complete understanding of the information system over time.

## 2.2. Emotion-based models

Emotions are integral to the use of information technologies because they are critical to human motivation, judgement and behaviour [2]. Increasing emotional commitment can lead to higher customer retention [22], and its in<sup>fl</sup>uence can be more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant than cognitionbased factors in some circumstances [23]. Inspired by these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, a growing number of studies address the role of emotions in user experiences with information systems.

These studies approach emotional experience mainly from two perspectives. The valence-based view regards emotions as either positive or negative; thus, valence becomes the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential aspect of emotions on judgement and decision-making. This perspective has been productive [24], but recent studies suggest that valence is only one dimension of emotions, as emotions with the same valence can lead to distinct reactions [25]. Such valence-based approaches may sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce speci<sup>fi</sup>city for parsimony [26]. The alternative perspective argues for discrete emotions. IS researchers thus consider the complexity of emotions during user interactions with information systems. For example, Beaudry and Pinsonneault [27] examine four discrete emotions (i.e. happiness, excitement, anger and anxiety) and identify di<sup>f</sup>erentiated e<sup>f</sup>ects on IT use, even though some emotions exhibit the same valence. Yin et al. [28] compare two negative emotions, anger and anxiety and <sup>fi</sup>nd distinct e<sup>f</sup>ects on the perceived helpfulness of online reviews. Both studies demonstrate that a more detailed approach that goes beyond valence can account for more speci<sup>fi</sup>c in<sup>fl</sup>uences of emotions on judgement and behaviour.

In summary, a review of the two major perspectives on continued IS use prompts two observations. First, research on continued IS use shows a gradual shift from an exclusively cognitive enterprise to a more balanced view that considers both emotions and cognition. Moreover, IS scholars have recognised that a valence-based approach is theoretically sterile; it is thus necessary to examine complex emotions in detail [29,30]. The second observation is that current studies are mostly retrospective in nature, regardless of the perspective they adopt, cognitive or emotional. Nevertheless, as stated by Helkkula et al. [7], users construct their value perception not only based on previous or current experiences but also based on future experiences. Today the standard release cycle across the entire software industry is moving at a much faster pace, and for mobile apps, this cycle can take one week only. With frequent updates, users are more likely to take into account thei future interactions with an information system in addition to past and current performances when making continuance decisions. This premise is partly supported by a recent study that <sup>fi</sup>nds a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect of software updates on users’ continuance intentions, depending on the type of updates [20]. The above discussion suggests that a forwardlooking perspective in the study of continued IS use is both essential and feasible.

## 2.3. A forward-looking perspective on continued IS use

Although forward-looking factors rarely appear in studies on continued IS use, several streams of literature suggest that the prospect of future events has an e<sup>f</sup>ect on individuals’ decision-making. Research in consumer behaviour demonstrates that future-oriented mental stimulations can substantially in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers’ behaviour [31]. For in stance, Nenkov et al. [32] <sup>fi</sup>nd that consumer behaviour, such as impulsive buying, may be controlled by envisioning potential future outcomes and that the level of elaboration of these outcomes is associated with the degree of self-regulation. Similarly, Hersh<sup>fi</sup>eld et al. [33] demonstrate that subjects actively interacting with virtual future selves exhibit an increased tendency to save for retirement.

The literature on customer relationship management also supports the notion that future considerations in<sup>fl</sup>uence customer retention, a construct conceptually similar to continued IS use. Boulding et al. [34] show that consumers’ expectation of the level of service that a supplier ‘will’ or ‘should’ provide a<sup>f</sup>ects their future intentions. Andreassen and Lervik [35] <sup>fi</sup>nd that perceived relative attractiveness today and perceived relative attractiveness tomorrow together shape customers’ future repurchase intentions of life insurance. Lemon et al. [36] incorporate expected future use and anticipated regret in the traditional satisfaction-retention model and <sup>fi</sup>nd that these future-oriented factors signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly a<sup>f</sup>ect consumers’ decision to keep or drop a service, especially for continuous services such as television entertainment.

Following these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, our study attempts to link future-oriented emotions to continued IS use, which to our knowledge is rarely investigated. A closely relevant study examines the antecedents of emo tions, such as pride and enthusiasm, and their e<sup>f</sup>ects on project continuance decisions [37]. This study distinguishes two types of futureoriented emotions, anticipatory and anticipated emotions. The former refers to a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to the prospect of a future event with po sitive or negative implications, such as hope and fear. Anticipatory emotion is thus currently experienced and involves uncertainty. In contrast, the latter is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as predicted a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to imaginary future outcomes, such as anticipated regret [38]. Hence, there is no uncertainty involved in anticipated emotions as the outcome is given and emotions will be experienced in the future. The two concepts are theoretically di<sup>f</sup>erent, and their discriminant validity has been empirically validated [39]. Our study distinguishes anticipatory and anticipated emotions and analyses the antecedents and consequences of hope and anticipated regret.

## 3. Theoretical background and hypothesis development

This section <sup>fi</sup>rst describes the appraisal theory of emotions, which is fundamental to the development of the research model. Next, it presents a detailed discussion of hope and anticipated regret. Finally, it develops six hypotheses regarding the antecedents and consequences of these two emotions.

## 3.1. Appraisal theory of emotions

Appraisal theory is used to understand experiences of future-oriented emotions, i.e. hope and anticipated regret. This theory views emotions as a process that starts from the appraisal of people’s cir cumstances, then leads to emotional responses that are associated with di<sup>f</sup>erent coping activities [40,41]. This suggests that the emotions people feel are the result of their evaluations and interpretations of circumstances or events. Therefore, appraisal theory di<sup>f</sup>ers from other theories of emotions by establishing a link between speci<sup>fi</sup>c conditions and their resultant discreet emotions. Scholars propose diverse ap praisal dimensions, with the following appraisals consistently emerging in di<sup>f</sup>erent studies: goal congruence, importance, certainty, agency and legitimacy [25,41]. Although the relevance of each dimension to the formation of a particular emotion di<sup>f</sup>ers, two appraisals are particularly important for most emotions: goal congruence and importance [40]. Arguably, emotional responses are much more likely to occur when the event in question involves a personal stake and can at the same time threaten or facilitate this stake. In line with this argument, our research focuses on these two dimensions when studying hope and anticipated regret.

The emotions resulted from appraisals are not simply reactions to a stimulus, they also have action tendency that represents one’s ‘readiness to engage in or disengage from interaction with some goal object’ [42,p. 213]). According to the appraisal-tendency framework [26], distinct emotions are associated with speci<sup>fi</sup>c action tendencies that keep the situation in line with the goal. This is part of the process of coping, which explains how people adapt to their emotions and the situation. Bagozzi et al. [40] di<sup>f</sup>erentiate coping responses to emotions elicited by anticipated pleasant goals or outcomes (e.g. hope) from coping responses to emotions elicited by anticipated unpleasant goals or outcomes (e.g. fear). The former usually leads to the intention to facilitate goal achievement and sustain commitment, while the latter usually leads to the intention to avoid undesirable outcomes. The general framework of appraisals → emotional response → behavioural intentions has a wide theoretical appeal to understand consumer intentions and behaviour. For example, Soscia [43] demonstrates how discrete emotions (e.g. gratitude, pride and happiness) elicited by different appraisals are linked to distinct post-consumption behaviour. Our study also uses the appraisal framework to investigate the antecedents and consequences of hope and anticipated regret.

## 3.2. Anticipatory emotion: hope

Hope is deemed a vital psychological resource in our everyday lives [44]. It is related to outcome-desire pursuits and re<sup>fl</sup>ects pleasure about the prospect of a desired future event. Two appraisals delineate the necessary conditions for evoking hope: goal congruency and im portance [45]. These appraisal dimensions are useful guides to identify potential determinants of hope.

Goal congruency is an assessment of whether the stimulus or environment is favourable to the attainment of goals. Goal congruency can be inferred from discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation, a result of the psychological interpretation of the discrepancy between expectations and perceived performance [21]. Positive discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation occurs when performance exceeds expectations, while negative discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation occurs when performance fails to meet expectations. Hope is a positive emotion arising from the expectation of a goal-congruency outcome. Hence, discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation can in<sup>fl</sup>uence hope because it directly shapes the belief of anticipated outcomes. According to the anticipation-level theory [46], one’s belief is constantly updated from prior beliefs by exposure to new stimuli. Therefore, people experiencing positive discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation will anticipate better outcomes, and the prospect of desirable future outcomes will lead to a higher level of hope. In contrast, people experiencing negative discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation will adjust down their anticipation of future outcomes, thus leading to a lower degree of hope based on appraisal theory. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed.

## H1: The extent of discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation is positively related to hope

The importance appraisal, also referred to as relevance, is essential to evoke hope. Some researchers suggest that this appraisal precedes others based on attention deployment and cognitive economy [25]. A stimulus or an outcome perceived as irrelevant and unimportant tends to suppress further cognitive processes; thus, the emotion of hope will not occur. A concept pertinent to the importance appraisal is user in volvement, which has a high theoretical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance in the IS literature. This notion refers to the importance and personal relevance of an object based on inherent needs, interests and values [47]. It is a subjective psychological state and is thus di<sup>f</sup>erentiated from behavioural concepts, such as system use or user participation. Highly involved in dividuals are more engaged and motivated to search for and process information. Therefore, outcomes associated with high involvement include higher e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of external word of mouth on sales [47] and higher brand commitment [48]. Likewise, a technology, such as a mobile app, is expected to have intrinsic importance for users when they are deeply involved. Higher personal stake in the technology (e.g. a mobile app that is useful at work) implies greater value attached to the occurrence of an outcome and more severe consequences in the event of failure. In this case, a stronger desire for a better outcome leads to a higher intensity of hope. Thus, the following hypothesis is pro posed.

H2: The extent of user involvement is positively related to hope.

In marketing, hope is claimed to have important implications on post-consumption behaviour [45]. The same logic can be applied to post-adoption intentions and behaviour in the IS context. Anticipatory emotions, such as hope, are expected to motivate volitional processes that include continuance intentions while using an information system [49]. Empirical evidence suggests that anticipatory emotions can be highly e<sup>f</sup>ective in guiding behaviour [50]. Consistent with these <sup>fi</sup>ndings in psychology, we argue that hope serves as an important cue for decision-makers who pursue superior future experiences in addition to past experiences. According to MacInnis and de Mello [45], the more hopeful one is for a given outcome, the more e<sup>f</sup>orts one devotes to achieving it. In contrast, the lack of hope may result in disengagement from further e<sup>f</sup>orts. In line with this, we suggest that if a satisfying future outcome is highly possible, the level of hope will also be high, which consequently motivates actions towards goal-congruent outcomes. Conversely, if a desired future outcome has a low probability, the level of hope will also be low; consequently, one may attempt to evade an aversive state by deterring goal achievement [40]. For instance, Harvey and Victoravich [37] empirically verify that managers with a higher level of hope for successfully completing an ongoing project show a higher tendency to continue investing in the project. Andreassen and Lervik [35] demonstrate that expected future relative attractiveness of encounters with life insurance companies has a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on customer retention. Based on the above discussion, the following hypothesis is proposed.

H3: Hope is positively related to continued IS use.

## 3.3. Anticipated regret

All behavioural choices encompass some regret potential. People often contemplate how they will feel if their choice compares unfavourably with the alternatives they can imagine. Perceived discrepancies in expected outcomes between a choice and its alternatives can induce anticipated regret [12]. Empirical research documents the various e<sup>f</sup>ects that anticipated regret has on judgement and decision making in di<sup>f</sup>erent domains, including exercise [51], escalation of project commitment [52] and new product development [53] to name but a few. Despite the reported e<sup>f</sup>ect of anticipated regret on decisionmaking in various contexts, research on continued IS use pays little attention to the topic. Fundamentally, continued use is a choice between maintaining the status quo and initiating a change, in which anticipated regret is expected to be common and prevalent under these circumstances [54].

Three appraisals are associated with the elicitation of regret. One salient appraisal dimension is self-agency [55]. It refers to the regret people feel when they think that they could have done things di<sup>f</sup>erently and that they are responsible for the unfavourable outcome [42,40]. For individual IS use, the continuance decision is primarily made by users themselves who bear the responsibility if the outcome of the decision proves undesirable. Thus, the agency condition for regret is met. Another appraisal is goal congruence. Contrary to hope, the level of anticipated regret is related to the degree of goal incongruence [55]. Again, this appraisal dimension can be linked to the concept of discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation. People experiencing positive discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation anticipate favourable outcomes, and the prospect of desirable future outcomes increases anticipated regret of discontinuance, as the consequence of adverse outcomes (e.g. inconvenience) is highly probable if s/he stops using the information system. Conversely, people experiencing negative discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation adjust down their anticipation of future outcomes and are unlikely to anticipate much regret of discontinuance [54]. Therefore, based on appraisal theory and the concept of discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation, the following is hypothesised.

H4: Discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation is positively related to anticipated regret.

Furthermore, like most emotions, the importance appraisal is also related to the experience of regret [42]. People are less likely to feel regret if the triggering event is considered trivial. As previously stated, the notion of involvement refers to a user’s perceived importance of, or interest in, an information technology. Users with high involvement are more motivated to use the information system due to its relevance. They are willing to spend more time and energy on it, which can strengthen their personal attachment to the system. In this case, a higher level of regret is expected if a discontinuance decision is made. This can be explained by the ‘sunk cost e<sup>f</sup>ect’, which suggests that an individual will experience greater pain when resources have already been devoted to the attainment of a desired outcome [56]. The opposite will occur for the low-involvement scenario. Hence, we argue that there is a positive relationship between user involvement and anticipated regret.

H5: The degree of user involvement is positively related to antici pated regret.

People generally anticipate certain post-decisional emotions and take them into account when making decisions. In the theory of planned behaviour, meta-analysis shows that anticipated regret has additive e<sup>f</sup>ects to the prediction of intentions over and above attitude, perceived control and subjective norm [57]. According to norm theory, changing the status quo results in a higher level of regret than sustaining it because preserving the status quo is supposedly more natural [36]. These studies suggest that compared to inaction, actions are higher in uncertainty and thus riskier. They also require deliberate efforts and become more salient in an individual’s mind. Hence, actions are associated with more intense concern of negative feelings. Applying these <sup>fi</sup>ndings to the context of continued IS use, users anticipate that switching to a new information system will lead to regret because it will remind them of the potential risks and e<sup>f</sup>orts of the decision. As experiencing post-decisional regret is unpleasant, continuing to use an information system is thus a rational decision to alleviate negative emotions when faced with a choice. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed.

H6: The degree of anticipated regret associated with stopping of using an app is positively related to continued IS use.

As numerous studies examine the latter [6,13,20], it is not formally hypothesised here. The conceptual framework shows the linkage of our new model to one of the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential theories in the IS continuance literature. Moreover, EDT serves as a base model to show the incremental exploratory power of our model that adopts a forward-looking perspective.

## 4. Research methodology

The hypothesised conceptual model (Fig. 1) was tested empirically using two studies of mobile app use. Since mobile apps are characterised by very short release cycle and relatively high frequency of updates, users are more inclined to develop a future-oriented mindset as they experience the mobile apps over time. Study 1 includes two waves of surveys. The <sup>fi</sup>rst wave was conducted at an initial stage of app use, and the second was conducted six weeks later. Study 2 is a cross-sectional survey in the context of note-taking apps targeting users having more than three months of experience using an app. The measurement scales of each construct are described in this section.

## 4.1. Study 1: a longitudinal study of mobile app use

In Study 1, the proposed model was tested across two time periods (ending in $T _ { 1 }$ and T ) to account for the potentially time-varying relationships. Paper-based questionnaires were used in both waves of surveys. The questionnaire was pretested with highly experienced app users and experts in IS research domain. Feedback regarding the face validity of the questionnaire and other issues was gathered for further re<sup>fi</sup>nement.

The survey participants were asked to report their experiences with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c app that had been tried within the most recent two weeks and was still in use. The apps could be any type but games, as the motivation to download and play mobile games can be very di<sup>f</sup>erent from most other app categories. Data collected at time T are designed to represent the app users’ initial use experience. Five hundred questionnaires were distributed to university students from the engineering and management schools, and 394 questionnaires were returned. The students were required to provide a unique personal identi<sup>fi</sup>cation such as their student IDs, email addresses, or instant messaging numbers. This information was used to match the respondents across the two waves of surveys and was kept strictly con<sup>fi</sup>dential. Of the 394 returned questionnaires, 57 reported that they had not tried any new apps in the past two weeks and another 12 were incomplete or provided unusable personal identi<sup>fi</sup>cations. All these 69 responses were excluded, which resulted in a total of 325 valid responses in the <sup>fi</sup>rst wave of data col lection.

The Wave 2 survey was conducted six weeks later (at time T ) when the respondents were supposed to have adequate experiences with the apps. The six-week time interval is reasonable compared with other longitudinal studies in IS research [21,15]. All 325 respondents were contacted and reminded of the app names they reported in the <sup>fi</sup>rst wave of survey, and a total of 296 questionnaires were returned, a response rate of 91%. No signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences were found between Wave 2 respondents and nonrespondents (i.e. Wave 1 only respondents) with respect to age, gender, operating systems (e.g. Android and iOS) and the research constructs measured at Wave 1. Of the 296 responses at Wave 2, 28 were dropped due to non-completion or mismatch of apps, resulting in 268 valid responses for further analysis. The median age of the respondents was 20 years, and 46% of them were male. About 53% used Android mobile devices, the rest using Apple devices.

## 4.2. Study 2: a survey of note-taking mobile app use

The second study examined the use of note-taking mobile apps among university students in a di<sup>f</sup>erent country. This category of apps are used to take text and image notes, such as Evernote and Microsoft OneNote. They were adopted as the research context because of their high popularity, which guaranteed a large sample base. Prior to ful-<sup>fi</sup>lling the questionnaire, respondents were required to report the names of the apps that they were using as well as the total months and frequency of use. This procedure is intended to <sup>fi</sup>lter out respondents who had inadequate experience with the referent apps.

A web-based survey was administered through a virtual learning platform that students frequently visit for course information and ac tivities. In total, 305 responses were collected. Eighteen of them were dropped due to non-completion. As the second study aims to examine the proposed hypotheses based on users with su<sup>fi</sup>cient experiences with their speci<sup>fi</sup>ed apps, only those responses that reported three months of app use or above were retained for further analysis. This procedure resulted in 239 usable responses. Approximately 58% of the respondents were male, and 48% used mobile device powered by iOS.

## 4.3. Development of measurement scales

The measuring items were mainly adapted from validated scale used in prior research. Necessary modi<sup>fi</sup>cations were made to <sup>fi</sup>t the context of the study. All items were measured on a 7-point scale. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation scale was adapted from Limayem et al. [17], and one additional item measuring problems during use besides the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and overall experience was included. Note that the bipolar scale was used for the measurement of discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation in Study 2 (e.g. 1 = much worse than expected; 7 = much better than expected). The scale for involvement was adapted from Seiders et al. [58]. Hope was measured with three items based on the work of Baumgartner et al. [39]. The measures of anticipated regret were adapted from Abraham and Sheeran [51]. Continued IS use was captured by three items adapted from Thong et al. [16]. The extent of satisfaction was originated from Oliver [59]. The complete list of measurement items can be found in the Appendix A.

In addition to the above constructs, actual app use behaviour was also measured for controlling purposes. The behaviour was con ceptualised with two dimensions, i.e. frequency and duration [15]. Frequency was measured by the question ‘on average, how often did you use the app in the past month?’ The respondents rated on a 7-point scale where 1 represents ‘less than once a month’ and 7 represents ‘several times a day’. Duration was captured by the question ‘on average, how much time do you spend every time you use the app?’ The respondents rated on a 7-point scale where 1 represents ‘less than three minutes’ and 7 represents ‘over half an hour’. It should be noted that duration was not measured in Study 2.

## 5. Data analysis and results

Data analysis proceeded in three stages. First, measurement model analysis was performed for both studies to assess the scale validity. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, potential common method bias was <sup>fi</sup>rst examined, followed by the con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA). Then the convergent and discriminant validities of the measurement scales were evaluated. The results demonstrate the adequacy of the measurement model. The second stage of data analysis compared the means of the constructs at the two time points in Study 1. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed through a series of dependent t-tests. Finally, the structural model was tested to analyse the hypothesised relationships, and the potential dy namic nature of these relationships was investigated.

## 5.1. Measurement model analysis

Common method bias may exist for cross-sectional surveys. Serious common method bias can distort statistical inferences, thus in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the interpretation of hypothesis testing results. Such bias can be partially neutralised by reducing item ambiguity, keeping questions simple and varying scale formats [60]. These factors have been carefully considered in the design of the questionnaire. Despite these procedural remedies, it is still essential to examine the degree of common method bias prior to other analysis.

For Study 1, <sup>fi</sup>rst, the Harman’s single-factor test was conducted separately for both waves of data. The model <sup>fi</sup>ts resulted from this test $( \mathrm { W a v e ~ 1 } ; \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 6 7 3 . 7 5 , d f = 1 5 2 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 , \chi ^ { 2 } / d f = 1 1 . 0 1 ;$ Wave 2: $\chi ^ { 2 } = 1 8 8 0 . 5 1 , d f = 1 5 2 , p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 , \chi ^ { 2 } / d f = 1 2 . 3 7 )$ are much worse than those from the trait model that loads the indicators to their hypothesised constructs instead of one single method factor (Wave 1: $\chi ^ { 2 } = 3 2 6 . 3 0 , ~ d f = 1 3 7 , ~ p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 0 1 , ~ \chi ^ { 2 } / d f = 2 . 3 8 ;$ Wave 2: $\chi ^ { 2 } = 3 2 0 . 7 2 , d f = 1 3 7 , p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 , \chi ^ { 2 } / d f = 2 . 3 4 )$ . This suggests that one single method factor cannot account for the majority of covariance among the measures. The second method to test common method bias is called the unmeasured latent marker construct (ULMC) approach, according to which a model with indicators loaded on both the artificial method factor and their respective latent constructs was built [61]. Loadings on the former and the latter were compared to identify the relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of latent variables to the method factor. The absolute values of the loadings on the method factor across the two waves of survey data range from 0.0004 to 0.10, averaging 0.021, which are negligible compared to the loadings on the theoretical latent constructs. Neither tests detect alarming common method bias.

Similar procedures were performed for Study 2. Likewise, in the Harman’s single-factor test, the model <sup>fi</sup>t indexes of the method model $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 8 3 0 . 4 5 , ~ d f = 1 5 2 , ~ p < 0 . 0 0 1 , ~ \chi ^ { 2 } / d f = 1 2 . 0 4 )$ are again far worse than those of the trait model $\begin{array} { r } { ( \chi ^ { 2 } = 2 0 7 . 0 5 , d f = 1 3 7 , } \end{array}$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1 , \chi ^ { 2 } / d f = 1 . 5 1 )$ . Additionally, the ULMC approach does not reveal any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant common method bias either. Therefore, we proceed to the CFA using AMOS 22.

Overall, the measurement model demonstrates an acceptable <sup>fi</sup>t for both Study 1 (Wave 1: $\chi ^ { 2 } = 3 2 6 . 3 0 $ , df = 137, $\chi ^ { 2 } / d f = 2 . 3 8 .$ $\textstyle p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1$ NFI = 0.92, IFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.94, CFI = 0.95, $\mathsf { R M S E A } = 0 . 0 7 2 ;$ Wave 2: $\chi ^ { 2 } = 3 2 0 . 7 2 , d f = 1 3 7 , \chi ^ { 2 } / d f = 2 . 3 4 ,$ $\textstyle p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1$ NFI = 0.94, IFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.96, CFI = 0.97, $\mathsf { R M S E A } = 0 . 0 7 1 )$ and Study 2 (χ<sup>2</sup> = 207.05, df = 137, χ<sup>2</sup>/df = 1.51, $p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 0 1$ NFI = 0.96, IFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.98, CFI = 0.99, $\mathsf { R M S E A } = 0 . 0 4 6 )$ . In Study 1, all factor loadings are above 0.7 except for one item measuring involvement $( \lambda = 0 . 6 4 )$ at Wave 1; in Study 2, the factor loading of an item measuring discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation is quite low $( \lambda = 0 . 4 7 )$ , but it is not deleted in order to maintain comparability between the two studies. A list of all the factor loadings can be found in the appendix. They are all signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 0.001 level.

The scales are further assessed in terms of convergent validity and discriminant validity. Convergent validity was evaluated based on three criteria: (1) Cronbach’s alpha should be greater than 0.7, (2) composite construct reliabilities should be above 0.8 and (3) average variance extracted (AVE) of each construct should exceed the variance attributable to measurement error, i.e. $\mathrm { A V E } ~ > ~ 0 . 5$ [62]. As shown in Tables 1–3, Cronbach’s alphas of the constructs measured across both time periods in Study 1 and Study 2 range from 0.78 to 0.97, the composite reliability indexes are all above 0.87 and the AVE values are all higher than 0.7, indicating excellent convergent validity. Discriminant validity was assessed by comparing the square root of each construct’s AVE with its correlations with any of the other constructs, and the former should exceed the latter. Tables 2–3 show that the highest correlation between any pair of constructs is 0.79 in both studies, while the lowest square root of AVE is 0.84. Therefore, the discriminant validity criterion is fully met for all the measurement scales.

## 5.2. Comparison of construct means

In Study 1, the data collected in a longitudinal setting provide an opportunity for us to discern the changes of user cognitions, emotions and intentions. The measurement items for each construct were <sup>fi</sup>rst averaged, and then the means were compared through a series of dependent t-tests. The results are presented in Table 1. Except for hope and anticipated regret, the changes of the other four constructs from time $T _ { 1 }$ to $T _ { 2 }$ are all statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. The respondents’ mean perceptions of discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation regarding a certain app drop from 5.16 at time $T _ { 1 }$ to 4.92 at time $T _ { 2 } ~ ( t = 2 . 9 3 , p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 1 )$ , and involvement means drop from 5.13 to 4.99 (t = 2.08, p < 0.05). The mean levels of satisfaction decrease from 5.40 to 5.16 $( t = 3 . 5 0 , p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and the means of intention towards continued use decrease from 5.44 to 4.89 $( t = 6 . 7 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . These changes resonate with <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the literature that user beliefs, attitudes and intentions do change as more experiences are accumulated over time [63], and the directions of changes are similar to those reported by Bhattacherjee and Premkumar [21]. In addition, such changes suggest that the proposed relationships between these constructs may be time-variant, which makes a long itudinal study imperative and much more insightful.

## 6. Discussion

This section <sup>fi</sup>rst discusses the proposed hypotheses based on the foregoing empirical results. Next, it summarises the theoretical and managerial implications based on key <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Finally, it addresses the potential limitations of the study and provides directions for future research.

## 6.1. Discussion of findings

The overarching objective of this study is to investigate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of future-oriented emotions on continued IS use. We speci<sup>fi</sup>cally focus on two types of emotions, namely hope and anticipated regret. Moreover, we capture the potential time-varying patterns of the proposed relationships.

## 6.1.1. Continued IS use and the time-variant efect of hope

The expectancy-discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation framework is a potent theory used to explain and predict continued IS use. The empirical results from the two studies further verify the importance and validity of this framework. A closer comparison of the results at di<sup>f</sup>erent phases of use in Study 1 reveals that the role of satisfaction is more central during initial use compared with the two future-oriented emotions. The e<sup>f</sup>ect of satisfaction on continued IS use is higher, while hope has no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect on continued use. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is surprising and di<sup>f</sup>ers from our hypothesis. This may suggest that during the <sup>fi</sup>rst few weeks of use, users attach higher value to the performance of apps. Ensuring that apps work as advertised may satisfy users and increase the retention rate. Although goal-congruent outcomes are essential to induce hope and in<sup>fl</sup>uence behaviour, this emotion also emphasises the relevance and sometimes personal meaning of apps. Developing personal signi<sup>fi</sup>cance and relevance is supposedly more di<sup>fi</sup>cult and takes more time. Hence, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of hope on continued use may not appear in the <sup>fi</sup>rst few weeks of use.

Interestingly, there is also evidence that the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation and involvement on hope are shifting. At time $T _ { 1 } ,$ the path coe<sup>fi</sup>cients from discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation and involvement to hope are 0.59 and 0.23, respectively. However, they change to 0.40 and 0.53, respectively, at time $T _ { 2 } .$ To some extent, these results support the claim that establishing personal meaning and relevance requires more time; thus, by the time hope is fully developed, it serves as an important de terminant of continued use. Additionally, its e<sup>f</sup>ect exceeds satisfaction at time $T _ { 2 }$ in Study 1, probably because of the personal attachment formed over time, whereas satisfaction is mainly a result of performance evaluation. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect of hope on continued IS use is corroborated by Study 2, in which each respondent had at least three months of experience with their apps (mean = 8.3 months), while in Study 1 none of the respondents had used their apps for more than three months. This suggests that hope has a sustained e<sup>f</sup>ect on continued IS use.

Anticipated regret consistently a<sup>f</sup>ects continued IS use across both time periods in a positive manner. Whether currently experienced or anticipated, regret is a negative feeling to which people are averse. Furthermore, it is also related to one’s orientation towards the statu quo. Regret is presumably less time variant, and its in<sup>fl</sup>uence on continued use is more stable over time. Therefore, although both hope and anticipated regret are emotions about the future, they a<sup>f</sup>ect continued IS use through di<sup>f</sup>erent mechanisms.

Overall, the foregoing discussion suggests that future-oriented factors can exert independent and additive e<sup>f</sup>ects on one of the most critical concepts in IS research, i.e. continued use, even after controlling for the previous usage behaviour. They con<sup>fi</sup>rm that ‘looking forward’ is as important as ‘looking back’ in shaping behavioural intentions. In addition, the study sheds light on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of past experiences on continued use. Among the two dimensions of use behaviour, frequency positively a<sup>f</sup>ects continued use while duration does not. Perhaps duration is more related to the nature of a task. As apps are not de signed for heavy and time-consuming tasks, duration is probably less indicative of one’s actual need for, and relationship with, an app.

## 6.1.2. Determinants of hope and anticipated regret

Both discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation and involvement are positively related to hope. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is in line with appraisal theory that proposes goal congruency and relevance as two triggering conditions of the emotion of hope. Current research demonstrates that discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation is a strong predictor of satisfaction, which is veri<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study. Our empirical results reveal an additional route from discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation to continued use of information systems by changing one’s outlook on an information system. High positive discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation leads to more optimistic emotion towards the future of a system and stronger will to keep using it in the anticipation of an even better one.

However, anticipated regret is only a<sup>f</sup>ected by the degree of in volvement but not by discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation in the <sup>fi</sup>rst study, which invalidates H4. This result is puzzling. The rationale for H4 is that achieving positive discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation means that one’s expectations are more or less ful<sup>fi</sup>lled; thus, switching apps becomes riskier, which increases the level of anticipated regret. This logic examines anticipated regret from a performance perspective. However, another perspective for anticipated regret is the status quo bias or inertia [36,57]. If one is inclined to maintain the current status and is generally reluctant to change, then his/her feeling of anticipated regret is likely to be more intense. This perspective is less related to the performance of an app, but the status quo bias may be reinforced with an increased level of involvement, for instance, when one perceives an app as important and meaningful. The results seem to favour this line of reasoning in the research context of Study 1, as the path coe<sup>fi</sup>cients from involvement to anticipated regret are high at both times $T _ { 1 }$ and $T _ { 2 } .$ However, in Study 2 both discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation and involvement have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ects on anticipated regret. It seems that discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation being a determinant is somewhat dependent on the type of apps and the length and depth of user experience.

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

This study makes two important theoretical contributions to research on continued use of information systems. The <sup>fi</sup>rst novel aspect is that our study justi<sup>fi</sup>es a forward-looking perspective to understand continued IS use. Numerous models incorporate past performances of information systems, but very few examine the e<sup>f</sup>ects of ‘looking forward’ in decision-making. This study is an important extension of the discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation paradigm, as the inclusion of two future-oriented emotions increases the variance explained for continued IS use to 74% in one model, which is higher than many current models [4–6]. Hope is a type of anticipatory emotion, while anticipated regret is a type of anticipated emotion. Studies of hope are traditionally limited to positive psychology and psychiatry research [64]. Empirical research rarely considers the implications of hope in business and management, although many advertisements are designed as elicitors of hope that promote sales, such as cosmetics [65]. The manipulation of hope is also a potential means of regulating unhealthy food consumption [8]. In spired by the pioneering research in consumer behaviour, this study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to introduce the notion of hope in models of continued IS use. Furthermore, the dynamic nature of the relationship between hope and continued use is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and justi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Regarding anticipated regret, research on consumers’ product upgrade decisions in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of information systems usually examines anticipated regret, but it is rarely taken into account in research on continued use. However, we agree with Larrick [66] that many decisions can be better understood by considering the motivation to protect oneself from undesirable psychological consequences.

The empirical results support the e<sup>f</sup>ects of these future-oriented emotions on users’ continued use of information systems, over and above the conventional satisfaction model, after controlling for two dimensions of past use behaviour, i.e. frequency and duration. These newly found factors provide some possible clues for irrational behaviour, such as users justifying the continued use of a system that is not ideal. In this situation, retrospective models, like EDT, usually predict discontinuance, yet the emotion of hope may predict the opposite because some degree of dissatisfaction with the status quo is a common component of hope. The results show that a forward-looking perspective is essential to understand continued use of information systems.

Second, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that emotions are inherent to con tinuance decisions, thereby providing a complementary perspective to the largely cognitive paradigm in this area. Research in psychology generally holds that emotions can exert sustained e<sup>f</sup>ects on judgement, decision-making and behaviour [26]. This conclusion may be of parti cular importance to today’s personal information technologies in which hedonic aspects are highly valued [67]. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the study provide a preliminary support for the notion that individual users will continue to use an information system if they expect it to bene<sup>fi</sup>t their emotional state. Furthermore, with the application of appraisal theory, our study progresses from the demonstration of a simple e<sup>f</sup>ect of emotions on continued use as in recent empirical inquiries to more nuanced understandings by investigating their speci<sup>fi</sup>c cognitive determinants and consequences, while current studies on emotions in IT use mostly adopt a valence-based view of positive versus negative emotions. By integrating the literature on both information systems and emotions, discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation and user involvement are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as two potential determinants of hope and anticipated regret. However, the results for the e<sup>f</sup>ect of involvement on anticipated regret are mixed, and this e<sup>f</sup>ect requires further investigation.

## 6.3. Managerial implications

Firms generally understand system users based on relatively static indicators, such as demographics, historical use patterns and user evaluations of past experiences. Sophisticated techniques have been developed for the collection and analysis of such information, and they are now further advanced with the advent of the big data era. This study suggests that a more dynamic understanding of users should be used, which includes not only past and present satisfaction but also future considerations. A forward-looking perspective is even more cri tical for <sup>fi</sup>rms developing mobile services in a market with rapidly evolving technologies and constantly changing user expectations. Failure to consider future-oriented factors may result in underestimating defection rates of satis<sup>fi</sup>ed customers and overestimating churns of dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed customers. Firms may consider requesting feedback from users about their attitude towards the future of products or services, in addition to conventional quality and satisfaction metrics. IT companies may also bene<sup>fi</sup>t from regular system updates signalling continuous improvement, thus enhancing user con<sup>fi</sup>dence about the future.

The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ects of hope and anticipated regret on continued use also present new opportunities for marketing communications. Tactics that induce and enhance hope may be adopted to increase user engagement, with appraisal theory providing clues on such potential tactics. According to this theory, what is hoped for is important and goal-congruent. Therefore, a <sup>fi</sup>rm may enhance hope by emphasising the importance of a system or by increasing the degree of goal congruity through communications with consumers. For example, some <sup>fi</sup>tness mobile apps have a tagline that highlights building a supporting community and encourages participants to stay active, as people usually feel hopeless when persistence is perceived as di<sup>fi</sup>cult. Productivity apps, such as note-taking, may sustain interactions with users by offering tips to organise e<sup>f</sup>ectively notes of di<sup>f</sup>erent types and formats. Enhanced hope may be achieved because a goal-congruent outcome is more possible when the process appears transparent and workable to users. It is worth noting that these tactics should be regularly reviewed to maintain hope, as user desires are not static. Firms may also prime anticipated regret for better retention through user communications. Explicitly pointing out potential future regret may motivate users to carefully consider the loss associated with giving up a system and appreciate more the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using it. In addition, frequent updates and releases of new features may intensify anticipated regret as they in dicate new possibilities for the future.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

As in many studies, the data used in our research are mainly col lected from students; thus, they may not be representative of the entire population. Although this may be a minor concern as smartphone app users are generally younger, future research could include samples with more diverse pro<sup>fi</sup>les. It should also be noted that our empirical results and conclusions are based on mobile applications only, which may inadequately capture the heterogeneity of other information systems. Although we believe that future-oriented emotions, such as hope and anticipated regret, are widely present in experiences with a variety of systems, the hypotheses in this study should be subject to empirical veri<sup>fi</sup>cation in a broader range of contexts. Moreover, the model may also bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a longitudinal study design within a longer time period. This may be especially critical for complex information systems that require a longer experience for users to form accurate and com prehensive opinions. The current study is exploratory and includes only two waves of data collection. Therefore, one or two additional waves of data may shed deeper insight into the dynamic patterns of forwardlooking emotions and intentions. For example, it may explain our mixed results regarding the relationship between discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation and anticipated regret.
