# Influencing the effectiveness of IT governance practices through steering committees and communication policies

Rui Huang<sup>1</sup>, Robert W. Zmud<sup>2</sup>, R. Leon Price<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Management, State University of New York, Binghamton University, Binghamton, New York, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Michael F. Price College of Business, University of Oklahoma, Norman, Oklahoma, U.S.A.

## Abstract

Information technology (IT) governance practices involve efforts by an organization’s leadership to influence IT-related decisions through the location of decision rights and the structure of decision processes. Our focus is on two specific aspects of IT governance: IT steering committees and IT-related communication policies. Adopting an inductive research strategy examining qualitative data, we provide evidence on the influence of these governance practices from three small-/medium-sized organizations. While each firm evidenced a centralized IT governance posture, differences in these firms’ IT steering committees and governance-related communication policies were found to explain differences in the firms’ IT use outcomes. Inferences from our findings are presented in the form of propositions informing future research. European Journal of Information Systems (2010) 19, 288–302. doi:10.1057/ejis.2010.16; published online 23 March 2010

Keywords: IT governance; IT use; steering committee; senior management involvement; communication practices; small- and medium-sized enterprise (SME)

## Introduction

Organizations design and orchestrate information technology (IT) governance mechanisms as a means of rationalizing, directing and coordinating an organization’s IT-related decision making. Well-designed and orchestrated IT governance mechanisms are expected to produce IT-related decisions, actions and assets that are more tightly aligned with an organization’s strategic and tactical intentions. Such governance mechanisms are directed toward a variety of IT-related issues, including but not limited to (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999; Weill & Ross, 2004) the manner by which critical IT decision processes are carried out, the policies put in place to guide these decision processes, and the assignment of accountabilities and participation rights regarding these decision processes.

A governance mechanism believed integral to an effective system of IT governance is the IT steering committee, or a body comprised of senior executives/managers convened to oversee and coordinate IT-related activities. While prior research has advocated and affirmed the beneficial effects of IT steering committees in leveraging the use of IT in support of business objectives (e.g., Doll & Torkzadeh, 1987; Vadapalli & Mone, 2000), empirical examination of the nature and influence of IT steering committees remains rather limited. The primary research objective of this study is to better understand the nature of effective IT steering committees.

A second element of IT governance practice that we believe influences an organization’s likelihood of appropriating significant value from its investment in and use of IT is the manner by which IT-related policies, guidelines and procedures are communicated to employees. As demonstrated by Lind & Zmud (1995) and Nelson & Cooprider (1996), building a shared understanding of appropriate IT-related behaviors is desirable in enabling appropriate and effective actions by and interactions among individuals involved with IT-related decisions and activities. Correspondingly, we expect that the manner by which IT governance policies, guidelines and procedures are disseminated across an organization will be observed to influence the organization’s success in deploying IT. As little prior information systems research has examined IT governance-related communication practices, a secondary research objective is to better understand the nature of effective IT governance communication policies.

While most research on IT governance has been conducted within large enterprises, we chose to examine small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) precisely because smaller organizations are expected to possess simpler organizational structures and decision processes compared with those of larger enterprises, thereby allowing for a greater transparency (and interpretability) of the nature and influence of IT governance practices. Another benefit derived from examining IT governance within the SME context is that we also assess the extent to which what has been learned about IT governance from studies of larger enterprises might be generalized to SMEs.

In the following sections, we first provide overviews of what is currently known about IT governance, IT steering committees and the SME context. We next describe three case studies, and then use observations drawn from within and across case analyses along with extant theory and the logic of induction to propose research propositions regarding effective IT governance practices related to steering committees and the dissemination of IT policies, guidelines and procedures. We conclude the paper with the discussions of what was learned as well as the implications of these learnings for research and practice.

## IT governance

The goal of IT governance is to direct and oversee an organization’s IT-related decisions and actions such that desired behaviors and outcomes are realized. The design of IT governance systems involves three primary issues: determining which IT-related decisions are to be addressed through governance mechanisms, determining which individuals are allocated decision rights for these decisions and the nature of the decision rights, and determining how associated decision processes are to be orchestrated such that the appropriate individuals are involved and that these individuals understand the implications of possible actions to all stakeholders. Research on IT governance can be traced back to the 1960s (Brown & Grant, 2005) with researchers examining, among other issues, the nature of IT governance (Weill, 2004), structural options for IT governance (Jenkins & Santos, 1982; Zmud et al., 1986) and the drivers of IT governance structural forms (Brown & Magill, 1998; Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999).

Prior research has also made very clear the complexities associated with designing and carrying out effective systems of IT governance. Effective IT governance practice is directed at ensuring that sanctioned policies, procedures and guidelines are complied with across multiple organizational domains and the multiple organizational levels associated with these domains (Fonstad & Robertson, 2006). Effective governance mechanisms must carefully balance the pent-up pressures toward centralization and decentralization that invariably play out within organizations (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999). As characterized by Brown (1997) and others, centralized IT decision making tends to produce enterprise-wide efficiencies but suboptimizes with regard to local work units; while decentralized IT governance tends to optimize with regard to local work units but yields enterprise-wide inefficiencies. Such enterprise-vs-local tensions are a recurrent theme of the IT governance research (Weill & Ross, 2004).

It is well recognized that an effective system of IT governance must be directed toward influencing the formal and informal behaviors of individuals engaged with IT-related decision processes (Chan, 2002). Prior information systems research has consistently observed the value of regular interactions between IT and business managers/professionals (Earl, 1993). In particular, it is generally accepted (Henderson, 1990; Keen, 1991; Rockart & Short, 1991; Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999; Kearns & Sabherwal, 2006; Kearns & Sabherwal, 2007) that the extent to which organizations are able to use IT effectively in supporting both their strategies and day-to-day operations requires the building of robust relationships between senior IT executives and senior business executives. A very powerful tactic to build and then reinforce such informal relationships is through collaboratively involving the targeted individuals in formal decision processes such as those associated with IT governance (Zmud, 1988; Chan, 2002; Kearns & Sabherwal, 2007).

In summary, extant information systems research examining IT governance practices has focused on the examinations of how alternative structural arrangements and interpersonal relationships operate in balancing the enterprise vs local work unit tensions that pervade IT-related decision making and action (Schwarz & Hirschheim, 2003).

## IT steering committees

IT steering committees are frequently advocated as effective governance mechanisms for aligning IT-related decisions and actions with an organization’s strategic and operational priorities. As typically described (McKeen & Guimaraes, 1985), the IT steering committee is a formal body that meets on a regular basis to address specific IT-related issues, and whose members interact during these deliberations to ensure that represented interests and perspectives are heard. In essence, an IT steering committee functions as a ‘board of directors’ for specific spheres of IT-related activities in setting policies, allocating resources, monitoring performance and otherwise facilitating inter-unit coordination (Torkzadeh & Xia, 1992). Without steering committees, ‘y individual managers are left to resolve isolated issues as they arise, and those individual actions can often be at odds with each other y’ (Weill & Ross, 2005, p. 26).

Firms using IT steering committees have been found to exhibit greater business executive attention to IT-related activities (Vadapalli & Mone, 2000), a greater commitment to IT planning practices (Doll & Torkzadeh, 1987), and a forward-looking IT project portfolio (McKeen & Guimaraes, 1985). In addition, steering committees can prove invaluable in establishing the tone of business–IT relationships (Ross et al., 1996).

The design (i.e., specifications of intent, the specific activities to be undertaken, who participates in these activities and how these activities are to be carried out) of IT steering committees varies considerably. Some prior research has been directed at understanding the nature of effective IT steering committees. For example, it is recommended that intent be forged early (Reich & Benbasat, 2000); and, it has been observed that steering committees largely comprised of enterprise-level participants emphasize enterprise standardization and efficiency in deploying IT assets and capabilities, while steering committees largely comprised of operating area participants focus on how best to allocate IT assets and capabilities in improving operational performance (Weill & Ross, 2005).

To summarize, IT steering committees serve to direct, coordinate and provide oversight regarding specific IT-related activity domains through the creation of forums involving executives, managers and professionals holding differing vested interests and perspectives. While the value of using IT steering committees is generally recognized, limited information systems research exists on their design.

## The SME context

Generally, the term SME applies to firms employing fewer than 500 workers (Levy & Powell, 2005). The organizational (i.e., institutional and managerial) structures of SMEs are distinct from those of large enterprises in a number of important ways (Meyer, 1972). Along with flat organizational structures and simple organizational processes (Storey, 1994; Ghobadian & Gallear, 1996), SMEs tend to exhibit rich information networks (employees at all levels are likely to interact with one another on a regular basis) and a high degree of internal transparency (employees at all levels are likely to be more cognizant of others’ work assignments and roles, perspectives and taken decisions) thus allowing coordination in the absence for formalized rules and procedures (Street & Meister, 2004). The SME owner/CEO has considerably more personal influence over a firm’s strategies, tactics and operations than her peers in larger enterprises as well as considerable personal opportunity to engage in decision processes across the firm (Levy & Powell, 2005). As a result, although a flat, informal organizational structure is likely to exist, decision making tends to be quite centralized around the owner/CEO (Bianchi, 2002), which engenders centralized IT governance structures (Brown, 1997; Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999).

Relative to larger enterprises, SMEs tend to be constrained regarding their endowments of financial resources and IT capabilities, prompting many SMEs to maintain quite small internal IT groups and to make extensive use of packaged solutions, third-party service providers and external consultants (Keasey & Watson, 1993). Invariably, the work processes and decision processes associated with this internal IT group tend to be less mature, lacking both rigor and consistency (Thong, 1999), compared to IT groups in larger organizations. As a result, the introduction of new IT-enabled business solutions often occurs in a fragmented manner without systematic, strategic foresight (Foong, 1999).

To summarize, SMEs are associated with a number of characteristics that distinguish them from larger organizations. The decision structures of SMEs tend to be centralized, flat and informal. Financial constraints limit their abilities to invest in IT. Finally, the internal IT groups of SMEs often lack process maturity and a longterm focus.

## Research design

In our examination of IT governance practices related to IT steering committees and the dissemination of IT-related policies, guidelines and procedures, a qualitative analysis of three SME case sites was undertaken (Yin, 1984). By using the interview as the data collection instrument, the cues used are framed within the interviewee’s specific context (Kaplan & Maxwell, 1994) and heightened confidence is maintained regarding collected data as appropriate organizational sources (i.e., senior managers) are identified and used given the nature of the specific questions asked.

We selected three organizations located in the mid-west U.S. – two for-profit SMEs (a manufacturing firm and a health services firm) and a non-profit SME – to achieve a sampling strategy aimed at realizing variation in organizational objectives, strategies, structures and processes so as to reduce contextual biases (Rumelt, 1991; Dube´ & Pare´, 2003). Pharma is a manufacturing subsidiary of an international pharmaceutical company, with businesses in Asia Pacific, North America and Europe. HealthCare is a health care management organization with multiple offices in three U.S. states that provides patient care services to physician-partners. Agriculture is a non-profit research foundation that engages in applied research as well as provides consultation services in agriculture techniques and advanced plant science. ## A priori specification of constructs

Following Bourgeois & Eisenhardt (1988), we identified four potentially important constructs from the literature that were associated with our research questions: IT governance structure, senior management involvement in IT governance processes, IT governance communication policies and practices, and success in IT use. A priori specification of constructs helps shape the research design and analysis, and permits constructs to be collected and measured in a more consistent fashion than would otherwise occur (Eisenhardt, 1989). Brief descriptions of these constructs are now provided.

IT governance structure IT governance structure deals with the decision-making structures (or, decision loci) adopted for IT-related decisions (Brown & Grant, 2005). Previous IT governance studies indicate that the three most prevalent governance structures are centralized, decentralized and hybrid structures. With centralized governance structures, IT decisions reflect a top-down, enterprise-wide perspective. With decentralized governance structures, IT decisions reflect a bottom-up, local work unit perspective. While the centralized and decentralized governance structures by definition are mutually exclusive, an organization’s critical IT decisions can also be orchestrated through a third type of governance structure, the hybrid structure, also referred to as the federal mode. Hybrid governance structures can refer to a variety of alternative structures, most typically: collaboratively engaging participants holding enterprise-wide perspectives with participants holding local perspectives, simultaneously using centralized governance structures for some IT decisions and decentralized governance structures for other IT decisions, or applying both of these designs.

Prior research indicates that the centralized and decentralized governance structures tend to be associated with distinct advantages and disadvantages (e.g., Brown, 1997). With centralized structures, IT decisions are managed from an enterprise-wide perspective, ideally producing operational efficiencies, economies of scale and scope, and enterprise-wide synergies but suppressing local autonomy and frequently producing suboptimal responses to local needs. With decentralized structures, IT decisions ideally provide the local control necessary to achieve flexibility and customization in responding to customer and competitive requirements but at higher operational costs attributed to redundancies regarding IT assets and solutions. Hybrid governance structures, if well-designed, provide the potential for obtaining the advantages from centralized and decentralized governance structures while reducing associated disadvantages (Zmud et al., 1986).

## Senior management involvement in IT governance processes

Senior management involvement refers to firms’ senior executives personally engaging in decision-making processes regarding IT-related issues (Rockart, 1988; Jarvenpaa & Ives, 1991; Feeney et al., 1992; Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999; Feld & Stoddard, 2004). This involvement finds participating senior managers interacting with one another in framing and then deliberating IT-related issues and occurs through formal and informal pathways. Formally, these senior managers interact through their participation on established IT governance bodies, such as IT steering committees, that shape and direct IT-related strategies, policies and actions (Doll, 1985). Informally, these senior managers interact while carrying out their day-to-day work responsibilities (Chan, 2002).

Advantages exist for senior managers to become engaged with IT decision making both formally and informally. Organizations formulate and implement formal policies, guidelines and procedures as a means of coping with environmental uncertainty so as to reduce the ambiguities and risks otherwise encountered (Hall, 1984). Informal interactions, however, provide for a flexibility that is often valued in being able to quickly adapt to changing circumstances. Empirical evidence exists that indicates establishing formal policies, guidelines and procedures is associated with improved IT-related organization performance (Ettlie, 1983).

IT governance communication policies and practices Considerable organization science research has made clear the value of shared mental models in enabling two or more interacting individuals to collaborate productively (Cannon-Bowers et al., 1993). In order for a common understanding of appropriate IT-related behaviors to frame the interactions of individuals involved in IT-related activities, it is necessary that these policies, guidelines and procedures regarding sanctioned IT behaviors be broadly disseminated (Uzzi, 1996; Walker et al., 1997). IT-related policies, guidelines and procedures can be communicated to an organization’s members through a variety of communication channels, each of which is characterized by distinctive attributes (Daft & Lengel, 1986; Carlson & Zmud, 1999). It is expected that the nature of the communication channels being used will influence the success achieved by an organization from its IT deployments.

Success in IT use There are several ways of assessing organizational success in IT use. Following Edmondson et al. (2003), we consider efficiency and breadth of IT use. Efficiency of IT use refers to the extent to which cost and productivity advantages accrue in the deployment of IT assets and capabilities. Breadth of IT use reflects the extent to which IT assets and capabilities are used in supporting work processes across the organization (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1992). Underlying the breadth of IT use construct are two distinct contexts: breadth in current IT use, and breadth in potential IT use. ‘Current use’ references work processes currently enabled or supported via IT, and ‘potential use’ references work processes not currently enabled or supported via IT but for which IT enablement/support is desirable.

## Senior management involvement in IT governance processes

Interviewees indicated senior managers were involved with IT decision making at all three sites through formal governance bodies such as IT steering committees, through informal pathways via personal relationships cultivated by the CIO, or both. Both formal and informal senior executive involvement were observed at Health-Care and Pharma, whereas informal senior management involvement alone was observed at Agriculture.

Formal IT steering committees enable participating senior managers to meet regularly to discuss, debate and contribute to IT decisions thus providing a recurring mechanism through which the CIO interacts with other senior managers. As described below in the remarks from the HealthCare CIO, HealthCare’s IT steering committee consisted of the CxO officers including the CIO. Divisional/operating senior managers had limited direct involvement in setting IT-related directions for HealthCare. Through the IT steering committee, the CxOs at HealthCare formally meet and take action on IT-related issues.

The IT steering committee is made up of the CEO, the COO, the CFO, and the CIO. We collectively make those decisions. Periodically, depending upon the issue or project, we have people come in and participate. But it’s senior level managers (who are associated with a specific project) that participate. Our goal is to basically give guidance and direction for technology in the company. That’s why we meet. CIO at HealthCare

An IT steering committee also existed at Pharma. Although decision rights were held by the CIO, divisional/ operational senior managers were directly engaged in IT decision processes through interactions and discussions enabled via this committee. Unlike HealthCare, however, enterprise-level senior management other than the CIO had limited direct involvement in setting IT-related directions for Pharma:

We do have a committee that when people look at different systems and prioritize overall IT resources, we have a committee made up of different functional areas y . Those decisions in terms of what we need are ultimately mine. But they are certainly made based on interaction and collaboration with the division directors of the three divisions y . ## IT governance communication policies

As the organizational structures of SMEs tend to be flat and tend to lack standardization and formally defined work processes, rich information networks among members serve as a primary vehicle for work direction and coordination (Ghobadian & Gallear, 1996). Consistent with such an orientation, we found informal, interpersonal channels (e.g. word of mouth) being used at each of the case sites in communicating IT governance policies, guidelines and procedures. Still, we did find differences across the three case sites with regard to their IT governance communication policies.

Pharma-indicated IT governance policies, guidelines and procedures were communicated through four channels, that is, an Intranet site, paper documents (i.e., employee handbook), formal meetings (i.e., employee orientation) and word of mouth:

We are in the process of writing a lot of formal policies and procedures y . We have a site-wide procedure system, so they are formal procedures that are signed off, they are out there for everybody to see y . The general policies, which there are really a few formal policies of IT, there are some in the employee handbook that all employees are assigned, which is the Internet is for appropriate use, your email can be reviewed, all the fundamental information confidential, all that is in the employee handbooks that every employee has been assigned. Other policies, as we write them, will be published on the company Intranet. When a new employee comes in, there is employee orientation, and the handbook. Typically when someone comes in, someone from IT spends a few minutes with them, how you do your mail, here’s this, here’s that. And then they are informed where these things are. CIO at Pharma

In contrast, HealthCare only used two channels (email and word of mouth) while Agriculture used three channels (documents, that is forms, formal meeting, that is employee orientation and word of mouth) to disseminate IT governance materials. ## Learnings from the study and associated propositions

We present a comparison between the learning from this study and that from existing literature in Table 3.

Consistent with the results of prior research on IT governance (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999; Weill, 2004; Brown & Grant, 2005), the patterns we observed across the three case studies allowed us to draw an inference that centralized IT decision structures are likely to be most prevalent within SMEs. Because all three cases utilized centralized governance structures, we could not generate propositions regarding the effects of different governance structures. However, consistent with previously published literature that centralized structures enable an enterprise-wide perspective focused on the efficient allocation of IT and business assets (Brown, 1997), our finding that all three firms performed best with regard to the efficiency of IT use suggests that:

## Proposition 1: Organizations applying centralized IT decision structures will be observed to exhibit efficiencies in IT use.

Despite the commonality of centralized IT decision structures observed with the case sites, differences were observed in the relative success in IT use achieved by these three firms. We now draw general inferences associated with the manner by which variations in both senior management involvement in IT governance and IT governance communication policies are associated with success in IT use.

## Senior management involvement in IT governance processes

It was observed that Pharma and HealthCare had implemented formal IT steering committees while Agriculture had not. Pharma and HealthCare generally outperformed Agriculture, though Agriculture and HealthCare were comparable in their efficiency of IT use. Overall, the picture that emerges from our data indicates the performance advantages tend to be associated with establishing a formal IT steering committee within an SME.

In order to theoretically ground such an observation, we adopt a network embeddedness perspective. Rooted in Granovetter’s original conceptualization of embeddedness is the distinction between structural and relational embeddedness (Granovetter, 1992). Structural embeddedness stresses structural position in a network, whereas relational embeddedness emphasizes characteristics of relationships, for example, the strength of relational ties, within a network (Gulati, 1998). Importantly, both types of embeddedness are associated with governance mechanisms (Rowley et al., 2000) in that structural embeddedness is involved with the fashioning of norms and practices at institutional level (e.g., sanctioned behaviors), while relational embeddedness facilitates the building of mutual understanding and trust at the dyadic level (e.g., directed interactions).

Drawing upon this network embeddedness perspective, we expect that the formal involvement of an organization’s senior managers on IT steering committees legitimizes policies, guidelines and procedures emanating from the committee but as well establishes and lubricates relationships between the CIO and other senior managers (Zmud, 1988). Thus, both structural and relational embeddedness are forged. Subsequent informal interactions among the managers while jointly considering and taking IT-related decisions then deepens and reinforces these interpersonal relationships, further institutionalizing the policies, guidelines and procedures.

When an organization’s senior managers are connected through both structural and relational embeddedness, a consistency in organization-wide IT norms and values is more likely to arise as IT-related decisions and actions are necessarily adjusted in meeting and coordinating across the needs of specific work units (Feldman, 1981). Enterprise norms about IT use are more likely to reflect an integration of enterprise and local perspectives, and an enterprise-wide understanding of the importance of IT and how IT should be used is shaped (Fulk, 1993). With a consistent enterprise perspective framing IT use decisions, gains in a firm’s efficiency of IT use is more likely to be observed through a greater leveraging of installed IT assets, and gains in a firm’s breadth of IT use is more likely to be observed through a heightened diffusion of ideas, experiences and expectations regarding IT deployment. These arguments lead to our second proposition:

## Proposition 2: Involving senior managers both formally and informally in IT-related decision processes will produce greater levels of success in IT use than will only involving senior managers informally in these decision processes.

Evidence from our study also indicates that who is involved in formal IT steering committees is also important in explaining variation in an organization’s success in using IT. As reported, Pharma outperformed HealthCare with both the efficiency of IT use and the breadth of current IT use but not with the breadth of potential IT use. At HealthCare, enterprise-level senior managers participated in the IT steering committee whereas at Pharma operational/functional senior managers participated. While enterprise-level senior managers are likely more knowledgeable than operational-level senior managers about enterprise-wide IT issues, they may lack deep knowledge of how IT can be and is being used within operating units to improve these units’ performance (Hadjimanoulis, 2000). Further, as they hold an enterprise-wide perspective and have access to enterprise resources, enterprise-level senior managers are better positioned to explore, dissect and direct new uses of IT (Jarvenpaa & Ives, 1991; Segars & Grover, 1998). We thus expect that:

Proposition 3: Greater involvement of enterprise-level senior managers in IT governance processes through IT steering committees will improve the breadth of potential IT use.

Operational executives, on the other hand, are expected to have a deeper understanding of local issues (IT-related and otherwise) but lack a broad, enterprise perspective regarding IT use. Given that operational senior managers are focused on meeting current operational and budget performance targets, it is expected their primary objectives in exploring, dissecting and directing IT use will focus on already-installed and currently-being-implemented applications targeted at enhancing operating area performance (Tallon et al., 2000). As a result, we expect that:

Proposition 4: Greater involvement of operational-level senior managers in IT governance processes through IT steering committees will improve the breadth of current IT use.

## IT governance communication policies

Lastly, our data suggest that Pharma’s relatively greater success in both the efficiency of IT use and the breadth of current IT use may be attributed, in part, to the communication channels it uses to disseminate IT governance policies, guidelines and procedures. Pharma used the greatest number of communication channels (four channels) and was also the only case site that archived IT governance materials on an Intranet site.

Communication practices that apply a variety of communication alternatives are likely to prove more effective than communication practices that apply only a few communication alternatives as targeted organizational members are more likely to access and, hence, be exposed to the communications (Lengel & Daft, 1988; Schmitz & Fulk, 1991; Te’eni, 2001). Therefore, when IT governance policies, guidelines and practices are communicated through a greater number of channels, organizational members are more likely to attend to these communications and, hence, be more aware of their organization’s IT-related policies, guidelines and procedures. As a result, the likelihood is increased that a consistent IT-related ‘frame of reference’ (Gioia, 1986) will be applied as managers across an organization deliberate on IT-related matters. Individuals’ IT-related values and perspectives greatly influence their attitudes and choices regarding IT (Ginzberg, 1981; Dagwell & Weber, 1983; Davidson, 2002), and many of the implementation problems that repeatedly surface in IT implementations can be traced to the incongruent frames of reference held by individuals involved with these implementations (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977). In general, when congruent frames of reference regarding IT use are applied across an enterprise, organizational members are likely to be more cooperative and less conflicted (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994; Nelson & Cooprider, 1996) in their IT-related decision making as they share similar expectations regarding the appropriateness of current IT deployment and use, thus improving (likely through economies of scale and scope) efficiency in IT use. Furthermore, with a shared understanding of how IT is expected to be used, organizational members are more likely to extensively use IT in desired areas. The extensive use of IT may create network externalities (Katz & Shapiro, 1986) and induce users to converge around the technology and adopt it in more areas, thus also improving the breadth of current IT use. These arguments lead to a fifth proposition:

Proposition 5: Communication of IT governance policies, guidelines and procedures through a greater number of channels will improve the efficiency of IT use and the breadth of current IT use.

It is also noteworthy that while Agriculture used more channels to communicate IT policies, guidelines and practices than did HealthCare, Agriculture was found to exhibit lower success than HealthCare regarding current and potential breadth of IT use. What Agriculture did not utilize were electronic channels, whose use was observed with both HealthCare (email) and Pharma (email and Intranet). Very possibly, it is the greater accessibility (O’Reilly, 1982) of electronic channels that may have increased the likelihood that IT governance policies, guidelines and procedures are attended to and applied by individuals in stimulating and framing IT-related decisions and actions (Huber, 1990). In other words, the more accessible are IT governance policies, guidelines and procedures, the more likely it is that organizational members will be aware of senior managements’ and others’ expectations regarding the potential for using IT to support work activities (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998) as well as the processes to be followed in making IT-related decisions. With such awareness, organizational members will use IT broadly, which is likely to generate network externalities (Katz & Shapiro, 1986) and increase the areas in which IT can be used. Network externalities will also create new opportunities for leveraging existing IT platforms (Schilling, 2002). Therefore, we expect that:

Proposition 6: Communicating IT governance policies, guidelines and practices through electronic communication channels will improve the breadth of IT use.

## Discussion

We have sought to better understand the manner by which two IT governance practices, senior management involvement through IT steering committees and IT governance communication policies, might enhance an organization’s success in using IT. Data collection was confined to SMEs, as we believed these simpler organizational contexts would enable a clearer understanding of examined organizations’ IT governance practices. As expected, each of the case sites was found to employ a centralized IT governance structure – with such a governance posture seemingly most successful in promoting the efficiency of IT use. Variance was observed in the three organizations’ success in IT use, variance that when combined with interviewee comments surfaced patterns that enabled us to propose propositions regarding the design of IT steering committees and the design of IT governance communication policies.

Employing formal IT steering committees (believed to enrich both structural and relational embeddedness across IT and business senior managers) rather than relying solely on informal interactions between the CIO and other senior managers (believed to enrich only relational embeddedness) was associated with higher success in IT use. In addition, who was involved, formally or informally, with an IT steering committee was also important. When enterprise-level senior managers were involved, better performance was observed with the breadth of potential IT use; when operational/functional senior managers were involved, better performance was observed with breadth of current IT use.

As none of the case sites were characterized by governance processes involving both enterprise-level and operational/functional-level executives, we are unable to determine if it might be best to employ a single IT steering committee comprised of enterprise-level and operational-level executives with the charge to direct both current and future IT use, or to employ two steering committees with one focused on current use and one focused on future use. A single steering committee comprised of both enterprise-level and operational-level executives charged with addressing current and future IT use issues would be best positioned to ensure the alignment of enterprise-level and operational-level IT-related activities over time. Nevertheless, with a greater diversity in represented vested interests, it might be more challenging for participants to frame their deliberations, to reach consensus and to implement decisions. Establishing two separate IT steering committees – one focused more on current concerns and biased in membership toward operating-level senior managers; the other focused on future concerns and biased in membership toward executive-level senior managers – would likely produce more smoothly functioning governance deliberations. Yet, by separating these two governance bodies, the likelihood increases that disjunctures will surface over time with IT plans and programs. Thus, mechanisms would likely be needed to coordinate the deliberations of these two governance bodies. Given the important role that IT steering committees play in IT governance, we encourage future research that explores these trade-offs between current and future IT direction and between enterprise and local perspectives in design ing IT governance solutions. We encourage as well future research examining the strategies and tactics used to coordinate the work performed by the multiple IT steering committees likely to exist in most, if not all, organizations.

Our findings also indicated that the manner in which IT governance policies, guidelines and procedures are disseminated across an organization also matters. Prior research has suggested an implicit connection between communication and decision structures – when decisions are decentralized, the potential exists for a greater number of communication exchanges between local and enterprise units than when decisions are centralized (Malone, 1987). Following such a perspective, then, with centralized decision structures explicit attention should be given to the facilitation of communication exchanges across enterprise and local levels. Our findings support this desirability of explicitly facilitating such communication exchanges: the use of a greater number of communication channels as well as the use of electronic channels seemed to promote greater organizational success in the use of IT. While our research design does not allow us to offer definitive explanations, underlying theory would suggest that disseminating IT governance materials both broadly and electronically would increase the likelihood that organizational members would be aware of organizationally sanctioned IT policies, guidelines and procedures. As a result, the likelihood increases that, first, an organization’s managers would be more attentive to IT use opportunities and, second, the reference frames being applied by these managers would exhibit greater uniformity, allowing greater consistency in the ‘what, where, how and why’ of IT-related decision making.

## Limitations and directions for future research

This study has three major limitations. First, data were collected within only three organizations. In selecting firms to study, efforts were taken to focus on organizations that were distinct in terms of operating environment and organizational aims, strategies and procedures – thus, proving a rich mix of organizational contexts. However, given this limited sampling strategy, we are unable to control for all the contingencies that are likely to differentiate organizations’ IT-related behaviors. We hope that our findings stimulate others to pursue these and related research questions with research designs involving more and a greater variety of organizations; in doing so, we expect a collective and more definitive understanding will emerge regarding organizations’ IT governance practices.

Second, as acknowledged earlier, we limited our selection of case sites to SMEs for two primary reasons: recognition of a lesser complexity in these organizations’ structures and processes, and a desire to broaden further the range of organizational contexts contributing to information systems research on IT governance. While we firmly believe the learning we gained in this research can be extended to larger organizations, we strongly advocate others assessing (and extending) our offered propositions within larger organization contexts in order to improve the generalizability of these findings (Yin, 1984).

Third, only a few executives were interviewed in each of the case sites. However, these interviewees were precisely the informants most appropriate for the questions being asked given these individuals’ organizational roles. Further, even with this limited number of interviewees, we were still able to differentiate our independent and dependent variables by limiting their coding to the comments provided by incumbents holding distinct organizational roles. But, as it would have been beneficial to interview additional stakeholders, for example, CxOs, to more comprehensively document the nature of these organizations’ IT governance practices and IT use, we encourage scholars studying IT governance phenomenon to identify and gain access to all stakeholders directly associated with the IT governance structures and processes being studied.

## Practical implications for SMEs

Our findings specify prescriptions regarding how SMEs might enhance the internal environment within which IT-related decision making occurs. First, we strongly advocate that organizations, regardless of their size, employ formal IT steering committees. IT steering committees enable regular interactions among IT and business senior managers and thus provide a formal avenue for managerial dialogues regarding critical IT-related issues. The institutional context of a sanctioned organization decision body serves to legitimize taken decisions, and participants’ involvement in regular meetings of such decision bodies create opportunities to build personal relationships with one another. It is through governance mechanisms such as steering committees and consistency in senior managers’ norms and values that IT decision making and IT use are most likely to be evolved. Second, we suggest organizations ensure that established committees are comprised of senior managers (enterprise-level, operating-/functional-level or both) holding orientations aligned with the committees’ purposes. As enterprise and operating/functional senior managers are likely to focus on different types of organizational issues, populating steering committees with executives holding interests and perspectives aligned with the purpose of the steering committee will produce more engaged participants and improve the effectiveness of these committees’ deliberations. Third, we encourage organizations to ensure that the outcomes of these committees’ deliberations are broadly disseminated to and easily accessed by all appropriate members. Information accessibility is essential for individuals across an organization to be aware of policies, guidelines and procedures associated with as well as the actions taken by IT governance bodies. We believe that SMEs following these three recommendations will produce IT governance policies, guidelines and procedures likely to be better aligned with organizational strategies and operations, and that the IT-related decisions taken by IT governance bodies are likely to be better attended to and conformed with by organizational members.

## Conclusion

The evidence from this study leads us to the following conclusions. First, we confirm an expectation that IT governance structures in SMEs would tend to be centralized rather than decentralized or hybrid. Second, in examining the formal/informal nature of SME IT steering committees, we reveal the value of establishing formal governance mechanisms along with facilitating the development of personal relationships across an organization’s IT and business senior managers (Chan, 2002). Third, we observed that the senior managers participating on a SME IT steering committee produced differences in the perspectives adopted and the concerns addressed by these committees. Stated most simply, IT steering committees comprised of executive-level participants seemed to adopt a broader, longer-term orientation while committees comprised of operating-/functionallevel executives seemed to adopt a narrower, shorter-term orientation. Finally, we observed that the SMEs whose IT governance policies, guidelines and procedures were broadly disseminated to and easily accessed by organizational members tended to exhibit greater success in IT use. Many, perhaps most, IT-related decisions are taken outside of proscribed governance deliberations and instead are largely framed, situated and exercised within senior managers’ day-to-day work practices. If sanctioned IT policies, guidelines and practices have not been widely disseminated or are not readily accessible, then it is less likely that consistent frames of reference will be applied by individuals involved in IT-related decision making.

Through our examination of IT governance practices in SMEs, we have produced a number of new insights regarding senior manager involvement in IT governance and regarding the manner by which IT governance policies, guidelines and procedures as well as the taken IT governance decisions are communicated to organizational members. We sincerely hope that these ideas prove to be of interest and use to scholars interested in better understanding the value-adding nature of the IT governance mechanisms being deployed by organizations in order to better align IT-related decisions with current operational goals and with future strategies.
