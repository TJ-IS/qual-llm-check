Abstract: Real options analysis is an important but costly tool for valuing many information technology (IT) investments. As a low-cost substitute for real options–based methods, firms often depend on managerial intuition, which sometimes approximates real options–based valuations and sometimes does not. Making good choices about how to value IT investments requires an understanding of why, and therefore when, intuitive judgment is more or less likely to be consistent with real options–based valuations. Field and survey studies have provided ex post observations of systematic variations in consistency by option type, but ex ante hypotheses explaining this variation have been rare. This study uses two behavioral economic theories to predict option-type-specific differences between intuitive judgments and real options prescriptions. Regret theory posits that individuals will value decision outcomes based on both the expected utility of payoffs and on anticipated regret for not having made an alternative decision. As a consequence, intuitive IT investment decisions are less aggressive as uncertainty increases (higher valuation of deferral options, lower valuation of growth options), in contrast to higher normative values for both real option types with higher uncertainty. Consistent with competitive behavior theories that predict overaggressive behavior to contest market behavior, intuitive IT investment decisions are more aggressive in the presence of a potential competitor (lower valuation of deferral and higher valuation of growth options), holding constant the normative value of the options. We present experimental evidence consistent with these predictions.

An important implication of our results is that future research should not test for general consistency between intuitive judgment and real options theory, but should identify and explain systematic variation in consistency across option types and settings. Such variation is important in practice because it determines when intuitive judgment is and is not likely to be an adequate substitute for costly formal real options valuation. It also determines when training in real options concepts needs to be more intensive to overcome inconsistency with intuitive judgment, and when the outputs of formal real options valuation are likely to be unintuitive and thus not readily acceptable to managers with limited option theory training.

Key words an d phrases: behavioral finance, IT investments, real options theory.

Real option s theory offers an importan t tool for valuing investments in information technology (IT), but how best to deploy the tool remains an open question. Some firms use formal real options analysis, which is theoretically superior but often complex and costly and vulnerable to error from inappropriate assumptions or estimation methods [13, 71, 78]. Other firms use lower-cost alternatives that can support effective investment decisions in at least some settings [25, 77]. These alternatives include simplified heuristic models, “real options thinking” (intuitive managerial judgment structured by option-pricing principles), or intuitive adjustments to conventional net present value (NPV) calculations that are not constrained to follow real options thinking but may have similar results [3, 27, 76, 77].

Understanding qualitative, intuitive judgment is crucial for a number of reasons. First, firms need to identify the settings in which intuitive judgment is likely to perform well and can therefore substitute for the more costly alternatives of full-scale or quantitative-heuristic real options analysis. Formal analysis may not be worth the cost if it does not make different investment recommendations than managers’ intuition or “gut feel.” Second, firms need to understand the potential gains from constraining intuitive managerial judgment to follow option-pricing principles (using “real options thinking”) rather than leaving judgment unconstrained. Third, firms need to understand when and how unconstrained intuitive judgment can undermine the effective use of real options–based methods. When real options–based recommendations diverge significantly from managers’ “gut feel” about appropriate investments, there is a danger that the analysis will be ignored or its inputs modified in suboptimal ways to generate more acceptable results.

The magnitude and nature of the divergence between intuitive judgment and real options analysis has therefore been an important topic of debate. Some studies have claimed that even without explicit real options methods or training, managerial intuition typically responds in the correct direction to the factors that determine normative real options values [15, 47, 48, 49, 52]. In this view, formal or heuristic real options analysis adds logical support and quantitative precision to managerial intuition but does not differ qualitatively from it.

Empirical research shows mixed support for this view, however (see Table 1). Intuition sometimes is qualitatively consistent with real options analysis and sometimes is not. Prior field and survey studies find that consistency between managerial intuition and real option values varies across option types and settings [11, 17, 76]. However, these findings are mostly ex post, incompletely explained observations in studies that include many option types and valuation-relevant variables. Without knowing more about why inconsistencies occur, it is difficult to identify the conditions under which they are likely to occur in the future and how best to reduce them.

Our study complements prior research by focusing more sharply on two common, well-researched option types—deferral and growth. We provide theory-based ex ante predictions of how consistency between intuition and normative real options value varies for these two options under differing conditions of uncertainty and industry structure. We also provide a highly controlled experimental test of these predictions using MBA students.

Our main findings are as follows. First, intuitive decision makers tend to value deferral but not growth options more highly as uncertainty increases, although uncertainty should normatively increase the values of both option types. Second, a potential<sup>1</sup> competitor’s presence (compared to a monopoly condition) decreases the intuitive value of deferral options and increases the intuitive value of growth options. That is, a potential competitor’s presence makes intuitive decisions to invest more aggressive, even when the information available to decision makers does not indicate that the aggressiveness is economically valuable.

We predict the uncertainty effect based on regret theory [8, 44]. When making decisions such as investment choices under uncertainty, individuals are influenced by the expected utility of the payoffs from alternative investments. Regret theory proposes that they are also influenced by concerns about whether they will “look bad” to themselves and others if their choice has a worse ex post outcome than the alternative, even if the decision was correct ex ante. We predict the competition effect based on behavioraleconomic research findings that competition is a driver of overaggressive investment behavior, including excess market entry [19, 26, 54] and excessive expenditure in reaction to entry by competitors [39].

Our empirical tests use a highly controlled and therefore somewhat artificial setting in order to limit alternative explanations for results, which are abundant in natural settings. However, our results are consistent with, and can cast light on, real-world observations. For example, consultants have claimed that using insights from real options theory to constrain intuitive decision making causes a beneficial “shift in outlook from ‘fear uncertainty and minimize investment’ to ‘seek gains from uncertainty and maximize learning’” [42]. Our evidence shows that intuitive decision makers are unduly averse to high-uncertainty growth options (i.e., they fear uncertainty and minimize investment like Leslie and Michaels’s [42] clients) in settings where they are unconcerned about competition. Thus, encouraging managers via real options thinking to invest more in high-uncertainty growth options could be beneficial in these settings. But such encouragement may be less beneficial in settings such as winner-take-all competition that encourage overaggressive competitive behavior [26] and possible overvaluation (undervaluation) of high-uncertainty growth (deferral) options. Overvaluation of growth options in these settings is consistent with real option overvaluation observed at Amazon.com and Enron [77].

## Theory and Hypothesis Development

## Intuitive Judgment and Real Options

Al though formal val uation methods such as real options analysis or conventional NPV exert a strong influence on IT investment decisions, they are often supplemented by managerial intuition. For example, surveys of chief information officers [27], senior IT executives [72], and IT professionals [5] in the 1990s found that qualitative, intuitive judgments of IT’s strategic value played a significant role in decision making. A goal of diffusing real options methods in more recent years has been to replace these qualitative intuitive judgments with quantitative analysis. Such quantitative analysis is often quite complex for IT investments, and researchers have provided a number of tools for dealing with this complexity, such as nested option models for interdependent projects [7, 12], frameworks for identifying risk and configuring real options to manage it [9, 11, 13], models that distinguish between IT development and acquisition [65], sequential decision analysis for partial IT investments with competitive implications [20], and dynamic analysis of IT infrastructure value [40]. But the replacement of quantitative analysis has been far from complete. Investment decision making remains a mix of real options analysis, quantitative real options–based heuristics, and conventional NPV with intuitive adjustments [77].

Intuitive adjustments to NPV valuations can be of two types. One type, often referred to as “real options thinking,” uses the basic option-pricing model as an explicit guide to intuition. Managers are encouraged to think about each parameter and how the model combines them, but they do not quantitatively estimate the model.

Sometimes managers want to use real options to get a better view of the key issues related to a strategic decision, but they don’t want a mathematical valuation. “If I were a CFO, I would use real options as a way of thinking” but not as a financial tool, because of the model’s complexity, says Martha Amram, interim CEO of Cupertino, California–based Vocomo Software Corp. [64]

The second type of adjustment is not explicitly constrained by real options models. Managers do not match their thinking step-by-step with the real options framework, although they may sometimes employ insights from real options theory or arrive at similar conclusions by other means.<sup>2</sup>

Recent studies show that this second type of intuitive judgment results in investments that are sometimes but not always consistent with real options prescriptions. It is difficult to be sure why these inconsistencies occur on the basis of field evidence alone, in which ceteris paribus conditions typically fail for the comparisons of interest. For example, Benaroch et al. [11], studying IT investments in a large organization with sophisticated risk management practices, find the firm often deploys options in areas of identified high risk, but options to grow via follow-up investments are an exception.

It could be that [such exceptions occurred] because [the firm’s managers] did not formally subscribe to the real options thinking, although our findings suggest that these managers’ natural intuitions match this thinking considerably. [11, p. 853]

Similarly, Tiwana et al. [76], studying 88 IT development projects at large firms, find that managers’ intuitive judgments of perceived project value for low-NPV projects appear to incorporate option values for four of six option types but not the other two. For high-NPV projects only scale option values are incorporated into perceived project values, but it is not clear why results differ across option types.

Differences between intuitive and normative real options values appear to be option and setting specific and thus need to be theorized with respect to specific options and settings. In the following subsections we introduce behavioral-economic theories to develop predictions about two specific, well-researched option types—deferral and growth—under different market structure conditions.

## Regret Theory and IT Investments

Payoffs from investments in IT are often subject to significant uncertainties, and the newsworthiness of major technologies (e.g., enterprise resource planning [ERP] systems, e-commerce, open source software) often means that information about the successes and failures of these technologies is available to firms that have not (yet) invested in them. These two factors provide the conditions for anticipated regret to affect IT investment decision making.

Consider an individual who turned down opportunities to invest in two risky software start-ups in the late 1970s. Suppose that both decisions were equally rational, given the individual’s risk preferences and the information available at the time. If the first opportunity was a firm that collapsed a few years later and the second was Microsoft, it is likely that the individual regrets the second decision considerably more than the first. However, this ex post regret is in some sense irrational. If individuals incorporate the anticipation of such regret into their decisions, attempting to avoid choices that will expose them to high regret levels, then their decisions will display a pattern inconsistent with expected utility maximization.

Bell [8] and Loomes and Sugden [44] proposed decision-making models influenced by anticipated regret and also anticipated “rejoicing” in cases when the outcome of a decision is better than the outcome of the nonchosen alternative.<sup>3</sup> Subsequent empirical research has provided evidence consistent with these models (see Table 2). In addition, recent neuroscience research suggests a physiological basis for including anticipated regret in decisions [21].

Our first hypothesis is a prediction that regret-based investment decisions will respond to uncertainty in a directionally similar way to real options theory for deferral options but not for growth options. A simple model is used to demonstrate this for both option types in the subsections below.

## Uncertainty and Regret Effects on Deferral Options

Deferral options exist when decision makers can choose between investing in a new IT immediately and deferring the decision to invest until uncertainties about the investment’s value are resolved. A deferral option has a positive value if the expected utility (EU) of waiting exceeds the EU of investing now. Equation (1) specifies this value at time t for a simple investment that has only two possible outcomes at time $t + 1$ , success or failure.

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text { EU(Wait) - EU(Invest now) = [p(a - c) + (1 - p)0]} \\ - [ p a + (1 - p) (- b) ] = b (1 - p) - p c, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $p$ is the probability the technology is a success; a is the payoff if the technology is a success; c is payoffs lost by delaying investment from time t to time $t + 1$ $1 - p$ is the probability the technology is a failure; –b is the payoff if the technology is a failure; and a, b, and $c > 0$

As uncertainty increases, there is a larger potential payoff from a successful investment (a) and a larger potential loss from a failed investment (–b). Thus, the deferral option’s value will increase with uncertainty due to the increase in $b . ^ { 4 }$ Intuitively, the larger the downside risk, the more valuable it is to avoid this risk by waiting to invest until it is known whether the technology is successful or not.

If decision makers experience regret, the modified expected utilities (MEU) for waiting and investing now are

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text { MEU(Wait) } = p [ (a - c) + r ([ a - c ] - a) ] + (1 - p) [ 0 + r (0 - [ - b ]) ] \\ \text {(i)} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text { MEU(Invest now) } = p [ a + R (a - [ a - c ]) ] + (1 - p) [ - b + R (- b - 0) ]. \\ \text {(iii)} \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \text {(iv)} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Here the $R ( . )$ and $r ( . )$ functions represent the decision maker’s anticipated regret (or rejoicing) when comparing the outcome of the decision with the outcome of the alternative; $R ^ { \prime } ( . ) > 0$ and $r ^ { \prime } ( . ) > 0$ . Because anticipated regret is greater for a choice requiring action than for a choice requiring inaction [60, 61, 83], we use two regret functions: R(.) is the function for action (invest now), and $r ( . )$ for inaction (wait to invest), and $| R ( . ) | > | r ( . ) | . ^ { 5 }$ For example, if the decision maker waits to decide, the technology proves to be a failure, and no investment is made, then the monetary payoff is 0. The anticipated-rejoicing payoff (term (ii) above) is $r ( 0 - [ - b ] )$ , representing rejoicing at not having invested early and lost b. Term (i) represents anticipated regret at the opportunity loss from waiting to implement a technology that turns out successful, term (iii) represents the anticipated rejoicing at avoiding this opportunity loss, and term (iv) represents anticipated regret at the loss from immediate investment in a technology that proves to be a failure.

The deferral option is positive if the difference between the MEU of waiting and the MEU of investing now is positive. This difference reduces to Equation (4).

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text { MEU(Wait) - MEU(Invest now)} \\ = b (1 - p) - p c + p [ r (- c) ] - R (c) ] + (1 - p) [ r (b) - R (- b) ]. \\ \text {(i)} \qquad \text {(iii)} \qquad \text {(ii)} \qquad \text {(iv)} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

As uncertainty increases, the increase in b increases the deferral option’s MEU, not only through the disutility of the loss –b (as in EU) but also through the two regret terms (ii) and (iv). Thus, the deferral option’s value in this example is strictly increasing with uncertainty under the assumptions of both expected utility and regret theory, but the increase is larger under regret theory.

## Effect of Uncertainty and Regret on Growth Options

Growth options exist when an initial investment enables decision makers to make follow-on investments later if uncertainties about the follow-on investments’ values are favorably resolved. A growth option has positive value if the EU of the initial investment, plus the EU of the follow-on investments later if they prove valuable, is greater than the EU of not making the initial investment. Equation (5) specifies this value for an initial investment that has a negative value in the absence of the followon investments<sup>6</sup> but offers the opportunity to make a follow-on investment that may turn out to have a positive value.

$$
\mathrm{EU} (\mathrm{Initial}) - \mathrm{EU} (\mathrm{noInitial}) = [ p (d - e) + (1 - p) (- e) ] - 0 = p d - e,\tag{5}
$$

where $p$ is the probability the follow-on investment has a positive value; d is the payoff from follow-on investment if positive; –e is the stand-alone initial investment value, not including payoffs from follow-on investments; and 1 – p is the probability the follow-on investment has a negative value.

An increase in the uncertainty about the payoffs from the follow-on investment will increase the growth option’s value by increasing d. The payoff of the follow-on investment if it turns out to be negative $( - f )$ does not appear in Equation (5). It is irrelevant to the value of a growth option because the follow-on investment will not be undertaken if it is negative valued.

When modeling regret and rejoicing, however, we propose that –f is relevant even though the negative payoff is not actually incurred.<sup>7</sup> Consider a decision maker who has laid out a substantial sum of money (–e) to make the initial investment. Consistent with the call option research, we propose that this outlay will feel like a worse decision ex post and will generate more regret if the outlay –e turns out to buy an opportunity to invest in a project that would be a major failure (large f) than if it buys an opportunity to invest in a project that would not quite be a success (small f). Even though the follow-on project is not undertaken, the decision maker will anticipate regret for making the initial investment if the only opportunity it provides is a very unattractive one.

Our assumption about the decision relevance of –f is consistent with evidence from experimental research on call options, which, like growth options, give the owner the opportunity but not the obligation to make a later investment. This research finds that (other things equal) the prices participants are willing to pay for the options decrease as the negative payoffs for the later investments become more negative, even though these negative payoffs are never incurred [53]. Participants do not make the second investment if it turns out to have a negative payoff, but they may experience more regret for purchasing the option in this case than when the second investment turns out to have a positive payoff.

The MEU of decisions to make or not make the initial investment are therefore

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text { MEU(Initial) } = p [ d - e + R ([ d - e ] - 0) ] + (1 - p) [ - e + R ([ w (- f) - e ] - 0) ] \\ \text {(i)} \end{array} \tag {ii}\tag{6}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text { MEU(no   Initial) } = p [ 0 + r (0 - [ d - e ]) ] + (1 - p) [ 0 + r (0 - [ w (- f) - e ]) ], \\ \text {(iii)} \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \text {(iv)} \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

where terms (i)–(iv) represent anticipated regret and rejoicing from comparing decision outcomes with the outcomes of the decision not chosen, and the $- f$ payoff is given a weight w between 0 and 1 to allow for the fact that individuals may weight this notional outcome less than the actual outcomes d and e.

MEU(Initial)–MEU(no Initial), the growth option value, reduces to:

$$
\begin{array}{c} p d - e + p [ R (d - e) - r (e - d) ] + (1 - p) [ R (w [ - f ] - e) - r (- w [ - f ] - e) ]. \\ \text {(i)} \qquad \text {(iii)} \qquad \text {(ii)} \qquad \text {(iv)} \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

An increase in uncertainty (increase in d and f) has two effects on the growth option’s MEU. First, as with the EU value, an increase in d increases the growth option’s value. Second, the regret terms modify the EU value. An increase in d has a positive effect on the MEU due to an increase in terms (i) and (iii), comparable to the increase in deferral option value with regret. But an increase in f has a negative effect on the MEU due to an increase in terms (ii) and (iv). Thus, if regret matters, an increase in uncertainty has a less positive effect on growth option values than on otherwise comparable deferral option values.<sup>8</sup> In the extreme, if the regret effects of –f are large enough, the effect of uncertainty on growth option values will be negative.

In summary, if decision makers’ intuitions do not include regret components, then they will value both deferral and growth options more highly with increasing uncertainty, consistent with real options theory. In contrast, if decision makers’ intuitions include regret components, then deferral and growth option values will respond differently to increases in uncertainty.

Deferral options will clearly increase in value as uncertainty increases, but comparable growth options will increase less or even decrease in value as uncertainty increases.

Hypothesis 1 (Increasing Payoff Uncertainty): Increases in uncertainty about payoffs from an IT investment will increase the intuitive value of a deferral option more than the intuitive value of a comparable growth option.

## Offsetting Biases and Competitive Behavior

Because behavioral economic research has identified a variety of biases with different directional effects, caution is required in drawing practical conclusions from observations of a single bias in limited settings. Effects of one bias can be partially offset by another bias. For example, optimistic forecasting that underestimates risk may be offset by narrow decision frames that create excessive risk aversion [36]. Observing and correcting only one of the offsetting biases can result in worse decisions than leaving the bias uncorrected [14].

The decision-making pattern predicted by regret theory in the increasing payoff uncertainty hypothesis (H1) appears underaggressive under high uncertainty conditions, resulting in excessive deferral and too little initial investment in growth options. We therefore consider an offsetting bias that could lead to more aggressive investment in the same options (less deferral and more initial investment in growth options). We use the deferral and growth options described in the previous section, treat a monopoly setting as a baseline, and consider whether adding a potential competitor to the setting creates an offsetting tendency to more aggressive investment. Fernandez and Bilan [24], cataloging errors observed in actual firm valuations performed by financial analysts, investment banks, and financial consultants as part of company acquisitions, sales, mergers, and arbitrage processes, identify a tendency to misestimate the effects of competitors’ behavior. For example, a firm’s option of enlarging a washing machine factory in China is overvalued because it does not correctly take into account the reduction in the firm’s market share that is likely to occur as competitors exercise similar options. A number of studies have documented unprofitably aggressive investment due to misestimating competitors’ behavior and its effects, including excess entry into competitive markets due to underestimating competitors’ chances and excessive (unprofitable) investment to combat new entrants [19, 26, 39, 54, 67].

Because IT investments can have significant effects on competitive success, the possibility of misestimating these effects is both real and important. In some cases, IT investments can reduce potential competitors’ chances of survival [58]. For example, early IT investment enabled Schneider National Inc., a large truckload carrier, to contain costs and survive in a deregulated environment where newer entrants failed [63]. In other cases, a particular technology is a better fit to one competitor’s organizational structure or competencies than to another’s, and only the firm with the “fit” advantage can use the technology successfully to compete. For example, Safeway and Tesco, two UK food retailers, invested in customer loyalty cards. Although the technology was a failure for Safeway, it was a success for Tesco because the firm was more efficient in leveraging its IT infrastructure [41, 46].

In such settings, the disadvantaged competitor would find it more profitable to choose another strategy and compete in a different market niche, rather than making the same investment as the more advantaged competitor. If the disadvantaged firm can be counted on to behave in this way, the other has a de facto monopoly. It is not clear that less-advantaged firms can be counted on to behave in this way, however, or that more advantaged firms will expect them to do so. Excess entry by the disadvantaged may occur when their disadvantage is not too great, and this in turn could trigger more aggressive investment by the more advantaged firm, in an attempt to provide a stronger deterrent.

Some researchers have explained competitive behavior that appears unprofitably aggressive by assuming that market participants believe there is a small probability that their competitor will not behave rationally (e.g., will be excessively aggressive), that this behavior will have significant damaging effects, and thus aggressive deterrent behavior is the best response [38, 51]. It is also possible that decision makers may act aggressively without rigorously working out the situation’s economic logic [79].

## Effects of Industry Structure on Deferral and Growth Options

We examine an IT investment context where the investment underlying the firm’s option is unprofitable for potential competitors due to an impregnable market position by the firm [63], or because the competitor lacks the ability to use the new technology efficiently [41, 46]. A rational potential competitor would refrain from making the investment, leaving the firm with a de facto monopoly in which early investment has no deterrent value for the firm. In this case, real option values will be the same in a scenario including a potential but disadvantaged competitor as in an otherwise identical scenario in which the focal firm has a de jure or de facto monopoly.<sup>9</sup>

If excess entry by the less-advantaged competitor is possible, however, the moreadvantaged competitor may be concerned about this possibility and may invest aggressively in turn, either as a rational deterrent to an irrational competitor or as an overreaction to competition. In such a case, the propensity to defer investment will decrease relative to the monopoly setting. Similarly, the propensity to make initial investments with follow-on potential will increase relative to the monopoly setting.

Hypothesis 2 (Potential Disadvantaged Competitor): The presence of a potential but disadvantaged competitor will decrease the intuitive value of a deferral option but increase the intuitive value of a comparable growth option, relative to the same options in a monopoly setting.

## Research Methods

To test the hypotheses, we ran two similar experiments. The research design for each experiment was a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ mixed design, with option type (deferral versus growth) as a between-subjects factor, and uncertainty (high versus low) and industry structure (monopoly versus potential competition) as within-subjects factors.<sup>10</sup>

The principal difference between the experiments was the pay scheme for participants. Participants received performance-based compensation to motivate them to make decisions that were qualitatively consistent with real options. In experiment 1, participants received a \$7.00 payment for answering all questions in the experimental instrument and could earn as much as \$10 in addition (\$2.50 for each of four investment scenarios) for making the optimal ex ante investment decisions as determined by “current financial theory.”<sup>11</sup> Total pay for participation in this experiment ranged from \$7.00 to \$17.00 (mean pay = \$11.17).

In experiment 1, individuals acted as the firm’s employees.<sup>12</sup> Like many salaried managers in firms, they did not directly receive the investment’s payoffs and were sheltered from uncontrollable risks. Experiment 2 offered different compensation in order to make sure that the experiment 1 results were not artifacts of its compensation plan. Because real options analysis identifies optimal investment decisions for the recipient of the investment payoffs, making participants the recipients of the investment payoffs might make their choices more consistent with real options analysis, and thus not support the increasing payoff uncertainty hypothesis (H1). Conversely, regret effects might be stronger, providing continued support for the increasing payoff uncertainty hypothesis (H1), if individuals directly received the investment’s payoffs and anticipated regretting a decision that paid off badly for them. Therefore, in experiment 2, we generated profit outcomes for the investments after the experiment by drawing a random number from a distribution with the same mean and variance as the cash flow distribution provided in each experimental scenario. Based on participants investment decisions, they were paid a percentage of the investment returns plus a \$2.50 base pay for each scenario. Because investment payoffs could be negative, the actual money received for each case could be more or less than the \$2.50 base pay. Total pay for participation in experiment 2 ranged from \$10.00 to \$15.87 (mean pay = \$13.22). No participant received less than \$10.00 (4 × \$2.50), because gains (additions to base pay) in some scenarios made up for losses (subtractions from base pay) in other scenarios. The payoff mean and range in both experiments indicate that most participants’ decision strategies generated a net gain above base pay. Thus, although participants exhibited some systematic suboptimality, their decision strategies were not unprofitable.

In summary, in the first experiment, participants acted as managers whose compensation increased if they made better ex ante decisions, rather than being completely dependent on the investments’ actual payoffs. In the second experiment, participants acted as owners of a firm, whose compensation depended on the actual payoffs of the investments they made. These two pay schemes represent the ends of the continuum on which most actual decision makers’ pay can be expected to fall. Consistent with prior studies that find regret effects with a variety of pay schemes, we anticipate and find directionally similar effects in both experiments. Aside from this difference in pay and a few other minor differences between experiments noted below, the methods described in the following sections are the same for the two experiments.

## Participants

In both experiments, participants were MBA students from a large midwestern public university. There were 33 participants in the first experiment and 37 in the second experiment. In both experiments combined, participants were 80 percent male, had an average age of 30, and 5.58 years of work experience. Using a scale ranging from 1 “no experience” to 11 “considerable experience,” the participants rated their experience with IT similar to the kind described in the cases as 6.38, their investment decisionmaking experience as 5.42, and their practical experience using formal real options analysis as 3.39. All participants had recent training in option pricing in finance courses, thus reducing the likelihood that deviations from “options thinking” were driven by simple, easily corrected ignorance. Their knowledge level was comparable to that of managers who have had some exposure to real options but are not real options experts. The only participant characteristics that differed significantly between experiments were experience with investment decision making (experiment 1 = 4.69, experiment $2 = 6 . 0 5 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and experience using real options analysis (experiment 1 = 2.53, experiment $2 = 4 . 1 4 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). However, as described below, the results were similar across experiments and thus appear robust to these differences.

## Experimental Materials

The experimental materials were adapted from the Harvard Business School’s “Royal Automobile Club” case [30], in which managers decide on investing in new IT to improve roadside rescue service and increase service purchases. This case can be presented plausibly in both monopoly and competitive settings. For example, the American Automobile Association has historically had no significant competition for membership in the United States, whereas the Royal Automobile Club and the Automobile Association have shared the market in Britain. We updated and expanded the technology description in the Harvard case to include a distributed database, a network using fiber-optic cable, mapping software, and an integrated neural network decision support tool.

The experimental materials included the investment’s NPV analysis prepared by the organization’s finance staff, as well as the other key parameters for evaluating an option using real options analysis: the investment’s cost, cash inflow present value estimates, net present values + one and two standard deviations, the risk-adjusted and risk-free discount rates, and supporting information explaining future cash flow estimates. We did not expect most participants to calculate real option values because the task was framed as a judgmental adjustment to conventional NPV, but we did want to ensure that deviations from normative judgments were not due to lack of necessary information. To make the deferral and growth options comparable, we made the key parameters identical except that for the growth option; costs and cash inflows were broken down into the initial investment and the follow-on investment.<sup>13</sup> Participants were told that the NPV analysis did not incorporate flexibility or optimal timing values, so participants would have to make these judgments as best they could on an intuitive basis. This setting mimics real-world settings in which investments are made by combining conventional NPV analysis with intuition about strategic flexibility.

## Independent Variables

## Option Type

In the deferral option condition, decision makers could invest in the entire technology package immediately or wait two years to make the decision. Uncertainty about the technology’s success would be resolved at the end of the two years. In the growth option condition, the technology package was split into an initial investment that decision makers could invest in immediately and a follow-on investment that they could make in two years if they had made the initial investment and if the uncertainty surrounding the follow-on investment’s outcome was resolved favorably.

To deter participants from assuming the presence of compound options that might have differed across conditions, we included the statement: “Assume that the decision [deferral: to invest now or wait; growth: to make initial investment or to not make initial investment] does not change A‑ 1’s flexibility in any way that is not described here: for example, it does not make it easier or more difficult to invest in any other technology.” In addition, because decision makers in the growth option scenarios might have assumed they could invest later if they decided against an initial investment now (an implicit deferral option, which would have decreased the attractiveness of investing now), we added a sentence to the growth option scenarios in experiment 2: “Assume that if A‑ 1 does not make the initial investment now, commitments to other projects in the future will prevent the firm from making the initial investment at a later date.”

The deferral option condition included four scenarios describing the deferral option, and the growth condition included four scenarios describing the growth option. The four scenarios within each option condition were high uncertainty–monopoly, high uncertainty–potential competition, low uncertainty–monopoly, and low uncertainty– potential competition.

## Uncertainty

Consistent with real options analysis, we define uncertainty as an investment’s risk or volatility in cash flows that results from the inability to predict behaviors related to the economy, market, technology, or organization [10, 73]. We focused on technological uncertainties (uncertainties about how well the technology would work) in our scenarios because they are prevalent in real-world IT investments [10].

In the low-uncertainty scenarios, participants were told that the technology was well established and needed only local modifications. It was uncertain how well these modifications would work, but because they were minor, their success or failure would have only a moderate effect on future cash flow variability. In the high-uncertainty scenarios, the technology was relatively untested and the effects of its success or failure on cash flow variability were larger. Experiment participants were told that the uncertainties about how well the new technology worked would be resolved in two years through the use of the technology by other noncompeting organizations in the country (low uncertainty), and also through the use of the technology by roadside rescue organizations in other countries (high uncertainty). Consistent with this information, the NPV analysis presented a higher standard deviation of expected cash flows in the high-uncertainty scenarios than in the low-uncertainty scenarios.

## Industry Structure

In the monopoly scenarios, participants played the role of managers (experiment 1)/ owners (experiment 2) of an automobile club in a small industrialized country, in which the club was either protected from competition by regulatory barriers (monopoly) or shared the market for auto-related services with another automobile club (potential competition). In the potential competition scenarios, participants were told that the potential competitor was not well positioned to invest in the new technology underlying the option and could not do so immediately. In two years after the uncertainties about the technology were resolved, it would be possible for the potential competitor to invest—and thus to become an actual competitor in technology-enhanced roadside assistance—if the decision maker had not invested early (deferral) or if the decision maker had made the initial investment (growth). If the potential competitor did invest in two years, it would reduce A‑ 1’s investment value by taking away a portion of A‑ 1’s expected increase in cash flows. However, this action would not be profitable for the potential competitor because the technology was a poor fit with its expertise and organizational structure and would be more costly for it to use, negating the increased cash flows.

## Dependent Variables

To capture the directional effects of uncertainty and industry structure on intuitive judgments, we asked participants to qualitatively value each investment independently (investment decisions) and then to compare investment pairs under low versus high uncertainty, and monopoly versus potential competition (investment comparisons). Participants whose intuition was qualitatively consistent with real options would have been able to make directionally correct answers to these questions without complex calculations.

## Investment Decisions

After each scenario, participants in experiment 1 were asked: “Which alternative is better for the organization financially?” Responses were on an 11-point scale, with “Investing now is much better” and “Waiting to decide is much better” as endpoints in the deferral condition, and “Not making the initial investment is much better” and “Making the initial investment is much better” as endpoints in the growth condition. In experiment 2, we changed the decision questions slightly to correspond with the revised pay scheme. Participants were asked: “Which alternative is more valuable for you as owner of the firm?” and made responses on a 10-point scale rather than an 11-point scale. There was no neutral midpoint because participants had to choose either to invest or not invest for us to determine their pay.<sup>14</sup> In the reported results, the response scale is reversed for the deferral options questions, so that higher response numbers indicate higher option values for both deferral and growth.

## Investment Comparisons

In addition to asking participants for an independent valuation in each scenario, we also asked them to compare scenario pairs. Specifically, we asked participants whether they thought waiting to decide was more valuable under low or high uncertainty, and whether it was more valuable under monopoly or competition. For example, in the deferral condition we asked participants: “Consider the two cases in which there is no competition but different levels of uncertainty. In these cases, do you think waiting is more valuable when uncertainty is low or when uncertainty is high?” Responses were on an 11-point scale with (for example) “Waiting is much more valuable in the monopoly, low uncertainty scenario” and “Waiting is much more valuable in the monopoly, high uncertainty scenario” as endpoints.

## Procedure

Participan ts were ran doml y assign ed to option -type con dition s. The four scenarios were presented in four different orders. Thus, they could go back and revise initial decisions if they chose.<sup>15</sup> Participants completed all four scenarios in one sitting and then answered postexperiment questions eliciting demographic and experience information and responses to the experimental materials. We gave no feedback on whether decisions were “right” or not to avoid demand effects.

## Hypothesis Tests

To test the hypotheses, we classified participants into three categories for both the decision questions and the comparison questions. For the increasing payoff uncertainty hypothesis (H1), these categories were (1) valuing the option more (choosing a higher number on the rating scale) under low uncertainty than high uncertainty, (2) valuing the option more under high uncertainty than low uncertainty, and (3) valuing the option equally under low and high uncertainty. For the potential disadvantaged competitor hypothesis (H2), these categories were (1) valuing the option more under monopoly than potential competition, (2) valuing the option more under potential competition than monopoly, and (3) valuing the option equally under monopoly and potential competition. Tables 3 and 4 show how many participants were in each category.

For the decision questions, we combined responses across monopoly and competition conditions to test uncertainty effects, and combined responses across high- and lowuncertainty conditions to test industry structure effects. For example, if an individual’s ratings in the two high-uncertainty scenarios (monopoly and potential competition) combined were higher than the ratings in the two low-uncertainty scenarios combined, the individual was classified as valuing the option more under high uncertainty.

For the comparison questions, we categorized participants based simply on whether their mean responses were above, below, or at the midpoint of the scale on the relevant questions. As with the decision questions, we test for uncertainty effects by combining data across monopoly and competition conditions, and test for industry structure effects by combining data across high- and low-uncertainty conditions. ## Supplementary Analyses

Participants’ answers to postexperiment questions support the reasoning put forward in the hypothesis motivation.<sup>16</sup> To assess regret effects, after experiment 2, we asked participants to agree or disagree with four statements about whether anticipated regret or rejoicing influenced their decision to invest immediately or to wait to decide (not invest or invest for growth option) (see Appendix C). We modeled the four statements after Zeelenberg and Pieters [82], making necessary changes to reflect our specific decision context. These four statements are analogous to the regret terms (i)–(iv) in Equations (4) and (8). Using the responses to these statements, we first evaluated the extent to which participants agreed that anticipated regret (rejoicing) played a role in their investment decisions. We found that 35 of the 37 participants chose a response higher than the scale midpoint (6) on at least one regret question; that is, they claimed that at least one of the regret terms in Equations (4) and (8) influenced their decisions. We also averaged the responses to the four questions to measure the strength and prevalence of anticipated regret. A significant majority of participants (27 of 37) had averages higher than the midpoint (binomial test comparing proportion to 50 percent, $p < 0 . 0 0 1 $ ).

Further supporting the reasoning behind the increasing payoff uncertainty hypothesis (H1), two questions were significantly associated with participants’ investment decisions. The increasing payoff uncertainty hypothesis (H1) proposed that decision makers would intuitively value a growth option less than a deferral option with increases in uncertainty because decision makers would anticipate regret at making the initial investment or rejoicing at not making it if the follow-on investment turned out to have a negative value. Regret question (1) for the growth condition measures participants rejoicing at not making the initial investment if the follow-on investment turns out to have negative value. Analysis showed that the more strongly participants agreed with this question, the more their growth option valuation decreased with increased uncertainty (Spearman $r = - 0 . 4 6 6 , p < 0 . 0 5$ one-tailed for comparison questions, and Spearman $r = - 0 . 2 6 6$ $p = 0 . 1 5 2$ one-tailed for the decision questions).

In the deferral condition, participants anticipated that the decision to wait would produce anticipated rejoicing associated with avoiding a failed investment. The third deferral question reflected this anticipated rejoicing and was associated with increased option valuation as uncertainty increased (Spearman $r = 0 . 5 0 4$ 2 $p < 0 . 0 5$ one-tailed for the decision question, and Spearman $r = 0 . 1 4 5$ 2 $p = 0 . 2 7 1$ one-tailed for the comparison question). Thus, the postexperiment questions provide some support for the assumption that regret influenced participants’ decisions and that this influence was associated with uncertainty’s differing effect on different option types.

The motivation of the potential disadvantaged competitor hypothesis (H2) argued that (1) a significant proportion of participants would expect the potential competitor to invest in the new technology, even though the experimental materials stated that investing in the technology would be an unprofitable strategy for the potential competitor; and (2) this expectation would make participants more likely to recommend investing immediately in the deferral condition and more likely to make the initial investment in the growth condition. In experiment 1, we asked participants if they believed that the competitor would invest in the new technology as soon as possible, and if this belief influenced their decision. In the deferral condition, 12 of 16 participants had this belief, and 11 of the 12 said they were influenced by this belief to invest now rather than wait (responses higher than the scale midpoint) (binomial test comparing proportions to 50 percent, $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . In the growth condition, 10 of 17 participants believed the competitor would invest, and 7 of the 10 were influenced by this belief to make the initial investment (responses lower than the scale midpoint). Although this was not a significant majority (binomial test comparing proportions to 50 percent, $\begin{array} { r } { p = 0 . 3 4 4 ) } \end{array}$ , it was still far from the zero level that would occur if participants did not expect or display aggressively competitive behavior.

In the second experiment, participants were asked on an 11-point Likert scale whether they agreed or disagreed that the competitor would invest as soon as possible. Twentythree of 37 participants agreed that the potential competitor would invest as soon as possible. These 23 participants’ investment choices were significantly influenced by the potential competitor’s presence (Fisher exact test, $p < 0 . 0 0 1 $ ). In the deferral condition, they were more likely to invest sooner (value the option less) under potential competition than under monopoly, and in the growth condition, they were more likely to make the initial investment (value the option more) under potential competition. Thus, we conclude that our reasoning for the potential disadvantaged competitor hypothesis (H2) is generally supported.

An alternative hypothesis could be that participants made directionally incorrect decisions in the growth scenario because of a naive rejection of all negative NPV projects. The postexperiment questions also allowed us to check this. In both experiments, most participants disagreed with the statement that “a company should never invest in something that has a negative net present value” (experiment 1, 14 of 17, binomial test, exact p-value < 0.05; experiment 2, 12 of 17 binomial test, exact $p \mathrm { - v a l u e } = 0 . 1 4 3 )$ ). Despite the high p-value for experiment 2, in both experiments, the naive rejection was not significantly associated with making the wrong directional decision (experiment 1, uncertainty decision question Spearman $r = 0 . 1 9 0$ , one-tailed $p { = } 0 . 2 3 2$ ; experiment 2, uncertainty decision question Spearman $r = 0 . 0 6 4$ , one-tailed $p = 0 . 4 0 4 )$ . Thus, overall, we believe that a naive rejection of NPV projects was not a reason for our results.<sup>17</sup>

## Sensitivity Analysis

We also performed a sensitivity analysis to examine the effect of observations that were clustered near the neutral midpoint. Participants who responded near the neutral point might have been confused and unsure of their decisions. By including their responses, we could be exaggerating the definiteness of their beliefs by classifying them into clear-cut categories of “believes investing now is more valuable under high uncertainty,” “believes investing now is less valuable under low uncertainty,” and so on. For experiment 1, we first reclassified decision question responses that were a 5 or 7 as a neutral 6 and recalculated the dependent variable. The increasing payoff uncertainty hypothesis (H1) and the potential disadvantaged competitor hypothesis (H2) were still supported. Then we further classified responses that were a 4 or 8 as a 6. This resulted in continued support for the increasing payoff uncertainty hypothesis (H1), but the potential disadvantaged competitor hypothesis (H2) was only marginally significant $( p = 0 . 0 7 )$ . Because the scale in experiment 2 did not have a neutral midpoint, we reclassified responses that were a 5 or 6 as a 5.5, and then further classified responses that were a 4 or 7 as a 5.5. In all cases for experiment 2, the increasing payoff uncertainty hypothesis (H1) and the potential disadvantaged competitor hypothesis (H2) were still supported at conventional levels of significance.<sup>18</sup>

## Discussion, Limitations, and Conclusions

Man agerial in tuition is a l ow-cost an d often effective basis for judging the approximate value of flexibility in IT investments, but intuition is also subject to identifiable weaknesses. Triantis considers unintended mistakes in investment decision making stemming from judgment biases as one of the “key issues of managerial behavior that need to be addressed” [77, p. 13] in real options research.<sup>19</sup> Prior field and survey research on IT investments has found that managers’ judgments are often qualitatively consistent with normative real options values but also diverge systematically (i.e., are biased away) from normative values for particular option types and economic settings. Explanations of these divergences have remained largely speculative, however. They are difficult to test in field settings because researchers are unlikely to find an adequate number of investment cases that differ from each other only with respect to option type and not with respect to other confounding factors.

This study complements the field literature on IT real options by providing and testing theoretically based predictions of directional consistency between intuitive judgment and the prescriptions of real options theory for two option types—deferral and growth. We find that as uncertainty increases, individuals judge deferral options as more valuable but growth options as less valuable; the deferral option judgments are consistent and the growth option judgments are inconsistent with real options theory. The reluctance to take advantage of high-uncertainty growth options is consistent with observations from field research [11] and practice [42]. We also find that a competitor’s presence decreases judged deferral option values while increasing growth option values, even when a normative response to a rational potential competitor would result in no change in the option values. This pattern of behavior is consistent not only with behavioral economic theories but also with rules of thumb supporting more conservative investment (more deferral, less growth) in response to increased uncertainty, and more aggressive investment (less deferral, more growth) in response to potential competition. These rules of thumb are intuitively plausible but are directionally consistent with real options prescriptions for only some, but not all, option types and settings.

## Limitations

A common concern in experimental studies is generalizability to environments with different investment and decision-maker characteristics. In particular, critics have sometimes attributed experimental findings of judgment bias to participants’ lack of expertise or the artificiality of the laboratory setting. Concerns about artificiality typically include the simplified tasks and the relatively low incentives for optimal decisions provided in the laboratory. We discuss each of these issues as limitations to generalizability next.

First, our investment scenario is relatively simple compared to many IT investments. There is, however, no obvious way in which simplicity as such would account for the pattern of decisions we observe, and no obvious reason that a more complex setting would (for example) result in more investment in high-uncertainty growth options. Complexity could, however, generate other more important decision errors that would mask the effects we observe in this experiment.

Second, the experiment held a number of investment characteristics constant across all conditions, resulting in a clean test of the predictions but also the possibility that the results are parameter specific or setting specific. Because regret and competitive behavior effects have been demonstrated with a variety of settings and parameters, there is little reason to suppose that these would be eliminated by many changes to our experimental setting, such as different sources of uncertainty (e.g., market rather than technological uncertainty). Two elements of the setting seem likely to be important, however. Regret effects can be much reduced when decision makers believe they will never know the outcome of alternative decisions [82]. Thus, decision patterns might be different for unique technologies that, if not adopted by the decision maker, will not be adopted by others. In addition, we speculate that the strong aversion to highuncertainty growth options in the experiment is due partly to loss aversion. If the standard deviation of high-uncertainty cash flows (i.e., the uncertainty) had been the same but the worst-case payoff for the follow-on had been zero rather than a negative number, the growth option results might well have been less extreme. Our pattern of results may therefore be less likely to occur in settings where the payoff distribution of high-risk investments does not include significant losses.

Third, the incentives we provided were modest. However, the deviations from expected utility maximization (and, thus, option pricing theory) that we observe are unlikely to be eliminated by larger incentives alone. Recent research in behavioral finance finds high-stakes investment behavior in the stock market that displays the same biases as undergraduates making hypothetical decisions in simplified settings (see [6, 33] for reviews). A review by Camerer and Hogarth [18] finds no replicated studies that reject EU theory in favor of well-specified alternatives when there are low stakes but support EU when there are high stakes. Camerer and Hogarth [18] also observe that although higher incentives do not eliminate judgment biases, they can change the mean and variance of observed bias. Thus, it is likely that the means and variances in our results would not replicate exactly to higher-incentive settings.

Fourth, our experiment participants had some experience with IT and investment decisions, but they were early career managers, not senior decision makers. Although more professional experience would improve their judgment in many ways, professional experience in general does not eliminate EU theory violations such as those represented in regret theory and prospect theory. Research on financial markets has found substantial evidence of investment behavior consistent with such judgment biases even among experienced investment professionals [43, 59, 66].<sup>20</sup> What professional experience provides, and this experiment does not capture, is a wealth of task-relevant factual knowledge and situation-specific heuristics that can support superior managerial performance in specific decision components and specific settings. Managers with industry-specific and technology-specific experience would have been far superior to our experiment participants in estimating expected cash flows and their risks. Making these estimates, however, was not the task in the present experiment. Moreover, being able to make these estimates well does not logically entail indifference to regret or to potential competitors and, thus, does not entail superior performance on the experi mental task of using the estimates provided.

The most important limitation of using less-experienced participants is that professional experience provides managers with situation-specific heuristics that can mitigate the effects of judgment biases in familiar, limited settings (e.g., [4, 35]). This knowledge tends to be “brittle,” that is, not to “travel well” from one situation to another, however [31, 32]. When the heuristics tailored to a previous setting perform poorly in new settings, experienced individuals often fall back on their basic instincts, resulting in judgment biases similar to those displayed by less-experienced individuals [23, 32, 35].<sup>21</sup> Thus, we would anticipate that in stable settings, managerial intuition unconstrained by real options thinking could include heuristics that mitigate overaggressive competitive behavior or excessive aversion to high-uncertainty growth options in less competitive settings. As noted above, judgment-bias effects can exist even among experienced professionals, but they are likely to be stronger in more volatile environments where situation-specific heuristics fail [23].

## Implications for Practice

Field and survey research has documented that consistency between managerial intuition and real option value varies across option types [11, 75, 76], but the reasons for these differences have often been unclear. Understanding why consistency differs across option types can help organizations create better tools and processes for IT investment decision making. Focusing on option types and settings where unaided decisions are more likely to err and directly addressing the causes of errors can make these tools and processes more effective.

Correcting faulty managerial intuition is a double-edged problem, however. On one hand, real options–based methods (whether full-scale formal analysis, real options– based heuristic models, or qualitative “real options thinking”) provide more benefit when they correctly support different decisions than unconstrained intuition. On the other hand, real options–based decisions are likely to meet with more resistance in organizations when they recommend decisions that are inconsistent with managers “gut feel.” Real options methods and training need to be tailored to the fact that some real options–based recommendations will “feel right” to managers, while other results will “feel wrong” and will require more extensive and persuasive presentation.

The behavioral economic research introduced in this study provides some techniques by which organizations can limit unwanted effects of regret and overaggressive competitive behavior. For example, overaggressive competitive behavior is higher when decision makers pay more attention to their own competencies than their competitor’s [54]. More balanced attention can be encouraged in investment decision processes. Similarly, regret effects can be exacerbated by decision makers’ concerns that others, as well as themselves, will take a negative view of them when a decision comes out badly, even if it was an optimal decision ex ante [8]. Such “outcome effects” can be mitigated by limiting outcome-based incentives [28] or by forcing consideration during the performance evaluation process of the other possible outcomes that could have occurred [2], or by making sure that the evaluator has some involvement in the evaluatee’s original decision process [16].

Finally, firms may be able to lessen or avoid deviations from real options prescriptions in other ways. For example, UPS provides additional encouragement to staged investment in uncertain IT projects by allocating specific funds to uncertain investments [62]. This procedure could be used to reduce the propensity to underinvest in high-uncertainty growth options that our study illustrates.

It is likely that many option-type-specific judgment effects exist besides those addressed in the current study. These effects can influence firms’ optimal choices among formal real options analysis, quantitative heuristic models, “real options thinking,” and intuitive adjustments to NPV. What these effects and their sources are, and how best to mitigate the decision errors they generate, remain to be investigated by future research.

## Appendix A: Regret Theory and Prospect Theory

Regret theory an d prospect theory are both members of the family of referencedependent utility theories [70]. Prospect theory has been more widely used in the study of financial markets [6, 33], but we believe that regret theory captures important elements of the IT investment decision-making setting that prospect theory does not.

Regret theory proposes that a decision outcome’s utility will depend not only on the payoffs received but also on how they compare with the payoff that would have been received if the decision maker had chosen an alternative action [8, 44]. Prospect theory, in contrast, proposes that a decision outcome’s utility depends on the payoffs received and how they compare to a single reference point, often the predecision status quo.

As an example of this difference and its effects, consider a manager who decides not to invest in a new technology, knowing that he or she will later discover from others experience whether the technology is a costly failure or a profitable success. We think it likely that the manager will anticipate negative feelings if the technology proves to be a success that he or she has missed and will anticipate positive feelings if it proves to be a failure that he or she has avoided.

The existence of both the negative and positive feelings is consistent with regret theory but not prospect theory, because the prospect theory reference point is not outcome dependent. Table A1 illustrates this point. If the prospect-theory reference point is the status quo (see column 1), then not investing preserves the status quo. There is neither a gain nor a loss, and the manager does not anticipate the positive and negative feelings we have ascribed to him or her. If the prospect theory reference point is higher—for example, if it is the success payoff (see column 2)—then not investing will result in a perceived loss (an outcome lower than target) regardless of whether the technology fails or succeeds elsewhere. In contrast, under regret theory (see column 3), the reference point is dependent on the outcome of the decision not taken. If the technology not chosen succeeds, that success is the reference point and the status quo preserved by not investing is disappointing (regret). If the technology not chosen fails, that failure is the reference point and the status quo preserved by not investing is seen as positive (rejoicing).

In addition, regret theory (but not prospect theory) stresses the decision maker’s sense of responsibility for the decision and concern about what others will think of him or her as a result of the outcome [55]. These factors seem likely to be characteristic of IT decision makers. Zeelenberg [80] suggests that regret is more likely to exist when the preferred alternative does not clearly dominate other alternatives, when outcome feedback will start to materialize soon after the decision is made, and when decision makers can take time to evaluate the alternatives. These factors are also characteristic of many IT investment decisions and suggest that regret theory has predictive power in this setting.
