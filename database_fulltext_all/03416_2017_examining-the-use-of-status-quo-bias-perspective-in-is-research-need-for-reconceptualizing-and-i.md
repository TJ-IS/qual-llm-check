# Examining the use of status quo bias perspective in IS research: need for reconceptualizing and incorporating biases

Kyootai Lee\* & Kailash Joshi<sup>†</sup>

\*Graduate School of Management of Technology, Sogang University, BW616, Baekbum-ro, Mapo-gu, South Korea, email: kyootai@sogang.ac.kr; kyootai@gmail.com, and <sup>†</sup>College of Business Administration, University of Missouri – St. Louis, 1 University Boulevard, St. Louis MO.63121-4499USA

Abstract. Kim & Kankanhalli introduced status quo bias perspective (SQBP) to help understand information systems (hereinafter IS) users’ resistance behaviour. Since then, scholars have widely referred to the theoretical perspective to understand user resistance to and adoption of new IS and information and communication technologies (hereinafter ICT). However, our analysis found that while adopting SQBP, researchers focused primarily on rational cost-and-bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis, rather than on the fundamental tenet of SQBP that highlights ‘bias’ in users’ decision-making on account of their cognitive limitations that lead to bounded rationality. In addition, some of the key constructs used in SQBP were not properly interpreted or were oversimpli<sup>fi</sup>ed in their operationalization. This research note aims to provide guidance for utilizing and analysing SQBP and its constructs for future IS user resistance/adoption research. Because SQBP provides unique insights into ‘bias’ in human decision-making in its presentation of bounded rationality, accurate interpretation of its concepts and their investigation can help better understand the different sources of user resistance derived from the status quo bias during new IS and ICT implementation. © 2016 Blackwell Publishing Ltd

Keywords: status quo bias, user resistance, transition cost, bounded rationality

## INTRODUCTION

IS research has focused on understanding user resistance to new IS and ICT implementations over the past several decades. On the issue of human decision-making, Samuelson & Zeckhauser (1988) made a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution in understanding the role of biases in con tributing to individuals’ tendency to stick to the status quo and resist change. In their exposition of the status quo bias perspective (SQBP), Samuelson & Zeckhauser (1988) demonstrated through several experiments that individuals are likely to disproportionally stick with the status quo. The authors explained this bias based on several theories such as prospect theory (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974), cognitive dissonance theory (Akerlof & Dickens, 1982) and self-perception theory (Bem, 1972). The tenet of SQBP is that individuals’ bias in favour of the status quo is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by not only their cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis (rational decision-making) but also other psychological factors (bounded rational decision-making). The authors identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three groups of factors that contribute to status quo bias in decision-making, namely, rational decision-making (including transition costs and uncertainty), cognitive misperception (including loss aversion and anchoring effects) and psychological commitment (including sunk costs, regret avoidance and control).

Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) utilized SQBP to study IS user resistance. They combined SQBP with other user acceptance/resistance models (e.g. the equity implementation model and technology acceptance models) to propose and empirically test a framework of user resistance. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, they stated that users tend to resist new IS adoption because of their tendency of sticking to the status quo on account of the factors identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Samuelson & Zeckhauser (1988). Since the publication of Kim and Kankanhalli’s study, as of 13th April 2015, the paper has been cited 277 times in the IS and ICT adoption and resistance studies. However, only a small portion of the studies utilized SQBP as their theoretical foundation, while the rest just mentioned it in passing. In studying resistance to the use of new IS and ICT, many researchers focused primarily on a rational cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis (e.g. switching costs/bene<sup>fi</sup>ts) and habitual behaviour. Very few, if not none, studies made concerted efforts towards recognizing and analysing the ‘bias’ in users’ decision-making arising from their cognitive limitations (e.g. bounded rationality), which should be the core of SQBP. Additionally, based on our review of these papers, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that some of the concepts in SQBP were oversimpli<sup>fi</sup>ed (perhaps misinterpreted) in some studies, diluting the role of cognitive biases in the decision-making process. This research note aims at reviewing the research that introduces and adopts SQBP and its constructs in the context of IS and ICT adoption/resistance to analyse the current understanding of SQBP and providing recommendations for advancing research on user resistance.

The next section provides a summary of the IS research that cited Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) and SQBP. Based on a review of the prior studies, we identify the prevalent interpretation of SQBP in the current IS and ICT adoption/resistance literature and provide suggestions for better understanding SQBP for the future IS and ICT adoption/resistance research.

## CURRENT UNDERSTANDING OF SQBP IN THE COMMUNITY

Through Google Scholar, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 277 research papers published up to 13 April 2015 that cited Kim & Kankanhalli (2009), SQBP and/or Samuelson & Zeckhauser (1988) in order to ex amine the current understanding of SQBP in the IS community. Among them, we could not analyse 55 papers because full papers could not be provided in the library and/or were not written in English. Among the remaining 222 papers, 64 were conference proceedings, and 16 were book chapters. Eleven were working papers, and 33 were dissertations or theses. Ninety-eight papers were published in international IS journals such as MISQ, ISR, ISJ, JMIS, CAIS among others. We believe that these 98 papers can be appropriate representatives of the IS and ICT adoption/resistance studies. Especially so, considering that many conference proceedings and dissertations are later published in the journals. Out of 98 papers, 14 were theoretica and/or review papers, and 84 were based on empirical research. These studies were extensively examined for the use of SQBP concepts. One of the authors and two research assistants categorized the 98 papers on their use of the SQBP concepts and the related factors. Differences in categorizations were examined, and after two iterations, a consensus was reached.

Table 1 provides a summary of the studies that used SQBP and/or referred to Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) and Samuelson & Zeckhauser (1988) on the topics of IS and ICT adoption/resistance. An analysis of aforementioned studies clearly shows that they cited Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) and/or SQBP in order mainly to explain user resistance to new IS and ICT (34.4% = 45/131 (total citations in the sample studies)) and users’ status quo bias in favour of the current IS and ICT (8.4%). Among the components of SQBP, even in rational decision-making components, they used Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) and/or SQBP in orde mainly to explain the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of perceived value based on transition costs (which also include switching costs) on the attitudinal and the behavioural responses towards new IS (19.8%). However, only a very few studies discussed uncertainty (0.8%), loss aversion (6.9%), sunk costs (3.8%), control (2.3%), anchoring effect (0%) and regret avoidance (0.8%), as shown in Table 1. Unlike Samuelson & Zeckhauser (1988) that highlighted the biases in decision-making, IS and ICT adoption/resistance studies seem to focus disproportionally on cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis.

In addition, several studies cited Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) and/or SQBP in order to explain the constructs (e.g. self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, organizational support, colleague opinion and social norms) that Samuelson & Zeckhauser (1988) did not include in their original paper. Based on a qualitative review of these studies, we realized that in adopting SQBP and its sub-constructs in IS research, some of these constructs were oversimpli<sup>fi</sup>ed (perhaps misinterpreted), where social norms and self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy were treated as regret avoidance and efforts to feel in control, respectively. Because Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) attributed social norms and self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy to SQBP, while utilizing these constructs in their research models, many other studies have also attributed them to SQBP and cited Kim and Kankanhalli. As Table 1 shows, these prior studies rarely considered the original two constructs, regret avoidance and effort to feel in control (only four studies in total); instead, they empirically identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the role of social norms, colleague opinion, organizational support and self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy (20 studies in total). Based on the previous review, the current understanding and application of SQBP constructs in the user resistance/adoption are elaborated in the following sections in detail.

## IMPORTANCE AND IDENTIFICATION OF BIAS

Literature on classical economics and psychology as well as IS argued that individuals decision-making is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by perceived value, which is a combination of costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Samuelson and Zeckhauser supported this premise and included transition costs while explaining perceived value. However, what Samuelson and Zeckhauser primarily highlighted is that the focus should not be just on the perceived value estimated by the difference between switching costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, but on the psychological factors inherent in individuals’ percep tions of the two. The following quote clearly states what Samuelson and Zeckhauser argued: ‘the explanations based on rational decision making would be inadequate for status quo inertia (p.35) and ‘although the af<sup>fi</sup>nity for the status quo might be interpreted as a rational response to real transition costs and to uncertain outcomes, the experiments suggest strongly that the answer lies elsewhere’ (p.41). In other words, the traditional rational decision-making view that focuses on the calculation of perceived values based on costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts has limitations in explaining individuals’ decision-making behaviour. Thus, following bounded rationality, Samuelson and Zeckhauser highlighted the limitations of rational economic cost/bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis and emphasized the role of bias in human decision-making.

Despite the importance of bounded rationality in human decision-making, many studies on IS and ICTacceptance/resistance focused primarily on a rational analysis of costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts as Table 1 shows, while explaining the status quo bias. In addition to the 26 studies that focus on rational value analysis, even the 11 studies that cited Kim and Kankanhalli and/or SQBP in explaining cognitive bias did not delve into the mechanism through which can distort individuals decision-making. That is, although all these studies commonly acknowledged the existence of bias in users’ adoption and usage decisions, only a few studies (e.g. Browne & Parsons, 2012; Polites & Karahanna, 2012; Hsieh, 2015) empirically or theoretically identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that bias related to cognitive misperception and psychological commitment can in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ decision to use new IS and ICT. For instance, Polites & Karahanna (2012) and Hsieh (2015) found that inertia as a surrogate for status quo bias affects intention and resistance to use, respectively. Browne & Parsons (2012) theoretical analysis stated and highlighted that bias can in<sup>fl</sup>uence users working memory (e.g. judgement, reasoning, choice and decision-making) and recognition. However, even many of these studies did not delve into how bias emerges and changes users perceived valuation of a new IS, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ decision-making. More speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, although a new IS can provide a higher perceived value, compared with a previous IS, how users’ bias (or the sources of bias) can diminish the perceived value, which can enhance their resistance, has been outside the purview of contemporary IS research up to now.

Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the bias mechanism, particularly status quo bias, can be critical because organizations have generally used many of the current IS for several years and incrementally re<sup>fi</sup>ned them. In such situations, users can become accustomed to sticking to the status quo and use the current IS habitually without much cognitive effort, showing automaticity in their behaviour (Kim et al., 2005) along with a higher level of inertia (Polites & Karahanna, 2012). One of the reasons for the lack of IS studies that empirically show the status quo bias, compared with behavioural economics and psychology, would be related to methodologies used. Unlike other disciplines that initially identi<sup>fi</sup>ed status quo bias based on their experiments, it can be dif ficult to conduct field experiments in the IS contexts (especially organizational settings). Considering the unique IS research context, we believe, the latent growth curve model approach (Endnote 1), which is used to examine developmental changes over time (Duncan et al., 2006), can be one of the useful analytical methods to identify users’ bias when researchers collect data longitudinally (where at least three time windows of measurement are required).

## REVISITING SQBP COMPONENTS AND RECOMMENDATIONS FOR FUTURESTUDIES

Table 1 provides three interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings about the use of different sources of biases. First, the research on IS and ICTadoption/resistance did not make concerted efforts to identify the underlying mechanisms related to the important SQBP constructs except cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis and/or perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Second, several prior studies seem to oversimplify and/or misinterpret some SQBP components (e.g. control and regret avoidance) while adopting and/or applying them to the IS and ICTadoption/resistance context. Third, few prior studies have addressed the role of anchoring (effects) and uncertainty, which is one of the bias sources in SQBP. These issues are discussed in detail next.

We believe that addressing and identifying these SQBP constructs and their mechanisms in the IS and ICT adoption contexts are critical in that these components can explicate how/why users are biased in favour of status quo, which can lead to their resistance to new IS and ICT. Table 2 presents a summary of the interpretations provided by Samuelson & Zeckhauser (1988) for major concepts used in SQBP and some of our recommendations for their correct interpretation and usage in future IS studies.

## Rational decision-making

Most IS studies have adopted rational decision-making perspective and focused on cost– bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis to determine users’ perceived value, which in<sup>fl</sup>uences their attitudes towards the new systems, and their acceptance/resistance to IS and ICT. Table 1 identi<sup>fi</sup>es the studies that adopted a rational cost-bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis approach. Very few studies have included uncertainty in their investigation with reference to SQBP; however, their interpretation has not always been consistent with that of SQBP. While, SQBP focuses on the cost of information search and analysis as a rational approach to resolving uncertainty, IS research has mainly considered un certainty as a psychological factor in terms of anxiety and stress arising from the unknown. Next, we discuss the SQBP’s view on uncertainly in greater detail.

## Uncertainty

Samuelson & Zeckhauser (1988) de<sup>fi</sup>ned uncertainty as the information search and analysis efforts imposed on users by the uncertainty and on their efforts to resolve it to make better informed decisions. In response to the uncertainty about the likely outcomes and/or their unknown probabilities. individuals may need to incur significant efforts (costs) in informatior search and analysis to assess the likely outcomes and their choices. When the search and analysis are not feasible, or the costs are estimated to be high and not viewed as providing fruit ful results, individuals may take no action, leading to a decision paralysis and maintaining the status quo by default.

It should be noted that there are several studies that highlight the role of uncertainty (e.g. Prekumar & Robert, 1999; Nicolaou & McKnight, 2006; Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009; Hung et al., 2010) for IS adoption, although there are few studies that focus on uncertainty based on SQBP as shown in Table 1. While we reviewed IS studies, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that most IS studies focused on uncertainty related to IS complexity, IS performance and quality. In other words, their operationalization of uncertainty represents the level of uncertainty anxiety experienced by users related to a change, which refers to the psychological uncertainty and the associated stress. Although this type of uncertainty can occur and lead to status quo bias (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009), it is distinct from the role of uncertainty as de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Samuelson & Zeckhauser (1988) in SQBP. In the IS context, users and decision makers face many uncertainties, which may play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in their maintaining the status quo and avoiding or postponing a change, in view of the cost of information search and analysis that would need to be incurred to resolve the uncertainty and reach a rational decision. Thus, although less in vestigated in the IS literature, uncertainty as de<sup>fi</sup>ned in SQBP may also play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role adoption/resistance behaviour of individuals in the IS context.

For instance, in some IS contexts, individuals may have the <sup>fl</sup>exibility of making autonomous decisions about a change. For example, a chief information of<sup>fi</sup>cer considering the adoption of new IS based on cloud architecture may act as an autonomous decision maker. In this case, the search and analysis efforts related to the resolution of uncertainty could play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in decision-making, and in the absence of adequate time and effort (costs), the decision may be simply postponed or delayed without reaching any conclusion, maintaining the status quo. Similarly, users may also have the <sup>fl</sup>exibility of making choices in IS selection (Murray & Häubl, 2011) and personal computing software/devices; both types of uncertainty can play a role in maintaining the status quo in such cases. However, when users are subject to the implementation decisions made by management, uncertainty anxiety costs are likely to be prominent in user resistance. Lack of knowledge about the future changes and the associated anxiety may impose uncertainty costs on users during implementation of a new IS, in addition to the search and analysis efforts. Thus, recognition of the different types of uncertainty and their distinct roles across different adoption contexts (cf. Polites & Karahanna, 2012) can contribute to our understanding of IS resistance behaviours.

## Cognitive misperception

## Loss aversion

The tenet of loss aversion is that individuals are likely to weigh losses greater than gains (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). This notion is clearly indicated in the following sentence in Kahneman & Tversky (1979, p. 288): ‘people are unlikely to (simply) perform the operation of subtracting the cost from the outcomes in deciding whether to buy a gamble’ (clarifying parentheses added). Rather, individuals would assess the utility of a probabilistic pay-off against that of the costs they face and consider the disutility of the same level of cost to be higher than the utility of the same level of gain. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of loss aversion can be particularly important when implementing new systems (Polites & Karahanna, 2012). In the context of IS and ICT implementation, as the dissimilarity between the current and the new ones increases, replacing the current IS is likely to become more and more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and costly for users, because their disutility from the loss of the current IS may loom larger than the utility gained from the new IS be cause of loss aversion (cf. Okada, 2001).

Despite the importance of loss aversion in the human decision-making process, IS scholars have not explicitly focused on it. Prior IS studies have limitations in capturing the relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of gains and losses and their non-linear utility functions. They assume that users’ net bene<sup>fi</sup>t calculations would implicitly include individuals’ in<sup>fl</sup>ated perception of costs arising from loss aversion. For instance, even though some IS scholars (e.g. Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009; Polites & Karahanna, 2012) admit the possible in<sup>fl</sup>uence of loss aversion on perceived IS value, none of the IS (if not a few) studies have empirically examined the mechanism of loss aversion. Based on our view, only one study (e.g. Li & Cheng, 2014) explicitly viewed loss aversion as a psychological construct that individuals tend to weigh losses higher than gains and directly measured it. However, while measuring loss aversion, the study asked the respondents to compare IS util ity with their costs and efforts (e.g. Compared with the utility of … , I care more about time costs …). The measures used in this study do not consider costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts separately, which independently in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ decision-making and behaviours. Thus, we believe that the measure confounds the constructs of costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Edwards, 1991). Therefore, the extent of the effect of loss aversion is yet to be fully identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the IS context.

Loss aversion is viewed as a human tendency that in<sup>fl</sup>uences individuals’ value assessment and decisions in the trade-off between costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Kahneman et al., 1991). If we treat loss aversion simply as an implicit in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the form of ‘a method of assessment’ (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009), it is not possible to isolate its in<sup>fl</sup>uence and examine its role in users’ assessment of change. To assess it, researchers would need to make efforts to operationalize and identify the effects of loss aversion by assessing and comparing the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of costs and gains on a decision. On the same lines, psychologists and economists (e.g. Kahneman & Tversky, 1984; Kahneman et al., 1991) have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed loss aversion by demonstrating and comparing that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of loss on decision is greater than that of gains.

There can be two options that can help researchers scrutinize loss aversion. First, researchers can assess if decision makers assign greater weight to costs, rather than bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (or savings), in their decisions regarding a new IS or technology. If the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the costs outweighs that of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, it can suggest support for loss aversion. Similar to this sugges tion, Hsu et al. (2010) compared the slope of loss with that of gain based on $\chi ^ { 2 }$ difference and graphically depicted that customer loyalty reduces more steeply when customers experience service loss. Second, because the ‘value function is generally concave for gains and commonly convex for losses’ (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979, p. 279), polynomial regression with response surface analysis (Edwards & Parry, 1993) can possibly examine unique aspects related to loss aversion by showing combined non-linear patterns (convex and concave) in a single equation. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we can create the following equation:

$$
\text { Value } = b _ {0} + b _ {1} \text { COST } + b _ {2} \text { GAIN } + b _ {3} \text { COST } ^ {2} + b _ {4} \text { COST } * \text { GAIN } + b _ {5} \text { GAIN } ^ {2} + e\tag{1}
$$

If COST and GAIN are measured separately, the correlation between the two can be calcu lated as follows:

$$
G A I N = r ^ {*} C O S T\tag{2}
$$

Therefore, Equation 1 can be transformed to

$$
\begin{array}{l} V a l u e = b _ {0} + b _ {1} C O S T + b _ {2} r C O S T + b _ {3} C O S T ^ {2} + b _ {4} C O S T ^ {*} r C O S T + b _ {5} r ^ {2} C O S T ^ {2} + e \\ = b _ {0} + (b _ {1} + b _ {2} r) C O S T + (b _ {3} + b _ {4} r + b _ {5} r ^ {2}) C O S T ^ {2} + e \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

$$

## Anchoring effect

Table 1 indicates that prior IS adoption and resistance studies have not investigated anchoring effects. Even studies such as Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) and Polites & Karahanna (2012), which adopted SQBP as major part of their theoretical framework, did not include anchoring effects in their research model. Of course, it may not be necessary to include all the applicable constructs in a research framework. However, we believe that anchoring effect is a key cognitive misperception that can impact users’ perceptions and resistance behaviour particularly in the new IS and ICT adoption contexts as discussed next. And, considering the lack of studies in this area, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of anchoring effects can open a new avenue for understanding IS adoption and resistance.

Anchoring effects refer to individuals’ propensity of setting a starting value and then assessing changes with reference to the initial state (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). Although they have not been investigated, anchoring effects can be viewed as analogous to some o the factors identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the IS adoption studies based on expectation–discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory (e.g. Bhattacherjee, 2001; Staples et al., 2002; Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010) and habit theory (e.g. Kim et al., 2005; Lee et al., 2011; Polites & Karahanna, 2012). Because the implementation of new IS generally involves the replacement of currently used systems nowadays, users may form expectations for the new IS based on their knowledge and experiences of past implementations. Especially, as the use of the current IS becomes habituated, such use can form a stronger anchor to evaluate new systems, which can in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ view of switching costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (cf. Polites & Karahanna, 2012) and sunk costs.

Psychologists and economists (e.g. Hertwig et al., 2004; Harman & Gonzalez, 2015) more recently found that decision-making does not always follow prospect theory. Particularly, for rare events, the costs may not be overweighed. For instance, Hertwig et al. (2004) found that in the case of decisions from description, people make choices as if they overweight the probability of rare events, con<sup>fi</sup>rming prospect theory. On the contrary, in the case of decisions from experience, individuals make choices as if they under weigh the probability of rare events because they depend more on a relatively small sample of information as well as more recently sampled information. In a similar vein, in the IS contexts, users may also form an anchor based primarily on a small sample of information gained in their recent experiences with the existing IS and ICT and/or form expectations about the new IS based on the descriptive information provided by their organization. In Figure 1, researchers can operationalize $I _ { 4 }$ and $I _ { 4 }$ as anchors that users may use for assessing the utility of new IS. That is, if users form their anchors based mainly on their experiences with the IS1, researchers may use $I _ { 4 }$ as an anchor and analyse the rela tionship between $I _ { 4 }$ and $S I O p e _ { 2 }$ to examine the anchoring effect of users’ experiences on the dynamics of intention to use (continue to use). However, if users build their anchors based pri marily on their expectation about IS2, researchers may use $I _ { 4 }$ as an anchor and analyse the relationship between $I _ { 4 }$ and $S I O p e _ { 2 }$ to assess the anchoring effect of users’ expectation on the dynamics of intention to use (continue to use). That is, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of costs on IS adoption decision-making may not always follow prospect theory, rather may depend on how users form their anchors based on their adoption contexts. Thus, the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the anchoring mechanisms (e.g. how users form their anchors and how the anchors can in<sup>fl</sup>uence IS adoption, value perception and usage) can extend our understanding of IS and ICT adoption/use/resistance behaviours.

## Psychological commitment Sunk costs

Although classical economic and normative decision theories explain that sunk costs should be irrelevant to future decisions because they are already incurred and cannot be changed by fu ture action (Whyte, 1991), Samuelson and Zeckhauser presented sunk cost as a psychological commitment that may influence individuals' intention to stick to their current course of action even if it is contrary to the rational choice based on costs/bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Thus, Samuelson and Zeckhauser viewed a direct role for sunk costs in motivating status quo bias, which would imply a direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence of sunk costs on user resistance.

However, similar to the case of loss aversion, very a few prior IS studies (3.8% as shown in Table 1) empirically identify the unique role of sunk costs in studying adoption, use and/or resistance behaviours. It should be noted that although many studies on IS/ICT adoption and resistance have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the role of sunk costs (generally negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on adoption) even without SQBP background, very few (if not none) investigated how they can create sunk costs as a bias factor, which can distort perceived value of new IS/ICT on top of transition costs. For instance, Hu et al. (2011), Maier et al. (2014) and Li & Cheng (2014) addressed sunk costs but did not include it as a part of their research model. Based on our view, Polites & Karahanna (2012) can be the only study that investigated the role of sunk costs independent from that of transition costs based on SQBP. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the authors measured it as the amount of time and effort to learn to use the current IS and found that both sunk costs and transition costs affect inertia, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence perceived ease of use and relative advantage.

When adopting new IS. users' learning efforts, experience and expertise in the current IS would represent sunk costs (Polites & Karahanna, 2012; Ray et al., 2012). Sunk costs can impact users’ acceptance of/resistance to the new IS in two ways. First, based on the classical costs–bene<sup>fi</sup>ts analysis, sunk costs as a part of switching costs can reduce the value of a new IS and therefore increase resistance to the new IS (e.g. Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009). It should be noted that the effect of sunk costs may not be at the same level as that of transaction costs on utility perceptions (Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988) and that its relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence should be assessed in future research. Second, closer to what Samuelson and Zeckhauser argued, even if the value of a new IS is high enough to adopt, psychological commitment derived from sunk costs may prompt users to resist it (e.g. Jones et al., 2002; Polites & Karahanna, 2012). For instance, when users adopt totally new IS, they may perceive higher levels of sunk costs compared with the incrementally improved versions of existing IS. If IS studies consider the difference in the perceived sunk costs between the two situations, they may be better able to identify the mechanism through which sunk costs in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ responses.

## Regret avoidance

Samuelson and Zeckhauser (p. 38) stated

individuals tend to avoid consequences in which they could appear after the fact to have made the wrong choice, even if in advance the decision appeared correct given the information available at the time. \~ \~ It (avoidance of decision regret) favors adherence to status quo norms or routine behavior at the expense of innovation, and it reinforces to the individual’s inclination to conform to social norms.

Samuelson and Zeckhauser viewed regret avoidance as a psychological commitment, which prompts individuals to avoid making decisions that they may regret later, and considered socia norms as a factor that in<sup>fl</sup>uences regret avoidance, on account of possible loss, which in turn leads to status quo bias. Thus here social norms appear to be represented by the organizational tolerance for risk taking and failure. Similarly, in the IS context, when making decisions such as a new IS adoption, individuals are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced not only by the expected utility from the alternatives but also by possible regret for making a wrong choice that they may regret later, for example, in selecting a new software or device.

In the IS context, although they did not utilize SQBP and Kim and Kankanhalli, Hung et al. (2007, p. 2096) argued that in addition to maximizing positive decision outcomes, decision makers frequently consider potential regret as a relevant factor and operationalized regret as ‘negative cognitive response resulting from comparing an actual result with a better one that was passed up by the decision-maker’. The authors adapted a scale from Tsiros (1998) with items such as ‘I feel sorry for having chosen…’ and ‘I feel regretful for having chosen…’. The authors identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that users’ regret is a critical factor that forms their attitudes towards accepting new IS and also found that gender and job responsibility can in<sup>fl</sup>uence regret avoidance in the context of new IS adoption. In addition, some of the likelihood of regret can depend on how any implementation failures would be viewed in the organization. which is contingent on organizational and social norms and cultures (L ankton & Luft 2008 Kane & Labianca, 2011). That is, taking risks in adopting new IS can be favourably viewed in organizations with an innovative culture, while it can be viewed more unfavourably when something goes wrong in organizations with a conservative culture. Thus, at the individual level, sticking to the current status and following the organizational norms can also help minimize possible regret.

Unlike the original notion of regret avoidance, prior studies including Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) seem to equate regret avoidance with social norms (operationalized as a colleague’s opinion and organizational support for change). Such operationalization of regret avoidance may have led to the current situation where scholars have not focused on regret avoidance (0 studies) but treated regret avoidance as social norms (6 studies), organizational support (6 studies) and colleague opinion (3 studies) that are favourable to adopt new IS, since the publi cation of Kim & Kankanhalli (2009). In addition to the disproportionate emphasis on social norms, such mis-operationalization can mislead future studies and cut off future research on re gret avoidance in IS literature because of the following reasons.

First and foremost, there is ample support in the literature for the role of social norms in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing user acceptance and/or resistance (e.g. Hong et al., 2011; Polites & Karahanna, 2012; Cheon et al., 2015). If the concept of regret avoidance is viewed as identical to that of social norms, the results related to social norms in prior studies can be mistakenly interpreted as those pertaining to regret avoidance.

Second, the role of social norms is contingent on the context. For instance, Lapointe & Rivard (2005) and Doolin (2004) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that IS user groups have negative attitudes to new IS, while Polites & Karahanna (2012), Cheon et al. (2015) and Baker-Eveleth & Stone (2013) recognized that other users’ support for new IS can form positive attitudes and enhance a user’s accep tance. Hence, whether users regret adopting or not adopting a new IS can also be contingent on whether social norms are (un)favourable to adoption. Thus regret avoidance and social norms can have distinct roles in understanding user resistance.

Third, an accurate interpretation of regret avoidance can also permit a better understanding of why/how social norms influence users' resistance to new IS. Even if adhering to organizational norms can minimize individuals’ regret (Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988), the level of in<sup>fl</sup>uence of norms on users’ decisions should be dependent on their propensity for regret avoidance, seeking social desirability (Lönnqvist et al., 2006; Hung et al., 2007) and other personal characteristics (Hung et al., 2007). That is, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of pro-adoption social norms on resistance to change may be lower for those who have high levels of self-esteem and willing to accept a relatively high level of regret (Hellström et al., 2002), compared with those who tend to seek a lower level of regret.

## Control

While citing Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) and/or SQBP, many studies considered control to be identical to self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy as shown in Table 1. However, Samuelson and Zeckhauser considered efforts to feel in control as an individual’s authority to control autonomous decision-making sit uations, rather than an individual’s ability to cope with the situation arising from the change. Thus there is a fundamental difference between the two concepts. The following excerpt from Samuelson & Zeckhauser (1988, pp. 40–41, emphasis added) clearly indicates the origina meaning of efforts to feel in control:

Making a decision enforces the individual’s perception that he or she controls the situation. … the average price in the ‘no choice’ group was found to be \$1.96 (about what one might expect). But the average price in the ‘choice’ group was \$8.67, more than four times the fair price.

The correspondence between effort to feel in control and self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy can be attributed to the non-recognition of the distinction between behavioural and decisional control. Behavioural control refers to a person’s belief in the performance of a behaviour (Ajzen & Madden, 1986), also called self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy. On the other hand, decisional control means authority to choose outcomes or to in<sup>fl</sup>uence decisions (Averill, 1973). Decisional control has been operationalized as a proxy of procedural justice and participation in decision-making in IS research (Hunton, 1996). Hence, the construct ‘control’ needs to be understood clearly in the IS context as numerous prior IS studies investigated the impact of perceived control and participative decision-making on users attitudes (e.g. Hunton, 1996; Shayo et al., 1999; Carr, 2007; He & King, 2008) and the role of self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in the context of new IS adoption and use (e.g. Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Agarwa et al., 2000). Treating self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and decisional control as identical concepts can mislead future research to view and operationalize these two variables interchangeably. For instance, while referring to Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) and SQBP as a theoretical framework, Yang et al. (2011) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumers’ switching behaviours. However, SQBP’s original notion does not include self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy.

As an illustration, if an organization permits and reimburses its managers to choose any carrier and mobile phone for their use, it would mean that managers would have control over their choices. However, the speci<sup>fi</sup>c features (e.g. calendar or video conference apps) they actually use would depend on their self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy. Moreover, as discussed in the section on Regret Avoidance, mixing the two distinct constructs and their interchangeable usages may dilute the underpinning mechanisms of the two variables. As the adoption and use contexts (mandated vs autonomous use context) are likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the IS use behaviours (Hsieh et al., 2012), we believe, reviewing how adoption/use/resistance behaviours are contingent on the level of decision control (mandated vs. autonomous use contexts) can help researchers to identify the bias from control and its in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user behaviours.

## CONCLUSION

Because Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) introduced SQBP for understanding user resistance, the theoretical foundations of SQBP and its components have not been fully utilized in the IS/ICT adoption/resistance studies, although it has been widely referred to. In some IS studies, SQBP has been referred to examine the role of habit, which has been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as an important factor with the potential to explain user post-adoption behaviours (Liao et al., 2006; Lee et al., 2011; Polites & Karahanna, 2012). However, the fundamental premise of SQBP in highlighting the role of cognitive biases in individual decision-making remains to be accurately interpreted and utilized in IS research. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, while employing SQBP as a theoretical foundation and using some of its sub-components for examining user resistance, ‘bias’ and bounded rationality highlighted in SQBP have not been investigated in IS studies. Instead, IS studies seem to have emphasized and investigated mainly cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis. As SQBP provides guidance on the sources of bias and the underlying mechanisms in the decision-making process, which contribute to status quo, focusing on user biases can provide new directions for IS research on user resistance. Especially, because several SQBP related constructs (e.g. loss aversion, regret avoidance and anchoring effects) have still not been utilized or investigated in prior IS studies, SQBP can expand opportunities for IS research.

This research note makes some important contributions to IS research. First, it highlights and elaborates the key tenets and components of SQBP that can be useful in advancing research on user acceptance/resistance to new IS implementations. Some of these have not been rec ognized or addressed in the IS research, although they have a great potential to help better understand user acceptance/resistance behaviours. Second, a detailed survey of IS and ICT adoption/resistance literature identi<sup>fi</sup>es the major focus of SQBP related IS research since its introduction in 2009. The review identi<sup>fi</sup>es two important weaknesses to be addressed in the fu ture research. One weakness is the almost exclusive focus on cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis in the current IS literature, while the main emphasis of SQBP is on cognitive biases in explaining resistance, which have been largely ignored in the IS research. The other weakness is that of misconceptualizing key SQBP factors, regret avoidance and efforts to feel in control, and viewing them as identical to social norms and self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, respectively, thus undermining and confounding the distinct role of the two set of important factors. Thus, this research note identi<sup>fi</sup>es the correct interpretation of the concepts and helps set the future research on the right track. Third, to carry forward research on SQBP factors in the context of IS implementation and user adoption/resistance, this study suggests some new methods (e.g. latent growth curve mode and polynomial regression with response surface analysis), which may help identify and assess the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of status quo bias factors related to cognitive misperception and psychological commitment. Overall, we believe that this research note should help draw attention to SQBP in future IS adoption/resistance studies and help in conceptualizing and analysing some of its core constructs more accurately, thereby encouraging effective use of SQBP for understanding user resistance behaviours.
