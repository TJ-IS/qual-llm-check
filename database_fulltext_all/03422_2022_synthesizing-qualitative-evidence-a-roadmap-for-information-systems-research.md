# Synthesizing Qualitative Evidence: A Roadmap for Information Systems Research

## Abstract

Qualitative synthesis research is an approach that consolidates the output of different qualitative studies to create new subject knowledge. Such work can help reveal more powerful explanations than that seen in a single study, thereby generating increased levels of understanding of a given phenomenon and greater research finding generalizability. Based on a review of the literature and a survey of qualitative researchers, we found that the information systems (IS) domain lacks a clear understanding of qualitative synthesis methods and, as a result, has largely failed to take advantage of this powerful, high-potential methodological opportunity. To address this shortcoming, this paper is the first to provide a rigorous overview of the full suite of 35 qualitative synthesis methods, as well as guidelines that include a three-tiered selection framework. By using the guidelines and framework in tandem, IS researchers are able to select the qualitative synthesis method most appropriate for a given research study, particularly when the research objective involves knowledge integration/aggregation, interpretation/theory development, and/or informing IS practice.

Keywords: Qualitative Synthesis, Metasynthesis, Meta, Synthesis, Qualitative, Research Method, Research Design, Knowledge Integration, Aggregation, Theory Development, Evidence-Based Practice

## 1 Introduction

Since the publication of Noblit and Hare’s influential book “Meta-ethnography” in 1988, a diverse set of academic disciplines have used qualitative synthesis techniques to integrate findings from across studies to generate new subject knowledge. Subsequently, breakthrough knowledge advances have been made in a variety of areas such as the social sciences (Major & Savin-Baden, 2010), computer science (Douglas et al., 2008; Dybå & Dingsøyr, 2008), and healthcare (Rains et al., 2020<sup>1</sup>). Given the significant impact of this synthesis form of research, it is therefore surprising that, with notable exceptions, the information systems (IS) discipline remains largely unaware of “qualitative synthesis.” <sup>2</sup>

It is important to recognize that IS researchers often deal with novel and varying technologies across heterogeneous contexts. Conducting case and field studies on the same technologies over time calls for the systematic condensation of evidence, thereby enabling the development of novel theoretical insights based on empirical data (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015). Thus, the IS discipline’s relative inexperience with qualitative synthesis is all the more frustrating given that in those IS studies where it has been applied, <sup>3</sup> its significant impact has been widely recognized (Berente et al., 2019).

In considering the consequences of the IS field becoming able to exploit this “new” qualitative methodology, one potential outcome would be to nourish the discipline’s burgeoning interest in qualitative research (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2020): “the use of new combinations and innovative styles of qualitative approaches are always welcome” (Sarker et al., 2013). However, such opportunity and exploitation come at a cost because qualitative synthesis guidelines are needed that align with the IS field’s predetermined level of methodological understanding. In this particular context, guideline creation is challenging, not only because acquiring this understanding requires input from a large number of IS qualitative researchers, but also because a multitude of qualitative synthesis methods exist from which to make a method selection. In referring to “qualitative synthesis,” this paper is solely denoting the broad, generic process that consolidates the output of different qualitative studies to create new subject knowledge. Within this overarching context, the authors have subsequently identified 35 individual methods, each providing a discrete approach to synthesizing qualitative evidence.

Given the extent of methods from which to choose, selection guidelines are required so that the most suitable qualitative synthesis method for a given research study can be determined. Consequently, this paper’s aim is to provide method selection guidelines to help IS researchers choose one or more of the 35 methods that best meets their needs and, further, provide reviewers with a conceptual framework with which to evaluate qualitative synthesis research. Inevitably, a concurrent objective is to provide a rigorous overview of qualitative synthesis so that IS stakeholders are able to familiarize themselves better with the different methods. This paper’s overarching goal is thus to familiarize the IS domain with knowledge of qualitative synthesis and thereby provide greater awareness of its methodological potential. Accordingly, an associated more strategic objective of this paper is to initiate an inclusive discussion on how best to undertake qualitative synthesis, thereby broadening the IS discipline’s knowledge via the multiple perspectives of its supporters. That is, we hope the knowledge we provide about qualitative synthesis will be used as a foundation for the evolving use of qualitative synthesis within the IS setting. To achieve this end, this paper necessarily devotes considerable attention to the methodological essence that defines “qualitative synthesis” as the overriding means for initiating this discussion. We therefore discuss these and other methodological principles in depth.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 introduces the origins of qualitative synthesis, reviews the characteristics of each synthesis method, and evaluates arguments leveled against qualitative synthesis use. In Section 3, we introduce a prescribed three-tier framework with accompanying detailed guidelines to help researchers navigate the process of selecting the appropriate qualitative synthesis method. Section 4 concludes with a discussion that includes limitations and recommendations for future research.

## 2 Qualitative Synthesis Research

## 2.1 The Origins of Qualitative Synthesis

Qualitative synthesis first attracted the attention of academic researchers via Noblit and Hare’s (1988) anthropological work on meta-ethnography. This method <sup>4</sup> involves selecting appropriate empirical studies to be synthesized, reading and rereading them, and recording the key concepts, or interpretive metaphors, that emerge. These metaphors consequently become the raw data for the synthesis, which is achieved through a reciprocal translation across all the studies that examine how the metaphors relate. Given that this translation process is idiomatic rather than a word-for-word translation, metaethnography’s objective is to derive new concepts that incorporate findings from more than any one of the studies being synthesized. Noblit and Hare (1988) suggest three forms of synthesis that are distinguished by how the primary papers being synthesized are related: reciprocal, refutational, and line of argument. The simplest use occurs when accounts across studies are similar because synthesis is achieved using reciprocal translation. Conversely, should accounts be conflicted, then a refutational synthesis can be constructed. The final synthesis type, the line of argument synthesis, is characterized by Noblit and Hare (1988) as “essentially about inference: What can we say of the whole (organization, culture, and so on.), based on the selective studies of the parts?” (p. 62).

As a notable example of qualitative synthesis and how its use can benefit the IS domain, Jasperson et al’s (2002) review of the causal structure and relationship between IT and organizational power is considered a seminal study on power and IS. Awarded the MIS Quarterly 2002 Best Paper of the Year, this paper uses metatriangulation as its methodology of choice. Metatriangulation is a threephased, qualitative synthesis process incorporating groundwork, data analysis, and theory building phases (Saunders et al., 2003). The authors’ use of metatriangulation is derived from its capability as “a theory-building process that assists researchers in recognizing, cultivating, and accommodating diverse paradigmatic insights (Gioia & Pitre 1990; Lewis & Grimes 1999).” (p. 405). As Jasperson et al. (2002) demonstrate, metatriangulation is an exceptional too for guiding an interpretation of the literature investigating the role of power in the management and use of IT and understanding the theoretical perspectives therein: “metatriangulation helps us view the multiple conceptualizations of the relationships between power and IT” (p. 427). The value of this paper therefore arises from it being able to describe the patterns emerging from previous power and IT studies, thereby providing the IS domain with an extensive and wide-ranging examination of IS research in the area of power and its role in different IT settings. In this context, the authors demonstrate metatriangulaton’s value, demonstrating “how metatriangulation can be applied to facilitate understanding complex phenomenon from various perspectives” (p. 426). Supporting Jasperson et al (2002), Saunders et al (2003) provide detailed methodological guidelines for exploiting meta-triangulation based on its use within Jasperson et al (2002).

Although meta-ethnography is perhaps the most established method for synthesizing qualitative data, other methods quickly followed its inception, including metatheory (Zhao, 1991), aggregated analysis (Estabrooks et al., 1994), interpretive metasynthesis (Jensen & Allen, 1996), interpretive phenomenological analysis (Smith et al. 1997), qualitative metasynthesis (Sandelowski et al., 1997), and grounded (formal) theory (Finfgeld, 1999). Subsequently, this evolution has shown little sign of abating, with novel qualitative synthesis methods continuing to emerge. As presented in Table 1, <sup>5</sup> the authors have identified 35 distinct qualitative synthesis methods previously used in academic research.

Given this large number of related, yet distinct, qualitative synthesis methods, there is a need to illuminate the individual essence that allows each method to be considered a member of the overarching qualitative synthesis methodological group. This communal identity is primarily conveyed through each method’s ability to synthesize data, and/or other coherent descriptions or explanations of phenomena, events, or cases (Sandelowski & Barroso, 2003b). Each individual qualitative synthesis method thus involves the analysis and synthesis of primary qualitative studies while remaining faithful to the original rendering in each primary study (Sandelowski & Barroso, 2003b). By being able to provide a broad overview of a body of qualitative research, a significant strength of qualitative synthesis use comes from its ability to reveal more powerful explanations than seen in a single study, thereby generating greater research finding generalizability and increased levels of abstraction (Sherwood, 1999). Accordingly, the novelty and value of qualitative synthesis derive from its multitudinous capacity for refuting, revising, and extending the current understanding of a particular phenomenon.

## 2.2 Criticisms of Qualitative Synthesis

Notwithstanding the nature and breadth of these benefits, various criticisms have also been leveled at qualitative synthesis and demand scrutiny. One common charge arises from the argument that since a qualitative synthesis typically involves topic selection, a literature search, data evaluation, and information presentation, which are subsequently encapsulated into a new “whole,” so it is therefore simply a form of literature review (Popay et al., 2007). However, qualitative synthesis also involves data reinterpretation through looking at the new whole created by individual parts (Major & Savin-Baden, 2010); thus, qualitative synthesis is more than a standard literature review (Thorne et al., 2004; Finlayson & Dixon, 2008).

A second criticism argues that synthesists are restricted to what is already available in the literature and that they are therefore bound to the specific questions posed in the primary research while concurrently being constrained by the information available in these works. But this challenge is negated by the variety of primary IS qualitative research available to qualitative synthesists. A related concern arises because qualitative synthesists often do not have access to primary data, meaning that any reanalysis of inherited data is impossible. Moreover, critics further suggest that if the aim of qualitative research is to acquire a thick, rich description of a phenomenon, then qualitative synthesis’s removal of context undermines such qualitative research. This perspective also presents qualitative synthesis as falling into a gap between primary and secondary research (Heaton, 2004). However, these viewpoints can be challenged by acknowledging the ability of qualitative synthesis to leverage contextual information, including research goals, topics, and designs from primary research, thereby incorporating adequate contextual information about the primary research site and setting (Savin-Baden & Major, 2007). Moreover, if the primary reviewer has interpreted primary data rather than simply reviewing it, then these interpretations can be preserved in the ensuing synthesis, meaning that synthesists can thus convey meaning nested in context in a way that primary data cannot (Doyle, 2003).

A final fundamental argument is that many different methods are used in primary qualitative research and researchers often inadequately describe the methodological sections in their publications, in turn intimating that qualitative synthesists do not have access to adequate information either to assess design viability or to determine the nature of the research design itself. However, such a viewpoint is conflicted because it forces a positivist perspective on potentially nonpositivist analysis, thereby leading to philosophical confusion. As presented in this paper, recognizing the role and impact of epistemology enables an appropriate method (or methods) to be chosen that matches the research team’s epistemological position. Moreover, by relying on heterogeneous methods for collecting and analyzing emergent themes and theories (Dixon-Woods, 2014), qualitative researchers can use contradictory evidence in a single study to expand and reformulate theoretical explanations and precepts (Barbour, 1998); by extension, it is therefore also feasible to do this when pulling disparate sources together. By focusing on contradictions, exceptions, and similarities across multiple studies, while respecting the diversity of the methods used in studies, the rich conceptual understanding of complex phenomena can be ported to a new context.

## 2.3 Qualitative Synthesis Labeling Issues

A related problematic area occurs in the area of method labeling, where multiple labels have been given to the generic construct of synthesizing qualitative data. In accepting that this heterogeneity potentially hampers qualitative synthesis’ recognition and use (Paterson et al., 2001); unfortunately, some of these labels have also been used by individual qualitative synthesis methods. <sup>6</sup> Moreover, the authors have additionally identified multiple individual methods that are distinct in design yet share the same label. <sup>7</sup> Furthermore, multiple individual methods also bear labels that are overly ambiguous in nature (Paterson et al., 2001, Sandelowski & Barroso, 2002, 2003a). This latter terminological confusion is particularly problematic considering the qualitative synthesist’s plight in choosing a method from such similar labels as “interpretive metasynthesis” (Jensen & Allen, 1996), “critical interpretive synthesis” (Dixon-Woods et al., 2006a), “metasynthesis” (Sandelowski and Barroso, 2007; Walsh & Downe, 2005), “qualitative metasynthesis” (Sandelowski et al., 1997), “qualitative meta-data analysis” (Sandelowski & Barroso, 2003a), “meta-data analysis” (Paterson et al., 2001), and “qualitative meta-analysis" (Schreiber et al., 1997) to name but a few. Indeed, the severity of this naming issue has compelled Thorne et al. (2004) to label these titles “terminological land mines” (p. 1343).

Unfortunately, the challenges related to these labeling issues have already been experienced within the IS discipline. Berente et al. (2019) used the qualitative synthesis method “meta-ethnography” <sup>8</sup> (Noblit & Hare, 1988) to generate a theoretical explanation of first-order and second-order responses when implementing enterprise systems as shown in their noteworthy paper on enterprise system implementation. They subsequently highlight how these responses are influenced by pluralistic institutional logics in combination with the relative power of organizational actors. Their choice of metaethnography arises from its value in enabling “the sweeping-in of context from rich descriptions to synthesize cases with each other in ways the original authors may not have foreseen, and thus formulate and extend novel theory” (p. 5). Interestingly, they do not explicitly label their selected method “metaethnography,” instead preferring to use the term “qualitative meta-analysis” in a general sense to explain their use of a qualitative synthesis methodology. However, notwithstanding the multiple benefits arising from their use of qualitative synthesis within the framework of their research, Berente et al.’s (2019) choice of label demonstrates the urgent need for a clear and widely agreed-upon labeling protocol, given that the label of “qualitative meta-analysis” has multiple definitions, being previously used to refer to:

An aggregative methodology in its own right, used for consolidating a group of studies to discover essential elements and then translating the results into an end product that transforms the original outcomes into a new conceptualization (Schreiber et al., 1997);

• An interpretive process used to analyze textual reports thereby creating new interpretations in the analysis process (Ke, 2009);

A generic term for the synthesis of qualitative data that leverages the quantitative sibling term of “meta-analysis” for use in the qualitative domain to facilitate conceptual methodological understanding (Thorne et al., 2004); and

An alternative name for individual qualitative synthesis methodologies, including “metasynthesis,” “qualitative metasynthesis,” “metastudy,” “meta-ethnography,” “grounded formal theory,” “aggregated analysis,” “systematic review,” and “meta-evaluation” (Douglas et al., 2008).

Even though this discussion of qualitative synthesis demonstrates both the potential for and challenges arising from, acquiring a deeper methodological understanding via qualitative synthesis, the fundamental challenge still remains: how can the qualitative synthesis method most applicable for a given research setting be determined? To this end, the next section introduces a three-tiered synthesis research framework to help guide researchers through making this important decision.

## 3 Introducing a Three-Tiered Research Framework and Guidelines

We envision a framework that allows qualitative synthesists, irrespective of skill level or experience, to choose the most appropriate qualitative synthesis method for their research. This is a critical requirement because it provides a conceptual foundation on which authors can create and/or strengthen their studies while affording reviewers and editors the opportunity to evaluate and make informed decisions about a paper (Venkatesh et al., 2013). But such a form must not only encompass the means for appropriate method selection, but also be transparent to the extent that researchers can understand how its different internal elements “hang together.” This is necessary because qualitative synthesis methods continue to evolve, and because unanswered questions about which synthesis methods are most appropriate for particular kinds of review, study types, or review questions remain outstanding (Noyes & Lewin, 2011). The value of having a qualitative synthesis framework therefore arises from its ability to ensure that researchers are able to inform their choice of research methodology by considering (1) whether the research question “fits with” qualitative synthesis use, (2) the impact of multiple influencing criteria, and (3) how to accomplish the final method selection process.

In meeting these requirements, the authors offer a three-tiered research framework to help qualitative synthesists make an appropriate method selection. Adapted from Noyes and Lewin (2011), the structure presented in Figure 1 comprises three tiers—I: Evaluate Research Question Fit, II: Review Criteria, and III: Select Method.

## 3.1 Tier 1: Evaluate Research Question Fit

In considering whether a research study will benefit from the use of qualitative synthesis, the first phase requires the objective of the research study to be determined and understood. This, in turn, obliges researchers to consider whether their research intention is to aggregate data, integrate findings, or develop new interpretations. In this regard, Saini and Schlonsky (2012) clarify that:

Data aggregation incorporates a quantitatively oriented approach to extracting, grouping, and formatting qualitative studies (Dixon-Woods et al., 2006b)—e.g., metasummary (Sandelowski & Barroso, 2007),

Data integration requires the creation of conceptual taxonomies to develop new phenomena that endure across multiple studies (Sandelowski & Barroso, 2007)—e.g., metasynthesis (e.g., Sandelowski & Barroso, 2007).

Data interpretation obliges the synthesist to “translate” findings across studies to create a new inductive understanding of the phenomenon, representative of research questions that seek to develop an explanatory theory or model. This, in turn, requires the researcher to make a further decision about whether to include comparable or different study designs—e.g., meta-ethnography (Noblit & Hare, 1988).

Given the differences between these approaches, having a clear understanding of the nature of the research question and the purpose of the research study allows IS researchers “to put new vigor into the debates in the IS field about the roles of theory and practice, (and) of rigor and relevance” (Willcocks et al., 2015, p. 3). Accordingly, IS research questions that use qualitative synthesis to create/expand theory, increase the relevance of IS research to IS practice, and/or exploit a wealth of primary data to generate new IS understanding are doing so in the knowledge that the methodology has already proven its research potential while concurrently meeting IS research demands. Therefore, to help research teams make informed choices in this area, we present common research question types previously used in qualitative synthesis research as guiding examples. Table 2 provides salient examples of previous empirical qualitative synthesis research.

Empirical qualitative synthesis research has been used to explore multiple theoretical elements, including creation, assessment, expansion, and tying research to practice (Ritzer, 1990; Ring et al., 2011). Qualitative synthesis has also been used to ground the role of research in influencing practice and policy, especially in the healthcare domain (Finfgeld, 2003). Through identifying and bridging gaps or omissions between research and practice, qualitative synthesis can allow papers in diverse areas to be assimilated and illuminate research areas where knowledge is missing (Popay et al., 2007). In this regard, Wainwright et al.’s (2018) noteworthy study of evidence-based research introduced evidence-based practice (EBP) as a holistic approach to synthesizing evidence for the purpose of improving both research and practice alike. <sup>9</sup> EBP has been used to interrogate quantitative and qualitative evidence to inform, develop, improve, and support IS practice through multiple sources, including empirical studies, practitioners, organizational internal data, and stakeholder evidence. In following this approach, EBP’s aim is for “the IS research and practitioner communities to engage with each other … to develop higher standards of professional practice based on cumulative knowledge, facts, and evidence” (Wainwright et al., p. 1053). However, in order to do so, “IS Researchers require greater training in … methods for synthesizing qualitative, interpretive research studies—so that the findings from high-quality individual research projects can be combined into useful models and guidance for practice” (Wainwright et al., p. 1038). In responding to Wainwright et al.’s (2018) appeal for instruction on the use of qualitative synthesis, the IS discipline has thus positioned itself to provide further recommendations to improve IS practice and facilitate new approaches to qualitative research.

Qualitative synthesis has further demonstrated its utility by being used both to illustrate the need for additional primary research and to exploit and synthesize primary data to enhance the knowledge on a given topic (Brunton et al., 2006; Weed, 2005). Qualitative synthesis can aid such efforts because it can contain and manage large amounts of information; as part of an initial literature review, researchers often identify large quantities of information to be incorporated, thereby raising the challenge of how to do so. By linking concepts across multiple reports (Noblit & Hare, 1988), helping resolve conflicting evidence reports necessary for building a research body (Whittemore, 2005), and connecting studies across time, research areas can be interrogated in a way that other methodologies cannot replicate (Booth, 2006). Qualitative synthesis can also help in identifying and bridging gaps and/or omissions in a body of research or between research and practice because it enables papers in diverse IS areas to be assimilated while also illuminating research areas where knowledge is missing.

## 3.2 Tier 2: Review Criteria that Inform Method Selection

After confirming their research objective, researchers must assess the multiple criteria that influence the selection process. Insofar as these can be external and/or integral to a given methodological setting, and given their significant role in the method selection process, each criterion is now presented with questions that require answers:

1. Epistemology: To what philosophical paradigm does the research team subscribe? Does this sit comfortably with the philosophical viewpoint associated with a given method?

2. Research approach: Is it “fixed,” or does it support an emergent context?

3. Time frame: How much time is available for undertaking the research, versus how timeconsuming is each method?

4. Resource intensity: How many, and what type of resources are available to aid the research effort? Do these meet the requirements of the desired method?

5. Data richness: How thick and rich are the data provided for the synthesis? Does the level of thickness and richness achieve the minimum standards demanded by a given qualitative synthesis method?

6. Data quantity: How much data does the research team have? Is the amount sufficient for the method of choice?

7. Data quality: Are the data of a minimally acceptable quality to leverage a selected method?

While several of these criteria (e.g., time frame and resource intensity) can be considered germane to research method selection in general, these seven criteria are deemed especially relevant to qualitative synthesis research in particular. The remainder of this section is therefore devoted to defining each criterion within the context of qualitative synthesis. Our objective is to help the synthesist identify a prioritized subset of methods for final consideration in Tier III of the selection framework (Figure 1).

## 3.2.1 Criteria 1: Epistemology

To demonstrate the importance of reviewing each criterion correctly, this paper first considers the function of epistemology. This refers to a branch of philosophy concerned with the nature, scope, and sources of knowledge (Iivari et al., 1998, Mingers & Brocklesby, 1997, Tashakkori & Teddlie, 1998). Prior to synthesis, investigators must have already determined their own epistemological stance because differing qualitative synthesis methods adhere to contrasting philosophical viewpoints (Saini & Schlonsky, 2012). Should this philosophical understanding be absent or defective, a method incompatible with the synthesist’s chosen paradigm could be chosen, leading to inconsistent research outcomes. To mitigate this undesirable scenario, researchers must therefore be able to distinguish between those characteristics common to all qualitative synthesis methods versus the methodological permutations affiliated with a particular method and a given paradigm.

Unfortunately, this complex judgment process is complicated by how impactful a given philosophy is on each method. For instance, methods such as metaethnography (Noblit & Hare, 1988) and grounded (formal) theory (Kearney, 1998; Finfgeld, 1999) consider epistemological perspectives at each stage of their respective review processes (Booth et al., 2016). However, others such as framework synthesis (Brunton et al., 2006), narrative synthesis (Barnett-Page &

Thomas, 2009), and thematic synthesis (Thomas & Harden, 2008) are more “epistemologically neutral,” insofar as they have little explicit recourse to philosophical considerations during the synthesis procedure. But this is not to suggest that these latter methods ignore the impact of philosophy—they do, albeit less “intensively.” Ultimately, the researcher needs to decide which epistemology a given method is associated with and evaluate the depth of relationship that method has with the selected epistemology.

## 3.2.2 Criteria 2: Research Approach

Qualitative synthesis can also be employed using either a fixed question approach or one that treats inquiry as more emergent in nature. Research questions that are considered fixed are inflexible, in that they remain constant throughout the research effort and employ a clear procedure to be followed. This contrasts with methods that embrace a more developing intent and treat the review question as negotiable and therefore open to evolution. Notwithstanding the nature of the differences between these question types, qualitative synthesis researchers must consider question style in detail, as a question may appear to adhere to an overly positivist or interpretivist epistemology. But it is important not to consider a fixed question as positivist and an evolving inquiry as interpretivist; a more appropriate consideration would be to position the questions as either procedural or substantive (Eakin & Mykhalovskiy, 2003). “Procedural” implies that quality arises from how the research is carried out and that this is achieved by matching the question with an appropriate method. Conversely, “substantive” infers a greater involvement with the analytic content of the research whereby the role of process is not diminished per se but considered more an aid to understanding rather than the object of judgment.

## 3.2.3 Criteria 3: Time Frame

Because the variables that influence time are broad and wide-ranging, it is challenging to characterize a qualitative synthesis method based solely on the time it takes to complete the synthesis process. This is because multiple characteristics are at play that include the method’s level of complexity, the nature and extent of the synthesis to be conducted, the number of studies to be incorporated, and the data’s richness and thickness (Booth et al., 2016). This critical criterion involves the researcher pragmatically matching a given level of “methodological intensity” with the time available for research. Put simply, if a method is selected that is regarded as intensively time consuming, then choosing such an approach while under a tight schedule may compromise the research effort in contrast to a less time intensive but still suitable method. Thus, as highlighted previously, we consider it important to reiterate that while time frame considerations (as well as resource intensity below) are not unique in terms of their impact on the research method selection process, their inclusion as an influential criterion is especially relevant given both the nature of many qualitative synthesis methods and their impact on this process.

## 3.2.4 Criteria 4: Resource Intensity

Resource intensity is a related practical issue that needs to be considered because resources such as people, funding, and time need to be allocated appropriately. In the unwanted scenario where allocation is considered a poor “fit,” for example, where available resources are light but the selected method is resource intensive, issues may arise whereby inappropriate skill levels are assigned to research tasks, deliverable dates are missed, and assignments are poorly executed. However, such issues can be overcome if the research project includes a project initiation phase that matches expertise levels to each project task to ensure the best fit between resource availability and method resource intensity. Too much expertise for a given task is wasteful—i.e., it would be better for a more experienced research team member to focus on a more challenging deliverable. Conversely, assigning a novice with little expertise to a difficult task may lead to poor implementation, improper execution, or even task incompletion.

## 3.2.5 Criteria 5: Data Richness and Thickness

When undertaking an interpretive investigation, a qualitative synthesist will often seek data that are sufficiently “rich” and “thick” to generate accounts that contribute to theory. By rich and thick data, we refer to qualitative primary data that are multilayered, representing “human behavior in a way that takes not only the physical and social context into account but also the actors’ intentionality. In this way, the meaning and significance of behaviors or events are made accessible to the reader” (Schultze & Avital, 2011, p. 3). But comprehending all these dimensions in a given setting is difficult; thus, the investigator may seek to understand as many different components as deeply as possible while acknowledging that some may be missed. A qualitative synthesist must therefore be able to understand how much data richness a given method is capable of investigating. If a researcher has data that are considered rich but chooses a method not designed for probing data sufficiently deeply, then outcomes may be light—i.e., a poor fit. Conversely, if a method is selected that is proficient at interrogating rich data but is presented with data that are thin, any effort expended is potentially wasted. Again, a poor fit. Thus, the optimal relationship occurs when methods adept at investigating such richness are presented with the matching forms of data. The researcher must thus be able to accurately judge both the richness of given data and a selected method’s ability to query them.

## 3.2.6 Criteria 6: Data Quantity

Another intricate criterion to be considered is data quantity, which questions the amount of primary data needed to warrant the use of a particular synthesis method. The inherent complexity of this question is aptly demonstrated by Hannes and Lockwood (2011), who suggested that “available primary research may be too few or too many, too homogeneous or too heterogeneous, to enact the procedures of a particular synthesis method in the way the developers prescribe” (p. 9). While this observation may appear to make an already difficult selection process even more challenging, it does imply a certain amount of flexibility when determining how many studies are required to use a selected method. Given that no one research setting matches another, this tractability is welcome to the extent that researchers can adapt a chosen synthesis method apropos their own requirements.

However, this methodological revision can only occur if the following guideline is respected: that when study data are rich, the research is limited to the number of studies that can be cogently assessed by the research team. Conversely, for research teams using methods designed for more descriptive data, the opposite is true—that is, they may incorporate comparatively larger study numbers but only as many as can be managed. Thus, when selecting a qualitative synthesis method from a data quantity viewpoint, matching it to optimal data quantity levels is achieved by undertaking a rigorous scoping process. Such a procedure can be used to limit the scope of a research question, thereby decreasing the number of studies needed for a study. Alternatively, if studies are less plentiful, then expanding the research question’s remit allows for more primary publications to be included. Ultimately, informational redundancy occurs when data saturation is achieved (Sandelowski, 2008), and theoretical saturation when “the complete range of constructs … is fully represented by the data” (Starks & Trinidad, 2007, p. 1375).

## 3.2.7 Criteria 7: Data Quality

The debate about the role and function of data quality in qualitative synthesis remains contentious because different schools of thinking have been unable to reconcile their differing beliefs regarding its nature (Finfgeld, 2003; Polit & Beck, 2010). This lack of consensus on how to assess quality ensures that the subject remains a pre-paradigmatic construct (Kuhn, 1970; Sandelowski & Barroso, 2007). But these disagreements are not only about how quality should be appraised, but also who should assess quality and, indeed, whether quality can or should be evaluated at all (Thomas & Harden, 2008). This lack of agreement has subsequently forced qualitative synthesis researchers to embrace a bespoke perspective when considering the notion of quality.

The key to understanding these different quality perspectives lies in the researcher identifying how individual synthesis methods have evolved, how they affiliate to a given paradigm, their level of commitment to the concept of quality, and their methodological purpose. In this regard, many qualitative synthesis methods are considered novel, in the sense that they have been developed specifically with qualitative synthesis in mind. However, others are the offspring of methods used principally in primary research (Dixon-Woods, 2011). This is an important classification because being a direct methodological descendant means inheriting tried and tested quality criteria and characteristics from an originating method. For example, grounded (formal) theory (Kearney, 1998; Finfgeld, 1999) uses the quality criterion for adequacy taken from its parent methodology of grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967, Wilson & Hutchinson, 1996) (Kearney, 2008). Thus, by having knowledge of a parent methodology, prima facie knowledge of its child is provided, thereby easing the knowledge consolidation process.

An alternative angle on the function of quality stems from the paradigm affiliated with a given methodology. In considering the philosophical dichotomy of positivist and interpretive research, interpretivist qualitative researchers argue the need to reject the notion of quality appraisal, as it impinges on interpretation and creativity. For example, Sandelowski and Barroso (2007) explicitly instructed researchers not to use quality criteria when determining whether a primary study be included in or excluded from a qualitative metasynthesis investigation. Conversely, others argue that appraising the quality of primary papers is key to ensuring the ongoing credibility and transferability of qualitative research, which are both positivist considerations (Hannes & Lockwood, 2013). Tying into this epistemological argument, a related perspective considers how “concerned” a synthesis method is with the quality appraisal process. For instance, thematic synthesis has a specific approach to quality appraisal, while others such as meta-ethnography (Noblit & Hare, 1988) are “less committed to the concept” (Ring et al., 2011, p. 13).

From a methodological purpose viewpoint, the practical outcomes of a given method may also inform the quality appraisal decision. Lockwood et al.’s (2015) healthcare study summarized how the applied nature of a synthesis’s outcomes can influence the need for quality appraisal. In summary, despite the dissimilarities between differing quality constructs, each context considers quality based on the relevance and likely contribution of a paper rather than on its methodological characteristics (Gough, 2007). Because the differences in the requirements of quality appraisal exist across all the qualitative synthesis methods and at different levels, it is incumbent on the synthesist to determine what approach to quality appraisal is used by which method and why. ## 3.3 Tier 3: Method Selection

Having considered and prioritized the criteria and interactions that influence method selection, the final stage occurs when the synthesist is presented with a smaller subset of different qualitative synthesis methods from which to make a final choice. Given that multiple methods deemed acceptable for use by differing criteria may have already been identified, the IS synthesist must now whittle down this list to make a final selection. We suggest a weighted scoring approach for the methods under consideration, whether it be a subset obtained using Figures 2 and 3 or the entire set of methods. This is achieved by subjecting each criterion to a weighted “score of importance” that requires the researcher to prioritize the different criteria apropos the nature of their research study. For instance, positivist researchers may give higher scores of importance to the data quality and quantity criteria, compared to interpretivist researchers who will be less concerned with these areas. Similarly, a research team comprising multiple investigators may be less concerned with high data quantity than a single scholar undertaking a similar study. By prioritizing the methodological choices arising from each criterion evaluation, each criterion’s relative score of importance can be assigned to each method selected. In subsequently summing the scores for each method in each criterion, those with the highest overall scores would be considered most applicable to the research effort. If a researcher is seeking only one method but two or more methods achieve similar weighted scores, the authors recommend a reassessment and subsequent readjustment of the weightings given to the scores of these “final” methods.

As a concluding check for assessing the final method selection, researchers may also wish to leverage the method similarity dendrogram obtained via hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA; see Appendix B). The dendrogram provides either confirmation of the candidate subset of methods thus far obtained or highlights additional methods for weighted scoring. Moreover, the dendrogram can also be used for researchers interested in enhancing the method already selected. Specifically, if another method joins the selected method close to the “root” node on the dendrogram, it suggests that this other method may not only share similar characteristics but also provide alternative complementary techniques for the synthesis process. Accordingly, these techniques may be able to be appropriated from an alternative method to further enhance the previously selected target method.

## 4 Discussion and Concluding Comments

To the extent seen in other disciplines, the IS domain has remained largely unaware of the potential of qualitative synthesis as a methodology of choice. This has been to the IS field’s detriment given the impact of those few IS papers that have previously used a qualitative synthesis methodology. In any case, only a small number of the methods available to the discipline have been applied, and thus the benefits of other qualitative synthesis methods have yet to bear upon the IS domain. For the IS field to benefit fully from qualitative synthesis, it needs to be made aware of the entire suite of available methods in order to make an informed method selection.

To this end, this paper presents a three-tier framework (Figure 1) that consolidates all 35 qualitative synthesis methods within a selection criteria process. It also provides IS stakeholders with the means to concurrently review previous empirical qualitative synthesis use (Table 2), the independent scoring of each method against each criteria permutation (Table 3), the filtering to a smaller subset based on the epistemological view/research approach (Figure 2) and empirical conditions (Figure 3), and the use of weighted scoring for final selection. However, while this paper presents the means for making an informed method selection, it is beyond the paper’s scope to cover how to use each individual qualitative synthesis method. Nonetheless, we believe that our summary of each method (Appendix A) provides sufficient information for researchers to begin exploring a given method, or related methods, in more detail.

By defining associated guideline components, the authors have correspondingly identified several distinct value propositions for different IS stakeholder types, which are presented in Table 4. In also identifying and articulating the labeling issues that have impacted both the IS and other domains, we argue that this identification also provides a related value benefit. With the lack of method labeling consensus remaining a long-standing concern, the need for all academic disciplines to observe a single protocol for labeling both the generic form of synthesizing qualitative data and each individual qualitative method therein remains paramount. Given the extent and impact of these issues, this problem is particularly important to overcome, not only for those submitting an article to a journal for publication but also for the reviewers who judge such submissions. In this context, we acknowledge the previous attempts seen elsewhere at presenting a single label for the process of synthesizing qualitative data and emphasize that “qualitative synthesis” is simply our recommendation for this generic term.

However, given that “qualitative synthesis” has not, to the best of our knowledge, been previously used or confused as a single qualitative synthesis methodology in its own right, we recommend this term to mitigate the terminological turbulence experienced previously. As a related benefit, as a future research opportunity, we identify the need to address the multiple labeling issues seen at the individual method level. Insofar as the many benefits arising from qualitative synthesis use may be compromised by these labeling issues, providing a solution will likely benefit IS qualitative research and other academic disciplines alike.

For additional future research directions, we suggest investigating the manner in which qualitative synthesis can provide different perspectives on research subjects that have previously used quantitative methodologies. Given that this approach has already been demonstrated outside of the IS domain (Finfgeld, 2003; Dixon-Woods et al., 2006a), asking the same research question in terms of an equivalent IS subject but incorporating a different set of evidence would potentially enable qualitative synthesis outcomes to complement existing quantitative findings. In this way, IS research areas that have been historically dominated by quantitative meta-analysis studies would thus become open to alternative lines of inquiry, thereby potentially inferring a higher level of knowledge. Furthermore, there is related potential in consolidating IS meta-analytic data with IS qualitative synthesis data as a form of meta-mixed methods research (Petticrew, et al., 2013). Given the IS field’s relative unfamiliarity with qualitative synthesis, this approach has the potential to unlock previously unexplored areas of knowledge.

The opportunity also remains for qualitative synthesis use to benefit IS research more widely, with IS healthcare research likely being a particularly rich area for investigation based on the already substantial qualitative synthesis research undertaken in this area. A related opening lies in exploring the evolution of qualitative synthesis as a whole and the individual methods therein. It is important to recognize that methods are “resources” with specific application criteria that can be mobilized to address specific research questions with a set of specific inferences. As such, individual methods will necessarily evolve over time. However, because only a paucity of research articles have applied qualitative synthesis methodologies within the IS domain, it is likely premature to undertake such an in-depth evolutionary investigation at this time. However, at some future juncture, it would undoubtedly be useful to determine the bases on which the IS discipline has appropriated these techniques to thereby confirm the nature of their evolution.<sup>10</sup>

The qualitative synthesis field is an area that is getting only more congested as existing methods continue to evolve and new ones emerge. Thus, one limitation of this paper is that it is necessarily explorative, insofar as this is the first study to investigate the breadth and depth of this methodological area in the IS discipline. However, we have investigated the potential of qualitative synthesis in other academic areas and designed guidelines around the perceived needs of the IS community. Moreover, by surveying prominent qualitative IS authorities and by undertaking an arduous process of investigation and research into the qualitative synthesis methods and their use in other disciplines, we believe we are as well-positioned as any to initiate a discourse into the potential of qualitative synthesis within the IS domain. Accordingly, we think it important to reiterate the overarching aim of this paper, that is, we wish to bring the suite of qualitative synthesis methods to the attention of the IS discipline and present a guideline selection process that we believe fits with the IS research context. Notwithstanding this intention, the authors embrace any form of critique, given that the IS discipline will only grow in knowledge while the process of qualitative synthesis continues to be analyzed and validated. We intend and indeed hope that others will evolve our guidelines and framework in the future. By initiating a dialogue, we aim to motivate other researchers to embrace qualitative synthesis and to use this paper as a starting point in order to enable a more productive usage of these techniques as they evolve in number and design.

In conclusion, we hope that our assessment provides a stimulus for synthesists to exploit what is undoubtedly a fascinating albeit complex suite of methods. However, much work remains if the IS discipline is to consistently leverage qualitative synthesis to the extent seen elsewhere. While knowledge of many of the synthesis techniques presented here may appear embryonic and in need of further exploration, we identify with Denyer and Tranfield’s (2006) view that “qualitative research synthesis already developed and in use in other fields can provide … a means of creating actionable knowledge in the future” (p. 224). Given the increasing popularity of qualitative research, we therefore urge IS researchers to be rigorous in choosing the qualitative synthesis method(s) most appropriate for their research while remaining vigilant to the central objective of generating new knowledge within the IS domain.

## Appendix A: 35 Approaches to Qualitative Synthesis

## 1. Aggregated Analysis (e.g., Estabrooks et al., 1994)

• Entails constant comparison and contrast of themes from multiple studies, which are aggregated to generate new and emerging theories

• Interpretive technique, but with pragmatic overtones

• Can be used for any qualitative method

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

Similar to meta-ethnography conceptually

• Aggregated analysis is explicitly concerned by the need to build theory

• In aggregated analysis, knowledge is cumulative

• Primary paper selection requires studies to:

▪ Use a common approach

▪ Focus on similar populations or themes

## 2. Content Analysis (e.g., Evans & Fitzgerald, 2002)

• Involves the condensing of text from multiple sources into fewer content-related categories, and then determining the frequencies of these categories

• Approach includes:

Reading/rereading reports to develop initial impressions of the literature

▪ Use of an abstraction tool to abstract themes from the data

▪ Different reviewers recording their findings

▪ Grouping the findings into categories

• Categories are required to be very precise to allow multiple coders to achieve the same results

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

• Draws on validity/reliability perspectives and therefore implicitly positivist

• Often used as a single phase (of many) in different synthesis methodologies

## 3. Critical Interpretive Synthesis (e.g., Dixon-Woods et al., 2006a)

• Adaptation of meta-ethnography but with a greater focus on critical analysis

• Critical = “actively seeking to provide a wider critique of the primary literature, including questioning epistemological/normative assumptions”

• Inductive and interpretive

Explicit towards theory generation with strong explanatory power

• Iterative approach to question definition—that is, a research question formulated at the outset should remain open to modification

• Use of organic, theoretical, and dialectic sampling for searching/selecting from the literature—necessary when the # of records is extremely high.

• Appraises quality premised on its likely contribution to theory development

• Primary paper selection not bound by particular study types or meeting of particular methodological standards

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

• Approach includes:

▪ Detailed inspection of primary papers to identify recurring patterns, themes, and categories

▪ Use of software to assist analysis (for example, QSR N5)

Focus on “criticality”—that is, questioning the literature vis-à-vis access, assumptions made, and the influences of any proposed solution

## 4. Ecological Triangulation (e.g., Banning, 2003)

Triangulation: the use of multi-data sources, multi-data collection methods, multi-researchers, and multitheoretical approaches to determine relationships between behavior, person and environment

• Seeks to answer the following generic questions arising from primary studies:

▪ What methods were used?

▪ What theoretical frameworks were employed?

▪ What interventions with what persons under what conditions produced what outcomes?

• Summary: what evidence across the primary papers does theory, method, and the analysis of persons/conditions support interventions with positive results?

• Tools must be used to help this process. For example, NVivo.

• Use of matrices to clarify attributes and values

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

• Also known as “ecological sentence synthesis”

## 5. Framework Synthesis (e.g., Brunton et al., 2006; Oliver et al., 2008)

• Rationale: qualitative research generates large amounts of textual data via transcripts and observational field notes

• This sheer wealth of information challenges rigorous analysis

• Framework synthesis uses an a priori “framework” that is informed by background material and team discussions to extract and synthesize findings

• Organizes and analyzes data by indexing—that is, using numerical codes, rearranging data into charts, and so on.

Unit of analysis: the data of individual qualitative studies

• Largely deductive, but with inductive elements

• Sometimes called framework analysis.

• Approach includes:

▪ Familiarizing with the literature

▪ Developing a conceptual framework using concepts in the review question/literature

▪ Applying the framework systematically to the primary paper data

▪ Rearranging the data in the framework to construct a chart for each key dimension

▪ Mapping across dimensions to find associations between themes to explain findings

The outcome product is expressed as a chart showing each key dimension identified, which in turn is used to map the nature and range of the concept under study, and find associations, or exceptions, between themes

## 6. Grounded (Formal) Theory (e.g., Kearney, 2001; Eaves, 2001; Finfgeld, 1999)

• Incorporates theoretical sampling and constant comparative analysis/interplay between theory and data

Realist—treats human experience as definable and measurable

To a certain extent also positivist because it involves extrapolation, abstraction, and deduction across contexts. But it is not so positivist to the point of losing sight of the subjective origin of definitions of situations—for example, the material under study

Whereas substantive grounded theory can display the vivid particularity of individuals situated in defined contexts, grounded (formal) theory may require reducing contextual particularities to combinations of conditions or contingencies

• Unit of analysis: the data of individual qualitative substantive grounded theory studies

• Study selection:

• Must be grounded theory papers

• Must demonstrate the development of concepts and categories that make up a substantive grounded theory

• Must use a theory-generating approach

• Must be of a sufficient quality per Glaser and Straus (1967)

• Data analysis/synthesis:

Choose the phenomenon that will be the initial focus of analysis

▪ Select a primary data source

Code data using descriptive and theoretical coding, noting that extracted data/theory is to be recoded from a higher-level perspective, that is, with a more current understanding of the subject

▪ Begin to write theoretical memos, incorporating the original ideas and the results of coding

▪ Using theoretical sampling, seek the next data

Select a different substantive area/situation in the phenomenon of interest, and continue comparative analysis

Note contrasts in sample, context, orientation, and model

▪ Continue theoretical sampling

▪ Look outside the immediate phenomena to widen the scope and acquire an alternative context.

Think comparatively—this enriches understanding of the shared aspects of diverse phenomena and reflects in the emerging theory the full range of variation across data sources.

▪ Make as specific as possible the connection of each concept to the core phenomenon

## 7. Interpretive Phenomenological Analysis (e.g., Smith et al., 1997)

• Double hermeneutic—that is, analyst seeks to make sense of the participant's attempts to make sense of their own experiences

• Interpretive, phenomenological, and ideographic

• Common primary sources include case studies and interviews

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies, most commonly case studies and interviews

• Analysis:

Read/reread individual studies noting points of interest/significance

▪ Document emerging themes using key words

▪ Look for connections between emergent themes, and between these themes and primary study data

Produce a master list/table of themes ordered coherently

Use this master list as the basis for the analysis of subsequent primary papers, engaging in theme comparison cyclically

▪ Examine all codes to ascertain opportunity to categorize them, and identify shared themes

Reexamine primary data through a more focused lens, using the shared theme as the context of interest

▪ Follow the above process (1-7) again, still using shared themes as the basis

▪ Explore patterns/relationships in and between groups through:

• Use of grounded theory to move between text to a more holistic perspective on the data

• Use of diagrams to capture relationships between emergent themes

Write-up, with the shared themes adopted as the paper’s structure

## 8. Interpretive Metasynthesis (e.g., Jensen & Allen, 1996)

• Desired outcome is acquiring an understanding of the interpretive explanations of a phenomenon

• Data retrieval aim is to determine basic comparability between primary studies

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

• Synthesis entails hermeneutic and dialectic phases to generate a new construction

## • Process:

Findings are standardized via common codes and outlines

▪ Lists of key metaphors, phrases, concepts, ideas and categories are created and juxtaposed

These are reciprocally translated into the terms of the other papers (and vice versa) per Noblit and Hare’s (1988) meta-ethnographical approach

Subsequent translations are synthesized, making clustered metamatrices that lead to a description of a phenomenon

Can be either inductive or deductive

▪ Primary papers using different qualitative methods may be used, but studies should not be combined.

## 9. Literature Review (e.g., Levy & Ellis, 2006)

• An evaluative, exploratory literature synthesis of primary sources

• Provides a subjective narrative summary of previous researchers to establish a research need

• Unit of analysis: the findings, and sometimes data, of individual qualitative studies

• Frequently used as a context-setting exercise—that is, to present the outcome of a literature review as an argument for a particular point of view/justification for particular research

• Lacks analysis or evaluation, instead offering tours around previous work that highlight key debates and issues

• Often descriptive and rarely attempts to make sense of what the collection of studies has to say

## 10. Meta-Aggregation (e.g., Pearson, 2004)

• Philosophically grounded in pragmatism and transcendental phenomenology

• An interpretive, aggregative methodology that seeks to generate practice theory—that is, it seeks to produce declarations to guide practitioners and policy makers

• Also known as aggregative review, aggregative synthesis, qualitative evidence synthesis or metasynthesis

Unit of analysis: the findings, and data, of individual qualitative studies

• Process:

▪ Review team composition and identify topic

▪ Decide how to synthesize, and how to acquire human, technical and material resources

Design the protocol, including title, background, question, inclusion criteria, search strategy, assessment criteria and data extraction approaches.

▪ Extract data using a two-phase approach:

General extraction of the particulars of the study, methodology and methods, settings, and phenomenon

Individual paper extraction of themes and metaphors using a framework of unequivocal or credible findings

## ▪ Data Synthesis

• Allocate a level of plausibility for each finding

• Develop categories for findings with at least two findings per category

• Develop 1+ synthesized findings in at least two categories

Software (for example, Joanna Briggs Institute [JBI] Qari) can be used to assist in this process

• Develop recommendations for practice

## 11. Metadata Analysis (e.g., Zhao, 1991; Paterson et al., 2001)

• A component part of the Metastudy process

• Unit of analysis: the data of individual qualitative studies from a data analysis perspective, and the findings therein.

• Consists of:

▪ Comparing different forms of data in terms of their quality and utility

▪ Studying the underlying assumptions of various data-analytic procedures

▪ The synthesis of findings of multiple research studies related to the same phenomenon

## • Process:

Identify an appropriate analytic strategy, including meta-ethnography, grounded theory, thematic analysis, and interpretive descriptive analysis

▪ Develop a filing/coding system

▪ Categorize data

▪ Obtain intercoder consensus

▪ Discuss/interpret findings as they relate to the research question(s)

▪ Also known as grounded theory metadata analysis.

## 12. Meta-Ethnography (e.g., Noblit & Hare, 1988)

Focus is on translation—that is, interpretations of data are translated across multiple studies into one another Different types of translation are possible:

▪ Reciprocal—constant comparison, investigating overlap, similarities, and contradictions

▪ Refutational—oppositional- and counterarguments via metaphors, themes, and concepts

▪ Line of Argument—used to develop new models/theories/understanding via synthesis

Explicitly interpretivist, with all research described as translation

• Distinguishing characteristic: differences across studies are portrayed and explicated using common terms in a final product versus being synthesized into a unified summary

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

• Process:

■ Decide what is relevant to the initial interest

▪ Read/reread the studies

▪ Determine how the studies are related—investigate the papers for common/recurring concepts

Translate the studies into one another—that is, find/use metaphors for each researcher's view of the phenomenon under study, and apply those metaphors to the other studies to expose contrasts and agreements

▪ Synthesize translations into second-order and third-order interpretations

▪ Depict the synthesis in writing or by other means

The final product is a juxtaposition of the key points of different ethnographies, as expressed in shared metaphorical frameworks that capture the similarities and differences in the individual studies

## 13. Metafamily (e.g., Kearney, 2001)

Includes the following qualitative synthesis approaches:

▪ Meta-Ethnography

▪ Metastudy (metamethod, metatheory, and meta-data analysis)

▪ Metasynthesis

▪ Meta-interpretation

▪ Aggregated analysis

▪ Grounded (formal) theory

• Defined as a continuum along which these synthesis methodologies are ordered from most interpretive to most theorizing:

• Interpretive: “focused on holistic portrayal and contextualized thick description without distillation to shared concepts and theory”

• Theorizing: “focused on building and refining concepts and theories that integrate the differences across studies into an explanatory model”

• Grounded formal theory is located at the extreme theorizing end of the continuum

• Between the poles of the continuum: closest to grounded formal theory is meta-interpretation and then aggregated analysis

• Theorizing with an interpretive edge: metastudy comes next

• Interpreting with theorizing components: metasynthesis follows

• Meta-ethnography is located at the extreme interpretive end of the continuum • Sliding along the continuum: the borders between interpretation and theory are not always clear or delineated by methodological labels

• Members of this metafamily of qualitative synthesis methods slide back and forth across these borders

• Unit of analysis: dependent upon each individual methodology

## 14. Meta-Interpretation (e.g., Weed, 2005)

• Uses meta-ethnography for procedural guidelines

Uses grounded theory as epistemological and methodological basis

• Interpretive and inductive

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

• Process:

Identify the area in which the synthesis is to take place

▪ Select 4-5 contrasting studies based on maximum variation sampling

▪ Undertake a concurrent thematic and context analysis of the primary studies

▪ Determine to exclude studies if necessary

Do a further literature search for studies that address the emerging issues

▪ Develop the dataset to include the initial and subsequent groups of papers

▪ Undertake a further thematic and context analysis

Determine further exclusions premised on the exclusion criteria from the first round, but also consider whether first round exclusion criteria are appropriate having assimilated further studies

▪ Continue until theoretical saturation is achieved

## 15. Metamethod (e.g., Zhao, 1991; Paterson et al., 2001)

• A component part of metastudy that studies the rigor, epistemological soundness, and philosophical traditions of the methods used in conducting primary qualitative studies

• The methodology considers the manner in which primary methodological approaches gather/interpret data to shape the findings that emerge from the primary studies

• Involves specifying the methodological characteristics of the primary studies, and searching for patterns and contradictions that explain the findings of different methods (for example, interviews versus ethnographies)

• Involves investigating the assumptions that underlie study methodologies, and appraising themes and patterns evident in the primary research

• Unit of analysis: the methodological approaches used to collect data of individual qualitative studies

• Also known as “critical appraisal” and “metamethod”

## 16. Metanarrative (e.g., Greenhalgh et al., 2005)

• Methodological aim is to select key papers from different research paradigms/traditions to provide the fullest perspective on knowledge generation

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

• The process involves:

▪ Planning phase:

• Assemble team, outline research question, agree outputs

▪ Search phase:

Initial search led by intuition, informal networking and browsing

Search for seminal conceptual papers and in databases.

## ▪ Mapping phase:

Identify separately the key elements of the research paradigm (conceptual, theoretical, methodological, and instrumental)

• Identify key actors and events, and prevailing language and imagery

## ▪ Appraisal phase:

Evaluate each primary study for its validity and relevance premised on quality criteria set by experts in that tradition

• Extract/collate key results grouping comparable studies together

## ▪ Synthesis phase:

• Identify all key dimensions of the problem

Taking each dimension in turn, give a narrative account of the contribution (if any) made to it

Explain in terms of contestation between different paradigms from which the data were generated

▪ Recommendations phase:

• Summarize the overall message with relevant evidence

## 17. Metastudy (e.g., Paterson et al., 2001)

• A multifaceted approach that includes:

Meta-data analysis (the analysis of findings)—analyzing theories underlying extant studies of a phenomenon of interest

Metamethod (the analysis of methods)—examining the assumptions and rigor of methods used in study of the phenomenon and developing new norms

Metatheory (the analysis of theory)—studying the form and content of data and synthesizing findings across studies

Concludes with metasynthesis that brings together the ideas that have been deconstructed in these three processes

• Applies meta-ethnographic techniques for developing new interpretations across extant texts, but also develops/tests hypotheses to support or negate emerging theory

• No such thing as absolute truth—that is, not positivist—leans towards an interpretive account

Unit of analysis: the findings, and data, of individual qualitative studies

• Highly systematic, but not prescriptive due to allowing flexibility in choice of methods for analysis/synthesis

• Primary goal is to develop midrange theory

## 18. Metasummary (e.g., Sandelowski & Barroso, 2007)

• A form of systematic review of qualitative findings that are themselves thematic summaries of data

• Positivist

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

• Process:

▪ Extract relevant statements of findings from each report

Reduce these statements into abstracted findings, thereby parsimoniously/accurately capturing the content and meaning of each report’s findings

Calculating effect sizes using quantitative techniques to determine the magnitude of each finding in relation to every other finding

Effect size refers to the magnitude of a treatment effect, achieved through measuring the occurrence frequency of an event sufficient to constitute a pattern or theme

▪ Also known as qualitative metasummary

## 19. Metasynthesis (e.g., Sandelowski & Barroso, 2007; Walsh & Downe, 2005)

Metasynthesis is defined in different ways:

• Definition 1

▪ An interpretive integration of qualitative findings that are themselves interpretive syntheses of data

Individual reports are analyzed hermeneutically (i.e., capturing situated meaning) and then compared in a dialectic process (i.e., comparing and contrasting meanings)

▪ Used for theory generation

Can accommodate primary studies using different methods, including phenomenologies, ethnographies, and grounded theories

Uses meta-ethnographic techniques—i.e., texts are described in terms of their concepts, ideas, and/or metaphors, which are subsequently translated into each other’s terms all the while preserving individual metaphors

A matrix of descriptive elements of different findings are then developed and distilled to arrive at a theoretical and comprehensive description of the phenomenon of interest

▪ Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

## • Definition 2

Metasynthesis is the most comprehensive generic term representative of the collection of qualitative synthesis approaches

▪ Can be described with different terms including metastudy, meta-ethnography, grounded formal theory, qualitative meta-analysis, aggregated analysis, qualitative meta-data analysis and metasynthesis

## • Definition 3

The final phase of metastudy—i.e., the outcomes arising from undertaking a meta-data analysis, metamethod, and metatheory

## 20. Metatheory (e.g., Zhao, 1991; Paterson et al., 2001)

• A component part of the metastudy process

• The process comprises critically exploring the direction in which theoretical frameworks gave to the primary research

• It analyzes the philosophical, cognitive, and theoretical perspectives, assumptions, sources, and contexts across multiple qualitative studies

• By examining the implications of the theoretical orientation, assumptions about any emerging theory in the primary studies may be made, including:

▪ Determining factors such as inclusion criteria, “angle of vision,” and “interpretive lens”

▪ Recognizing the major paradigms used

Understanding how the underlying theoretical framework influenced the choice of primary research question/research topic

▪ Ascertaining how primary data was collected and interpreted

▪ How theoretical assumptions were presented

▪ How the context of the primary reports influenced subsequent theoretical claims

▪ How the historical context of the primary studies and theory developed over time

▪ Critically analyzing the sociocultural and political context in which the studies were written

▪ Evaluating the quality of any emergent theory

• Similar to metanarrative, with some similarity with critical interpretive synthesis

• Unit of analysis: the theoretical structures used to collect data of individual qualitative studies

## 21. Metatriangulation (e.g., Lewis and Grimes, 1991; Saunders et al., 2003)

• A synthesis approach that explores multiple constituent studies in order to acquire greater insight from an emergent theme and/or resultant theories

• Emphasizes the relationships between the data in the primary studies and the inferences drawn therein premised on the influence of selected paradigms

• Substantive theory emerges from the meanings uncovered by different paradigms, occurring due to paradigmatic diversity fostering greater insight/creativit

Unit of analysis: the findings, and data, of individual qualitative studies

• A type of qualitative meta-analysis

• Describes any method that uses multiple sources of information to draw inferences about some domain of interest

## • Process:

▪ Groundwork

Define phenomenon of interest to guide metatheoretical sample selection

▪ Focus paradigm lenses to allow all major phenomenon constructs to be viewable

Determine sampling frame to identify best sources of information

▪ Collect metatheoretical sample ensuring that sample is representative of phenomenon of interest

▪ Data analysis

▪ Plan paradigm itinerary parsimoniously/thoroughly, including researcher thoughts

▪ Pilot test the itinerary

Conduct multiparadigm coding using tools to reduce coder bias

▪ Write paradigm accounts, including those tools used by researchers to construct the accounts

▪ Theory building

▪ Explore metaconjectures using appropriate lenses for viewing the phenomenon such as:

• Rational

• Pluralist

• Interpretive

Radical

▪ Attain a metaparadigm perspective, using tools to assist in managing data volume

▪ Articulate critical self-reflection at all times of the process

## 22. Narrative Summary (e.g., Evans & Kowankwo, 2000; Sleutel, 2000)

• Produces a general description/overview, and summarizes the major themes, of primary study findings and relevant issues

• May be theory-led, and include triangulation of different evidence types

• Many variants, with an absence of procedures/standards, and thus largely dependent on skills/prejudices of reviewer

• Close to a literature review

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

• Variations include:

▪ Recounting and describing findings

Interpretive and explicitly reflexive accounts that include commentary and higher levels of abstraction to account for complex dynamic processes

• Often used in systematic reviews alongside systematic searching and appraisal techniques

## 23. Narrative Synthesis (e.g., Barnett-Page & Thomas, 2009; Popay et al., 2007)

• Aims to arrange primary studies into homogenous groups, thereby making transparent any heterogeneity between them

• Study characteristics, quality, context, and findings are reported using a standard format, with similarities and differences being compared across studies.

• Use of word and text to summarize/explain findings

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

• Process:

▪ Develop a theory of how an intervention works, why, and for whom

▪ Develop a synthesis of the primary research

Use relevant tools/techniques to acquire textual descriptions that allow grouping/clustering of findings

▪ Explore and describe relationships between the data

▪ Determine outcome variability using conceptual, investigation, and methodological triangulation

▪ Translate data using thematic analysis

▪ Appraise synthesis outcomes to explain/identify moderators for relationships between data

▪ Undertake a critical reflection

## 24. Qualitative Comparative Analysis (e.g., Rantala & Hellström, 2001)

• Incorporates a Boolean analysis of necessary and sufficient conditions for particular outcomes to be observed founded on the presence/absence of independent variables and outcomes in primary studies

• Causation is understood in terms of necessary and sufficient conditions

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative case studies

• Process:

▪ Identify relevant cases and causal conditions

Construct a “truth table” showing all possible combinations of the presence and/or absence of independent/dependent variables

▪ Seek cases that match possible combinations of independent variables and outcomes

▪ Eliminate all logically inconsistent variables via a Boolean minimization process

From many potential explanations, only small numbers of combinations of variables remain to account for particular outcomes

• The methodology encourages an integrative/evolutionary approach to the creation of knowledge

• Systematic

• Positivist—is appropriate when a causal pathway is sought, and is thus ill suited to interpretivism

• Designed primarily to investigate/synthesize across case studies—does not cope well with other forms of qualitative study

## 25. Qualitative Cross-Case Analysis (e.g., Mays et al., 2005)

• Several strategies for conducting cross-case analyses exist that are suitable for synthesizing across different studies:

▪ Metamatrices for partitioning and clustering data in various ways,

▪ Content Analysis involving summary tables

▪ Case-ordered displays/time-ordered displays

## • Process:

A “start list” of codes is developed to provide broad categories for organizing data into meaningful clusters for analysis

In case analysis—that is, examining each case in isolation—is undertaken by coding individual reports and writing a case summary

▪ Additional categories are then added to accommodate and differentiate subthemes.

Tables summarizing each study according to these categories are then reduced into a table comprising 3/4 conceptual descriptors for each category

▪ Cross-case analysis is undertaken, noting commonalities and differences between the studies

• Interpretive

• Highly systematic

Unit of analysis: the findings and data of individual qualitative studies

• No advice on sampling or appraisal of the primary papers is given

• Can be for exploratory, explanatory, or descriptive research

## 26. Qualitative Meta-Analysis (e.g., Schreiber et al., 1997; Schreiber & Stern, 1997; Saunders et al. 2003; Berente et al., 2019)

• Defined in different ways:

• Definition 1: Generic term that focuses on theory generation

▪ Synthesizing qualitative findings into a theoretical model:

Theory building—that is, developing a formal theory by interrogating data from diverse sources (for example, metatriangulation)

▪ Theory explication—that is, elaborating abstract concepts

▪ Theoretical development—that is, synthesis findings are thickly descriptive

▪ Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

## • Definition 2: Aggregation

Aggregating a group of studies to discover essential elements, and then translating the results into an end product that transforms the original outcomes into a new conceptualization.

## • Definition 3: Interpretive

A process that follows the same, replicable procedure of a quantitative meta-analysis, but is more interpretative than aggregative

Instead of a statistical data analysis, the researcher analyzes textual reports, creating new interpretations in the analysis process

▪ Uses grounded theory techniques

## • Definition 4: Generic label

A generic term used to describe the synthesis of qualitative data that uses the findings from other qualitative studies linked by the same or a related topic

## • Definition 5: Alternative name

▪ An alternative name for metasynthesis, qualitative metasynthesis, metastudy, meta-ethnography, grounded formal theory, aggregated analysis, metatriangulation, systematic review or metaevaluation.

## 27. Qualitative Meta-Data Analysis (e.g., Sandelowski & Barroso, 2003a)

• Defined in different ways:

• Definition 1:

▪ An alternative label for qualitative meta-analysis, qualitative metasynthesis, and meta-ethnography • Definition 2:

A hermeneutic/dialectic approach that groups data based on method, sample, date, and specific issues related to the phenomenon of interest

▪ Influenced by meta-ethnography

Unit of analysis: the data and findings of individual qualitative studies

▪ Enables researchers to:

Apply an overarching theoretical lens to interpret and integrate findings into a guiding framework

• Consider existing research with a new lens

• Asks new/critical questions of the data, with the purpose of constructing new interpretations

## 28. Qualitative Metasynthesis (e.g., Sandelowski et al., 1997)

• Defined in different ways:

• Definition 1:

▪ A generic term for undertaking qualitative synthesis using an interpretive, integrative approach • Definition 2:

Depending on purpose and what primary findings allows in the way of analytic treatment, incorporates several qualitative methodological approaches:

▪ If the purpose is to develop theory in a target domain, grounded theory may be appropriate

If the purpose is to address the relationship among the findings in individual reports, a translation technique may be suitable, such as meta-ethnography

If the purpose is to show how research findings are both products and producers of discourse, a narrative synthesis methodology is appropriate

• Definition 3:

An alternative label for/known as metasynthesis, metasynthesis, metastudy, meta-ethnography, grounded (formal) theory, qualitative meta-analysis, qualitative meta-data analysis, qualitative research integration, and aggregated analysis

## 29. Qualitative Research Integration (e.g., Thorne et al. (2004)

• Generic term that refers to empirical studies directed toward the combination of research findings in qualitative studies

• Aim is to systematically and judiciously appraise reports of completed qualitative studies in a target research domain

• Used as an overriding term to include qualitative metasummary, qualitative metasynthesis/qualitative metasynthesis, and qualitative meta-analysis

• Unit of analysis are findings in reports, with orientation to data being empirical

• Alternatively, the original label “metasynthesis” was subsequently changed to “qualitative research integration”

## 30. Realist Synthesis (e.g., Rycroft-Malone et al., 2012)

• Theory-driven synthesis that uses purposive sampling in primary studies

• Basic theory is refined concerning applicability in context

• Starting with the theory that underlies a particular piece of research, it seeks evidence across multiple forms, subsequently integrating them as forms of proof or refutation of theory

• Involves ascertaining underlying causal mechanisms, and then investigating how they work and under what conditions

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

• Philosophically rooted in realism—that is, social reality is mainly an interpretative reality of social actors,

causal explanations are achievable, and social actors evaluate their social reality

• Stages include:

▪ Defining the scope of the review—that is, concept mining and framework formulation

▪ Searching for, scrutinizing, extracting, and synthesizing the evidence

▪ Developing a narrative, including hypotheses

## 31. Secondary Analysis of Primary Data (e.g., Heaton, 2008)

• Analysis and synthesis of existing qualitative primary databases as a means to review the literature, answer the original research question(s) using different methods, or answer new questions using “old” data

• Unit of Analysis: primary data sets from original qualitative papers

• Requires access to the original dataset used in primary qualitative studies

• Often undertaken by original primary researcher/collaborators/colleagues due to difficulty accessing primary datasets

• 2 types of synthesis possible:

▪ Amplified analysis—2+ datasets are compared/contrasted

Assorted analysis—reuse of existing qualitative data is performed alongside collection/analysis of primary qualitative data

• Allows different methodologies to be mixed, but only if subject is common to both datasets

• Also used as a generic term for qualitative synthesis

## 32. Systematic Review (e.g., Denyer & Tranfield, 2006)

• Positivist, aggregative

• Defined in different ways:

• Definition 1:

▪ A form of literature review premised on an explicit, rigorous, and transparent approach that collects and critically analyzes multiple research studies

▪ Employs strict exclusion criteria while aiming for comprehensive sampling

Unit of analysis: the data of individual qualitative studies

▪ Process:

▪ Identify a review focus, search for and map the available evidence

▪ Specify a review question

Identify studies to be included

▪ Extract data and appraising study quality

▪ Synthesize—often a narrative synthesis

▪ Report the results of the review/dissemination

Definition 2:

▪ A generic term that potentially covers any (quantitative or) qualitative synthesis approach

Definition 3:

▪ A way of comparing quantitative research that proceeds according to well-determined steps that include statistical analysis of the pooled results of studies—that is, an alternative name for metaanalysis.

## 33. Textual Narrative Synthesis (e.g., Lucas et al., 2007)

• An approach to the systematic review/synthesis of findings from multiple studies.

• Relies primarily on the use of text and words to summarize/explain the findings of the synthesis

• Defining characteristic is the adoption of a textual approach to the process of synthesis that “tells the story” of the findings from the included studies

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

• Focuses on describing/summarizing primary data in a highly structured and detailed way, and then translating the studies into one another

• Output is more directly relevant to policy makers and designers of interventions than methods with a more constructivist orientation

• Involves a commentary reporting on context, study characteristics, quality, and findings, using the differences, scope, and similarities among the studies, to draw conclusions from across the studies.

• Structured summaries can be developed that elaborate on, and put into context, the extracted data

• Embraces critical realism—that is, our knowledge of reality is mediated by our beliefs and perceptions

## 34. Thematic Analysis (e.g., Garcia et al., 2002; Dixon-Woods et al., 2006b)

• Systematic identification of significant/reoccurring/common themes in primary research and summarizing them under thematic headings.

• Can be either:

Theory driven—that is, oriented so as to be able to evaluate particular themes by interrogating the literature

Data driven—motivated by the themes identified in the literature itself

• Overlaps narrative summary and content analysis

Unit of analysis: the findings and data of individual qualitative studies

• Process:

▪ Identify prominent or recurrent themes in the literature

▪ Summarize findings of different studies under these thematic headings

▪ Produce summary table(s) that describe key points

• Allows prominent themes to be clearly identified, thereby providing an organized/structured means to manage literature in such themes

Challenge—not explicit about procedures and aims—that is, the extent to which thematic analyses should be interpretive or descriptive.

## 35. Thematic Synthesis (e.g., Thomas & Harden, 2008)

• Methodology identifies recurring themes in the primary literature, analyses these themes and draws conclusions via systematic reviews

• Purpose is to develop analytical themes via a descriptive synthesis, and then determine explanations relevant to a particular review question

• Synthesis component involves an iterative process of grouping themes inductively from primary papers into overarching categories that capture the differences, similarities, and relationships between themes

• Free codes of findings are organized into descriptive themes, which are then interpreted to yield analytical themes that produce new interpretations above and beyond those seen in primary studies

• Use of software for coding acceptable

• Unit of analysis: the findings of individual qualitative studies

• Interpretive, inductive, with themes developed using a constant comparison approach

• Combines and adapts elements of meta-ethnography and grounded theory

## Appendix B: Hierarchical Cluster Analysis

In presenting the means for qualitative synthesis method selection, the authors recognize that being exposed to unfamiliar methods that are similar to already known methods provides a valuable opportunity for acquiring new knowledge outcomes. To this end, the authors present the outcome of an agglomerative hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) to present which qualitative synthesis methods are most similar.

The procedure used for conducting the HCA included selecting a sample of objects, defining a set of data variables on which to measure the entities, calculating measures of proximity between all entities using subjective reasoning and reviewing in a matrix of similarity, grouping entities based on proximity scores via a clustering algorithm using HCA software, and creating a graphic depiction of the emergent groupings to facilitate interpretation of the results via a dendrogram output. The authors thus created a synthesis method characteristic table, whereby each method’s characteristics were reviewed against a set list of characteristics previously used to determine a qualitative synthesis method’s “essence” (Sandelowski & Barroso, 2007) <sup>11</sup>. Thereafter, and with a similarity matrix being required for HCA, a similarity score was generated for each method versus every other method using scores between 1-100; a score of “1” represents two methods being identical, with a score of “100” being two methods unable to be more different. The authors subsequently undertook a subjective investigation whereby each method was compared against every other method using the descriptions generated (Appendix A) to derive a subjective score of method similarity (Appendix C). The output is presented in a dendrogram format that provides a graphical representation of the hierarchical graduation of groupings<sup>12</sup>. The dendrogram’s horizontal axis represents the distance, or dissimilarity, between each method, with the vertical axis signifying the methods and how they cluster. The level at which different method branches subsequently merge relative to the "root" of the dendrogram is related to the level of similarity. Thus, the closer to the dendrogram root a group of methodologies converge, the closer their strength of method similarity. Conversely, should methods form a grouping further away from the dendrogram root, so their relative strength of similarity is less. Figure B1 shows the resultant dendrogram output:

As presented, grounded (formal) theory (GFT), qualitative meta-data analysis (QMDA), meta-interpretation (MI), metafamily (MF), and interpretive metasynthesis (IM) are the methodologies that cluster most closely from a similarity perspective at the dendrogram root. GFT and QMDA being most similar as a cluster of two followed by MI joining. Separately, IM and MF form a cluster of two before joining to form the cluster of 5. It is also important to note that the position of the vertical line presented at 0.0484667 in Figure B1. can move either left or right based on the level of abstraction preferred by the research team; moving to the left enables the researcher to determine those methodologies that cluster based on a greater strength of similarity. Conversely, moving to the right allows the research team to discriminate between methodological groupings premised on their distinct differences. As a result, the synthesist that is knowledgeable of one synthesis methodology becomes better placed to consider alternative approaches to qualitative synthesis that are different but similar to other methodologies. Given this learning, a synthesist is subsequently able to leverage alternative methodologies not previously considered.

## About the Authors

Richard Skinner is a professor of practice in the Department of Decision and Information Sciences at the C.T. Bauer College of Business, University of Houston, and a visiting professor at the Royal Agricultural University (United Kingdom). Prior to entering academia, Richard enjoyed 20 years of professional experience in information systems management within the energy and investment banking sectors in the UK. He received an executive MBA from Henley Business School, University of Reading in 2013, and a PhD in information systems from the C. T. Bauer College of Business, University of Houston in 2018. Richard’s research interests include IS project management recovery, IS project retrospectives, Meta-methodological research, and the Delphi method. In his spare time, Richard likes to relax with his wife Katy and his cat Roxy.

R. Ryan Nelson is the Murray Research Professor and director of the Center for the Management of Information Technology (CMIT) at the McIntire School of Commerce of the University of Virginia. He received his PhD in business administration from the University of Georgia in 1985 and spent five years on the faculty of the University of Houston before joining the University of Virginia. Ryan has published papers in a variety of academic and professional journals, including MIS Quarterly, MIS Quarterly Executive, Journal of Management Information Systems, Communications of the Association for Information Systems, Communications of the ACM, and Information & Management, among others. He has also published two books, and in 2021, he published a monograph based on his research over the past 20 years entitled IT Project Management: Lessons Learned from Project Retrospectives 1999- 2020 in Foundations and Trends in Information Systems. Ryan currently serves on the editorial board of MIS Quarterly Executive. When he is not traveling with his wife Joelle, Ryan enjoys playing with his cat Griffin (a synthesis of lion and eagle).

Wynne W. Chin is the C. T. Bauer Professor of Decision and Information Sciences in the C.T. Bauer College of Business at the University of Houston. He received AB, MS, MBA, and PhD degrees in biophysics, biomedical/ chemical engineering, business, and computers and information from the University of California–Berkeley, Northwestern University, and the University of Michigan, respectively. He is one of the foremost exponents of the partial least squares path modeling technique and is credited for keeping it afloat in the 1990s with his PLS-Graph software made available since 1990 to more than 9000+ researchers worldwide. Going back over 30 years, he was the first to introduce the use of Monte Carlo simulation to the IS discipline for evaluating structural equation modeling algorithms. He also introduced bootstrapping for PLS analyses in 1988. Wynne’s research has received over 75,000 citations, with a top-ten most cited paper in MIS Quarterly and top-five most cited papers in Information Systems Research, as well as a Google Scholar H-index of 66, which places him among the most impactful researchers in his field. He was awarded with Fellow of the Association of Information Systems in 2013. He hangs out with Yoshi, Hiro, and his cat Shitsuteki.
