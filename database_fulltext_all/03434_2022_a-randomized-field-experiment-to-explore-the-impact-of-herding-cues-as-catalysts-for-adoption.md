# A RANDOMIZED FIELD EXPERIMENT TO EXPLORE THE IMPACT OF HERDING CUES AS CATALYSTS FOR ADOPTION<sup>1</sup>

Yue (Katherine) Feng Faculty of Business, Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hong Hom, HONG KONG {katherine.feng@polyu.edu.hk}

Jennifer L. Claggett School of Business, Wake Forest University, Winston-Salem, NC 27109 U.S.A. {claggejl@wfu.edu}

Elena Karahanna Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602 U.S.A. {ekarah@uga.edu}

Kar Yan Tam School of Business and Management, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, HONG KONG {kytam@ust.hk}

A herding cue is a lean information signal that an individual receives about the aggregate number of others who have engaged in a behavior that may result in herd behavior. Given the ease with which they can be leveraged as implementation interventions or design features on online sites, herding cues hold the promise to provide a means to influence adoption behaviors. Yet, little attention has been devoted in the IS adoption literature to understanding the effects of herding cues. Given that herding cues are just one of several forms of social influence on adoption behaviors and are relatively lean in nature, understanding their viability as an implementation intervention necessitates understanding their effects in the presence of (1) other forms of social influence, which also serve to reduce uncertainty and signal the appropriateness of technology adoption, and (2) an individual’s own beliefs about adopting. In this vein, we conducted a randomized field experiment to examine the use of a herding cue as an implementation intervention to hasten adoption behaviors. The research model was evaluated using survival analysis by combining the data from the field experiment with two waves of surveys, and archival logs of adoption. Our results show that a herding cue (1) directly impacts the time it takes an individual to adopt a technology, (2) amplifies the effects of peer behaviors (another type of informative social influence), but has no impact on the effect of subjective norm (a form of normative social influence), and (3) dampens the effects of an individual’s private beliefs about the usefulness of a technology. Our paper disentangles herding information signals to define a herding cue as distinct from other herd behavior triggers, explores how it may interact with other forms of social influences and private beliefs to influence adoption behaviors, and, on a practical level, provides evidence of how a herding cue can be a tangible intervention to accelerate technology adoption.

Keywords: Herding cue, herd behavior, social influence, time of adoption, randomized field experiment

## Introduction

A herding cue (HC) is a lean information signal that an individual receives about the actions of others (typically in the form of how many others have engaged in a behavior), that may result in herd behavior (Çelen & Kariv, 2004; Cheung et al., 2014). HCs influence an individual’s own actions by signaling the appropriateness of the specific behavior, including adopting technology (e.g., Duan et al., 2009; Sun, 2013), thus reducing uncertainty surrounding the decision (Cialdini and Goldstein, 2004) and causing an individual to follow the herd (Banerjee, 1992; Bikhchandani et al., 1992). Given the ease with which HCs can be leveraged as implementation interventions or online design features (e.g., showing number of downloads), they may prove an effective means to influence adoption behaviors. However, HCs are just one information signal that may initiate herd behavior, and they are relatively lean in nature. An important first step to exploring the impacts of HCs is to disentangle the herding information signals that may be present in adoption decisions. Further, while herding provides one perspective on adoption, a parallel stream of research in technology adoption provides another. With the exception of Sun (2013), these two streams have developed in parallel. A more holistic understanding of how HCs influence adoption requires that we integrate the two. The objective of the study is to gain a deeper understanding of whether and how HCs, as a form of social influence, impact adoption behaviors in the presence of other forms of social influence and one’s own beliefs about adopting.

Understanding the viability of a HC as an intervention necessitates understanding its effects in the presence of other forms of social influence, which also reduce uncertainty and signal the appropriateness of adoption. In addition to HCs, herding research has also identified the importance of observations of others’ behavior in the environment (referred to as peer behavior), while technology acceptance literature has focused primarily on subjective norm. These may make a HC redundant and, therefore, not a significant influence. On the other hand, a HC may reinforce or diminish the impact of other forms of social influence on adoption. Given the absence of empirical evidence on this, the first objective of the study is to examine the effects of a HC on adoption (and, specifically, speed of adoption) in the presence of other forms of social influence and to understand how the HC interacts with these in complementary or substitutive ways. In doing so, we aim to take a step toward theorizing the synergies, or lack thereof, among different types of social influence and contribute more broadly to avenues for future research that take a more nuanced and multifaceted view of social influence effects.

Prior research in herd behavior suggests that a likely mechanism of the phenonmenon is an information cascade, where one’s own private beliefs are discounted (i.e., their personal opinions about the product or service) in order to follow the herd. Yet private beliefs about the technology make up the core components of most technology adoption models. So, while one stream of research stresses the importance of private beliefs, the other argues social influences suppress their role. Exploring this, Sun finds that willingness to discount own information affects how people adjust their private beliefs during adoption, but not that HCs drive the propensity to discount beliefs. Therefore, the second objective of our study is to improve our understanding of the interplay between a HC and the relevant private beliefs in technology adoption. The diminishing effects of previously held private beliefs in the presence of a HC may potentially occur via two mechanisms: information cascades or revised private beliefs. The former suggests that users discount private beliefs and follow the herd, aligning with a herding perspective. The latter suggests a process of social learning where private beliefs are changed by the HC, aligning with a technology acceptance perspective. We probe into these two potential mechanisms to discern the underlying process via which this occurs, contributing to our understanding of the mechanisms via which a HC interplays with private beliefs.

Our dependent variable focuses on time of adoption: how fast a user adopts. Using time of adoption addresses a previously noted limitation of herd behavior research and a call to move beyond a dichotomous “yes/no” dependent variable for capturing a user’s decision (Çelen & Kariv, 2004). A dichotomous variable assumes a user must decide at a given point in time, where in reality users may opt to postpone the decision and continue to gather information (Bikhchandani et al., 1998). Viewing the dependent variable as how long it takes a user to adopt allows us to detect changes brought about by a HC; it may be that a user would have eventually adopted anyway, but, because of the HC, adopts sooner. The speed to action is important when considering volitional technology adoption, as a core lesson from innovation diffusion theory is that earlier adoptions from individuals lead to a condensed diffusion cycle in a social system (i.e., more widespread adoption) (Rogers, 1983). This aligns with an underlying principle of social influence, which is that one user’s adoption influences others, such that group momentum in technology adoption can be a powerful, virtuous cycle. Further, in technology adoption, the competition for people’s attention is increasingly difficult, and having a fast adoption cycle is viewed as useful, if not paramount, to success in volitional situations (Bullock, 2018).

We tested our model through a randomized field experiment where we implemented a HC intervention. Examining a realworld context, where other forms of social influence and communications about the technology abound, provides a strong test of the impact of a HC on adoption. Our analysis combined data from the field experiment, two waves of surveys, and archival logs that captured time of adoption. Our findings show that a HC exerts significant influence on speed of adoption beyond other forms of social influence, and beyond the effect of individuals’ private beliefs. Further, the HC amplifies some forms of social influence (peer behavior, which is a similar informational form of social influence) but not others (subjective norm, which is a normative form of social influence). The HC’s dampening effect on private beliefs is similarly nuanced: it suppresses the effects of perceived usefulness but not the effect of perceived ease-of-use. Our post hoc analysis suggests that the dampening effect is due to people discounting their own information (i.e., information cascade perspective) and not due to an underlying change in those beliefs (i.e., social learning perspective).

## Theoretical Background

## Social Influences on Technology Adoption

Social influence occurs when individuals are affected by others in their social environment to adapt their beliefs, attitudes, or behaviors (Cialdini & Goldstein, 2004; Kelman, 1958; Latané, 1981, 1996). The desire to conform due to social influence is driven by two basic human needs: the desire to be right, where information gathered from others is factored into decision making (informative motivation), and the desire to be liked, where conforming to the positive expectations of others leads to social acceptance (normative motivation) (Cialdini & Trost, 1998; Deutsch & Gerard, 1955). Two broad forms of social influence are prevalent in the information systems literature: herd behavior—an example of social influence motivated to inform, and subjective norm— an example of social influence motivated by norms. While these are by no means exhaustive, we believe that they represent the dominant forms of social influence in herding studies on adoption and in technology acceptance studies.<sup>2</sup> Yet prior research examining the effect of HCs rarely considers other forms of social influence, such as subjective norm, or users’ private beliefs, and research on technology acceptance has largely ignored HCs. A notable exception is Sun (2013) who, in a lab experiment, has examined the effect of a HC on an individual’s adoptive and post-adoptive beliefs and intentions to adopt a technology. The study focused on exploring two underlying mechanisms for the effect of a HC: imitating others (which was influenced by the presence of a HC) and discounting own information (which was not significantly influenced by the presence of a HC). While the focus of Sun’s study was on the effect of a HC on one’s private beliefs through imitating others and discounting own information (treating subjective norm as a control), we focus on how a HC interacts with other forms of social influence that the individuals are experiencing in their natural context. Further, we explore Sun’s finding that the HC did not influence adoption through discounting own information, by examining the interplay between the HC and private beliefs in a real-world context where the adoption decision is more consequential and also delve into the underlying mechanisms for the effects we observe. We thus complement and extend Sun’s work that integrates HCs into technology adoption by considering both information signals available to initiate herd behavior (peer behavior and HCs) as well as subjective norm.

## Herd Behavior and Information Signals

Herd behavior is defined as a sequence of individuals making the same decision and, therefore, converging on identical actions (Çelen & Kariv, 2004). It has been used in a variety of contexts to study the phenomenon at the herd level (e.g., Avery & Zemsky, 1998; Cipriani & Guarino, 2014; Drehmann et al., 2005). Although herd behavior has been examined in the IS literature as noted by Sun, more research is needed to elucidate herd behavior on adoption at the individual level. Herd behavior is the result of individuals being influenced by the actions of others, and it may be an important factor in understanding why and when people choose to adopt a technology.<sup>3</sup> A necessary first step in studying herd behavior at the individual level is to develop a more nuanced understanding of the herding information signals that the individual receives that may convey information about the actions of others.

The idea of a herding signal—an information signal about what other people (i.e., the herd) are doing—is assumed in herd behavior literature, but not explicitly addressed or defined. In early lab experiments in psychology, subjects could see the actions of others in front of them in line (e.g., Anderson & Holt, 1997), but did not have any personal ties or knowledge about the individuals. Presumably then, the number of people that chose an action before them was the catalyst for people following the herd (Anderson & Holt, 1997; Çelen & Kariv, 2004, 2005). Two important implications should be noted from how these experiments created herding information signals. First, participants noticed the people before them who were in the same environment and faced a similar decision. Even if participants did not consciously count the number of people engaging in the behavior, they were left with an impression of those actions. Second, the number of people engaged in the behavior mattered (i.e., the more people an individual observed, the stronger the herding signal).

These implications exemplify two different types of herding information signals prevalent in technology adoption decisions: peer behavior and a herding cue of aggregated information about the actions of others. Though prior work has not made a clear distinction between these, as we illustrate below, we believe that these are distinct and exert their influence through different mechanisms. We next discuss these two types of herding information signals to bring out their distinctions and clearly define HCs, which is the focus of our study.

## Peer Behavior

Watching what others do in one’s environment in order to inform a decision can serve as a signal that triggers herd behavior. For example, when entering a restaurant, a person may notice what menu items others are enjoying when trying to decide on an order. These naturally occurring information signals come from the actions of others in the individual’s environment, which they may then observe and use to inform their decision making (Valente, 1995). The influence of peers’ actions on an individual’s decision to adopt has been studied as network proximity effect (e.g., Rice & Aydin, 1991), personal network exposure (Hsieh et al., 2008), and peer influence (e.g., de Matos et al., 2014). According to Latané (1996), people tend to be heavily influenced by those who are close in physical space, resulting in local agreement on important values and attitudes. For brevity, we refer to the social influence of potentially observing peer behavior in one’s environment as peer behavior. Given that peer behavior influence is considered a local phenomenon, and is best studied in communities of individuals in close proximity to one another (usually groups of less than 100) (Leskovec et al., 2009), we define peer behavior as the potential influence from observing people in close proximity (e.g., those located and working in the same physical space as the individual) who have already adopted a technology.

## Herding Cues

While peer behavior occurs by observing people in one’s environment, a herding information signal can also be designed to specifically inform individuals about the number of people already engaged in the activity, information that aggregates others’ behaviors and which we refer to as a herding cue. Technology has made it easier to calculate and share this type of information with users. Although the term herding cue is rarely used, this type of signal has been typically operationalized as the number of software downloads displayed on a website (Duan et al., 2009), as statements about the number of adopters already using a technology (Sun, 2013), and as statements about the rapid increase in the number of users and market share gains (Vedadi & Warkentin, 2020). These HCs signal to potential adopters that others are using the technology and are typical of the types of signals to which internet users are routinely exposed, such as aggregated user review scores or number of “likes” or views on content.

We argue that HCs are a form of social influence especially implementable by organizations, because they can both design and make these visible in a variety of ways. Further, due to their lean nature, HCs are cognitively easy to detect and process (MacMillan, 2002). Focusing on the potential power of making the actions of others visible to invoke herd behavior, we define a herding cue as an information signal designed to convey the number of other people that are engaged in a specific behavior.

Despite their potential, only a handful of individual-level IS studies empirically consider HCs. Specifically, Cheung et al. (2014) examined purchase decisions in a field experiment where subjects could see the purchase history of others in their social network<sup>4</sup> and Huang and Chen (2006) noted the effects of visible high sales volume signals on purchase preferences. Although not related to technology adoption, these studies indicate how HCs can be delivered over digital platforms. However, they do not capture individuals’ private beliefs and other forms of social influence. The only study that we are aware of that has examined the use of HCs in technology adoption is Sun, which we discuss later.

## Subjective Norm

Defined as “the perceived social pressure to perform or not to perform the behavior” (Ajzen, 1991, p. 188), subjective norm is a prominent form of social influence in technology acceptance research (e.g., Lewis et al., 2003; Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006; Venkatesh et al., 2003), especially when examining adoption (rather than post-adoption) behaviors (e.g., Karahanna et al., 1999). Subjective norm reflects a user’s perceptions that people around them (1) have beliefs regarding the user’s technology use and (2) that it is important to comply with those beliefs.

Subjective norm and information signals that trigger herding behaviors are theoretically distinct (Hsieh et al., 2008; Sun, 2013). Sun articulates well the distinctions between the two, which we summarize here. HCs are received as observations about the actions of prior adopters, while subjective norms are the perceptions about what others think related to the adoption decision. As such, HCs are used to infer the value of the technology, and are useful in overcoming uncertainties about whether or not to adopt. From a social influence theory perspective, these information signals would be considered to be informative in nature. In contrast, subjective norm is an assessment of the opinions of those important to the adopter, and may help make a decision that is favorably judged by those people. It is a reflection of the weight of social pressure by influential, to the adopter, people, and from a social influence perspective it would be considered a normative form of influence. ## Theoretical Model and Hypotheses

Our focus is on the effect of the HC on time of adoption, and its interactions with other forms of social influence and private beliefs (see Figure 1). Our theorizing articulates why we expect the HC to interact with subjective norm and peer behavior, both of which have been shown by prior research to exert a direct influence on adoption (e.g., Hsieh et al., 2008; Karahanna et al., 1999). Further, herding theory suggests that individuals may discount their private beliefs when influenced by herding information signals, suggesting that the HC moderates the effect of private beliefs on adoption. The technology acceptance literature, identifies the most salient private beliefs to be perceived usefulness and perceived ease-of-use, with perceived usefulness exerting a more consistent influence (e.g., see meta-analyses by King and He (2006) and Schepers and Wetzels (2007)). We do not include hypotheses for the direct positive effects of peer behavior, subjective norm, and private beliefs on adoption because these have been posited and tested in prior work and are not the focus of our study.

## Main Effect of Herding Cue

Herd behavior occurs when individuals imitate the actions of others (Bikhchandani et al., 1992). To summarize, this occurs when a HC provides information conveying that others are engaged in the specific behavior, which may cause an individual to “follow the herd.” A reaction to a HC has been explained as usually a rational decision, as the actions of others is a proxy for information other people may have about a decision not available to the individual (Banerjee, 1992; Bikhchandani et al., 1992). This is referred to as social proof—gathering information from others about what to do (Cialdini, 1993)—and is especially helpful if one is uncertain about the decision (Cheung et al., 2014). In the context of technology acceptance, imitating others had a positive impact on behavioral intention (Sun, 2013), and the number of people that downloaded software positively impacted the count of future downloads (Duan et al., 2009). In addition to reducing uncertainty, herding behaviors reduce or eliminate the effort for personal cognition about the decision. Given that people tend to be cognitive misers (e.g., Fiske & Taylor, 1984), we expect that following a HC allows for a simpler, faster, and less effortful decision-making process. Therefore, consistent with the literature, we expect the presentation of a HC to cause individuals to adopt technology sooner than individuals without exposure to the HC.

H1: The presence of a herding cue will result in faster adoption of an IT.

## Moderating Effects of Herding Cue on Other Forms of Social Influence

Peer behavior influence can vary greatly between individuals in the same environment due to awareness (Yi & Davis, 2003). If a potential user simply fails to notice relevant behaviors of those around them, then they are likely not influenced (Rice & Aydin, 1991). However, once a user is attuned to those behaviors, the influence can be impactful in their adoption decisions. This suggests that the effects of peer behavior may be influenced by priming. Priming occurs when stimuli implant a concept in working memory that alters subsequent behaviors or impressions (Bargh & Chartrand, 1999; Molden, 2014). A core idea is that once a construct is activated in the mind by the stimulus, its accessibility in memory is heightened (Srull & Wyer, 1979) and cognitive processes such as selective attention and information retrieval, related to the construct and to linked constructs, may be affected (Wheeler et al., 2014; Yi, 1990). This, in turn, may shape how a perception or evaluation is formed by the individual (Higgins & Eitam, 2014).

We expect the HC to act as a stimulus that causes individuals to be more aware of the actions of others regarding a technology, which may, in turn, hasten the decision to adopt. An individual who receives a HC indicating numerous others have adopted the system, will be primed to notice (or remember) others in their environment using the software (peer behavior influence). Both of these forms of social influence, peer behavior and HCs, are informational in nature, which further lends credibility to the priming mechanism: they are closely related cognitive constructs and serve similar purposes (i.e., mitigating uncertainty). Therefore, the presence of one could prime increased attention to the other. In contrast, the presence of proximal peers engaging in the behavior may simply go unnoticed if people’s attention to the behavior is not successfully triggered, thus reducing the potential for social influence (Rice & Aydin, 1991). Without the nudge provided by priming to direct attention to the actions of others, the effect of peer behavior may not occur at all or, even if it does occur, it may take more time for people to notice peer behavior. The herding cue mechanism prompts attention to existing peer behavior that demonstrates use of technology. Therefore, we posit the priming effect of the HC will hasten the adoption decision<sup>5</sup> by reducing the time it takes to notice peer behaviors (which may also otherwise go unnoticed), enhancing the potential for social influence by the actions of others to occur faster.

H2(a): The presence of a herding cue will positively moderate the impact of peer behavior on time of adoption such that the presence of a herding cue will strengthen the effect of peer behavior on time of adoption.

While a HC is a form of informational social influence, subjective norm is a form of normative social influence, as the motivation to follow the opinions of salient others is tied to avoiding negative judgments or gaining positive ones. We expect the two forms of social influence—subjective norm and HC—to be mutually reinforcing signals. As shown in laboratory experiments, the combination of observing and being given verbal advice by other participants made people more likely to be socially influenced than either one alone (Çelen et al., 2010). This is referred to as the advice paradox, since the verbal advice does not, objectively, add any new information, but nevertheless strengthens the effects of observational learning. Similarly, we expect a HC to amplify the influence of subjective norm by reinforcing the effect of the normative influence of what important others’ think one should do by providing information about unknown others behaviors, even in cases when this may not add any new information.

Further, receiving various social influence signals, that are in agreement, amplifies the total impact by increasing the sense of urgency around the decision to act (Byrnes, 2013; Sieber & Lanzetta, 1964). This can be thought of as a bombardment effect: multiple signals (informative and normative) from the social environment are being received and suggest the same course of action. Such a bombardment effect, precipitated through the introduction of the HC, makes the individual more acutely aware of the normative social influence exerted by their important others. In turn, this increases the pressure to comply with the influence and creates an increased sense of urgency, catalyzing faster adoption. For these reasons, we posit that the HC’s signal will strengthen the impacts of subjective norm<sup>6</sup> and hasten the decision to adopt.

H2(b): The presence of a herding cue will positively moderate the impact of subjective norm on time of adoption such that the presence of a herding cue will strengthen the effect of subjective norm on time of adoption.

## Moderating Effects of Herding Cue on Private Beliefs

A common mechanism underlying herd behavior is an information cascade,<sup>7</sup> which occurs when individuals ignore their private information (i.e., their own beliefs or opinions about the action or product), and follow the observed behaviors of others (Bikhchandani et al., 1992). While this might seem like the proverbial “lemmings following each other” phenomenon, information cascades have been described as often rational, since individuals are presumably looking for information in order to make the best decision (Bikhchandani et al., 1992). People are thought to discount their own private beliefs in making a decision in the presence of a HC because they believe the collective wisdom of those before them that made the decision may be superior to their own information. Therefore, the impact of individuals’ private judgment will be largely reduced when information cascades occur (Bikhchandani et al., 1998; Duan et al., 2009). It is possible that this effect also persists in settings where individuals form their private beliefs based on a wealth of information available to them (e.g., information and training sessions on the software, etc.). Further, this discounting phenomenon can occur regardless of whether the private beliefs are positive or negative in nature, as the HC dampens the reliance on private beliefs.

To capture this discounting effect, Sun developed a “discounting own information” construct, defined as the degree to which people disregard their own private beliefs when making an adoption decision. Interestingly, though discounting own private information had an effect on how one’s private beliefs changed, it was not influenced by the HC (a message disclosing the number or number and identity of early adopters), which seemingly contradicts prior work in herd behavior (Anderson & Holt, 1997; Çelen et al., 2010; Cheung et al., 2014). The study, though, did not directly examine the moderating effect of the HC on the effect of one’s initial or adjusted beliefs on adoption behavior. Notwithstanding, given the preponderance of supporting evidence, we posit that a HC will make it more likely that individuals discount their own private beliefs in making an adoption decision. Thus, we posit:

H3(a): The presence of a herding cue will negatively moderate the impact of perceived usefulness on time of adoption such that the presence of a herding cue will weaken the effect of perceived usefulness on time of adoption.

H3(b): The presence of a herding cue will negatively moderate the impact of perceived ease of use on time of adoption such that the presence of a herding cue will weaken the effect of perceived ease of use on time of adoption.

We base our third set of hypotheses on the underlying logic that information cascades drive many instances of herd behavior (Bikhchandani et al., 1992), including in technology adoption. However, an alternative explanation is that users learn from social influence, and change their own private beliefs (Bandura, 1985). These revised private beliefs then drive adoption. Empically, this implies a weaker relationship between the original set of beliefs and adoption behavior; that is, we would observe a similar negative moderation as with discounting of beliefs. This alternative mechanism more closely aligns with Sun’s findings. Therefore, our post hoc analysis (see Post Hoc B) examines whether the HC causes individuals to discount their own private beliefs (as suggested by the theorizing of information cascades in herd behavior literature and as we hypothesize) or whether they actually change their own private beliefs because of the social learning that may occur via social influence, accounting for the same effect.

## Methodology

We tested our hypotheses via a randomized field experiment in the context of Microsoft Office 365 implementation. Our hypotheses were evaluated using data from the field experiment, two waves of surveys, and archival logs. We used a survival model to evaluate the effects.

## Experimental Design

The field experiment was conducted at a university in Asia, where the undergraduate students were encouraged to adopt the cloud-based services provided by Microsoft Office 365. An added benefit was the offering of lifetime email service which they could continue to use after graduation. If they chose not to migrate, they would continue, while enrolled at the university, to have access to the Microsoft office tools they were already using. The students’ decision was completely voluntary as the same general functionality would be available to them irrespective. An invitation email from the university with instructions and a link for migration was sent to students (see Appendix A for the email script). We randomly divided the participants into two groups (treatment and control). In the treatment group, the email had one piece of additional information: the herding cue. This sentence was presented just below the migration link and read: “No. of students currently migrated to Office 365: XXXX”.

Figure 2 shows the Office 365 implementation and research design timeline. The university started promoting Office 365 about six weeks before implementation. During that time, students formed their private beliefs about using Office 365 and their perceptions of the expectations of top university administration and peers based on a series of activities including information sessions and training programs. We sent the pre-experiment survey (T1) to undergraduate students three weeks before the launch of Office 365, which 923 students completed and agreed to participate in follow-up research related to Office 365 implementation. The students were not made aware of the purpose of our experiment and were assured confidentiality. Those who completed the questionnaire received HK\$50 and a chance to win one of ten Apple iPads.

The experimental HC manipulation via email invitation was conducted one week after the soft launch to ensure that some students had migrated to Office 365 prior to the experiment. The number of a student’s peers who migrated during the week between the Office 365 launch and our manipulation and whose behavior could be potentially observed was used to construct peer behavior. The email was sent over two days, due to system constraints.<sup>8</sup> Students were randomly assigned to treatment and control across the two days. After the invitation email, we tracked participants’ adoption for one month. Time to adoption was measured from the release of the invitation email to when the student migrated. We then emailed the participants a post-experiment survey (T2) which included a manipulation check of the HC. After combining the data from the field experiment, two waves of surveys, and the archival logs of adoption, and screening responses, we ended up with 426 responses.<sup>9</sup>

Attrition can threaten validity if those who drop out of the treatment group differ from those who drop out of the control group (selection-maturation threat; Campbell & Stanley, 1963). The results of our analysis (Table B1, Appendix B) show no differences between the treatment and control groups in the (1) rate of attrition (T-statistic = 0.401, p-value = 0.688); (2) mean scores on all constructs in T1 for the subjects who did not respond to the T2 questionnaire (all p-values for construct differences were greater than 0.05); and (3) mean scores on all constructs for the subjects who responded to both the T1 and T2 questionnaires (all p-values for differences >.05). In addition to comparing treatment and control groups, we also compared the subjects who responded to both T1 and T2 questionnaires to those who responded only to the T1 questionnaire. No significant differences were found.

We checked the effectiveness of our randomization using ANOVA and Tukey’s pairwise tests to compare the mean values of our measures and demographics across the experimental and control groups and across day 1 and day 2 of sending out the invitation emails (Table B2 in Appendix B). We found no differences, suggesting equivalence of treatment and control groups.

## Manipulation Check

To check the effectiveness of our HC manipulation, we compared participants’ awareness of prior adopters between the treatment and control groups. The treatment group had a significantly higher awareness of prior adopters than the control group (4.112 versus 3.794; p-value = 0.005). The significant difference also alleviates the concern that participants in the treatment and control groups communicated with each other to “spread” the HC manipulation effect.

## Empirical Model

We used survival analysis to test our hypotheses. This models the time it takes for an event to occur and its relationships with the associated covariates. We used a Cox proportionalhazard model (Cox, 1972), a widely used method for survival analysis. The time to event in the hazard model is evaluated through the hazard rate $h ( t )$ , which is the probability that the event occurs at time t conditional on survival until that time.

The Cox proportional-hazard model is specified as

$$
h (t) = h _ {0} (t) \exp (\beta X)\tag{1}
$$

where X is a vector of covariates, $\beta$ is a row vector of coefficients to be estimated, and $h _ { 0 } ( t )$ is a baseline hazard with X = 0. The model is semi-parametric because the baseline hazard can take any form and the covariates enter the model linearly. The effect of a unit increase in a covariate is multiplicative with respect to the hazard rate. That is, given the other covariates at mean level, for a one-unit increase in the covariate (x ), the hazard ratio for the two observations would be

$$
\frac {h _ {i ^ {\prime}} (t)}{h _ {i} (t)} = \frac {h _ {0} (t) \exp (\beta_ {j} (x _ {j} + 1))}{h _ {0} (t) \exp (\beta_ {j} x _ {j})} = \exp (\beta_ {j})\tag{2}
$$

which is independent of time t. If the coefficient is positive, $\exp ( \beta _ { j } ) > 1$ , the hazard rate increases, suggesting a shorter time of adoption; if negative, it indicates a longer time.

By specifying equation (1), we are able to estimate the main effect (â) and moderation effects $( \gamma _ { s } )$ of HC on time of adoption. The hazard model is specified as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} h _ {i} (t) = h _ {0} (t) \exp \left(\theta_ {1} S N _ {i} + \theta_ {2} P B _ {i} + \theta_ {3} P U _ {i} + \theta_ {4} P E O U _ {i} + \right. \\ \beta_ {1} H C _ {i} + \gamma_ {1} S N _ {i} * H C _ {i} + \gamma_ {2} P B _ {i} * H C _ {i} + \gamma_ {3} P U _ {i} * \\ H C _ {i} + \gamma_ {4} P E O U _ {i} * H C _ {i} + \delta c o n t r o l s _ {i}) \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Survival analysis accounts for data censoring. In our study, the data points were right-censored in cases where students didn’t migrate to Office 365 within one month and their time of adoption was assumed to be longer than the observation period. Ignoring right-censoring underestimates time of adoption. As such, we coded the censoring status of each observation. Partial likelihood was used to consider the censored points and parameters were estimated by maximizing the joint partial likelihood of all occurred events.

## Post Hoc Analyses

We ran a number of post hoc analyses to (a) examine the effects of the HC under low and high values of other forms of social influence and private beliefs (specifically, peer behavior and perceived usefulness since these were the two significant interactions we found); (b) delve more deeply into the mechanisms via which the HC interplays with one’s private beliefs; and (c) examine the effect of the HC on a dichotomous dependent variable capturing the decision to adopt or not (rather than the time of adoption).

## (A) Exploring the Effect of the Herding Cue for Low and High Values of Peer Behavior and Perceived Usefulness

We examined the effects of a HC when peer behavior and perceived usefulness (the two significant HC interactions found) have low or high values to provide more nuance on how the HC interacts with these.<sup>13</sup> To examine this, we replotted the interaction patterns for our significant interactions by switching the main and moderator variables on the plots such that the effects of the HC is evident when PB/PU are at a high level (mean + 1 std) versus at a low level (mean – 1 std) (see Appendix C). As the plots show, when peer behavior is high, the effect of HC on time of adoption is enhanced by peer behavior social influence (simple slope test is positive and significant: slope = 0.307; p-value = 0.002) but is non-significant (slope = -0.010; p-value = 0.231) when PB is low. This suggests that when information from peer behavior does not indicate peer adoption, the HC has no significant effect, but when peer behavior indicates adoption, the HC has an amplifying effect. This provides support to our underlying mechanism of the HC priming observation of peer adoption behavior that may have otherwise gone unnoticed. If peers have not adopted, then there are no relevant peer behaviors to observe, whether or not you are primed to notice them. As such, the HC has no effect on the relationship between peer behavior and adoption in situations with low peer behavior.

The opposite pattern is observed between HC and PU. When PU is low<sup>14</sup>, the slope of the HC on time of adoption is significantly positive (slope = 0.384; p-value = 0.016), while when PU is high, HC does not have a significant effect (slope $= 0 . 0 4 0 ; \mathrm { p } \mathrm { - v a l u e } = 0 . 5 3 3 )$ . This suggests that people tend to disregard their own beliefs and rely on the wisdom of the crowd but only when they don’t have a strong positive belief about the usefulness of the technology.

## (B) Exploring Mechanisms via Which the Herding Cue Interplays with Private Beliefs

The rationale for our hypothesis 3 is based on information cascades, where individuals discount their own private beliefs and instead follow the herd (Bikhchandani et al., 1998; Çelen & Kariv, 2004; Duan et al., 2009; Walden & Browne, 2002). We suggested an alternative explanation where instead of moderating the relationships between private beliefs and adoption (our hypothesis), the HC could provoke observational learning, where individuals reassess and change their private beliefs (Bandura, 1985; Fiol & O’Connor, 2003; Walden & Browne, 2009). In this case, the observed negative moderation may be masking an internalization mechanism, where the HC causes the individuals to increase their perceptions of usefulness or ease of use which, in turn, drive faster adoption, making the relationship between the original set of beliefs and adoption behavior weaker. So to probe the mechanisms via which the HC exerts its influence, we evaluated whether or not the HC changed the individuals’ private beliefs.

We collected measures of PU and PEOU at both T1 and T2, that is, a month before and a month after a HC was introduced for the experiment. If the HC had an effect on private beliefs, then we would expect to see a difference in how beliefs in the treatment and control groups changed from T1 to T2. We assessed this using ANOVA and Tukey’s pairwise tests. The non-significant pairwise comparison results (see Table D1 in Appendix D) suggest that a change in private beliefs is not due to the HC. As an additional analysis, we ran the following regression model to evaluate the impact of HC on the change of private beliefs:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text { DiffPrivateBeliefs } _ {i} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} H C _ {i} + \beta_ {2} S N _ {i} + \\ \beta_ {3} P B _ {i} + \delta c o n t r o l s _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

Ordinary least squares regression with robust standard errors was applied for the estimation and the difference in PU and PEOU between T1 and T2 were treated as the dependent variables. Table D2 in Appendix D shows the results. The coefficients of the HC are not significant for changes in PU (Model 6) or changes in PEOU (Model 7), suggesting that presenting a HC does not cause a reassessment of private beliefs. This is in line with the notion of information cascades in herding theory, which implies herd behavior occurs at the expense of following one’s own private information (Çelen &

Kariv, 2004). Therefore, the impact of our treatment on time of adoption is purely due to the HC, and not due to a change in the participant’s private beliefs.

## (C) Exploring the Effects of Herding Cue on the Decision to Adopt

We also ran a logistic regression to examine the effects of the HC on an individual’s decision to adopt (i.e., whether an individual adopted or not within one month of receiving the invitation email). Results remain qualitatively consistent with the survival analysis (see Table D3, Appendix D). The effect of HC on the adoption is significant (Model 10: p-value = 0.023), indicating that individuals are more likely to adopt in the presence of a HC. Moreover, a HC will enhance the effect of PB (p-value = 0.027), and substitute the effect of perceived usefulness (p-value = 0.03). Interestingly, the effect of SN is positively moderated by the HC (p-value = 0.045). As a robustness check, we also ran a Probit model and the results exhibit a similar pattern.

## Limitations

Like all studies, this paper has limitations. In order to allow time for organic social influences and private beliefs to manifest and for time to adoption to be a meaningful dependent variable, our experimental design has temporal gaps between the measurement of our independent and dependent variables. We conducted a number of post hoc tests and comparisons between treatment and control groups to alleviate such concerns. Further, our field experiment was conducted in a single organization and, while this strengthens internal validity of our findings by controlling for organizational-level factors, generalizability of the findings to other settings needs to be empirically evaluated. Additionally, the current survival model only included covariates measured at the time when the experiment was conducted. Future research could add time-dependent covariates by creating a panel dataset. Researchers can then understand how others’ behaviors change over time and affect an individual’s adoption and use decisions.

Our dependent variable of interest was time to adoption. However, when measuring the independent variables subjective norm, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use, we used the wording in established scales that asked about perceptions around adopting the technology. This nuanced difference—adopting the technology versus adopting the technology sooner—may represent a misalignment between the independent and dependent variables, which can be problematic (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). The fact that our post hoc analysis that examines adoption as the dependent variable provides consistent results, suggests that this is likely not a significant threat to our findings. Nevertheless, we recognize this as a potential limitation.

Network externalities may influence adoption of our focal technology (Office 365) and confound our results. However, we believe that the effects are minimal in our experiment. First, Office 365 is compatible with previous versions of Office and alternative software solutions. Therefore, students could email and share documents (e.g., spreadsheets) with their classmates without upgrading. The only function of Office 365 which may trigger network externalities is OneDrive. However, students could continue using free cloud collaborative tools such as Dropbox and Google Docs to share documents. Further, only 23% of participants reported using OneDrive a month after adopting Office 365. Finally, we ran our models with an additional control variable that serves as a proxy for network externalities. This was the average of two items measured at T1: (1) If I migrated to Office365, I would use features that help me collaborate and share documents with others; (2) If I migrated to Office365, I would use features that help me edit and synchronize documents in the cloud. Our results remained substantively the same, further alleviating concerns. However, future work may examine the effect of HCs in contexts where significant network externalities exist and explore possible interactions.

## Discussion

We focus on the effect of a HC on time of adoption, directly or interactively with other constructs known to impact adoption behaviors. Our study makes contributions to technology acceptance models by enhancing our understanding of how herding theories can be leveraged, with respect to social influence constructs, and by examining time of adoption as our dependent variable. We also contribute to the herding literature by disentangling the various forms of herding infor mation signals, suggesting that they have distinct effects and should not be confounded, and defining a HC as a distinct form of a herding information signal.

## Contributions from Integrating Herd Behavior and Technology Adoption Models

Integrating concepts from the technology acceptance and herding literatures into a more holistic understanding of adoption, we sought to better understand how a HC impacts a user’s adoption behavior. Our study compares and contrasts how a HC interacts with other prevalent forms of social influence (namely, peer behavior and subjective norm) and how it interacts with the dominant adoption private beliefs (namely, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use) to hasten adoption. The nature of these two interaction patterns are quite distinct and in opposite directions. The former is a mechanism of amplification, where a HC strengthens the effects of (some) other social influences on time of adoption. The latter is a mechanism of substitution, where a HC dampens the effects of (some) private beliefs. Furthermore, the effect differs across peer behavior and subjective norm, and across the two private beliefs, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. These patterns are insightful of how a HC exerts its influence. With a focus on HCs, our study makes four related contributions on this front.

First, given the lean nature of a HC, one of our objectives was to examine whether a HC has an effect in the presence of richer information signals, namely other forms of social influence and the private beliefs about a technology that form in a field setting. Most HC studies have been conducted through laboratory experiments, where researchers controlled the private and public information known to each participant and eliminated other sources of influence on the decision during the experiment (e.g., Anderson & Holt, 1997; Çelen et al., 2010; Huang & Chen, 2006). Therefore, testing the efficacy of a HC in a real-world context, where other forms of social influence and communications about the benefits of the system abound, and private beliefs and other forms of social influence are accounted for, provides a strong empirical test of the impact of a HC on adoption. To this end, our findings from a randomized field experiment provide causal evidence that exposure to a HC positively impacts a user’s adoption of a volitional technology and causes them to adopt more quickly, above and beyond the effects of other forms of social influence and private beliefs.

Second, we examined how the HC interacts with other forms of social influence, a relationship on which the literature is silent. In line with our theorizing, our results suggest that a HC amplifies the effect of peer behavior, presumably by focusing attention of the individual on these other signals (i.e., priming) that may have been otherwise overlooked. Indeed, our split group analysis shows that peer behavior has no effect on time of adoption in the absence of a HC, but that the presence of a HC makes this peer behavior salient and influential on time of adoption. Providing further support for a priming effect, our post hoc analysis of the interaction shows that the HC has a positive effect when peer adoption behavior is high and has no effect when peer adoption behavior is low. This indicates that when peer adoption behaviors are present, a HC amplifies their effect, leading to faster adoption. Contrary to our hypothesis, the HC did not increase the effect of subjective norm on time of adoption. Subjective norms and herd behavior cues are inherently distinct forms of social influence (subjective norm is a form of normative influence and a HC is an informative influence) that have different sources, messages, and motivations (Sun, 2013). The interaction with peer behavior, but not subjective norm, on time to adopt may be because peer behavior and HCs are both information signals, while subjective norm is a normative signal. Future research should explore whether social influence forms only amplify each other when they are of similar nature (both informative or both normative). Distinct categories of social influence may be additive instead of multiplicative. However, our post hoc analysis found a significant interaction effect between a HC and subjective norm on the decision to adopt. So, although the two may not interact to generate an amplified sense of urgency, as our bombardment effect theorizing suggested, a user may feel amplified social influence to adopt (just not necessarily more quickly), which raises additional questions for future research.

Third, we hypothesized that a HC will dampen the effect of private beliefs, consistent with prior work on information cascades, where people may discount their own information in order to follow the herd (Anderson & Holt, 1997; Bikhchandani et al., 1992). However, our findings provide a more nuanced understanding of how HC interacts with private beliefs. We found support for our hypothesis (H3a) that a HC will negatively moderate the impact of perceived usefulness. Our split group analysis shows that perceived usefulness has no significant effect on adoption in the presence of a HC, but has a significant effect in its absence. Furthermore, our post hoc analyses show that the HC has no significant effect in the presence of strong positive perceived usefulness beliefs (when PU is high) but has a significant effect when perceived usefulness beliefs are lower. The pattern of results is the opposite to the pattern we found for peer behavior, further highlighting the distinct mechanisms via which the HC interacts with other forms of social influences versus private beliefs.

While we found significant interaction effects of the HC with perceived usefulness, we did not find any support for an interaction with perceived ease of use (H3b). This may be due to the fact that HCs provide social proof (Cialdini & Goldstein, 2004) that indicates other users found value in the technology. This is more likely to assuage concerns about a technology’s usefulness than its ease of use. This would suggest that the private beliefs that are diminished are the ones that can be inferred by the HC. Of course, another possibility, is that Office 365 is not viewed as a difficult to use technology by the respondents and, as such, there is little uncertainty in ease of use that can be alleviated by the HC signal.<sup>15</sup> These alternative explanations provide avenues for future research about which types of private beliefs may be more or less affected by a HC.

Fourth, an important secondary contribution of our study to the IS literature on herd behavior is examining the underlying mechanism of why private beliefs have a diminished effect on time of adoption in the presence of a HC. A common mechanism discussed in the literature for this effect is information cascades, where individuals discount or ignore their own private beliefs and instead follow the herd (Bikhchandani et al., 1998; Çelen & Kariv, 2004). However, the HC could provoke observational learning, where individuals instead reassess and change their private beliefs (Bandura, 1985; Fiol & O’Connor, 2003). Our post hoc analysis suggests that the participants did not change their private beliefs of usefulness and ease of use because of exposure to the HC. This finding may suggest that adoption spurred by a HC is less “sticky,” in that people may be more likely to abandon a volitional technology, since their beliefs about the technology did not change (Rao et al., 2001). Sun (2013), suggests that it may be important to consider factors that may offset the influence of the low information signal of a herd, such as switching and sunk costs (Farrell & Klemperer, 2007), and network externalities (Li, 2004). We suggest that the priming effect that the HC has on noticing and observing peer behavior may be such a factor, as it helps provide richer information. Future research should examine the interaction of the HC with other forms of social influence on abandonment and sustained use.

## Importance of Time to Adoption and the Herding Cue as a Catalyst

Our study underscores the importance of time to adoption over and beyond a dichotomous view of adopting. This is important for two reasons. First, herding studies have called for research that moves beyond a dichotomous adopt/not adopt dependent variable to better understand the influences of herding signals on behavior (Bikhchandani et al., 1998; Çelen & Kariv, 2004). Second, from an innovation diffusion perspective, most social influence sources will be fueled by others “jumping in” on the decision to adopt (Rogers, 1983). As is evident by phrases such as “going viral” and the quick spin-up of user excitement over products, successful adoption is increasingly dependent on a critical mass of users adopting the technology quickly (Bullock, 2018). Focusing on speed of user adoption of a technology, our study shows that a HC has a catalytic effect and causes users to adopt a new technology sooner (those exposed to a HC are in fact 36.2% more likely to adopt early). It may be that many users would have eventually adopted the software, but understanding how to speed their adoption may create a virtuous cycle of adoption. Our evidence that HCs act as an accelerator to adoption make them an especially powerful tool in technology situations where momentum is especially important.

## Disentangling Information Signals: Disambiguating the Definition of a Herding Cue

While many studies implicitly create some type of information signal to evoke herd behavior (e.g., Sun, 2013; Vedadi & Warkentin, 2020) or identify a likely signal in the environment (e.g., Cheung et al., 2014; Duan et al., 2009) the term HC is rarely used, and herding information signals are rarely theoretically defined. Herding information signals can vary from a lean aggregate number, summarizing others’ behavior, to the ability to observe peers. Each of these, as we have discussed and empirically illustrated, suggests different social influence mechanisms. Clear definitions of the various forms of herding information signals can avoid their confounding in an empirical setting and can enhance accretion of knowledge by disentangling their distinct effects. For example, by including the ability to observe peer behavior in Cheung et al. (2014), it is unclear if the social influence triggered is exclusively due to the HC which is the signal operationalized in the analysis, due to peer behavior, or due to their interaction. Toward this end, our study offers a clear definition of a herding cue: it is an information signal designed to convey the number of other people that are engaged in a specific behavior. We hope that this definition contributes to the herd behavior literature, disambiguates it from other forms of herding information signals, and becomes a useful concept for future studies to leverage.

## Practical Implications

Our focus on a HC has practical implications for organizations, especially in volitional adoption situations. Our context of students being offered recommended, but optional, software by a university is not unlike organizations that make software available (but not required) to employees or their customers (e.g., a bank offering its online banking app to its customers). Those interested in improving adoption rates can make a HC visible, which our results suggest will directly encourage and speed adoption, direct attention and strengthen the effect of peer behavior influence, and dampen the need to have positive private beliefs in order to adopt. Further, the lean and inexpensive nature of HCs make the lever an attractive one to organizations. Technology has made calculating aggregations of users seamless, and there are ample ways to automatically share these numbers with potential customers or users.

## Future Research on Herding Cues in Technology Adoption

## Herding Cue Attributes

The main focus of our research is the use of HCs as implementation interventions and understanding how this catalyzes time to adoption. Our field study utilized one type of HC that was released at one point in time during the users’ decision making process. Given its promise, examining the design of the HC in a systematic manner, such as its structure, framing, presentation, and timing, is a promising area for future research of particular relevance to IS research. While not an exhaustive list, we see three research avenues related to HC design that could be fruitful: what to present, how to present, and when to present.

First, in terms of what to present in a HC, the HC in our study conveyed the number of students engaged in a specific behavior. This suggests both the number of adopters as well as the fact that these adopters are students. Similarly, Sun (2013) included two forms of HC messages in his experiment: the number of others, or both numbers and group membership (e.g., members of the Fortune 500). These may represent two distinct dimensions of a potential HC: (1) number of people engaged in a specific behavior and (2) identity of some or all of these people. These may impact technology acceptance differently and may interact with each other. For example, knowing the number of student adopters in our study may have served as a stronger information signal than knowing the number of adopters in general, since student adopters are similar to the individual adopter and, therefore, their behavior more likely to be relevant information.<sup>16</sup> Social influence theories (e.g., Latané’s (1996) social impact theory), theories of persuasion that focus on the impact of peripheral cues (e.g., cues related to source of a message/behavior) on persuasion (e.g., Petty & Cacioppo, 1986), and social identity theory (Tajfel & Turner, 1979) may prove promising theoretical lenses to suggesting under what conditions others’ identity should be included and what this referent group should be for the HC to be more effective.

Furthermore, identifying effective ways of presenting these signals (i.e., how to present) is in need of further study. For example, herd behavior was manipulated by including statements designed to indicate a software was increasing in popularity in various ways and providing adoption ratios (e.g., one out of three internet users are using these tools), instead of offering actual user counts (Vedadi & Warkentin, 2020). Theories of framing (e.g., Goffman, 1974) may be useful theoretical lenses to leverage in theorizing and identifying effective presentations as would theories of cognition. For instance, under what conditions would it be more effective for the HC to present a number versus a percentage or ratio of people engaged in a behavior? The cognitive bias of “denominator neglect” would suggest that presenting the number of people rather than a percentage may be more effective (Reyna & Brainerd, 2008). Relatedly, the fact that we found no significant differences in the effects of the HC included in the invitation on the first day (stating 1,095 people had already adopted it) versus the second day of the experiment (stating 1,234 had already adopted it), leads to the question of when does the magnitude of the numbers matter in the HC? Do people cognitively group all numbers over a certain size as “big” or is it an estimate of the percentage of people in the population? Are some HC numbers so small the cue is not interpreted as herding, but a signal of poor adoption rates by others?

Finally, the timing of a HC in an individual’s decision process (i.e., when) may trigger different results. Our participants formed their own perceptions of subjective norm and private belief over several weeks prior to the introduction of the HC. Would an earlier introduction of the HC act as an influential antecedent to the development of these perceptions? A promising direction for future research is to examine the effect (and effectiveness) of the HC at different points in the adoption process and examine its effects not only on adoption behaviors but also its impact, at different points in time, on other related antecedents of adoption such as subjective norm and private beliefs. This would extend of work looking at the antecedents of perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (e.g., Karahanna & Straub, 1999), as well as further untangling how different forms of social influence may interact with one another.

## Delving Deeper into Herding Cue Conflicts and Uncertainty

Our study did not observe many people with strongly formed negative beliefs. As is common in many adoption studies of utilitarian software (e.g., King & He, 2006; Schepers & Wetzels, 2007), our respondents had mostly neutral to positive perceptions about usefulness (mean = 4.9, SD = 1.0) and ease of use (mean = 5.0, SD = 1.0), and neutral to adoptionsupportive subjective norm (mean = 4.3, SD = 0.9). However, other settings (e.g., hedonic software, difficult or complex enterprise systems) may evoke stronger and more varied private beliefs, observations of negative peer behaviors (e.g., frustration or abandonment of the technology), or normative pressures toward not adopting. A HC in such contexts would be in conflict with these other antecedents. Our data did not allow us to examine such situations. Future research would benefit from exploring cases of tension between HCs and negative private beliefs and social influences.

Further, people are more receptive to social influence when they are uncertain about what they should do (Cialdini & Goldstein, 2004), and this includes receptiveness to following the herd (Sun, 2013). This may mean that HCs presented after a person is more or less certain about their decision may have different effects. Or, a HC may cast doubt on a decision not to adopt, increasing their uncertainty and making the person receptive to a new decision-making process. An interesting future direction would be to investigate HCs across different levels of uncertainty in user’s private beliefs in order to better understand the full range of HC effects.
