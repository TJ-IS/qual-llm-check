# Social power and information technology implementation: a contentious framing lens

Bijan Azad\* & Samer Faraj<sup>†</sup>

\*Olayan School of Business, American University of Beirut, Bliss Street, 11-0236, Beirut, Lebanon, email: ba20@aub.edu.lb, and <sup>†</sup>Desautels Faculty of Management, McGil University, Montreal, QC, Canada H3A 1G5, email: samer.faraj@mcgill.ca

Abstract. Research on the organizational implementation of information technol ogy (IT) and social power has favoured explanations based on issues of resource power and process power at the expense of matters of meaning power. As a result, although the existence and importance of meaning power is acknowledged, its distinctive practices and enacted outcomes remain relatively under-theorized and under-explored by IT researchers. This paper focused on unpacking the practices and outcomes associated with the exercise of meaning power within the IT implementation process. Our aim was to analyze the practices employed to construct meaning and enact a collective ‘definition of the situation’. We focused on framing and utilizing the signature matrix technique to represent and analyze the exercise of meaning power in practice. The paper developed and illustrated this conceptual framework using a case study of a conflictual IT implementation in a challenging public sector environment. We concluded by pointing out the situated nature of meaning power practices and the enacted outcomes. Our research extends the literature on IT and social power by offering an analytical framework distinctly suited to the analysis and deeper understanding of the meaning power properties.

Keywords: social power, IT implementation, meaning power, meaning construc tion, framing, signature matrix

## INTRODUCTION

Social power and organizational politics are issues of concern during the information technology (IT) implementation process, a fact that has long been known to IT and organizational researchers (Pettigrew, 1973; Markus, 1983). Recently, a steady stream of innovative studies has generated more systematic theorizing on the subject of social power and IT (e.g. Jasperson et al., 2002; Howcroft & Light, 2006; Avgerou & McGrath, 2007). IT researchers have shown that actors attempt to inscribe their interests in enacted IT artefacts using organizationa politics and power plays (e.g. Willcocks, 2003; Lin & Silva, 2005), and that power and politics are critically important to meaning construction, which is based on the actors’ competing definitions of reality (e.g. Sillince & Mouakket, 1997). However, judging by a review of socia power/IT research (Jasperson et al., 2002), less than one-fifth of IT papers actually incorporate such a perspective.

We begin with the prevalent theoretical understanding among IT researchers that socia power is implicated in the construction of meaning, and these constructed meanings in turn mediate how actors interact with the process of IT design and development (Bradshaw-Cambal & Murray, 1991; Sillince & Mouakket, 1997). We refer to this mode of social power as meaning power and posit that it is often exercised during organizational implementation of IT projects. Meaning power, along with resource and process power, has been found to be important in technology innovation research (e.g. Dougherty & Hardy, 1996; Swan & Scarborough, 2005).

Although the prior research has established the importance of meaning power during the organizational implementation of IT (Jasperson et al., 2002), the relationship between social power and meaning construction is still unsettled. That is, the exercise of meaning power, and the associated practices as well as the enacted outcomes, remain black-boxed and relatively under-theorized. This paper focuses on the latter, making it our focal research question, i.e. what is the relationship between social power and meaning construction within the confines of IT project implementation process?

To analyze the exercise of meaning power and the enacted outcomes, we use a case study of the implementation of a large scale public sector multi-component IT project (costing over \$50 million and lasting over 11 years) in a challenging institutional environment in a Mediterranean country. We examine the politically contentious process of implementation within this IT project. The project in our case study is officially considered ‘successful’ (The World Bank, 2003; United Nations Development Programme, 2005), but during implementation it wen through periods of significant political and social contention entailing visible exercise of meaning power. We view the case as uniquely revealing for resource and process power, the customarily available sources of power, were curtailed in that public setting, thus raising the importance of meaning power.

The contributions of this paper are twofold. First, it de-black-boxes the relationship between social power and meaning construction as exemplified within an IT implementation projec trajectory. Second, we demonstrate the usefulness of frames and contentious framing to the study of meaning power. These findings and insights on meaning power enrich the nexus of IT and social/organizational power research.

## THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS

## Nascent understanding of meaning power within IT research

A leading perspective to studying the role of power in technology innovation has been the multi-modal approach to social power that recognizes different sources and workings of socia power (Dougherty & Hardy, 1996; Hardy, 1996). First, the resource power mode refers to the control of scarce resources that other parties depend on, a fact that increases the power of the controlling actor. Second, the process power mode refers to the control of decision-making and its associated rules and procedures. Third, the meaning power mode refers to the control of shared meaning among a group of social actors by another group of actors. Consistent with this threefold approach, IT researchers have traditionally highlighted the importance of the exercise of hierarchical authority (process power) as well as the control over available resources (resource power) for bringing about IT-based organizational change (Jasperson et al., 2002). However, with a few recent exceptions (e.g. Silva & Backhouse, 2003; Howcroft & Light, 2006; Avgerou & McGrath, 2007), IT researchers examining social power have been less concerned with the inner workings of the meaning mode.

For example, Silva & Backhouse (2003) have used the ‘circuits of power’ framework to analyze the development of a national health information system. Their analysis offers insights into the practices of causal, dispositional and facilitative power. Howcroft & Light (2006) provided a detailed application of Hardy’s (1985) notion of unobtrusive power, focusing on four aspects that are key in the selection of software packages: technical, structural, conceptua and symbolic. Avgerou & McGrath (2007), in turn, offer a novel approach to the question of power, applying the Foucauldian notions of the rationality of power and the ethics of self-care to their analysis of the workings of power within the Greek national social security organization. The notions of power and meaning are present in all these works to varying degrees, though the distinctive relationship between social power and meaning construction is not the foca point of analysis.

## Black-boxed relation between social power & meaning construction

IT researchers offer a variety of definitions of meaning power, but generally describe it as the ability to ‘direct the construction of organizational realities’ (Jasperson et al., 2002, p. 407). Therefore, those who exercise meaning power may be expected to ‘exert influence by constructing the meaning of what others experience’ in systems design and development activities (Bradshaw-Camball & Murray, 1991, p. 382). However, the theoretical relationship between social power and meaning construction has remained relatively opaque: what contributes to its exercise? How it is practiced and what are the outcomes? This may be partially because of the fact that, the IT researchers who do focus on meaning power often presume that it is devolved from the other more tangible modes of power; in other words, resource and process power are implicitly conceived to enable meaning power (e.g. Lin & Silva, 2005). More generally, the IT literature, though recognizing the different dimensions of power (resource, process and meaning), has often viewed implementation as a mobilization effort that subsumes meaning power within the other two dimensions (Markus & Bjørn-Andersen, 1987).

The likely underpinning for this conventional wisdom among organizational and IT research ers is eloquently articulated by Pfeffer (1981), who states that meaning-based ‘analyses of power fail to answer . . . how . . . competing claims become resolved’ (p. 184). The theoretical grounding for this formulation may be traced back to Berger & Luckmann (1966). that meaning power is nothing less than the ‘power to produce reality . . . [by] those who occupy the decisive power positions . . . [and] use their power to impose the traditional definitions of reality.’ (pp. 110–112) Pfeffer’s, as well as Berger and Luckmann’s claims may be accurate. Nevertheless, there is need to study up close the practices and enacted outcomes associated with the exercise of meaning power – vis-à-vis complex IT project implementation – so as to help clarify distinctive relations of social power and meaning construction theoretically.

## Meaning power: a collective ‘Definition of the Situation’ and contentious framing

In line with our interpretive and symbolic focus, we build on Bourdieu and Passeron’s (1970 1990, p. 4) definition of meaning power as ‘power which manages to impose meanings. Meaning power has been found to be important in innovation research as perceptions regarding the direction of the organization and the legitimacy of demands make change acceptable despite ambiguity about the nature of the change (Dougherty & Hardy, 1996). Within an IT implementation context, we augment this definition of meaning power as follows: the meaning constructed by organizational actors in order to tilt in their favour IT design and configuration choices during implementation. Our focus is on the enactment of a specific set of interpretations and symbols that aims to construct collective definitions of the situation among a group of stakeholders (Berger & Luckmann, 1966). The ‘definition of the situation’ is the core concept in the constructivist theoretical enterprise (Berger & Luckmann, 1966, p. 181) which was originally formulated by W. I. Thomas and D.S. Thomas (1928/1970) as ‘If men [sic] define situations as real, they are real in their consequences’ (p. 154).

Such a formulation sheds light on the struggles that take place when a newly introduced system changes the work processes and potentially redraws the interests and political map of an organization (e.g. Sillince & Mouakket, 1997). That is, competing claims to socially shared meaning are made by different groups who profess to conflicting preferences for system configurations vis-à-vis the intended IT design and development process. These claims often entail struggles over meaning among the users and designers both of whom aspire to have their definition of the situation (e.g. features or workflow choices) to be the collectively enacted meaning inscribed in the working system. The exercise of meaning power is often contentious as it involves changes to initial interpretations of system designs and functionalities leading to the evolutionary enactment of a collective definition of the situation in the working IT system.

Within the above formulation, we posit that meaning power during an IT implementation is simultaneously an outcome and a process. First, meaning power is an outcome because socia actors have different stakes in specific meanings of design configurations and attempt to inscribe their self-serving or preferred ones. Second, meaning power is also a process characterized by evolving struggles as actors try to influence the construction of evolving meanings regarding ‘what the system is about’ among various stakeholders. In such conflictua settings, meaning power around a preferred IT design and configuration is likely to be exercised via: ‘framing contests, in which opposing actors, each seeking to achieve its goals, struggle against one another to frame and reframe the meanings of relevant issues’ (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2006, p. 869). Within this formulation, our proposed notions of frames and framing are consistent with Goffman’s (1974) views regarding the organization of socia experience and its construction by social actors:

The definitions of a situation are built up . . . and our subjective involvement in them; frame is the word I use to refer to . . . these basic elements . . . That is my definition of frame. My phrase ‘frame analysis’ is a slogan to refer to the examination [of this phenomena]. (pp. 10–11, italics added)

A frame can refer to relatively stable interpretive schemes through which actors makes sense of events and situations they come across. Such frames refer to schemata that are said to be applicable to a range of phenomena that are socially recognizable. Framing, on the othe hand, is an activity that involves the virtual drawing of a boundary, much like a picture frame, emphasizing what is inside vs. outside and thereby making the former more salient. Furthermore, framing is effectively an invitation by the framing actor to the audience to ‘read’ or ‘interpret’ the targeted discourse about IT configuration in a particular and specified way which gives it a more strategic character than frames. Although analytically, the distinction between frames and framing is important, they tend to reinforce each other through social interactions among actors and are mutually constitutive (Benford & Snow, 2000).

Benford & Snow (2000) introduce the notion of contentious framing to denote the active process by which interpretive frames are generated or modified to construct a collective definition of the situation. Contentious framing is performative in the sense that it is formed in practice via social interactions among actors. It is processual in the sense of taking place over time via a recognizable sequence. Building on their formulation, we posit that for IT implementation, this sequence can be expected to traverse a path along the following steps: formation of opposing frames, contention among opposing frames and enactment of a frame. In the frame formation phase, the social actors, through interaction with the process of IT implementation, begin noting issues of concern to them as they relate to the design and development of the system. In other words, this prompts social actors into recognizing the potential for meaning construction in the first place, i.e. begin exercising meaning power. In the frame contention phase, these frames become manifested in conflictual terms. In the final phase, an enacted frame often emerges via the framing contest albeit as a highly situated collective definition of the situation.

There is a family resemblance between the above notion of frames and framing and those prevalent within extant IT research (e.g. Davidson, 2002; Faraj et al., 2004). One stream of IT Research has principally conceived of frames as socio-cognitive schema of the underlying artefact (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994). Accordingly, the existence and surfacing of differing frames plays a significant role in how stakeholders perceive and react to an IT implementation process (e.g. Davidson, 2002). Another stream of IT research has emphasized the stabilization aspects of the technology frames (e.g. Faraj et al., 2004; Lin & Silva, 2005) consistent with social construction of technology formulation (Bijker, 1997). The latter perspective focuses on the emergence of a stable technological configuration over time largely based on the interaction among relevant social groups who flexibly interpret the features of the focal technology. Both of these conceptualizations of frames tend to be relatively static and focused on dimension.

We refer to this relation as meaning power. That is, the agencydriven practices to construct meaning so as to influence the contents of preferred IT design configurations via the enactment of a collective definition of the situation among a group of actors in reference to system design and development. Furthermore, these meaning power practices can be represented as a contentious process of framing involving frame formation, frame contention and frame enactment. ## Capturing meaning power and framing via the signature matrix

Building on the work of communications researchers, we posit that contentious framing involves ‘selection, emphasis, and exclusion’ (Gitlin, 1980, p. 7), which highlights ‘some aspects of a perceived reality and make them more salient’ in a communication text (Entman, 1993, p. 52). These framing constructions can be embedded in discursive devices such as metaphors, exemplars, catchphrases, depictions, visual images, roots, consequences and appeals to principle (Gamson & Lasch, 1983). Going further, frame analysis researchers who use frames as an explanatory tool share the underlying assumption that frame properties can be determined by grasping and analyzing the ‘text’ of an underlying frame (Gamson & Lasch, 1983; Benford & Snow, 2000). Text is broadly conceived here as a collection of idea elements that may include ‘discourses, patterned behavior, and systems of meaning, policy logics, constitutional principles, and deep cultural narratives’ (Creed et al., 2002b). A piece of text can serve as a basis for studying the exercise of meaning power via frame analysis if it consists of ‘packages of idea elements held together by some unifying central concept, called a frame (Gamson & Lasch, 1983).

In practical terms, in order to capture the essence of a frame, a signature matrix can be used (Gamson & Lasch, 1983) to construct these elements. A signature matrix is an analysis tool to interpret and classify the constituent elements of frames (Gamson & Modigliani, 1989). It offers an effective approach to categorize and sort core idea components (e.g. metaphors, images, exemplars and appeals to reason or emotion) and shows how these components are inte grated and deployed in support of a unified frame (Creed et al., 2002a,b). Until recently, the use of framing was limited to a few social movement researchers (see for example Benford & Snow, 2000). The pioneering work of Creed and colleagues (2002a,b) provides a methodological boost through a straightforward adaptation of Gamson and colleagues’ (Gamson & Lasch, 1983; Gamson & Modigliani, 1989) signature matrix for organizational research on framing. To our knowledge, our application of signature matrix in analyzing the contentious framing process underpinning the exercise of meaning power is among the first within IT research.

Our adaptation of the signature matrix to capture and analyze the contentious framing by opposing sides through the study of specific frames which were employed by actors within the IT implementation process contains six main elements. We use six of Creed et al.’s (2002b) nine frame elements. Our rationale is that we consider their notion of catchphrases to be very similar to and more relevant than metaphors and exemplars; therefore, we decided in favou of the former instead of the latter two. We apply the same reasoning to empirical credibility vs. roots whereby we also opted for the former instead. Thus, our adapted signature matrix though more compact, i.e. 6 vs. 9 elements, is still capable of adequately representing the frames and the framing employed by both sides in the contentious IT implementation process.

First, there are master frames that serve as the basis for derivative frames. These derivative frames share tactical aspects with each other while individually helping the key actors exercise meaning power. Second, there are specific instances of frames, which are usually held to include catchphrases, metaphors, stories, other symbols, etc. Third, there is the problem elaboration component, i.e. what the frame considers to be the key problem. Fourth, there are the frame content elements, i.e. what the frame selects to highlight as salient. Fifth, the empirical credibility of the frame is based on its connection to the key cultural/environmental elements. Finally, a framing effort is effective if it manages meaning and symbols so as to render its frame resonant among the key stakeholders – it becomes to be perceived as a collective definition of the situation.

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

## Case background

Our case involves a land register and map records computerization project called the Cadastre Operations Modernization and Automation Project (hereinafter referred to as the Project), which took place under the aegis of the General Directorate of Land Registration and Survey (hereinafter referred to as the Land Registry). A two-component \$50 million project (focusing on the computerization of both real property registers and maps) was initiated in early 1990s through a loan agreement between the World Bank and the national government. After two decades of low investment, the government’s land registration and mapping apparatus was in need of radical transformation, as most of the register and map records were in paper forma and work procedures were outmoded.

According to the World Bank estimates, the volume of real estate transactions in the country at that time was \$6 billion per annum (The World Bank, 1994). The fees paid to the government were approximately \$100 million per annum, which constituted close to one-tenth of the public finances, making them an essential source of revenue. However, the World Bank study also found that citizens and businesses incurred additional informal costs for real estate transactions equal to the formal government fees (informally referred to as ‘\$100 million annual pie’), because of bribes, delays, productivity losses and lost revenue. Therefore, both the nationa government and the World Bank regarded this sector as a priority for reform to reduce corruption, raise operational efficiency and enhance revenues. Thus, a loan package was designed that included components to address each of these problem areas, with specia emphasis on the use of IT to redesign and improve administrative procedures.

The project protocol specified three goals: improvements in service to citizens and businesses; reform of the administrative organization and internal operations; and building the technological capability of the agency (The World Bank, 1994). The planning and implementation of the project spanned more than a decade – 1994–2005. The main objective was to improve the business processes and operations of the Land Registry. The technical scope of this project included: (1) automating the issuing of title register abstracts and real estate transfers (which are key Land Registry processes) within an Integrated Registry System (hereinafter referred to as the Registry System); (2) computerizing the issuing of property map abstracts and land parcel subdivision transactions via an Integrated Surveying System (hereinafter referred to as the Survey System); and (3) computerization of the existing stock of 2.4 million title registers and 7000 parcel map sheets. According to a recent independent evaluation by the World Bank, ‘Overall work on this component has progressed well and has obtained excellent results’ (The World Bank, 2003, p. 4). A similarly favourable evaluation was provided by a United Nations watchdog agency (United Nations Development Programme, 2005).

At a high level, the project aimed to take an underlying existing stock of paper real property title registers and map sheets and convert them to a digital format and thus enable electronic transactions. Our research effort traces the disputes between users and designers centred around two very different perspectives on what IT should achieve within the focal project: office automation of existing work patterns versus systematic change of work and workflows. The designers attempted to impose their perspective which entailed a high degree of process automation and electronic data supporting them – we refer to this perspective as ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’. On the other hand, the users generally opted for a minimalist approach using IT to automate and support their existing mode of operation – we refer to this perspective as ‘Using PCs to Support Existing Work Patterns’.

The case selected for this study provides a rich setting for investigating organizational social power dynamics during the IT implementation process. The project involved the use of IT to change work processes. The implementation process generated significant disagreement, leading to continuous efforts to influence outcomes and decisions through the exercise of social power. This process arose for several reasons. First, the project attempted to rearrange procedures to reduce administrative waste and corruption, which had the effect of potentially reallocating the \$100 million annual pie. Second, the project organizational structure generated disputes. On the one hand, planning and implementation resources were largely under the control of a reform-minded team that was brought into the Land Registry but did not report to its director, consistent with the World Bank policy on reform project designs. On the other hand, decision-making authority with regard to work practices and associated rules still resided in the Land Registry – the very target of the proposed reforms. This situation resulted in a relatively equal balance of power between the Project Team and the Land Registry with regard to resource and process power in thei favour, respectively.

Together, these factors created a situation in which the Project Team could not impose its ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’ designs, while the Land Registry was unable to do away with the reform orientation of the project. As a contextual factor, this relative parity of resource and process power increased the likelihood not only that the competing groups would resort to meaning power in order to resolve the situation in their favour but also that these efforts would be more visible to research examination all else being equal.

## Research approach

We think that the increased visibility (to research investigation) of meaning power because of the relative parity of resource and process power makes this a ‘unique or revelatory case’ (Yin, 2003). Studying a unique case is warranted if the phenomenon under study is revelatory and otherwise hitherto inaccessible to researchers. In addition, a revelatory case is a means of theoretical sampling that makes it possible to develop an analytical framework and analytica generalizations (Yin, 2003). Following a similar logic, by studying this revelatory case we seek to bring to light the key aspects of exercising meaning power, namely, underlying practices, enacted outcomes and contextual conditions within an IT project implementation process. We also intend to illuminate the viability of frame analysis as a methodological innovation that can capture the elements of associated frames and contentious framing.

The case study was conducted by the two authors, who formed an ‘insider-outsider research team, as recommended by Evered & Louis (1981). This approach has been successfully employed in IT research (e.g. Boudreau & Robey, 2005). The ‘insider’ researcher in our case joined the project in August 1995 and served until July 1998 as its overall architect. From June 1998 through February 2004, he served as project manager. Because of his direc involvement with the events herein analyzed, this researcher was uniquely sensitive to specific episodes in which meaning power was exercised and could be captured fairly accurately by frame analysis. The ‘outsider’ researcher had no involvement with the project other than through observation, interviews and analysis of archival records.

The involvement of the insider researcher with the project is a concern which may potentially create a familiarity bias vis-à-vis the results. Several safeguards were developed to reduce this potential threat. First, the insider-outsider team can reduce familiarity bias if the outsider researcher serves in a ‘devil’s advocate’ and sense-making role (Eisenhardt, 1989) and has been successfully employed by IT researchers (e.g. Azad & Faraj, 2009). Second, to reduce bias, the outsider researcher served as an active participant in the majority of the interviews. Third, as a control, detailed descriptions of different frames and their elements (instances o meaning power actually being enacted) were prepared and shown to two former project stakeholders, one of whom sympathized with the ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT perspective while the other was critical of them. Their comments were incorporated into the frame summaries to reduce researcher bias.

## Collecting data on framing and frames via signature matrix

Underlying our meaning power analysis are frame data which are composed of two types of ‘texts’ from two different sources: project documents and interviews. Because the actors in the organizational environment meant each of these texts (as a whole or in part) to affect the course and content of the IT project implementation, we can assume that each text potentially contains frames through which the protagonists signal their preferred IT project path. These data allowed us to explore how protagonists and antagonists attempted to manage meanings and symbols via a specific framing of their ‘definition of the situation’ as regards the IT project. These ‘definitions’ further allowed us to discern how the contents of specific frames were designed to alter project-related meanings among stakeholders

The first ‘text’ source included general project documentation, both official and unofficial. This encompassed the project’s initiation, planning and implementation documents. It is worth noting that after the project’s launch, the majority of the documents constituted formal and informal communications between the government and the World Bank, and between the Project Unit, the vendors and the focal organization.

The second ‘text’ source was based on interviews in which key actors within the organizational environment described their perspectives, i.e. project-based frames. The insider-author, assisted by the former national project coordinator and a former Advisor to the Registry, compiled an initial list of 51 contacts. This list included key stakeholders who had some sort of involvement with the project and were thought to have influenced its course, either prior to or during project implementation. Twenty-four of these individuals agreed to participate in the interviews, including 5fiveof 12 regional managers of the focal organization; eight of the 16 key individuals within the organization who had formal ties to the project and were sympathetic to the ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’, including the former staff of the Project Unit; eight of the 20 people with or without formal organizational ties who were unsympathetic to the ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’ and tended to favour ‘Using PCs to Support Existing Work Patterns’ view of the project; two advisors to the General Director, one of whom was sympathetic and one unsympathetic to the ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’; and a former government minister who had been the highest level government representative to the project’s steering group. To reduce familiarity bias, more than 60% of the initial list comprised people who had voiced opinions that were considered to be either unsympathetic or neutral towards the ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’ perspective; however, the final list was almost evenly split.

The interviews were conducted in two phases. The first series of interviews were conducted from December 2005 to June 2006 by the two authors. The second series of interviews were conducted from September 2006 to June 2007 by one of the authors and a research assistant. A total of 33 interviews were conducted with the 24 interviewees; the average session lasted 90 min. The interview data were first used to uncover instances of contentious framing, as well as the associated frame elements that appeared to have been used by the protagonists and antagonists. Then the project reports, memos and meeting minutes were used to corroborate these frame elements. A list of interviewees and specific documents consulted can be found in Appendix A. Each interview session is assigned a reference identification number which is then used to reference the source(s) of quotes in presentation of the findings later in the paper (e.g. Int-ID-20a).

## Protocols of data analysis via the signature matrix

A signature matrix is a method of extracting frames from underlying discourse and textual materials. We developed our ‘signature matrix’ based on the six major elements, focusing on a given ‘text’ and classifying frame-related data into the following categories: master frames, frames, problem definition, frame content, empirical credibility and resonant caption (Gamson & Lasch, 1983; Creed et al., 2002a,b). This enabled us to (1) extract potential master frame catchphrases; (2) sort the idea elements so that the catchphrases that actually signified a frame could be distinguished; (3) identify the core problem definition of the frame, as seen by its proponents; (4) single out the salient part of the frame; and (5) identify the sources of empirical credibility, mainly through the catchphrases themselves.

We extracted frames and the associated information elements from our data in the following manner. First, the ‘insider’ author used the interview data and his own personal recollections, as well as those of the former national project coordinator, to develop a list of catchphrases representing the master and derivative frames. This resulted in the initial identification of a total of two master frames and 15 ‘single’ frames. The master frames were identified as ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’ and ‘Using PCs to Support Existing Work Patterns’. We argue that that these labels accurately capture the essence of contested framing during the implementation process. That is, they have high fidelity to the intent and content of the various frames used by the two sides when they engaged in contested framing within the organizationa environment to influence the direction and content of the IT project. Extracting master frames has often been used in studies where technologies are novel and evolving (e.g. Dowell et al., 2002; Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2006). Our master frame approach is consistent with that proposed by organizational researchers – e.g. Dowell et al. (2002) who studied industry struggles over high-definition TV technology. Hargrave & Van de Ven (2006) have gone further and posited that this approach is highly suitable for analyzing contentious technologica innovations.

Second, we pared down the list of 15 ‘single’ frames, retaining only those framing contests that involved an opposed pair of frames (i.e. each frame was tightly coupled with an antagonist) with a direct relationship to the master frames. This procedure is consistent with Creed et al. (2002a,b) recommendations to lower the risk of bias in frame ‘extraction’. This yielded a tota of 14 frames making up seven pairs of frames and counter-frames. Third, we obtained the opinions of the key influential actors – a former minister, the director-general of the Land Registry agency, two advisors to the director-general, a former manager of the Land Registry and the former national project coordinator. These were narrowed down to four ‘critical’ frames pairs – two of which were resonant frames favouring the ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’ view of the project, while the other two were resonant frames favouring the ‘Using PCs to Support Existing Work Patterns’ view. Additionally, we interviewed four key influentia informants to confirm that the final project outcomes bore the imprint of each side’s framing. The results were used to contrast the outcomes of single framing episodes vs. the overal framing outcome.

We opted to select these four critical frames, i.e. two of the resonant frames employed by each side, for two reasons. First, per Creed et al. (2002a) we attempted to enhance the validity of the conclusions, so we chose and analyzed a ‘balanced’ set of frames. Second, we wanted to streamline the reporting of frame analysis data, the details of which can be tedious and overwhelming. We are aware that this process was highly subjective and there is no guarantee that all potential frames were exhaustively identified. However, using the above-mentioned procedures outlined by Creed et al. (2002b), we have taken steps to increase the credibility of our results. Using our case data, the exercise of meaning power via contested framing and frames are described and analyzed next.

## FINDINGS

## Exercising meaning power during frame formation

Although the IT project has been completed and has formally generated satisfactory results (according to an audit unit of The World Bank, 2003, and United Nations Developmen

Programme, 2005), at several points during implementation the continuation and completion of the project was uncertain. In fact, there were several instances when the project was experiencing major disagreements over its scope and goals, which boiled down to a contention between two master frames. That is, whether the project should be about ‘Using PCs to Support Existing Work Patterns’ that would limit it to providing PCs, printers and office software to the Land Registry, or should instead be about ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT that would automate work processes to improve service to the public. Markus (2004) makes a similar distinction between pure IT projects and those that enable organizational change.

The government and World Bank representatives had originally made the loan agreement contingent on ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’ to the Land Registry’s basic work procedures. At this preliminary stage, the opposition to the project was not vocal. However, soon after the pilot testing (less than 0.2% of all computerized data) phase was completed, vocal opposition to the project’s scope began to be heard. Basically, a group within the Land Registry preferred to use the loan to obtain IT equipment and software for office productivity rather than engage in any kind of computer-based procedural reform and/or systematic document automation. The former head of the Land Registry articulated this view as follows

You see, our people were still working with pen and paper, which dated back not only to the Colonial Mandate time but even back to the 19th century . . . Going from pen to compute in one step is going to be difficult, so we preferred first getting our employees used to PCs and word processing and things like that before performing the transactions on the computer . . . So I told the World Bank people, the Project Unit and the minister that this business of computerizing the work is way too sophisticated for our people . . . they should just give us PCs . . . [Int-ID-24a]

This general framing of the preferred scope of the IT implementation as a ‘Using PCs to Support Existing Work Patterns’ appears at first glance to be linked to the low level of sophistication of the intended users and their difficulties in absorbing the new technology. However, as one senior government official noted, there was more to the sympathy with the ‘Using PCs to Support Existing Work Patterns’ perspective than met the eye:

We have a bureaucracy that has become comfortable during the last two decades with extracting ‘rent’ through the signature power, and now during the reconstruction we hear all kinds of excuses which we cannot afford . . . As soon as the pilot finished we are hearing grumblings on all kinds of excuses to not do reforms! Why we did not hear these complaints before . . . I regard at least some of the complaints as a red herring . . . We have to go ahead with reforms and you cannot but do so hand-in-hand with computerizing our work procedures . . . [Int-ID-3a]

The opposition to the reform-orientation of the project was initially less visible and vocal among the users or managers. Most registry staff regarded the survival of the project with a very low probability. However, as the project went through different phases and it managed to negotiate the procurement, design, and pilot testing phases, the opposition began to emerge. A registrar made the following comment:

Most of us did not think this project would last since there were several attempts before to automate the title registers and maps . . . Registry representatives on the steering committee started feeding back information to us that we need to make sure the systems are properly built to satisfy the work of the registry . . . I guess we did not take it [project] seriously before that . . . [Int-ID-1]

After the pilot testing phase, the opposition to the two Systems (Registry and Survey) became very vocal, and included written letters to the General Director. A highly influential Registry advisor’s explanation for this behaviour is consistent with the fact that as users interacted with the system’s reality (definition of the situation) their opposition began to solidify consistent with their master frame:

After the pilot as well as acceptance testing, I think our employees realized that we will no be able just avoid the system . . . I had heard a lot of murmurs before the system was actually tested, but the loud opposition which surfaced after the registry’s steering committee representatives reported the test results shows that the rank and file were suddenly awakened to the changes in processes of work being targeted by the system . . . [Int-ID-17]

These vignettes indicate that initially, users were somewhat non-vocal in their views towards the project. Nevertheless, the users started expressing their opposition towards system designs and functionalities through interactions with project deliverables. Via these interactions, both the users and the Project Unit came to embrace interpretations of the system designs and functionalities which would increasingly highlight their differing ‘definition of the situation’. The project unit regarded the system design as a matter of fact and hardly questionable. On the other hand, the Registry users took a wait and see if this gets off the ground attitude. However, both groups through interactions with the design and development process and each other came to an awareness of the contentious nature of the situation. We refer to this process as the frame formation among the opposing groups – a situated activity largely based on the practices of design and development process as they evolved in the IT project.

## Exercising meaning power during frame contention

A contentious process of framing was triggered largely because of the opposition between the two perspectives or master frames – whether the IT project should proceed as planned (the ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’ frame) or change its scope or direction (the frame that called for ‘Using PCs to Support Existing Work Patterns’). The contentious framing involved intense debate and organizational conflict that sometimes threatened to stall the project. The contentious framing is largely how the exercise of meaning power was practised in the case study project. To exemplify the flavour of frames and contentious framing, one framing instance is described in detail for each of the master frames, which are selectively highlighted in terms of the following components: catchphrases, frame-based problem definition, frame content (diagnosis and prognosis), empirical credibility and resonance. In addition, the social context within which the process of contested framing took place is also described.

Tables 1 and 2 provide details of dominant frames belonging to each side. To exemplify our employment of the signature matrix technique (for frame analysis) to capture the instances of exercise of meaning power, only one instance of resonant frame for ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’ and one for ‘Using PCs to Support Existing Work Patterns’ are presented in the tables. However, we analyzed additional frames, totalling eight (inclusive of Table 1 contents). Because of space constraint, the detailed analysis of all the frames is not included here but is available from the authors.

## ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’ master frame

Early in the implementation phase of the project, the information systems (IS) contractor produced digital parcel maps of a pilot area to work out the problems associated with digitizing and database development. But the senior Land Registry manager in charge of liaison with the IS contractor rejected these digital maps because of a lack of confidence in their accuracy. The reason given was that the process of digitizing was not supervised by employees of the Land Registry. In addition, concerns were raised that the parcel boundaries on the maps might have been altered inadvertently because of this alleged inaccuracy, which would have ‘dire consequences’ for the property owners.

After a few meetings involving the Project Unit, the paper-to-digital map conversion contractor and the Land Registry members, it became clear that the Land Registry was rejecting the digital maps because it had not been involved in the paper-to-digital conversion process. It was also clear that any arrangement that did not allow the Land Registry to directly supervise the conversion process would be regarded with the same suspicion. For example, a surveyor voiced the following opinion:

Only people with experience know what the lines mean on the maps. Some of these maps are 70 years old, how can anybody who has not worked with them know how to handle them. This is not like typing or word processing. This is technical work . . . We do not want to mess around with people’s property which is sometimes the cause of fights among neighbors . . . [Int-ID-22]

This episode galvanized the Project Unit, the contractor and a few sympathetic members of the Land Registry to work out a response to these concerns. These actors thought that longstanding doubts about ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’ were the true reason for these supposed objections to the quality of the digitizing. For example, a regional Land Registry department head sympathetic to reforms said:

I don’t think it is a practical request for us to be involved in the conversion of these maps . . . the contractor is doing the work [abroad] . . . what are they asking, do they want the contractor to move its work here, or fly us out there and put us in a hotel? . . . because for sure we [the government] do not have the money to raise the contract price for the work to be done here since our labor is more expensive . . . I think these are excuses . . . [Int-ID-5a]

Several efforts were made to convince the Land Registry manager that because these maps were digital versions of current paper maps, no new errors were being introduced. What was already on the maps would simply be reproduced. However, initially this approach did not yield positive results. The Land Registry manager and several other regional department heads appeared to stick to their guns. After going through this process several times and trying out various ideas with sympathetic members of the Land Registry, the Project Unit, with the assistance of the former senior manager of the Land Registry, decided to try a more ‘public approach. Accordingly, the Project Unit wrote a short letter to the director-general of the Land Registry that was distributed to all department heads and employees, restating the initia response to the objectors’ qualms. This letter simply stated that the digital maps were mere electronic ‘photocopies’ of the existing maps, and that a facsimile of a document does not alter its contents – ‘Digital map is a photocopy, hence same quality’.

In the course of several further formal and informal meetings it became clear that this framing of the problem had appealed to the common sense of some of the letter’s recipients. This appeared to work much better than the previous attempts to focus on complex technica aspects of the digital line maps. This framing also implied that photocopying does not require the same sort of skill as map drafting, so that a clerk with knowledge of map digitizing would be qualified to do the job. As far as empirical credibility was concerned, this framing effor appealed to the audience’s general familiarity with photocopying as a mundane office chore that almost everybody is familiar with, which by implication hardly requires the technical skills of professional Land Registry employees such as surveyors and draftsmen. The comments of the former senior manager of the Land Registry show that this framing resonated well with the key stakeholders:

These [digital maps] are developed using scanned images, so are they really all that different from photos or photocopies . . . I mean they are as bad or as good as the maps . . . they even show all the coffee stains . . . Maybe I am exaggerating but all the IS contractor did was remove the coffee stains from the maps . . . [Int-ID-3b]

This comment demonstrates that the ‘photocopy’ framing had struck a chord, helping the audience understand this complex matter. It also had significant empirical credibility that most people could relate to, because everybody knows about office equipment, photocopiers and photocopying. Most importantly, by drawing parallels with photocopying, the frame made the point that when it came to the digitizing process, it would have been overkill to use exper surveyors.

This frame appeared to have produced the desired effect on the audience. After a couple of months of ‘lobbying’ by the Project Unit, a meeting was held between the manager in question, the director-general of the Land Registry and all the regional department heads. During this meeting, the sympathetic regional registry department heads demonstrated the ‘photocopy effect’ to all attendees, and from then on there appeared to be no formal objections to the map digitizing process. Furthermore, at the insistence of the Project Unit the minutes of the meeting stated that the digital maps would be submitted to the Land Registry’s regional offices, where they would be checked according to specifications without further delay. As of this writing, the project has been completed, with all maps digitized and checked by the regional Land Registry offices. Some errors have been found; however, the majority of these were already in the paper maps. Majority of the errors found on the digital maps were based on errors on the paper maps. The digital maps, however, did contain errors (often within the 95% tolerance specified in the contrast) which were largely because of problems because of scanning distortions and edgematching of map sheets digitally to form seamless geographical boundary districts. These errors were corrected by the contractor in consultation with the Survey Department Surveyors.

So, although the Land Registry framed the problem as one of the questionable quality of the digitized maps, this may never have been the real issue. This contentious framing episode lasted over 3 months. The enacted outcome appeared to have resulted in a collective definition of the situation largely in keeping with the ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’ perspective. There were three other episodes which were analyzed (not reported here because of space constraint) where targeted definition of the situation appeared to share greater affinity with ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’ master frame.

## ‘Using PCs to Support Existing Work Patterns’ master frame

During the first site implementation, a disagreement broke out over the method of quality control for the digital data. The plan had been for the Project Unit to perform quality control fo all the digital data for each site before electronic information products (e.g. electronic title abstracts and map abstracts) were offered to the public. This plan was followed for the first Land Registry regional department until approximately 30% of the checking was finished. At that point, the Land Registry head and one advisor to the director-general objected that the quality control process was taking so long that it was jeopardizing the entire project’s timelines.

Underlying this objection was an unspoken wish to take over the quality control process, because in the short term it represented a large pool of discretionary funds to hire temporary employees and curry favour with potential permanent Land Registry employees. The govern ment had allocated 5% of the project costs (or approximately \$2.5 million) for this activity, which represented a major source of patronage, because at any given time, approximately 50–60 temporary clerks were supposed to be employed at each site to assist with quality control. Initially, control of this task was left to the jurisdiction of the Project Unit. The objectors understood the benefits of being able to control the hiring and firing process. The national project coordinator spoke to this issue directly:

I guess we should have been happy to see the registry so eager to take over the IT project personnel administration. We knew the real reason behind the registry’s push to take over so early; it was to put in place IT support people who would be loyal to the registry, so that in case of any potentially damaging discovery of past ‘problems’ [e.g. corruption] they will be fixed before there is any negative fallout for the registry. [Int-ID-20a]

Nevertheless, this issue was presented in terms of its impact on the speed of implementa tion, that is, the Project Unit was criticized as being too stringent in its requirements. The objectors framed the issue in terms of the excessive amount of time stringent quality control was taking. After a few months of debate over the pros and cons of various quality control methods, it was decided to put the quality control team under the supervision of the Land Registry because ‘they could get it done quickly.’ For empirical credibility it was argued that the Project Unit was a highly specialized team that was too perfectionist regarding the digital data. This perfectionism was wrong, it was said, because all that was needed was a quick check, because the Land Registry employees would have to check the records anyway, whenever new transactions took place in the future. This framing of the issue resonated well with the World Bank and the government (especially the minister overseeing the land registry). Subsequently, the project steering group transferred the hiring and firing decisions for the quality control team, as well as day-to-day operational management, to the Land Registry. At the same time, it left the Project Unit in charge of technical quality control standards. The minister’s reflections on this decision confirm the resonance of this framing approach:

We knew at the time that the high-precision quality check of all the digital records was probably better because we would get 100% accuracy. But sometimes timely results are more important, even if they are not 100% right . . . the rest can be done by the employees later . . . we don’t necessarily have to wait for this . . . [Int-ID-19a]

It is significant that following the transfer of responsibility for the quality control team to the Land Registry, the public discovered several instances of errors in title abstracts. This seems to confirm the substantive arguments of the Project Unit. Nevertheless, the minister’s statement indicates that the ‘project delay/timeliness’ framing of the issue had resonated with the World Bank and the government to such a degree that they were willing to tolerate the occasional minor ‘scandal’ because of the errors the Land Registry was making in exchange for getting the job done quickly (and perhaps they also harboured an unspoken desire for the public ‘ribbon cutting’ that went along with completion). This shows that the ‘quality’ framing the Project Unit had adopted has a lower resonance, and highlights that contentious framing can be largely strategic in intent rather than purely based on cognitive schemata of social actors.

This example illustrates two additional characteristics of contentious framing process. First, it indicates that the content of the process of contested framing evolves and changes over time relative to the associated master frame. Second, it shows that contentious framing is at times a strategic endeavour and is less of a stable interpretive schemata a la socio-cognitive frames. That is, the initial framing instances were characterized by opposition to the ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’ perspective. However, the later framing efforts clearly had a strategic flavour of cooptation to them. This point is underscored by a former government official’s comment:

Look, the government had a hiring freeze, and since the project was almost finished and there was no way to stop it, the administration, I think, was trying to make the best of it . . . by taking control of quality control and the computer support team, which were covered through discretionary funds. They would kill 2 birds with one stone . . . they would curry favor, play patronage, and start controlling who is promoted to become part of the IT support team . . . They might also be able to blame any problems on ‘the computer.’ [Int-ID-14]

Although there are discernible differences between this frame and the ‘Using PCs to Suppor Existing Work Patterns’ master frame, these results are consistent with other researchers findings (e.g. the evolution of high-definition TV framing; Dowell et al., 2002). In other words, situated framing that is employed to construct meaning is often strategic and pragmatic depending on how well it can serve the focal group’s purposes and less on its consistency with the master frame or prior framing efforts.

This contentious framing episode lasted over 4 months. The enacted outcome appeared to have resulted in a collective definition of the situation largely in keeping with the Using PCs to Support Existing Work Patterns’ perspective. There were three other episodes which were analyzed (but not discussed here) where collective definition of the situation appeared to share greater affinity with ‘Using PCs to Support Existing Work Patterns’ master frame.

## Exercising meaning power during frame enactment

Here we highlight two key aspects about the exercise of meaning power: (1) that meaning power practices via each instance of contentious framing were highly situated, emergent and unpredictable; and (2) that meaning power practices across these individual instances of contentious framing were highly episodic and transient – difficult to aggregate overall.

First, the contentious framing pitted groups of actors with conflicting views of the IT project scope and direction against each other. The intensity with which the social actors engaged in managing the meanings attached to specific aspects of the IT project was notable. It was almost as if the situations that arose tended to gain momentum of their own. The national project coordinator put it this way:

I am of the school of thought that says ‘no shots fired shall go unanswered,’ because eventually these small volleys grow into something big and their sheer momentum can be unstoppable regardless of how they started . . . [Int-ID-20b]

Furthermore, the situational contingencies in specific framing instances indicate that the stakeholders were almost working on ‘autopilot’ in defending what they perceived as thei personal interests or those of the professional groups they were associated with. A comment by a former registrar supports this situated practice view:

I can say that at least some registrars thought coding was just the beginning, and that it would gradually lead to digital signatures and eliminate the need for legal expertise to ensure that real estate transactions are compliant with the law . . . I am a lawyer by training, so as a registrar I would also automatically think of changes that might come later on. [Int-ID-7b]

This is an oblique discussion of the impact of IT on the registrars’ work practices. The informant describes the way in which they tend to automatically counter any perceived threat to thei professional dominance over the real estate registration process. Ironically, each side probably thought the same. We might observe that once contentious framing is in motion, it is likely to continue because of ongoing momentum. Each side may perceive the definition of the situation evolving to its disadvantage. This gives the activation of meaning power via contentious framing a self-propelling character that may be difficult to control once it is started.

Second, according to our informants, the perception was that the ‘Using PCs to Support Existing Work Patterns’ and the ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’ master frames each ‘prevailed’ in the 2 instances (total of four instances or frame-pairs) which we analyzed. That is, each of these episodes of exercise of meaning power, resulted in a collective definition of the situation that shared more or less a greater affinity with the initial definition of the situation of one side or the other. During data collection, we asked four influential informants (two from each of the opposing frames), to tell us whether they thought their side or the other side managed to impose their views (definition of the situation) on the project as a whole. Interestingly, they could recall the specific instances of contention but they could not unequivocally tell us which side had managed to achieve an overall collective definition of the situation. One Land Registry manager expressed his opinion on this as follows:

At the time when we were attempting to make sure the project was truly supportive of the work procedures of the Land Registry, [now] I am not sure overall can we say we really changed the project direction, scope or design the way we thought . . . [Int-ID-24b]

This view was more or less shared by other informants. In other words, the exercise of meaning power via contentious framing may have resulted in relatively clear cases where one side or the other managed to enact a collective definition of the situation vis-à-vis the other side’s definition – regarding system design, system functionality, work procedure, system configuration, personnel control, etc. However, these individual instances cannot be easily aggregated into an overall notion of constructed meaning partial to the views of one side. Thus, meaning power may be activated and deployed towards the enactment of a collective definition of the situation advantageous to one side. However, this meaning power once activated cannot be easily ‘stored up’ for later use. It is almost as if it disperses with time. Figure 2 illustrates this notion graphically. The horizontal bars depict instances of contentious framing and meaning power construction which resulted in the enactment of a collective definition of the situation to the advantage of one side or the other. However, it is very difficult to aggregate across these horizontal bars to the whole project.

## DISCUSSION

This paper contributes to understanding of the theoretical relation between social power and meaning construction within the IT project implementation process in an organizational envi ronment. We represent this meaning power construct via the contentious process of framing which can result in a collective definition of the situation when the enacted frame is resonant among the stakeholders. Our case study involved an IT project implementation in a challenging environment, characterized by vested interests and corruption. In such a conflictual environment there can be greater propensity to resort to exercise of meaning power to enact a collective definition of the situation favourable to either side’s interests. As such, the exercise of meaning power – which has remained relatively underexplored and under-theorized by IT researchers – is rendered more visible to research scrutiny and thus its study up close can shed light on its practices and enacted outcomes.

Our paper contributes to the extant research by pointing out the importance of meaning power in and of itself in the IT project implementation process. The prevailing view is that often meaning power, which aims to construct the reality of IT design and development among social actors (Jasperson et al., 2002), is enabled via the distribution of existing resource and process power (e.g. Markus, 1983; Markus & Bjørn-Andersen, 1987; Nidumolu et al., 1996). This tight coupling of meaning power with resource and process power privileges the latter in a way that may not work well in certain important IT implementation contexts – e.g. in our case, where there was rough resource and process power parity. Thus, our research expands on the concepts of resource and process power as key determinants of meaning power outcomes that are well-established in IT power research (Bradshaw-Camball & Murray, 1991; Sillince & Mouakket, 1997). Our research shows that through the enactment of meaning power, certain aspects of IT design and development are affected and take on consequential meaning(s) for the stakeholders.

Our findings contribute to the literature by clarifying the relationship between meaning construction and social power. To be sure, the importance and linkage between meaning construction and social power in IT implementation have been noted in the past (e.g. Lin & Silva, 2005). However, the relationship between them has remained theoretically opaque. The prior research often assumes that, in most if not all cases, the existing social power enables the construction of meaning (e.g. Sillince & Mouakket, 1997; Lin & Silva, 2005). For example, Lin & Silva (2005) propose that ‘understandings, interpretations and expectations of information systems are framed and reframed through the exercise of power’ (p. 58). In contrast, our research posits and demonstrates that social power can also be constituted through meaning construction via the process of contentious framing. Especially, we point out that a collective definition of the situation in an IT implementation project setting can be enacted through the practices of contentious framing. This can result in constituting meaning power to the advantage one side or the other in the form of preferred designs, functionalities, configurations, procedures or policies related to the IT systems. Thus, we have extended the research by pointing out tha meaning power as a power mode can be constituted through contentious framing.

The prior research is often silent on whether meaning power is static or dynamic (Jasperson et al., 2002). Our research has elaborated this aspect of the relation between social power and meaning construction represented as the process of contentious framing – revealing two processual characteristics. First, the contentious framing process exhibits a recognizable pattern – a form of structure: frame formation, frame contention, and frame enactment. The goal of these framing activities was to enact the preferred choices of IT users vs. designers in the form of a collective definition of the situation in regards to various aspects of IT design, development, and configuration. Second, our findings also offered support for the conceptualization of meaning power as an unfolding process which is highly episodic, situated, emergent and thus must be studied up close by scrutinizing the associated practices. Indeed by focusing on the numerous instances of contentious framing we have demonstrated that our results extend the literature (Bradshaw-Camball & Murray, 1991; Sillince & Mouakket, 1997) by showing that meaning power, as represented through our framework, exhibits both structura and emergent properties.

Another contribution of this paper is the adaptation and employment of the signature matrix as a method of researching contentious framing within IT implementation process. The signature matrix has previously been applied in social movement literature (Gamson & Lasch, 1983; Gamson & Modigliani, 1989) and more recently in organizational research (Creed et al., 2002a,b). Our adaptation and application of signature matrix to capture opposing actors frames during the contentious episodes within the system implementation process is among the first in IT research. This application demonstrates the utility of using signature matrix to extract and represent frame elements in a very transparent manner. In doing so, our signature matrix application has strengthened discursive theoretical approaches in IT research and has effectively answered the call for further development of such sociocognitive analytical tools (Barrett et al., 2006; Davidson, 2006).

## CONCLUSIONS

Our research focused on the conflictual process of IT implementation where users and designers attempt to exercise meaning power as a means of influencing their desired configuration of the focal system. Our framework and case study data (an IT project that aimed to alter work procedures in a challenging institutional environment) portrayed the exercise of meaning power as a trajectory of contentious framing. Through this process of contentious framing competing definitions of the situation are fought over, negotiated and enacted in a highly situated and emergent manner. Specifically, in our case, the contentious framing took place through a process where often two master frames were pitted against each other: ‘Using PCs to Support Existing Work Patterns’ vs. ‘Changing Work and Workflows through IT’.

Our findings demonstrate that the process of exercising meaning power follows incipient acts of contentious framing. In particular, there are instances where opposing actor groups make strategic attempts to alter the ‘definitions of the situation’ – vis-à-vis the focal system design. Though emergent. we find that instances of contentious framing are not chaotic. they exhibit a recognizable pattern – frame formation, frame contention and frame enactment. We further show that many framing instances involve an identifiable set of frame elements consistent with the notion of signature matrix. We also showed that the progression of changes to system design configurations is at least partly reliant on the definitional claims actors attempt to impose on the evolving situation within the implementation process. Hence, our analysis finds that within the conflictual IT implementation process, the exercise of meaning power can be a sustained activity enacted through the strategic and emergent framing efforts of actors.

Another core finding of our research is that exercising meaning power through contentious framing is a highly interactive affair. For example, the framing and counter-framing regarding the ‘digitization moves property boundaries’ vs. ‘digital map is a photocopy, hence same quality’ frames came about as a result of the situated interaction of the users and designers with the IT design and development process. In other words, meaning construction and social power, as the exercise of meaning power via contentious framing is a highly performative affair. In particular, our contribution is to show that specific instances of framing and counterframing are not something that actors pull out on a need basis from ‘a closet full of frames.’ Rather, the specific frames evolve from interaction between users and designers, and their interaction in turn with the IT implementation process. Our findings have thus extended the research by pointing out this important characteristic of contentious framing within the IT implementation process – i.e. frames as borne within situated practices and follow trajectories that are highly emergent and unpredictable.

Our case study was selected for its unique qualities which proved valuable because it provided access to empirical phenomenon inaccessible to regular research scrutiny. However, this is also a limitation of our research being confined to one case and thus making it difficult to generalize from. The following recommendations aim to rectify this limitation through further research. First, in this case, the exercise of meaning power is revealed because of the context of an organizational environment characterized by parity of resource and process power. Clearly, follow-up studies could test the generalizability of our findings by studying less unique cases – i.e. where resource and process power parity is absent. As a related research question, we did not address the meaning power relation(s) to other modes of power: to what extent the exercise of meaning power by social actors is a feature of politically gridlocked settings – e.g. cases that are less subject to the influence of vested interests and corrupt practices. Second, a further extension would be to explore the extent to which the exercise of meaning power was enabled by actors’ existing resource or process power endowments, and whether the exercise of meaning power in turn influences the subsequent endowments of resource and process power. Finally, further application of signature matrix and examining the robustness of frame elements across settings and IT projects can potentially enhance the credibility and applicability of this method within IT research. Focusing on these questions would help advance the interpretive research agenda of social power and IT.

We summarize our conclusions by suggesting the following. First, meaning power reflects agency-driven practices to construct meaning so as to influence system design during IT project implementation. Second, the exercise of meaning power is processual, in that it unfolds over time through a recognizable pattern. Third. meaning power is performative and highly interactive; it is borne in practice through intense and contentious process of framing among actors attempting to enact a collective definition of the situation around specific design and configuration choices of a given IT project. Fourth, the instances of contentious framing in practice may be numerous and result in enacting a collective definition of the situation which is more advantageous to one side than the other. Finally, we believe that framing and frame analysis, especially through the application of a signature matrix, can be a fruitful and rewarding methodological step for those interested in a better understanding of the contentious process of exercising meaning power during organizational implementation of IT projects.
