# Focus groups and critical social IS research: how the choice of method can promote emancipation of respondents and researchers

Bernd Carsten Stahl<sup>1</sup>, Monica Chiarini Tremblay and Cynthia M. LeRouge<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Information Management, De Montfort University, The Gateway, Leicester, U.K.; <sup>2</sup>Decision Sciences and Information Systems, Florida International University, Miami, FL, U.S.A.; <sup>3</sup>Decision Sciences/Information Technology Management, Saint Louis University, Saint Louis, MO, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Bernd Carsten Stahl, Information Management, De Montfort University, The Gateway, Leicester LE1 9BH, U.K. Tel: 44 116 207 8252; E-mail: bstahl@dmu.ac.uk

## Abstract

Critical social research in information systems has been gaining prominence for some time and is increasingly viewed as a valid research approach. One problem with the critical tradition is a lack of empirical research. A contributing factor to this gap in the literature is the lack of agreement on what constitutes appropriate methodologies for critical research. The present paper contributes to this debate by exploring the role that focus group research can play in the critical approach. This paper outlines the main characteristics of critical research with an emphasis on its emancipatory faculties. It then reviews the focus group method from the perspective of critical approach and provides a critical account of two research projects that used focus groups as a method of data collection. The paper presents the argument that focus groups, if designed and executed in light of a critical approach, can contribute to the emancipation of researchers and respondents. This argument is built upon the critical theories of the two most influential theorists in critical social information systems research, namely Ju¨rgen Habermas and Michel Foucault. Critically oriented focus groups have the potential to improve communication and move real discourses closer to Habermas’s ideal speech situation. At the same time, they can contribute to challenging the prevailing orthodoxy and thereby overcome established regimes of truth in the Foucauldian tradition. The paper ends by developing a set of guiding questions that provide a means for researchers to ensure that the emancipatory potential of focus group research can be achieved. ## Approach and motivation

Information systems (IS) researchers have embraced the critical approach as viable and adequate for studying information technology (IT) problems (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Trauth & Jessup, 2000; Richardson & Robinson, 2007; Stahl & Brooke, 2008; Myers & Klein, 2011). The critical approach allows for a departure from the purely descriptive approaches that are commonly used in IS. As Richardson & Robinson (2007, p. 264) indicate, ‘Critical research in IS serves as a reminder that IS is about the people as well as the business strategy, competitive advantage or systems development regardless of human cost’.

Despite the increasing prominence of the critical approach in the IS field, there is no agreement regarding appropriate methodologies for critical studies (McGrath, 2005). Although it has been observed that methodological issues are not necessarily central to the critical agenda (Avgerou, 2005; Walsham, 2005), it would be desirable to have a clearer understanding of the relationship between certain methods and critical research (Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2001). The present paper contributes to this debate by offering a discussion of focus groups as a method of data collection and their relationship to critical research.

The potential of critical focus group research has received attention from social researchers (Kitzinger & Barbour, 1999; Webb & Kevern, 2008). The focus group method can support emancipation of all individuals involved in research because each participant brings his or her own experiential insights, which may change as he or she learns of other participants’ experiences and insights.

In this paper, we compare the ability of focus groups and other data collection methods to contribute to the emancipation of both respondents and researchers. We demonstrate that critical theory provides criteria for evaluating data collection methods, and we provide empirical evidence of the appropriateness of the criteria. We offer guiding questions that support critical researchers in reflecting on the emancipatory effects of their research. We begin by reviewing research on critical social IS, including the contributions of Habermas and Foucault. We highlight the core concepts (e.g., emancipation, reflexivity, rationality, discourse, and the co-creation of reality) of our perspective on focus groups. This literature review leads to a discussion of focus groups and their possible contribution to the critical agenda to advance theory, including a comparison of focus groups with other types of data collection methods. Two case studies of research projects using focus groups are introduced and analysed from a critical perspective. The analysis shows that the choice of focus groups as a data collection method can promote the emancipation of respondents and researchers. The paper ends by critically reflecting on the findings and suggesting a set of guiding questions that will allow researchers to evaluate their use of focus groups.

## Critical social research

In the field of IS, critical research is seen as an alternative to the more established paradigms of positivism and interpretivism (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). We follow the suggestions of Harvey (1990) and Klein (2008) and add the term ‘social’ to critical research to specify a particular tradition of thought that goes beyond the critical considerations of established facts, theories, and data that pervade all research. Critical social information systems research (CSISR) can be defined using a number of different but interlinked characteristics. In this paper, we follow Stahl’s (2008a) view that the most important defining feature of critical social research is its intention to promote emancipation. This critical intention is justified by a perception of the world as imperfect and unjust and by the belief that research should address this imperfection and aim to improve the status quo. This foundation sets the critical approach apart from both the positivist and interpretivist traditions, which are predominantly descriptive. It is our aim to explore the emancipatory properties of the focus group methodology both for the participants and for the researcher.

The concept of emancipation raises a number of complex issues. The first issue is its definition. A wide definition, such as Klein & Huynh’s (2004, p. 163) ‘Emancipation means that more people can achieve their potential to a greater degree’, may be too broad to be helpful. Consequently, there have been many attempts to provide a more detailed definition. For example, Hirschheim et al. (1995, p. 83) define emancipation as ‘all conscious attempts of human reason to free us from pseudo-natural constraints’. Hirschheim & Klein (1994, p. 98) suggest that emancipation has both psychological and organisational dimensions: alienating circumstances can be found in the environment as well as the agent. Emancipation, therefore, must address issues of ‘false or unwarranted beliefs, assumptions, and constraints’ (Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997, p. 151). Waring (2004) seems to suggest that emancipation is about avoiding or overcoming conflict. In any event, it is difficult to clearly describe what constitutes emancipation. Attempts to define it substantively risk a dictatorship of the intellectual (Stahl, 2006), whereas more procedural approaches can lead to outcomes that the critical researcher can view as undesirable (Broadbent & Laughlin, 1997).

Despite these and other open questions, we believe that emancipation is the basis of most CSISR characteristics. Critical social research has a history of concentrating on social structures that alienate humans, which notably include economic structures (Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2005). Because of the emancipatory intention, CSISR tends to concentrate on those topics that are most relevant to overcoming alienation and oppression. Critical work outside of the field of IS often concentrates on classical topics such as class, gender, and race (Harvey, 1990). In IS research, critical scholars often focus on issues of power and control and the influence of technology on the distribution of power (Doolin, 1998). Other topics of interest include purposive rationality (Kumar et al., 1998; Avgerou & McGrath, 2007), the role and justification of managers (Introna, 1997; Ciborra, 2002; Avgerou, 2005), and the influence of the economic constitution on the development and use of IS (Saravanamuthu, 2002) or digital divides (Parayii, 2005).

The emancipatory intention has implications for epistemology and research strategy. Critical scholars tend to be sceptical of truth claims that are based on objectivist ontology (Alvesson & Willmott, 2003). The constructed nature of social reality (Gergen, 1999) requires that scholars understand the construction of individual and collective life-worlds. This leads to a dialectical understanding of research that is in direct opposition to positivist approaches. Critical researchers understand that knowledge claims are always contested, and they aim to understand under what circumstances they become accepted as truth. To change the status quo, social constructions need to be recognisable as such. Criticalists also realise that all knowledge claims are based on particular interests. Research cannot be an independent description of an external reality, but it is rather a process of simultaneously co-creating relevant knowledge by the researcher and the respondents (Trauth & Howcroft, 2006). There is no value-free and objective description of social realities. Therefore, knowledge must be understood in its historical context.

Given the scepticism of truth claims and the starting assumption that the world should be improved, critical social researchers tend to have an ambiguous relationship with empirical data, which can only say what the world is like but rarely gives indications of what it should be. This ambiguity may explain, to some degree, the ongoing problem of methodology in critical social research in IS.

CSISR is not algorithmic, and there is no way in which it can be done ‘by the book’. The desire to improve the status quo and promote emancipation requires critical scholars to critically evaluate every step of their own research. An ongoing problem of CSISR may best be explained by using the terms ideology and hegemony. Ideologies are particular and dominant worldviews that advantage some and disadvantage others (Hawkes, 1996; Freeden, 2003; Hawkes, 2003). Ideologies are not falsehoods; rather, they constitute central parts of the shared worldview of a society or group (Stewart & Gosain, 2006). As such, they can even be supported by empirical evidence (Gouldner, 1976). They are accepted by a group or society as correct descriptions of reality; however, from a critical perspective, ideologies can be seen as partial and alienating. Ideologies can only persist if they evade critical questioning and analysis. To remain stable, they must blend into the background. The mechanism by which this is achieved is sometimes called hegemony. Hegemony renders ideology invisible, often by rendering it natural and beyond discussion. The concept of hegemony is closely linked to Gramsci’s work, in which the main question was why people acquiesce to oppression (Kincheloe & McLaren, 2005). The problem of CSISR is that by establishing itself as a successful research approach, it is in danger of turning into another ideology, one that is supported by hegemonic means that have been drawn from the critical tradition. Empirical research as engagement with external realities is one way of ensuring that ideologies are questioned. Therefore, one important reason for considering alternative methods for empirical research, such as focus groups, is to prevent the critical approach from turning into an ideology.

The critical approach, therefore, requires scholars to be aware of the issues that are raised by their approach and to consider ways of addressing them. Reflexivity is the term that is often used to describe this awareness (e.g., Kvasny & Richardson, 2006). It encompasses all of the research process, including the research question, motivation, assumptions, outcomes, and methods. Reflexivity means that critical scholars intentionally and expressly question their own views, beliefs and values and own up to their agendas and intentions (Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2001). Reflexivity is thus very much about the application of critical thoughts to themselves. Whereas critical research is about questioning assumptions and beliefs and raising the awareness of problems, these activities are aimed at the critical research process itself (Doolin & McLeod, 2005).

Another aspect of the critical approach that has bearing on the role of focus group research is theory. A considerable number of theories have been used in critical research. In the field of IS, the works of Ju¨rgen Habermas and Michel Foucault have gained prominence (Brooke, 2002). Their theoretical positions in relation to discourse allow for a wider interpretation of the role of focus group research in the context of critical social research.

Habermas is considered to be a leading representative of the Frankfurt School of social theory. Whereas the first generation of Frankfurt scholars were more closely aligned with Marxism, Habermas developed social critique by establishing the Theory of Communicative Action (TCA) (2006), which posits that there are different ways of coordinating social action and that the most desirable method is communicative action. This means that interlocutors are accepted as equal and valuable individuals and that the aim of communication is consensus based on a rational exchange of arguments. Central to this idea is the concept of discourse. In any speech act, the speaker raises a number of validity claims (truth, legitimacy, authenticity). Whenever these are contested, discourses take place in which arguments are exchanged to clarify differences and reach a conclusion. Such discourses presuppose the (counterfactual) ideal speech situation in which all speakers are endowed with the same ability to express their views and are willing to accept one another’s arguments. Owing to the construction of discourses and their presuppositions, the TCA implies certain ethical views that Habermas has developed in his discourse ethics (1983, 1991). These views have recently been applied to IS (Mingers & Walsham, 2010). Important aspects of Habermas’ work that inform our interpretation of focus groups include the discursive nature of social reality, the ideal speech situation as an aim that indicates the way toward emancipation and the ethical nature of communicative interaction.

The second theorist who has gained prominence in critical social IS research is Michel Foucault. Like Habermas, Foucault sees the importance of discourses in establishing social reality. Unlike Habermas, however, Foucault is interested in who is included or excluded from discourses and on what grounds (1971). Where Habermas implies a view of truth that is based on a consensus (albeit often a fictitious one), Foucault holds that there are ‘regimes of truth’ that determine what counts as true in a particular context. In his extensive studies of different social realms, Foucault undertook genealogies of historical discourses to determine the ways in which legitimate knowledge is created. Some of the interesting aspects of his work that inform our interpretation of focus groups include the emphasis on bodily discipline in establishing discursive dominance, the concept of regimes of truth and the importance of power relationships in discourses.

A complex question that exceeds the limits of this paper is how to interpret the relationship between Habermas and Foucault (Kelly, 1994; Ashenden & Owen, 1999). It can be argued that both approaches further the emancipatory agenda by concentrating on different aspects of discourse (Stahl, 2004, 2008b). In this paper, we follow this line of argument when we apply both Habermasian and Foucauldian ideas to focus group research with the aim of establishing the emancipatory properties of this methodology.

A final theoretical remark about the role of empirical data in critical research should help to clarify the focus of the paper. As previously indicated, the relationship between critical theory and empiricism is ambiguous. Nevertheless, most researchers interested in CSISR view empirical engagement as conducive to the overall critical project. Given the theoretical positions of Habermas and Foucault, different justifications for such empirical work can be given. From a Habermasian perspective, engaging with respondents represents an engagement in practical discourses that allows researchers to develop their own and respondents’ understandings and, thus, their lifeworlds. This engagement allows researchers to collect and categorise truth claims that can then be analysed and used in further academic discourses, leading to a better understanding of social reality. From a Foucauldian perspective, empirical research provides a better understanding of existing regimes of truth and allows the researcher to trace why certain positions are seen to be legitimate or true. Neither position assumes that there is an unproblematic way of understanding an independent truth, but both positions see the value of considering varying and diverse viewpoints to achieve a more emancipated social reality. Our question in this paper is whether there are relatively better ways of considering these viewpoints and, in particular, whether focus groups lend themselves to such critical empirical work. The following section describes focus groups in more depth.

## Focus groups for critical research

Having introduced the theoretical background of this paper, the present section provides an overview of the literature on focus groups as a research method, followed by a discussion of why focus groups are a suitable method for critical research projects. This discussion is followed by a comparison of data collection methods in IS research using criteria derived from critical theory.

Powell and Single define a focus group as ‘a group of individuals selected and assembled by researchers to discuss and comment on, from personal experience, the topic that is the subject of the research’ (1996, p 499). A small group of individuals possessing certain characteristics are assembled to provide qualitative data via a focused discussion that is led by a moderator (Krueger, 1994). The focus group draws upon respondents’ attitudes, feelings, beliefs, experiences, and reactions in a way that is not feasible using other field methods (e.g., one-on-one interviews, surveys, and observation).

Although focus groups may initially be considered from a ‘mainstream’ perspective (e.g., commercial focus groups or as an adjunct to quantitative testing), focus groups have ‘emerged as a powerful tool in academic research’ (Davies, 1999, p. 295). Focus groups have a long history in market research, social research (Morgan, 1988), and, more recently, in medical research (Powell & Single, 1996). In fact, focus groups are a widely used method of social research, with more than 100 articles referencing this method published in social science journals each year. Despite this recognition, focus groups continue to be under-utilised in IS research as a primary method of data collection, even though they can be particularly valuable in exploring and recognising the socio-technical nature of IS. Notable exceptions that have used focus groups as a primary method of data collection in research include recent works by Smolander et al. (2008), Dickinger et al. (2008), Tremblay et al. (2010), and LeRouge & Niederman (2006).

The critical use of focus groups can be traced to its original application by Merton and his colleagues’ attempts to identify new strategies for data collection in which the researcher takes a less directive and dominating role and attention is shifted towards the respondent (Merton, 1987; Krueger & Casey, 2000). Conversing among peers in a comfortable setting with limited moderator prompts may result in more information about organisational issues, knowledge structures, and interactions with users or IT staff than participants may otherwise provide. When an individual user or IT staff member’s perspective is limited, focus groups can address these gaps with information from the full set of participants in the focus group. Contextual development is triggered by multiple participants’ statements as well as prompts from the moderator representing the research team (Krueger & Casey, 2000). Thus, gaps within an individual’s understanding of the IS can be addressed directly by information provided by fellow participants or indirectly through comments that stimulate recall or idea association. In addition, because the participants are exposed to a wide range of ideas and perspectives on the IS of interest, the focus group method can enhance a participant’s reflections and build on these conceptual insights via other participants’ perspectives. In essence, ‘collective sense is made, meanings negotiated, and identities elaborated through the processes of social interaction between people’ (Wilkinson, 1999, p. 225).

Multiple researchers (Wildinson, 1998; Kevern & Webb, 2001) have noted that focus groups are often underanalysed; analysis takes place at the content level alone and ignores interaction features that may be of particular interest in social research (particularly of a critical nature).

From critical theories such as Habermas’ and Foucault’s, we learn about the importance of power in any social interaction. Focus groups are no exception. Using a critical lens, scholars become more sensitive to both obvious power issues, such as the choice of focus group topics and selection of respondents, and less obvious ones, as expressed in moderation techniques or seating arrangements (e.g., elevating a passive participant to a position of power by seating him or her to the right of the moderator). Wilson (1997) suggests that the focus group is a ‘public’ place in which respondents may be guarded; however, it should not be assumed that groups are, by definition, inhibiting, or that focus groups are inappropriate when researching sensitive topics. Group work can actively facilitate participants’ empowerment to discuss taboo topics because the less-inhibited members of the group ‘break the ice’ for shyer participants (Kitzinger, 1995). Participants can also provide mutual support for expressing feelings that are common to their group, but which they regard as deviating from mainstream culture (or the assumed culture of the researcher). In addition, although the attitudes, experiences, and beliefs that are manifested by individuals exist independently of the focus group, they may be tacit and more easily explicated through interaction (Kevern & Webb, 2001).

Criticalists realise that one can never escape power relationships and that this would not even be desirable. The way to address these relationships is to make them explicit and to open them up to discussion and revision. Focus group discourse may expose asymmetric power relations in an organisation and allow silenced voices to be heard. The focus group may also affect balance by raising a participant’s or the group’s collective consciousness of dormant or unrealised issues (Wilkinson, 1998).

In comparison with other methods, such as interviews, critical social scientists report that focus groups may help to break down the disparity in power relationships between researchers and respondents (Johnson, 1996; Staveren, 1997; Wilson, 1997; Wilkinson, 1998, 1999; Lichtenstein & Swatman, 2003). A recent editorial on the topic of increasing the relevance of IT research proposes the use of focus groups as a means of receiving critical reviews and implicitly democratising the research process and outcome (Glass, 2009, p. 95). Morgan (1997) asserts that a focus group provides a means to obtain assessment from a group of stakeholders through the collaborative construction of independent attitudes, experiences, and beliefs rather than through consensus or negotiation. This may be true if the focus group is designed and executed with a critical ‘eye’ and with consideration of participant selection, protocol, seating placement, and careful selection of a moderator who can promote free participation by all present.

From Foucault, we learn that power is always bi-directional, and all power leads to potentially unforeseen reactions. It should be recognised that subtle bi-directional situations of power occur whenever focus groups are used as a primary method of data collection. Wilson notes, ‘By re-conceptualizing the focus group interview as a discussion amongst respondents, the researcher goes some way towards “democratising” the process and it is likely that more naturally occurring language will result in what still remains a socially contrived situation’ (Wilson, 1997, p. 217). Therefore, the role of the researcher should be discussed in addition to other influencing factors, including the power that the focus group members have over the researcher.

The critical perspective requires a different interpretation of research findings. Given the contingencies of power relationships and their corresponding reality constructions, it is clear that no research, including research based on focus groups, can produce a true representation of reality. Rather, all research needs to be understood as a contribution to the discourses that lead to accepted truth claims (Habermas) and that establish regimes of truth (Foucault).

Focus groups are arguably better equipped to occupy this role than other means of empirical data collection because of their discursive nature, which allows for the easy recognition of factors of interest to critical research. In the next section, we compare focus group interviews to other techniques that may be appropriate for this scope.

## Comparing focus groups to other empirical primary data collection techniques for critical research

Rather, the appropriateness of methods and their application need to be assessed based on the epistemological assumptions and the kind of critical questions that are investigated (Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2001). To evaluate each of the aforementioned issues for each data collection method, three researchers agreed on scales for each response set. Two researchers independently evaluated the key issues for each method of data collection using pre-established response scales and noted additional factors to consider. The researchers then discussed responses until a consensus was reached. The resulting table was reviewed and validated by a noted critical research scholar.

Based on the assessments provided in Table 2, the methods that appear to provide the most emancipatory faculties when they are utilised for data collection and subsequently analysed with a critical lens are semistructured and unstructured interviews, focus groups, and participant observation. We provide further assessments of each factor below:

\- Ideal speech situation: For this emancipation factor, focus groups seem to be the most promising. If correctly set up, focus groups allow all speakers the same ability to express their views and engage in one another’s arguments. An individual interview provides a one-dimensional perspective, whereas a focus group elicits multiple views through processes that can prompt reaction, the synthesis of new information and the experiences of others, and the recall of forgotten information. In general, interviews are controlled by someone who is likely to be a complete stranger, leading to a lack of trust and potential Hawthorne effects (Myers & Newman, 2007). General group interviewing emphasises questions and responses between the researcher and the participants rather than interactive conversation. Semi-structured and unstructured group interviews have more flexibility and allow for some improvisation, but they lack the natural conversation flow of focus groups. Additionally, as Myers (2009) points out, most interviewing techniques suffer from ‘Elite Bias’, which describes the tendency of researchers to interview people of high status.

\- Discursive flexibility: Both focus groups and observational techniques score highly in discursive flexibility. Focus groups are distinguished in that they rely on collaborative construction within the group in order to inform topics that are provided by the researcher (Morgan, 1997). Although a moderator leads the discussion, the moderator’s role is to provide prompts to facilitate the flow of discussion on a topic rather than elicit discrete answers to questions. The group participants are allowed to determine their own agenda as much as possible (Wilkinson, 1998), and, through their language, concepts, and frameworks for understanding the world, respondents take a position of importance in the hierarchy (Kitzinger, 1994). In comparing focus groups to group observation, it is noteworthy that in a focus group, the researcher prompts things to happen in an organised fashion, whereas, in observational methods, the researcher waits for things to happen in a natural way. Focus groups are the most ‘naturalistic’ when group members already know one another (Wilkinson, 1999).

\- Control: Observation is the only technique over which the researcher does not have control. Focus groups can achieve shared power between participants and researchers. A good focus group moderator knows how to keep the conversation flowing so that participants have equal power to express their views, particularly by ensuring that one strong participant does not dominate.

\- Democracy: Group interviews, focus groups, and observation all allow for the exploration of dominant and shared assumptions and may help to reveal shared beliefs. Focus groups and direct observation are probably best suited for this factor. The interaction (perhaps with probing from the moderator) in focus groups may render explicit participants’ assumptions. Direct observation may or may not involve democratic interaction.

\- Flexibility of the researcher: In direct observation, an open-minded researcher is capable of putting aside his/her biases and uncovering unintended results. Similarly, in focus groups, the interaction and camaraderie that is evident in focus groups can guide the researcher to reflect on his/her pre-conceived notions and to investigate areas that the researcher may not have intended to cover.

We argue that these factors collectively have the potential to promote emancipation. These factors set the frame in which the researcher and respondents communicate, thereby influencing the degree to which the research is beneficial and allows them to improve their lives or reach their potential. It is important to note that focus groups are not the only data collection method that can have emancipatory aspects. This underlines our previous claim that focus groups can have considerable value for critical research, but they do not constitute the only 'critical method'.

In the next section, two research studies illustrate how focus groups can be utilised for critical analysis.

## Description of two research studies

In this section, we describe two different IS studies within the health-care context to illustrate how the discursive nature of focus groups contributes to critical social research. The health-care industry and process is familiar ground for the critical perspective. Our first study focuses on a type of technology-mediated service encounter, telemedicine, in the form of medical video conferencing for patient medical encounters. Telemedicine can address a range of problems, particularly with regard to a better distribution of access to healthcare (Bashshur, 1995), and distance communication may impact the balance of power and control between patient and provider. Telemedicine is therefore a good topic for critical research.

The second study investigates issues of data quality in the context of public policy health planning. Three data quality problems were identified, and a set of data quality calculations were designed and implemented in an Online Analytical Processing (OLAP) system so as to support improved decision-making. The original intent was to use focus groups as an evaluative technique for an enhanced decision-support tool; however, the use of focus groups ultimately took the research down a critical path.

## Emancipating participants: exploring patients’ concepts of telemedicine encounter quality

The extent to which telemedicine positively impacts the health status of patients and equalises access to care are social issues that are at the core of telemedicine. This is underscored by an organisation’s motives for providing telemedicine services and the socio-technical nature of the telemedicine encounter itself. The organisations that participated in this study were part of a large government-run telemedicine network with a mandate to reach those requiring care within its network. Medical vision conferencing, which is the form of telemedicine that is addressed in this study, involves conducting patient encounters (such as clinical exams and consultations) using video conferencing equipment and related peripherals. The telemedicine network has initiated medical video conferencing as a means to provide highlevel specialty care to remote patients and to reduce patients’ wait times for care, rural patients’ anxiety and the time associated with travelling to an urban area for care, and travel reimbursement costs paid to patients. The purpose of this study was to identify the sociotechnical attributes that contribute to a successful medical video conferencing encounter workflow and process.

A critical question arose among the research team regarding informants: who should define utility, efficacy, and process in the telemedicine context? In the medical domain, it is acknowledged that the patient underpins each dimension of success and justifies each decision. However, this does not necessarily translate into a participatory democracy or a public sphere that encourages debate, deliberation, agreement, and action between the patient and provider (Villa, 1992). In a health-care service encounter, the relationship between patients and medical providers is asymmetrical: the patient subscribes to the professional authority and knowledge of the physician in seeking medical care. Even though patients may be considered partners in their medical care, patients have some degree of dependence upon medical care providers in the context of a medical encounter. Research indicates that a patient’s dependence on a medical care provider (particularly a physician) is based on the physician’s command of an esoteric body of knowledge that has been acquired through training and experience and the ability to justify his/her authority and to evoke the client’s trust, confidence, and norm of obedience in following prescriptions for care (Parsons, 1975). Despite this power difference, which is of intrinsic interest to the critical perspective, there are functional reasons for exploring patients’ views: their description of symptoms is required for diagnosis, they represent the ‘consumers’ of healthcare, and they shape the competitive market of healthcare. Therefore, it is plausible and arguably necessary for a health-care technology study to include data that have been directly collected from patients.

Early forms of data collection in this study and reviews of telemedicine patient satisfaction literature (primarily through quantitative means of data collection) indicate that patients are generally satisfied with telemedicine encounters (Collins et al., 2000); however, these studies provide limited critical analysis from the patient perspective. Critical social research seeks to question shared assumptions and unearth social realities behind such simplistic views. In their review, Collins et al. (2000) note some of the limitations of the quantitative measurement of patient satisfaction, including the propensity for patients to express high levels of satisfaction due to their reluctance to say the wrong thing or complain for fear of unfavourable medical treatment in the future. Direct observation provided relevant data for the general purposes of this study but did not seem to adequately augment the patient perspective.

Focus groups were chosen to give the patients a stronger voice in the study. In the spirit of ideal speech, every subject with the competence to speak and act was allowed to take part in the discourse. Patient encounters may be sporadic or limited, precluding a rich breadth of experience and the chance to share experiences with others so as to understand norms and nuances. We looked to focus groups to provide a means of obtaining patient ‘expertise’ through collaborative construction and learning from the related experiences of others (Morgan, 1997). In addition, the process of interactive discourse in the focus group may empower the patient to overcome his/her reluctance to say the wrong thing or provide an acquiescent response set.

Deliberate attempts were made in the design of the focus groups to enhance patient empowerment and the voices of individual patients who might find it difficult to express their attitudes, desires, and needs. Efforts included the use of aliases by participants, the application of rules to promote an ‘ideal speech’ environment, which were provided to participants in writing and read to the groups during the session, and the strategic placement of participants around the table. Prior to the focus group, the researchers informally interacted with the participants to identify potential candidates who might dominate or fail to participate in the focus group discourse. Placecards for participants were strategically placed (e.g., a timid participant to the researcher’s right) to equalise vocal power and attention. An attempt to enhance and empower patients was also made through the use of a trained peer moderator as opposed to a research team member or health-care provider. The principal researcher subordinated her focus group role to that of a scribe and assistant to ensure methodological rigour (Krueger & Casey, 2000). The researcher, in her role as an assistant, commanded a voice only at the end of the focus group so as to clarify areas of confusion, facilitate complete information and accurate coding, and provide a summary of interpretations of the focus group’s comments at the end of the session. This closing discourse between the researcher and focus group members was an initial effort toward validating interpretive analysis; it also provided some degree of shared power with participants in the analysis process.

## Emancipating the researcher: evaluating data quality in health policy decision-making

The second study focused on issues of the quality of aggregated data that are used by decision-makers for community health planning. Data quality is important in public health decision-making because IS are an integral part of information gathering. Health-care enterprises can be regarded as ‘data rich’ (Abidi, 2001) because they generate massive amounts of data, such as electronic medical records, clinical trial data, hospital records, and administrative reports. To improve the efficacy and profile of public health, both practitioners and researchers need reliable and timely information to make information-driven or evidence-based decisions (Friede et al., 1995).

This research was partially motivated by observations in the field, particularly the implementation of an OLAP interface on a data warehouse that is used by knowledge workers at a regional health planning agency in Florida. Local health planning agencies study the impact of various initiatives on the health-care system, provide assistance to the public and private sectors, and create and disseminate materials that are designed to increase their communities’ understanding of health-care issues (LeRouge et al., 2007).

This field study provided a rich understanding of the health planners’ tasks and their use of business intelligence technology. It has highlighted the need to understand patterns in data quality problems that are potentially masked by aggregation. Based on these observations, three data quality calculations and presentation methods were designed to inform the decisionmaker of three data quality problems in a health planning/aggregated data environment: (1) missing data, (2) small sample sizes, and (3) erratic trends in underlying data that could lead to poor decision-making.

The design science research paradigm was used for the design and evaluation of this study (Simon, 1996; Hevner et al., 2004). There are two stages in design-science research: the development of the artefact and its evaluation (with a number of iterations for refinement of the design). The focus groups were the final step in this study and were meant to act as a feedback mechanism for refinement of the design and an evaluation of the proposed data quality presentation methods. ‘Vignettes’ or story lines were used to create fictitious decision scenarios based on current health-care situations and sample health-care data. (Table 3 contains some examples for cases of missing data). The applied strategy was to present the data with and without the data quality information to detect differences in the collective decision-making processes. The moderator presented the experimental vignettes and encouraged the participants to play the role of a health-care decision-maker.

Five focus groups of 6 to 12 participants were conducted. The first focus group was a pilot to help identify timing issues, refine the questioning route, evaluate the moderator’s style, and reveal any potential logistical issues. The pilot data were not used for further data analysis. The following requirements were outlined for the participants: previous experience with decisionmaking in the health-care field, an advanced college degree, and some training in statistics and decisionmaking software systems. The selected participants had jobs that required the use of data analytics (e.g., spreadsheets, business intelligence tools, and statistics packages).

The focus groups were held in state-of-the-art conference rooms. The participants were seated in a U-shape arrangement to encourage collaboration (Krueger & Casey, 2000) and to allow space for the moderator to demonstrate the OLAP tool and PowerPoint presentation. The moderator presented the experimental vignettes and encouraged the participants to play the role of a health-care decision-maker. The focus group guided the moderator in exploring the health-care data. For example, participants were encouraged to ask the moderator to ‘drill down’ or ‘roll up’ data to thoroughly understand and compare data for different counties as part of their decision-making process.

The intended use of the focus groups was to empirically demonstrate the utility and efficacy of the addition of data quality information to OLAP tools for improved decision-making; however, the open-ended discourses generated by the focus group techniques perplexed the main researcher in this study. This researcher’s background as a programmer and computer scientist had guided her research topic choices, initial assumptions, and objectivist approach to research. In the focus group discourses, the researcher noticed non-obvious facts and relationships about how decision-support tools were used in decision-making that were contrary to her initial assumptions. This motivated a retrospective analysis of the focus group transcripts using a critical lens.

## Analysis using the critical approach

When focus group dynamics work well, the participants work alongside the researcher, taking the research in new and often unexpected directions (Kitzinger, 1995, p. 300). In this section, we illuminate some of the unexpected issues that were introduced by the focus groups that caused certain assumptions to be questioned. We use Habermas’ concept of ideal speech and Foucault’s concept of regimes of truth to highlight some unexpected directions and the questioning of assumptions.

## Study one: patient focus groups and the ideal speech situation

Although attempts were made to create a microcosmic public sphere that was supportive of debate, deliberation, agreement, and action, and even though signs of this were evident, there was no guarantee that every participant was emancipated in the discourse. Participants related positive views of process and action in the telemedicine encounters as well as imperfect experiences and possibilities for enhancement that they had not previously revealed in organisational or research surveys or as complaints or compliments. As the focus group conversation transpired, shared stories seemed to evoke a greater attention to detail and recall and the ability to examine the process with a critical lens. For example, comments regarding potential issues with the provider interacting with technology included the following:

Well, I found that there was a lot of time wasted in adjusting the cameras. Half the time was adjusting the cameras and instructing the nurse to focus in on different parts of my body, rather than to talk to me directly about my symptoms and my problems. I’m sure it was important, but to me that was a negative. In person, he wouldn’t have to adjust anything.

It is noteworthy that the aforementioned issues were not reported in open-ended survey responses or by patient reactions during the direct observation of telemedicine encounters.

Empowerment moved from a description of patients’ reality to a collective, inter-subjective vision of an ideal medical video conferencing encounter. In building this vision, participants interjected prescriptions for process and performance. For example, in discussing the role of the medical assistant who is responsible for operating cameras in the room with the patient, one patient commented that the assistant should

y be familiar with the doctor, have, you know, just don’t have, it has to be someone that and make them like a team. She is that doctor’s representative here, and so she has to know the doctor well enough that if he does say something and it can be taken three different ways, she usually knows which way he means it.

Focus group participants also prescribed special training for medical professionals who are involved in such encounters, as demonstrated by the following comment:

They ought to have some video training and to have it critiqued on their performance, because it’s really a performance; they’re on television.

For many such prescriptions, ensuing comments seemed to rally support; however, not all statements produced unanimity. There were differing opinions on the mix of in-person and video exams, although most of the six focus groups included members who supported an initial in-person visit as a means to start discourse with the doctor, as described below:

There’s a lot you can learn about the doctor by the way he handles you and how close he gets and his demeanour. It’s important to the care of the patient, and that’s what you get from that first visit in person.

Some patients also seemed at liberty to report on issues of their own shortcomings and awkward moments and were particularly forthcoming once one focus group member ‘broke the ice’. For example, some patients commented that they were captivated by the ‘television and therefore missed some of the doctor’s directives. During the follow-up, another patient recounted that he did not realise they did not have to speak very loudly; when told that he did not have to shout, the embarrassment provoked him to cease free-flowing conversation with the doctor. Other patients indicated that they did not realise or were not informed that they were not having an in-person exam, and they shared their reactions:

I was shocked. I did not know I was having a television exam. My wife was with me and she says, ‘Who’s talking?’ I said it was somebody over there. I’d just tell them to give us just a brief orientation, so that they would not be shocked.

The question for the research team was whether and how to share the ‘patient voice’ that was expressed in the focus groups beyond intended research outlets. The telemedicine coordinator and a leading doctor at one of the organisation’s most active telemedicine sites were invited to participate in the coding process so as to afford a more direct means of ‘hearing the voice’ of the patients that was beyond what general reports to participating organisations could provide. The practitioners received training and successfully completed the coding task. Practitioner coding was compared to researcher coding. In addition to providing the practitioners with direct access to patient comments, this process provided a means of analytical discourse among the researchers and the practitioners. The convergence between the coding of the research and practitioner teams provided another level of validation for the study. In the spirit of ideal speech, this process also introduced an unexpected discourse into the study. Although practitioners are often the subject and source of data in IS research, they are not often brought into the analysis process. Through their elevated role in this study, the practitioners voiced their contextual experience as well as their emotional and intellectual reactions into the design, experiences, and observations that are associated with the analysis process. The researchers felt that this discourse complemented and expanded their capabilities and insight.

Practitioners who are involved in the coding and reconciling process were alarmed by some patient comments indicating that they had not received a telemedicine encounter orientation that suited their particular concerns and needs for information. Critical research entails transformation in response to insight and critique. Once management was apprised of the patient orientation issues, the researchers were asked to work with the practitioners to develop patient orientation materials with content that reflected patient orientation needs as indicated by the focus group discussion. To continue the discourse, a validating survey was sent to focus group participants by the research team so as to confirm analytical insights. Additionally, at the request of the organisation, a copy of the prototype for the patient orientation brochure that emerged from this study was sent to the patients for evaluation with the previously discussed survey packet. Practitioners initiated a patient evaluation of the patient orientation materials prototype after their review of the patient validating survey questions and discussion with researchers about validating procedures. Historically, the standard process for such materials has been an internal review/approval by the patient education committee and various other personnel without such a patient review. This procedure of soliciting patient feedback on patient education materials provides further indication of the practice of transforming patients’ voices.

## Study two: questioning the orthodoxy: challenging regimes of truth

The second study investigated issues of data quality in the context of public policy health planning. The intent was to aid human decision-makers in better understanding the quality of their data and the presence of potentially incomplete and unreliable information from multiple data sources. The underlying premise at the start of the study was that additional data quality information would be valued by respondents. Thus, the core issue of the study was what data quality information should be presented and how to present it. Focus groups were used to inform the design of the decision-support tool, which was enhanced with data quality information, and to evaluate the ability of this tool to improve decision-making for public policy health planning issues. The loosely structured format of the focus groups (as opposed to individual interviews with structured questions or controlled experiments, wherein all variability is carefully controlled) had interesting, yet unexpected, outcomes.

The focus group format allowed the conversation to navigate to areas that the researcher had not intended to cover. For example, much of the conversation included speculation on why the data quality problems existed. These new discourses added rich information to the research topic and contradicted some of the initial assumptions held by the researcher. As focus group participants became more comfortable with sharing their ideas or when they stumbled onto a particular aspect of the topic that excited or bothered them, their resulting solidarity shifted the power from the moderator to the participants. The participants chose to speak about aspects of decision-making that they encountered when using this type of decisionsupport tool and not necessarily about data quality issues. Because of the unintended discussions, the problems that motivated the researcher to pursue the study appeared in a different light and forced her to question whether the chosen research focus of presenting data quality information to decision-makers was pressing or even important. In this section, we illustrate these points with examples from the study.

The major assumption made by the researcher was that decision-makers understand that poor data quality is prevalent in many data sources; however, it appeared that most decision-makers believed that data quality did not cause problems in their organisations. They indicated that they knew which data sources were not reliable, and they simply avoided using them. This attitude is evident in the following participant’s comment:

I think some of it’s probably documented, but there are some variables like that every researcher would know, like don’t even bother using it. It’s so bad. But you know- it has a reputation for being bad. And you might know that by reading the literature, but most likely, you’re probably gonna know by informal network.

In fact, the participants had a difficult time conceptualising decision-making with data, wherein the data quality is unknown and potentially poor:

We’re typically more hands on. At least I know for us three, it’s – we are aware of why things are missing in general and what happened that it was missing. So I guess for me it’s a little bit harder to talk about something that’s completely unknown because I don’t have a context.

In another vignette, participants were asked to determine whether there was a correlation between smoking and certain types of cancer in the presence of significant amounts of missing data. Surprisingly, in all of the focus groups, the participants indicated that it depended on the stakeholder. The information about the missing data as well as the drilling capability of the OLAP tool could be used to argue either side. This situation was treated with humour, but it was apparent that in these tools, data could be (and were, at times) manipulated for a desired outcome.

Another assumption held by the researcher was that decision-makers would use the provided data quality information for improved decision-making. However, when presented with the system prototype showing data quality information, the participants collectively began to speculate on the reason for the data quality problem rather than concentrating on using the tool to help them with the test case decision tasks. For example, in the vignette in which participants were asked to explore health-care disparities in cancer screening for Hispanics, the participants speculated on the cause for the missing data, exhibiting discourse that deviated from the researcher’s initial intention:

Years ago, we started to do press screening in migrant Mexicans – among migrant Mexicans – and we tried to give them the free ones, and they didn’t feel like they deserved that kind of care. That was for rich people and wasn’t for them, which would, you know, sort of figure in some of the cultural issues related to this beyond just sort of race.

In several cases, the decision-making task was ‘nitpicked’ by the participants for small nuances, and the data quality information was largely ignored.

I think that ideally your decisions would be more effective, but I can see you might spend a lot of time if you are digging through that (data quality information), and that time could be wasteful in and of itself.

The solidarity that formed among the participants had an interesting effect. In one particular focus group that consisted of Ph.D. statisticians at a large hospital centre, participant solidarity actually shifted the power and control from the moderator to the participants. The group disliked the decision task vignettes that were chosen as test cases and refused to make decisions with the data that were presented to them. This refusal was interesting because the examples were based on simplified versions of real decision-making scenarios within their respective contexts. This particular group resisted any sort of ‘watering down’ of tasks that were similar to their everyday tasks. In retrospect, the participants were incensed that the task design did not consider the situational nuances and unique decisionmaking skills that they had acquired by doing this kind of work. They felt that the data quality issues that were being covered minimised the actual issues they faced. For example, in the Hispanic disparities case, one participant noted,

My concern is that the census classification of Hispanic allowed for people that aren’t Hispanic, for documentation purposesyHispanic is anything that doesn’t fall under white or black or that some people may not consider themselves Hispanic if they are of a mixed race.

As the moderator tried to re-focus respondents’ attention on the data quality issues, the participants basically revolted and said it was just too much to consider in addition to the information that they normally consider in their decision-making processes. Their consensus was that the addition of data quality information to the decision-support tool might force them to consider too much information and could actually decrease their decision-making efficiency.

## Reflections

Critical research aims to be reflective and to question its own assumptions, beliefs, and outcomes. This paper seeks a better understanding of the role that focus groups can play in critical research, or, to put it differently, whether and how the use of focus groups for data collection can further emancipation. This section reflects on the possible emancipation of respondents and researchers. It concludes the paper by developing a list of guiding questions that allows researchers to reflect on whether focus groups are used in the best way to achieve emancipation.

## Emancipation of the respondents

The stories that were shared by patients in the first telemedicine study eroded misapprehensions about sharing aspects of their medical video conferencing experiences that were not revealed by other methods of data collection. Positive experiences were not negated, but perceptions of the ideal sometimes shifted. In the telemedicine study, each patient had unique telemedicine exam experience and opinions of success that he or she brought into the group. The sharing of unique situations, experiences and commonalities evoked a new social reality of the steps that were necessary to achieve an ideal telemedicine process. Although some individual differences in opinion still existed, a shared common knowledge base of histories transformed the participants. A richer analysis evolved in recognition of the fact that even positive situations can benefit from a critical lens. This recognition occurred when multiple organisations applied results from the study to move to a higher level of service and understanding regarding medical video conferencing. Consciously improving communication situations in accordance with Habermas’ ideal speech situation can improve individuals’ understanding of technologies and lead to a better understanding of different stakeholder groups (e.g., patients and clinicians). Focus groups are uniquely suited for such improvement of speech situations because, by their very nature, they are close to the ideal speech situation. Making positive use of social dynamics can help researchers achieve the emancipatory consequences of qualitative research by using focus groups.

## Emancipation of the researcher

The interaction and camaraderie that is evident in focus groups guided the researcher of the data quality study to reflect on some preconceived notions about decision-making in the important context of decisionmaking on health public policy. It is evident that providing information about the quality of the data should empower the decision-makers to improve their approach to decisions. Using Foucault’s concept of regimes of truth, we witnessed the power of focus groups to question established beliefs. IS researchers tend to adopt an unproblematic view of agents’ rationality, which, in this case, translates into the belief that high-quality data will lead to good decisions. Exploring discourses during the focus group research showed that this orthodoxy is fundamentally flawed. Respondents were not as interested in data quality as standard theories suggest that they should or would be. In reality, most decision-makers paid limited attention to data quality issues in contrast to alternative realities about decisionmaking that emerged in this context. Personal biases in decision-making (such as questionable racial stereotypes), speculation about reasons for data quality problems, stakeholder issues and limitations in the amount of data that a person can or is willing to process objectively were in themselves problematic. These findings allowed the researcher to gain new insights into data quality issues in health-care settings and to question the very assumptions on which the research project was based. By coming to this new position, the researcher was emancipated from preconceptions and was free to explore alternative explanations of social realities.

Guiding questions on focus groups as a critical method We believe that this paper gives a convincing account of the positive contribution of focus group research to the critical tradition in IS. It can be emancipatory for participants as well as researchers, and there are diverse theoretical explanations of the critical faculties of the focus group method. This account should not be understood to say that focus groups should be viewed as a ‘critical method’ per se. Focus groups can involve a disregard of participants, alienation of stakeholders and the brute exertion of intellectual power. The mere use of a particular data collection method says little about the overarching aims and objectives of the research for which it is employed.

In this paper, we have argued and supported with two examples that focus groups can have emancipatory faculties. It would not be unreasonable to ask how a researcher can know whether a particular focus group fulfils its emancipatory potential; however, in light of the nature of critical research, one cannot expect the development of straightforward guidelines or criteria. The attempt to develop these would be akin to the alienating use of unjustified powers or to the establishment of a new regime of truth. In the spirit of critical research, a set of questions can be devised to help the researcher to critically reflect on the research for which the focus group was employed as a data gathering method. Below, we have collected such a set of questions. These questions must be read in the spirit of critical reflexivity so as to allow the researcher to question his/her own biases, beliefs and assumptions. These questions are not simple yes or no questions, and in many cases, they will not have right or wrong answers. Instead, they should be seen as initiating a discourse that will allow researchers to focus on the emancipatory faculties of their research.

Question 1: Is the research project recognised as going beyond a description of social realities, and does it aim to create a better world? This is the criterion that sets critical research apart from non-critical interpretive or positivist research. If this basic criterion is not fulfilled, the research may still turn out to be emancipatory, but this will not be by design. Focus groups are unlikely to develop their full emancipatory potential if emancipation does not play a role in the development of the research. This question is also relevant for research projects that are functional or alienating by design (e.g., when focus groups are used for marketing research for non-desirable products).

Question 2: Have conscious efforts been made to align the focus group with the ideal speech situation? While the ideal speech situation is transcendental (i.e., it describes the conditions of the possibility of communication rather than any real discourse), it can serve as guidance for real-life interactions. This can be broken down into sub-questions, such as the following:

\- Are all relevant and identifiable stakeholders represented?

\- Are the involved individuals capable of representing their position?

\- Has individual dominance in the group been avoided?

\- Have different types of claims (e.g., truth, rightness, and authenticity) been explicitly raised, and have there been opportunities to challenge them?

Question 3: Have different levels of discourse been explored or could they have been explored? It is typical of Habermasian discourses that they not only cover a particular contentious validity claim but also can cover the discourse itself. In terms of a focus group discussion, this means that it should be possible to discuss unexpected issues, such as the role of the researcher or the validity of the research question and project itself.

Question 4: Has the mutuality of power relationships been explored? Power relationships between participants in discourses are always reciprocal, but this reciprocity is often ignored. Respondents can influence the researcher, and there can be complex power relationships among respondents.

Question 5: Has there been an exploration of dominant and shared assumptions? To understand the social reality of the focus group, it is important to be aware of and explicitly render the shared beliefs of the participants. This awareness centrally includes the question of which topics, concepts, and contentious issues were not covered or were rendered invisible.

Question 6: Has the researcher reflected on her or his role and been explicit about the emancipatory potential of the focus group? This can be broken down into further sub-questions:

\- Is the researcher open to a two-way communication (i.e., willing to let participants influence the research project)?

\- Does the researcher have a positive view of the democratic potential and the obligations of research?

\- Is the researcher willing to subject her or his own biases, assumptions, and beliefs to the same critical scrutiny as those of the respondents?

This list of questions, which is visibly influenced by Habermas and Foucault in similar measures, does not claim to be comprehensive; however, it does claim to focus on some of the points at which focus groups can be or fail to be emancipatory. Some of these questions must be considered during the early phases of deciding on a methodology, whereas others will be more pertinent during the implementation of the focus group. Some questions will only become relevant during data analysis and interpretation. Thinking through these questions implies an awareness of the imperfection and fallibility of all research. As McGrath (1981, p. 189) remarked in a discussion of research methodologies, ‘Methodological discussions should not waste time arguing about which is the right strategy, or the best one; they are all lousy’. This sentiment can easily be transferred to critical research. The emancipatory agenda raises so many questions in its own right that it is gratuitous to ask whether it was done well. What counts is whether a conscious and realistic attempt was made to promote emancipation through the use of the focus group.

## Conclusion

One can never achieve the ideal speech situation. The researcher will always play a central role that has a tremendous influence on outcomes. The researcher sets the topic, establishes the legitimacy of the research question, selects the participants, influences the discourse situations, controls the physical resources, and decides how to use the data. Although the process can be made as transparent and democratic as possible, it will always remain skewed toward the perspective of the researcher. An awareness and sensitivity toward critical topics is necessary for focus group research to display its critical characteristics.

Critical reflexivity should be applied to all critical work, including the present paper. It is appropriate, therefore, to point out the limitations of our argument. One core problem with our approach in this paper is that it is fundamentally functional: it does not question the technology and its context of usage. It is not obvious that the socio-economic framework in which our investigations took place is truly geared toward emancipation. Accepting the description of healthcare as a market raises a number of problems. It means that financial considerations are central to healthcare, which certainly not all critical scholars would find acceptable on the grounds of distribution and justice. Using a Habermasian perspective, one can say that this perspective represents the colonisation of the life-world, which Habermas would not find acceptable. Our approach is thus open to the charge of being fundamentally uncritical because it uses the critical repertoire of ideas in order to improve a social context and a use of technology whose legitimacy per se is not certain. We have tried to refer to this problem in our first guiding question by underlining that critical research must always consider the purpose of the study and can never be confined to issues of methodology.

A further problem that is fundamental to all critical social research is the question of successful emancipation. Owing to a number of conceptual and epistemological issues, most active critical researchers settle for the idea of ‘transformative redefinition’ (Alvesson & Deetz, 2000) as the hallmark of success. The idea is that allowing people to reconceptualise their world can effect emancipatory change. The question for us, then, is whether the focus group approach is truly conducive to such transformative redefinition and whether it is possible to provide evidence of it. Our account of the research suggests that the interaction during the focus groups has given rise to such redefinition, but whether they were truly transformative is a difficult question.

In summary, we think there is a good case to be made that focus groups are a research method that goes well with the critical approach. At the same time, the focus group approach raises a number of questions that require caution from the critical researcher. It is particularly important to underline the fact that the choice of method, including focus groups, does not automatically render a piece of research ‘critical’. Criticality requires ongoing attention to issues of emancipation that may, nevertheless, remain elusive.
