# The Role of Multidimensional Social Capital in Crowdfunding: A Comparative Study in China and US

## Abstract

An online crowdfunding platform is an important social network community fo entrepreneurs to raise money to support their entrepreneurial projects. However, the value of an entrepreneur’s social capital embedded within the social network does not gain enough attention in crowdfunding literature. How does an entrepreneur’s social network impact the performance of crowdfunding? Based on social capital theory, we developed a research model to investigate the impact of an entrepreneur’s social capital that includes three dimensions, i.e., structural, relational, and cognitive. We conducted a comparative study of the three dimensions using objective data collected from China and the U.S. We found that an entrepreneur’s social network ties, obligations to fund other entrepreneurs, and the shared meaning of the crowdfunding project between the entrepreneur and sponsors had significant effects on crowdfunding performance in both China and the U.S. We also revealed some cross-cultural differences. We found that the predictive power of the three dimensions of social capital was stronger in China than in the U.S.; obligation, the relational dimension, also had greater impact in China. Implications for research and practices are discussed.

## Key words

Crowdfunding, social capital, obligation, crowdfunding performance, comparative study

## Introduction

Web 2.0 technologies empower firms to outsource their internal business tasks and activities to individuals, or the crowd, on the Internet who are capable of completing these business tasks (Howe, 2006). The crowd can also invest in business ideas and projects initiated by entrepreneurs in the crowd who do not have the traditional monetary resources, such as banks and investment agencies, to implement their ideas. These entrepreneurs can pledge for financial resources from the crowd in various open social communities. This emerging approach of raising money via the Internet is called crowdfunding. Massolution (2013) reported that the volume of crowdfunding grew 81% from 2011 and reached \$2.7 billion in 2012. In addition, the reward-based type of crowdfunding (Lambert and Schwienbacher, 2010; Ahlers et al., 2012) was expected to reach \$1.4 billion in 2013 (Massolution, 2013). According to Kickstarter.com, one of the most popular reward–based crowdfunding sites, more than 5 million people have pledged over \$900 million to fund 13 million projects, 43 % of which have successfully raised money.

The popular practice of crowdfunding has drawn the attention from the research community. Some preliminary research findings are revealed in the following three areas. First, some studies have examined under what conditions entrepreneurs adopt crowdfunding rather than other fundraising approaches (Schwienbacher and Larralde, 2012; Belleflamme et al., 2010). Schwienbacher and Larralde (2012) find that successful crowdfunding projects are generally appealing to the crowd, and these projects request a smaller amount of capital. The entrepreneurs of these projects are knowledgeable and skillful with Web 2.0 technologies. They are also willing to expand their expertise and skill sets. Second, some studies have explored the motivations of entrepreneurs and sponsors to participate in crowdfunding (Lambert and Schwienbacher, 2010; Gerber et al., 2012). Entrepreneurs seem to have a diverse range of motivations to start crowdfunding projects, such as raising money, attracting the public, and obtaining feedback from the crowd for their products and services (Lambert and Schwienbacher, 2010). Project sponsors, on the other hand, are motivated to support those individuals who have similar interests and expertise and are like-minded. They are also motivated to become a member of the crowdfunding community where they can learn and share their knowledge and business ideas (Gerber et al., 2012). Third, some scholars have investigated the determinants of crowdfunding performance (e.g., Lambert and Schwienbacher, 2010; Mollick, 2014). Crowdfunding projects that are focused on non-profit purposes tend to be more successful, and projects that aim to produce tangible products rather than provide service tend to attract more capital (Lambert and Schwienbacher, 2010).

In addition, some studies have investigated the social network community where an entrepreneur is embedded. The amount of capital collected in crowdfunding is heavily dependent on the range of social networks where entrepreneurs belong (Lehner, 2012, Mollick, 2014). Social network sites, such as Twitter and Facebook, are important platforms for entrepreneurs to connect with fans and friends who are willing to provide financial and information supports (Bechter et al., 2011). These studies have investigated the formation of social network ties, the geographic locations of sponsors, and the amount of an entrepreneur’s fans on crowdfunding performance (e.g., Mollick, 2014; Agrawal et al., 2011; Gerber et al., 2012; Lehner, 2012; Preece, 2002).

This study extends the prior effort that examines the effect of social networks in two ways (e.g., Agrawal et al., 2011; Mollick, 2014). First, the current literature is mostly exploratory and adopts research methods such as case study (Ordanini et al., 2011) and grounded theory approach (Gerber et al., 2012). There is a lack of underlying theories and theoretical support in the current crowdfunding literature. This study aims to be one of the first to introduce the theory of multidimensional social capital to crowdfunding literature. The theory suggests that the social networks where individuals are embedded can facilitate resource exchanges and knowledge sharing among the individuals through structural dimension (e.g., network ties), relational dimension (e.g., trust), and cognitive dimension (e.g., shared narrative) (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998; Tsai and Ghoshal, 1998; Liu et al., 2010). These three dimensions are widely adopted in the business and management literature (e.g., Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998; Tsai and Ghoshal, 1998; Liu et al., 2010). Based on the theory, this study develops a theoretical model to examine the effects of the three dimensions of social capital on the performance of crowdfunding.

Second, social capital may have different impacts on crowdfunding in different countries when crowdfunding has become a global business practice. For example, while Kickstarter and Indiegogo have become the well-known crowdfunding platforms in the U.S., some competing platforms have emerged in China, such as

Demohour.com and Dreamore.com. To our best knowledge, little research has examined crowdfunding from a cross-cultural perspective. The cross-cultural literature and Guanxi literature suggest that culture has an important effect on an individual’s social life and that people from different culture backgrounds have different beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors (Chua et al., 2009; Staber, 2006; Taormina and Gao, 2010; Xiao & Tsui, 2007). This study is one of the first to conduct a comparative empirical study to reveal the differences of crowdfunding across cultures. We test our research hypotheses using data collected from China and the U.S. The results of such a comparative study will help researchers and industry practitioners understand how the basic principles of crowdfunding are applied worldwide and whether some universal rules can be revealed.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We first provide a literature review of the current research in crowdfunding and the theory of multidimensional social capital. We then develop a research model and the corresponding research hypotheses. Next, we present an empirical study conducted in China and the U.S. Finally, we discuss the findings and draw some implications for research and practice.

## Literature review

## Crowdfunding

The concept of crowdfunding evolves from crowdsourcing, which was first coined by Howe (2006) as “the act of a company or institution taking a function once performed by employees and outsourcing it to an undefined (and generally large) network of people in the form of an open call.” Companies can obtain ideas, feedback, and solutions from their customers and business partners through crowdsourcing mechanisms (Belleflamme et al., 2010). In addition to collecting creative business ideas and solutions, crowdsourcing can be used to tap people’s excess capacities and resources, such as monetary and financial resources (Howe, 2006). This type of crowdsourcing where customers play as investors is called crowdfunding. Crowdfunding is an effective funding channel to get small to medium-size investments from the crowd (Ordanini, 2009; Ordanini et al., 2011).

In the current study, we focus on reward-based crowdfunding, which is defined as “crowdfunding involves an open call, essentially through the Internet, for the provision of financial resources either in the form of a donation or in exchange for some form of reward and/or voting rights in order to support initiatives for specific purposes” (Lambert and Schwienbacher, 2010 P.6). This definition of crowdfunding does not cover other models, such as lending-based crowdfunding (Lin and Viswanathan, 2013) and equity-based crowdfunding (Ahlers et al., 2012).

Crowdfunding is a popular practice in the music industry, where fans are not only consumers of the music but also involved in the production of albums. Some Internet-based platforms, such as SellaBand, MyMajorCompany, and Artistshare, provide music artists the access to raise funds from fans. At first, artists post their songs on the website. Fans then access and listen to these songs for free. These fans then decide whether to invest in the production of the songs. If the total amount of money raised for an artist reaches the threshold of the funding goal, the artist can produce the album with the raised money. Sponsors or investors of these songs are compensated by receiving some revenues from the album, or they are rewarded by participating in the album production process (Belleflamme et al., 2010). In addition, crowdfunding is also seen in the publishing industry, where the crowd helps professional journalists research and publish articles (Burtch et al., 2011).

Belleflamme et al. (2011) summarize three characteristics of crowdfunding. First, crowdfunding initiatives often rely on advanced purchase of products that are not available on the market. Entrepreneurs who start crowdfunding projects describe what the final products are and offer a list of monetary or nonmonetary rewards for sponsors who are willing to invest. Second, consumers or sponsors pay more in the pre-ordering process than traditional consumers who wait to buy the finished products on the market. Third, sponsors identify themselves as members involved in the production process that ranges from initial donation of money to direct involvement in the whole project. The third characteristic is considered one major benefit of crowdfunding over traditional funding approaches, because crowdfunding can involve consumers or sponsors and thereby enhance their experiences in the complete production process. Schwienbacher and Larralde (2012) identify three crowdfunding business models: donation, passive investment, and active investment. The donation model has long been implemented in non-governmental organizations. Passive investment means that crowdfunding sponsors have limited interaction and communications with entrepreneurs but still aim to get rewards, tailored products, honorary recognitions, or other forms of revenue sharing from the crowdfunding projects. On the contrary, active investment provides crowdfunding sponsors the opportunities of not only investing money but also constantly interacting with entrepreneurs, such as helping entrepreneurs with new features, testing products, and providing directions and feedback (Schwienbacher and Larralde, 2012; Lehner, 2012).

## Social capital theory

Social capital lies in an individual’s social structure, which is different from other types of physical capital (Coleman, 1998). There are different definitions and conceptualizations of social capital in different academic disciplines. For example, Coleman (1998) defines social capital in terms of its functions: “it is not a single entity, but a variety of different entities, with two elements in common: they all consist of some aspect of a social structure, and they facilitate certain actions — whether persons or corporate actors within the structure” (P.98). In business and management literature, a commonly cited definition of social capital is “the sum of actual and potential resources embedded within, available through, and derived from the network of relationships possessed by individuals or social unit” (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998 P.243).

Social capital is a multidimensional concept (Coleman, 1998; Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998; Hazleton and Kennan, 2000). In management literature, Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998) suggest social capital in terms of three dimensions, i.e., structural dimension, relational dimension, and cognitive dimension. The structural dimension means that structure characteristics, such as network ties and network configuration, are necessary for the development and utilization of social capital. The relational dimension refers to the capital derived from obligations, expectations, and trustworthiness of the social network. The cognitive dimension suggests that shared language and schema, as well as shared narratives, can facilitate people in an organization to gain social capital.

This conceptualization of multidimensional social capital is widely accepted in the information systems research (e.g., Wasko and Faraj, 2005; Chiu et al., 2006). The structural dimension is often investigated together with the relational dimension and cognitive dimension in order to understand an individual’s behavior, such as knowledge contribution in an online community (Widen-Wulff and Ginman, 2004; Wasko and Faraj, 2005; Chiu et al., 2006), the intention to obtain knowledge (Zhao et al., 2012), loyalty in a virtual community (Hsiao and Chiou, 2012), the usage of instant messaging tools (Lin, 2011a), and job commitment and job effectiveness in virtual teams (Lin 2011b). In addition, researchers integrate social capital theory and other theories, such as social cognitive theory (e.g., Chiu et al., 2006) and motivation theory (e.g., Hau et al., 2013; Chang and Chuang, 2011), to explain a member’s behavior in online communities.

A social network is the source in which social capital is embedded (Coleman, 1998). There are two types of social networks in the context of crowdfunding. The first is the social network that an entrepreneur is embedded in the crowdfunding platform, such as Kickstarter. The second is the social network that an entrepreneur develops in other third-party social network websites, such as Twitter and Facebook. In both types of social networks, information technology, communication technology, and online community are effective means to foster the development of the entrepreneur’s social capital (Preece, 2002). Entrepreneurs who have some business initiatives and need financial support can connect with people on the social network sites. Because sponsors of crowdfunding projects do not have the opportunities to experience the qualities of the products and service before consumption (Ward and Ramachandran, 2010), it is necessary to improve the confidence of these sponsors in crowdfunding.

## Research model and hypotheses

Following Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998), we define an entrepreneur’s social capital as the total range of actual and potential resources (financial and non-financial) embedded within the crowdfunding social networks where the entrepreneur is attached. Based on the multidimensional social capital theory (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998), which is widely accepted in management literature and information systems literature, we develop a research model in Figure 1. The dependent variable is crowdfunding performance. Three dimensions of social capital, i.e., structural dimension, relational dimension, and cognitive dimension (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998), are identified as antecedent factors. We also model culture as a moderator in the model to reveal some cross-cultural differences. Specifically, we study the moderating roles of culture on the prediction power of social capital in two different countries and the relationship between the relational dimension and crowdfunding performance. ## Structural dimension: social network ties

The structural dimension of social capital has gained extensive attention in social capital research (e.g., Tsai, 2000; Moran, 2005; Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998; Yli-Renko et al., 2001). The prior research has frequently studied social network centrality (e.g., Wasko and Faraj, 2005). The fundamental proposition for the structural dimension is that network ties provide access to resources, such as knowledge and funds (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). In this study, we focus on the amount of an entrepreneur’s social network ties, i.e., the degree of the ties, which refers to the number of an entrepreneur’s friends in the crowdfunding platform and in third-party social network websites.

Prior studies have found that social network ties provide benefits for information sharing in three forms: access, timing, and referrals (Hazleton and Kennan, 2000). Based on 158 venture capital investment decisions in China, Batjargal and Liu (2004) find that an entrepreneur’s positive relationship with a venture capitalist is very important at the selection stage of private equity. Such a relationship is supplementary and additive to other determining factors that spur the capitalists to invest. In addition, social capital is also a main factor in the process of investment negotiations.

In crowdfunding project to the crowd through social network ties and call on his/her friends to invest in the project. Kapipal, a crowdfunding website, states “the more contacts you have, the greater your chances of reaching the amount you need” (www.kapipal.com/manifesto). A study of lending-based crowdfunding finds that social capital is a significant antecedent of funding performance and that the amount of endorsements from a lender’s group significantly affects the likelihood of funding (Greiner and Wang, 2010). Regarding reward-based crowdfunding, Mollick (2014)

finds that an entrepreneur’s personal network ties on Kickstarter, measured as the amount of Facebook friends, help predict the success of crowdfunding. Therefore, we propose

H1: The degree of an entrepreneur’s social network ties is positively associated with crowdfunding performance.

## Relational dimension: obligation

While the structural capital determines the range of potential investors within an entrepreneur’s reach, it is the relational embeddedness that determines the likelihood that an investment is achieved (Moran, 2005). The relational dimension of social capital can be viewed as the strength or quality of the relationship and evaluated by means of trust, norms, obligations, and identification (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). From the relational perspective, an obvious approach for an entrepreneur to develop social capital is to invest in other entrepreneurs’ crowdfunding projects, which may develop the obligation of other entrepreneurs to fund the entrepreneur’s crowdfunding project (Staber, 2006).

Obligation means a commitment or duty perceived by an individual to undertake future actions in response to another individual (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). If individual A does something for individual B and trusts B to reciprocate in the future, this develops an expectation in A and an obligation in B (Coleman 1998). It is the same as the reciprocity of social capital (Portes, 1998). Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998) consider obligation as a “credit slip" held by an individual. They also apply the notion of ‘there is no free lunch’ to suggest the importance of obligation in an exchange between two individuals. Previous research suggests that obligation can influence knowledge sharing and exchange (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998), as well as financial capital exchanges (Coleman, 1998).

In the context of crowdfunding, an entrepreneur may feel such obligation in his/her crowdfunding initiative. If an entrepreneur receives funds from other sponsors, the entrepreneur may feel that it is a duty or commitment for him/her to give back to other sponsors by means of investing in other entrepreneurs’ future crowdfunding projects. From the reciprocity perspective, an entrepreneur can create and maintain his/her social capital by investing in others’ projects. The obligation to fund other entrepreneurs can increase social capital in two ways. First, sponsoring others can generate strong ties with other entrepreneurs, which can increase relational closeness. Second, sponsoring others can develop an entrepreneur’s reputation in the social network, which may lead to an increase in the entrepreneur’s trustworthiness. Previous studies have found that relational closeness and relational trust positively increase social capital (Moran, 2005). The higher the level of obligation developed in the crowdfunding platform, the more likely the entrepreneur will be funded for his/her own projects. Therefore, we propose

H2: The obligation to fund other entrepreneurs is positively associated with crowdfunding performance.

## Cognitive dimension: shared meaning

Shared meaning is an important element of the cognitive dimension of social capital (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). It is similar to the communication dimension proposed by Hazleton and Kennan (2000). Communication is needed to access and develop social capital through the exchange of information, identification of problems and solutions, and conflict management. Meaningful communication requires at least some sharing of context between the parties in an exchange (Boisot, 1995; Boland and Tenkasi, 1995). Cognitive dimension is focused on the meaning and understanding that individuals or groups have in common with one another. Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998) explain shared meaning in two ways. First, shared meaning comes from the existence of shared language and vocabulary, which are useful tools for communication. Second, shared narratives, such as myths, stories, and metaphors, can foster knowledge transfer to increase social capital (Coleman, 1998; Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998).

Crowdfunding is a co-production or co-creation process where the sponsors work closely with the entrepreneur in a cooperative manner and actively participate in the development of the crowdfunding project, such as testing early prototypes and promoting the project through word-of-mouth (Lehner, 2012). Most crowdfunding platforms, including Kickstarter, provide online communities for entrepreneurs and sponsors to share ideas with each other. This is an example of co-production or co-creation behavior (Yi and Gong, 2013). Shared meaning between entrepreneurs

and sponsors may play important roles in the production process. For entrepreneurs, crowdfunding is the approach to raise money, advertise products and service, and obtain feedback to improve the products and service (Lambert and Schwienbacher, 2010). For investors or sponsors, participating in crowdfunding projects can result in enhanced financial return, social reputation, and enjoyment from the success of business initiatives (Lambert and Schwienbacher, 2010; Gerber et al., 20 an entrepreneur to develop a clear project description to share with the sponsors in collective manner.

Entrepreneurs can narrate the stories about their crowdfunding projects using a variety of communication channels and technologies, such as their own personal websites, blogs, Facebook, Twitter, and the crowdfunding platform (Lambert and Schwienbacher, 2010). In a crowdfunding platform like Kickstarter, entrepreneurs can describe their projects using words, pictures, and video. In addition, entrepreneurs can provide the latest updates about their projects in the discussion forum. Sponsors can provide comments and feedback and inquire about the project process. Entrepreneurs can also communicate with sponsors through third-party social network websites, microblog, and instant message tools (Lambert and Schwienbacher, 2010).

Crowdfunding provides a way for sponsors to become a part of the community of like-minded people and share their knowledge and business ideas (Gerber et al., 2012; Ordanini et al., 2011). Therefore, sharing stories about crowdfunding projects with sponsors can effectively involve the sponsors in crowdfunding projects (Lambert and Schwienbacher, 2010). Additionally, building shared meaning helps entrepreneurs advertise their crowdfunding projects. Ordanini et al. (2011) find that it is necessary for an entrepreneur to post crowdfunding projects on Facebook, Twitter, and other social networks in order to gain visibility. Therefore, we propose

H3: Shared meaning about a crowdfunding project is positively associated with crowdfunding performance.

## Cultural differences

The impact of social capital on crowdfunding can be different between cultures (Chua et al., 2009; Staber, 2006; Batjargal, 2007; Xiao and Tsui, 2007). According to

#

cross-cultural literature (Hui and Triandis 1986), the collective culture values sharing of benefits and resources among people who are more likely to share material benefits and nonmaterial resources than their peers in the individualistic culture. People in the collective culture are also concerned about how their own decisions and behaviors may affect others. The collective culture takes it for granted that an individual contributes to the benefit of others (Hui and Triandis, 1986). These fundamental characteristics of the collective culture suggest that it may be easier for Chinese entrepreneurs to obtain funds from the sponsors. Chinese people may be very proactive in providing financial support for those in their close relationship circles and those who are in need.

Chinese culture also emphasizes Guanxi, which is defined as “drawing on connections in order to secure favors in personal relations” (Luo, 1997 P.44) and to fulfill personal objectives (Wong et al., 2003). According to the theory of Guanxi, “Ganqing,” “Xinren,” and “Renqing” are three dimensions of Guanxi, which emphasize the importance of personal relationships between people (Barnes et al., 2011). “Ganqing” means personal feeling that indicates the existence of emotional attachment among members of a network (Wang, 2007). “Xinren” means trust that focuses on the credibility and benevolence between individuals (Barnes et al., 2011). “Renqing” is similar to favor, which is often seen in the form of giving gifts to one another (Luo, 2001; Wong and Leung, 2001).

An individual can take two types of actions in a “Guanxi” network (Taormina and Gao, 2010). The first is giving unsolicited help and favors to foster favorable interpersonal relationships (e.g., helping friends). The second is seeking favors for a specific purpose (e.g., seeking family assistance). Because of the reciprocal nature, “Guaxi” is useful in many contexts in China, such as obtaining access to ‘insider information and resources, gaining contact to governmental officials, getting connected with partner organizations, and improving business relationships (Gu et al., 2008; Barnes et al., 2011). In the importer-exporter context, “Ganqing” influences cooperative behavior, and “Renqing” affects coordinative behavior (Barnes et al., 2011). Based on the above, we posit that the social capital theory may be more powerful in explaining the fundraising behavior in China than in the U.S.

H4: National culture moderates the effect of social capital. In other words, the three dimensions of social capital explain higher variance of crowdfunding performance in

#

China than in the U.S.

Further, we also propose that the impact of obligation that emerges from reciprocity exchanges, i.e., investing in others’ projects, is more powerful in China than in the U.S. First, people in the collective culture have stronger feelings of being involved in and contributing to the collective as a whole as well as to benefit other people (Hui and Triandis, 1986). They strongly believe that their own behaviors reciprocal than their American counterparts (Buchan et al., 2002). An entrepreneur’s investment in others’ projects is one indicator of such reciprocity (Fang, 1999). The entrepreneur’s obligation to fund other crowdfunding projects may gain more reciprocal investments from other sponsors.

Second, obligation is a type of economic exchange, through which the entrepreneur can be acquainted with other sponsors who can be considered “Shuren” (familiar persons or friends). This is an important part of the “Guanxi” network in China (Fu et al., 2006; Luo, 1997). Chua et al. (2009) find that the effect of economic exchange on affect-based trust is more positive for Chinese than for Americans. In addition, affect-based trust and cognition-based trust are more intertwined for Chinese than for American managers. Thus, the obligation of a Chinese entrepreneur may motivate more sponsors to invest in the entrepreneur’s crowdfunding projects.

H5: National culture moderates the effect of obligation. In other words, obligation has a stronger effect on crowdfunding performance in China than in the U.S.

## Research method

## Data collection

We collected data from www.kickstarter.com in the U.S. and www.demohour.com in China. Kickstarter is the largest crowdfunding platform in the U.S. Since Kickstarter was launched in 2009, there have been more than 5 million individuals participating on the site, pledging more than \$900 million in more than 13 million creative projects. Demohour was founded in Beijing in 2011 and was one of the largest crowdfunding websites in China.

The two crowdfunding platforms provided similar data items about crowdfunding projects and entrepreneurs who initiated these projects. Data about a project included the goal of fundraising, the duration of the project, the description of the project, and the final pledge amount. Data about an entrepreneur included the number of fans in the entrepreneur’s social networks and the number of projects funded by the entrepreneur to support others in the crowdfunding platform. Kickstarter provided the number of an entrepreneur’s Facebook friends. Demohour provided the hyperlink of an entrepreneur’s Weibo (China’s biggest tweeter website) page, through which we collected the number of the entrepreneur’s followers on Weibo.

We collected 607 projects from Kickstarter and 310 projects from Demohour. We removed several projects whose founders did not have followers from either Facebook or Weibo. We also identified and removed some projects where ratios of pledge over goal were extremely large, acting as outliers. In the end, there were 515 crowdfunding projects in the U.S. sample and 270 projects in the Chinese sample. The descriptive statistics of the projects and entrepreneurs are shown in Table 1.

## Measures

Crowdfunding performance, the dependent variable, was measured as the ratio of pledge over goal. This measure of dividing pledge by the goal of fundraising was used in other studies (Lambert and Schwienbacher, 2010). The structural dimension was measured as the number of an entrepreneur’s social network ties, which was similar to previous studies (Rhee, 2004). For the U.S. data, the amount of an entrepreneur ’s Facebook friends was used to measure social network ties. For the Chinese data, we collected the amount of fans on an entrepreneur’s Weibo page. Consistent with Chiu et al. (2006), we measured obligations using the amount of others’ projects in which an entrepreneur invested before the expiration of his/her own crowdfunding project. We measured shared meaning of a crowdfunding project as the length of the description of a crowdfunding project. Project description in the text format was an important narrative of a project that was shared with the sponsors. In addition to text, both crowdfunding sites also enabled users to upload videos and pictures. We did not utilize the data from these multimedia formats.

We also collected data for two control variables. The crowdfunding goal was measured as the total amount of money that an entrepreneur aimed to raise for a particular project. Crowdfunding duration was the amount of days from the start to the end of a project.

## Data analyses and results

All the variables were log-transformed to prepare for data analysis. The results are show in Table 2. Model 1 only included two control variables, while Model 2 included the three dimensions of social capital. VIFs for all the variables were less than 2, indicating that there was no serious problem of collinearity.

The results confirmed the significant effect of the structural dimension of social capital on crowdfunding performance (US: b=0.05, p<0.001; China: b=0.07, p<0.001). Therefore, H1 was supported. The effect of obligation to fund other projects on crowdfunding performance was also significant (U.S.: b=0.06, p<0.01, and China: b=0.18, p<0.001). Therefore, H2 was supported. The result also showed that shared meaning was positively correlated with crowdfunding performance (U.S.: b=0.08, p<0.001, and China: b=0.10, p<0.01), supporting H3.

## Discussions

This study examines the role of an entrepreneur’s social capital in crowdfunding. We have demonstrated significant effects of structural, relational, and cognitive social capital using two different datasets collected from the U.S. and China. The results indicated that structural social capital measured in terms of the degree of social network ties was a significant antecedent of crowdfunding performance (H1). This finding confirmed the importance of an entrepreneur’s social network structure (Ordanini et al., 2011, Mollick, 2014). It was also consistent with Mollick (2014), who found that an individual’s personal network helped predict the success of crowdfunding. Mollick (2014) measured personal social network ties using the amount of an entrepreneur’s Facebook fans. In a different study, Lambert and Schwienbacher (2010) measured social network in terms of the usage of one’s social networks to test the effect of social network in crowdfunding. They found that usage had a significant negative affect rather than positive affect on pledge, which was inconsistent with our finding. Besides, they did not find a significant effect of social network usage on the ratio of pledge over goal. One possible reason for the insignificant result was that usage of social networks may not be a proper measure of the size and scale of social networks.

The obligation to fund other entrepreneurs in the crowdfunding platform was found to be a significant predictor of crowdfunding performance (H2). This finding confirmed the role of reciprocity exchanges in crowdfunding, which is one of the most important sources of social capital (Portes, 1998). Portes (1998) argued that the return of the obligations developed by an individual was based on the common social structure, which was the crowdfunding platform community in our study. The

#

collectivity of the crowdfunding platform may repay an entrepreneur for his/her obligations in the form of his/her status and honor on the platform (Portes, 1998), which will lead to a high level of crowdfunding performance. In addition to status and honor, obligation can motivate those people who are funded directly by the entrepreneur to reciprocate with a greater amount of funding (Buchan et al., 2002; Barnes et al., 2011).

In addition, this paper also verified the importance of shared meaning between an entrepreneur and other participants who sponsor the crowdfunding project (H3). This finding confirmed the significant role of shared meaning, such as myths and stories, in social capital development (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998; Chow and Chan, 2008). Improving shared meaning of a crowdfunding project is an effective way to attract individuals to invest in the project (Ordanini et al., 2011). The detailed narrative of a project is an important way to communicate with the potential sponsors and involve Schwienbacher, 2010; Ordanini et al., 2011).

As for culture differences between China and the U.S, we found that the predicative power of the research model was higher for the Chinese sample than for the American sample (H4). The finding indicated that social capital seemed to be more influential in China than in the U.S. Some of the “Guanxi” in crowdfunding already exist in an entrepreneur’s real life, such as relatives and close friends. In addition, computer-mediated communication (CMC) technologies, such as Facebook and Weibo, also contribute to the development of “Guanxi” in crowdfunding. Ou et al. (2013) found that CMC technologies could mimic traditional interactive face-to-face communications to form “Guanxi” in traditional online marketplaces, such as Taobao in China.

In addition, we found that the effect of obligation had higher weight in China than in the U.S (H5). This finding was consistent with the research in “Guanxi,” which found that Chinese valued reciprocity more than Americans (Fang, 1999; Buchan et al., 2002). This finding also confirmed Ou et al. (2013)’s study of how CMC tools enabled “Guanxi” in an online marketplace. Prior research found that the Chinese culture of “Guanxi” limited the role of structure holes for brokers (Xiao and Tsui, 2007). However, the “Guanxi” culture had strong and positive impact for the in-groups.

## Conclusions

## Limitation

There are several limitations in this study. First, we measured the degree of social network ties as the amount of fans that an entrepreneur had on either Facebook.com or Weibo.com, rather than the total amount of friends in all social network communities. Further research can investigate the effects of the ties in other social network sites, such as Linkedin.com, which seem to be more professional than Facebook, which is more known for personal entertaining purposes. Second, we only used the length of the text description of a project to measure shared meaning. Text description is not the only channel to share projects with sponsors. At the two crowdfunding platforms, an entrepreneur can upload one video file for each project, which complemented text description. All the projects in our sample had video presentations as a part of the project description, so that we could not perform any analysis of how video affected crowdfunding performance, as conducted by Mollick (2014). Future studies can examine the effect of the content of the video on crowdfunding performance. Third, we did not consider other moderating variables, such as uncertainty of the crowdfunding project. Fourth, our sample size was relatively small as compared to the current trend of big data analysis that has drawn attention from the research community.

## Implications for research

This study makes several contributions to the literature. First, we are one of the first to investigate the issues of crowdsourcing from a specific theoretical perspective. Compared with prior literature that was largely exploratory (e.g., Ordanini et al., 2011; Mollick, 2014), we have conducted a confirmatory study based on a solid theoretical foundation to test the role of social networks and social capital in the success of crowdfunding. Consistent with prior literature that confirmed the role of social network theory in the traditional context, such as resource exchanges inside the firm (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998; Tsai and Ghoshal, 1998), we have found that social capital was a valuable asset in the online context of fundraising through crowdfunding.

#

We have studied multidimensional social capital and included structural, relational, and cognitive dimensions in our study. The integration of these three dimensions provides an inseparable view of social capital in an entrepreneur’s effort in fundraising (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998).

Second, we are one of the first to examine the role of social capital in crowdfunding from a cross-cultural perspective. We have extended the prior literature, which was based on the western culture (e.g., Ordanini et al., 2011; Mollick, 2014) to examine crowdfunding in the eastern culture. On one hand, we have demonstrated that multidimensional social capital had a universal power in explaining the success of crowdfunding in different countries. The empirical evidence from the crowdfunding contexts in the U.S. and China has provided complementary and robust support for the multidimensional theory of social capital. On the other hand, we have revealed different predictive powers of multidimensional social capital across China and the U.S. Because the cultural roots vary in different countries, the effects of social capital and its three dimensions were found to have different impacts. Compared with America, China is a collectivism society in which people place more importance on personal relationships (“Guanxi”). They have much higher confidence in the members (Fan and Zigang, 2004). To improve the external validity of the role of social capital in crowdfunding, future research can investigate this in other countries.

Third, we have suggested and demonstrated that each dimension of social capital had important impact on the success of crowdfunding. The role of social network ties was found to be significant in crowdsourcing, consistent with the prior literature (e.g., Tsai and Ghoshal, 1998; Wasko and Faraj, 2005). However, such a significant impact was not different between different cultures. In addition, we identified that the obligation to fund other entrepreneurs based on reciprocity norms played an important role. This is consistent with Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998), who proposed that trust, norms, obligations, and identification were important relational social capital dimensions. However, the extant research seems to be focused on trust (e.g., Cousins et al., 2006), while ignoring other elements of relational social capital. In a study of the role of social capital in knowledge sharing, Chiu et al. (2006) found that norms of reciprocity, rather than trust, significantly influenced the quality of knowledge sharing. This suggests that future studies go beyond trust and investigate other important factors of the relational dimension of social capital.

Further, shared meaning about a crowdfunding project was identified as another key factor for crowdfunding performance, although the effect was not different across cultures. We posit that shared meaning is an effective way to advertise a crowdfunding project and involve sponsors in the co-production process. While we have studied the value of shared meaning in the initiation or fundraising process of a crowdfunding project, future studies can investigate the roles of comments and feedback in other phases of crowdfunding (Lehner, 2012; Brabham, 2008).

## Implications for practice

This research also provides several practical implications for crowdfunding platform providers and entrepreneurs. First, it is critical for providers to leverage the power of social capital. Crowdfunding platforms can bridge different social networks (Lehner, 2012), so crowdfunding providers should collaborate with other third-party social networks in addition to Facebook and Weibo. Crowdfunding platform providers should motivate entrepreneurs to participate actively in other third-party social networks. We found that obligation matters more for the crowdfunding in China. Thus, crowdfunding platforms in China should motivate entrepreneurs to support other entrepreneurs. This will increase the likelihood of successfully funding their own crowdfunding projects.

Second, a crowdfunding platform acts not only as an integrator of third-party social network communities but also as an independent social network community. Providers should develop mechanisms to promote an entrepreneur’s participation and engagement in the community. The results in our study indicated that the obligation to fund other entrepreneurs had positive significant effects on crowdfunding performance, especially in the Chinese context. The providers of crowdfunding platforms in the U.S. may need to examine the strategies used by their Chinese peers and apply similar strategies in the U.S. For the leading crowdfunding platforms, such as Kickstarter and Indiegogo, which may expand their operations to a different culture, it is critical to pay attention to the impact of culture, especially, the “Guanxi” culture in China.

Third, providers should motivate entrepreneurs to share information about crowdfunding projects with sponsors in different formats. In addition to the text description of projects, which was found to be important in the current study, platforms are encouraged to integrate a diverse range of media, such as pictures and video, to help entrepreneurs tell stories about their projects to improve shared understandings between entrepreneurs and sponsors. In addition to publishing project narratives in crowdfunding platforms, entrepreneurs should also use other communication channels, such as instant messaging, to facilitate their communications with sponsors.

To summarize, we have applied the theory of multidimensional social capital to study the success of crowdfunding in a cross-culture context, in an effort to extend some of the earliest studies of crowdfunding. We have provided consistent supports to demonstrate the strong impacts of social capital in crowdfunding, using data collected from China and the U.S. We have also revealed different effects of social capital between China and the U.S.
