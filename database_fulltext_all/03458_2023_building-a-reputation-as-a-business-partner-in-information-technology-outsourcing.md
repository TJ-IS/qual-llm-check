# BUILDING A REPUTATION AS A BUSINESS PARTNER IN INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY OUTSOURCING<sup>1</sup>

Julia Kotlarsky University of Auckland Business School, Auckland, NEW ZEALAND {j.kotlarsky@auckland.ac.nz}

Suzanne Rivard Department of Information Technology HEC Montreal, Montreal, QC, CANADA {suzanne.rivard@hec.ca}

Ilan Oshri University of Auckland Business School, Auckland, NEW ZEALAND {ilan.oshri@auckland.ac.nz}

One noticeable trend in the maturing information technology (IT) outsourcing industry is the growing interest from client firms seeking to benefit from supplier-led innovations. Yet IT outsourcing suppliers still find it challenging to shift their reputation from the competent provision of a low-end service to a high-value innovative line of services, thus becoming known as business partners. We address this issue by examining the reputation formation efforts of an IT supplier experiencing a reputation deficit in terms of quality (its ability as a business partner) and intent (its intention to adopt trustworthy behavior). We develop a model based on a case study of a large IT supplier engaged in reputation formation with its outsourcing clients. We portray reputation formation as a process wherein an IT supplier alternately emits signals of quality and intent from a repertoire of signals. Our process model distinguishes between signaling at the market level, which relies on rhetorical mediums to broadcast a message promoting the supplier’s ability as a business partner, and signaling at the client level, which relies on substantive mediums such as demonstrations of the supplier’s ability to solve the client’s business problems and behavioral mediums that allow the client to assess the supplier’s intent to adopt trustworthy behavior.

Keywords: IT outsourcing, reputation formation, signaling theory, reputation deficit, qualitative case study

## Introduction

The information technology (IT) outsourcing sector has matured, moving from a cost-reduction focus toward a strategic focus. One noticeable trend in the IT outsourcing industry is the shift from transactional outsourcing engagements to suppliers assuming the role of business partners (Oshri et al., 2018; Aubert et al., 2015; Weeks & Feeny, 2008). An example of such a business partnership is supplier-led innovation, when “outsourcing partners … introduce innovative technologies and associated services” (Su et al., 2016, p. 1).

Yet, while the increasing involvement of suppliers in delivering innovation for their clients has been acknowledged (e.g., Gambal et al., 2022; Susarla & Mukhopadhyay, 2019; Su et al., 2016; Oshri et al., 2015), IT outsourcing suppliers nevertheless find it challenging to expand their reputation beyond being known for the provision of IT services to also become known as business partners. Indeed, a 2016 survey conducted by the CIO Executive Council and IDC reports that the reputation of IT suppliers as strategic partners who go “beyond effective delivery of systems and services to become consistently transparent, responsive, and trusted collaborators in creating value for the enterprise” remains underdeveloped. <sup>2</sup> Only one in ten client-IT leaders surveyed were highly satisfied with their more strategic IT suppliers, pointing to a reputation deficit.

Other research supports the survey’s findings, depicting the reputation of IT suppliers as wanting with regard to both quality and intent. In terms of quality, Su et al. (2016) question whether suppliers possess the ability to be business partners, reporting client disappointment over the seeming inability of their suppliers to deliver innovation as business partners. Concerning intent, the opportunistic behavior (Aubert et al., 2015; Dawson et al., 2010) assumed in the contractual view of IT outsourcing (e.g., Susarla & Mukhopadhyay, 2019) is reflected in clients’ concerns about suppliers’ intent to adopt trustworthy behavior.

Although the information systems (IS) outsourcing literature emphasizes the role of supplier reputation in winning contracts (Ravindran et al., 2015; Hoecht & Trott, 2006) and the positive relationship between reputation and contract outcomes (Banerjee & Duflo, 2000), it does not explain how IT suppliers can build a strong reputation. Indeed, although there is a rich body of literature on IT outsourcing, it provides a limited understanding of reputation formation, which is becoming crucial within the IT outsourcing ecosystem. Accordingly, and given the reputation deficit of IT outsourcing suppliers concerning business partnerships, we address the following research question: How does an IT outsourcing supplier build its reputation regarding its ability as a business partner and its intent to adopt trustworthy behavior?

We build on the organizational reputation formation literature (Ravasi et al., 2018) to answer our question, particularly the perspective that mobilizes signaling theory (e.g., Basdeo et al., 2006). A core assumption of this perspective is that a reputation deficit exists when there is asymmetry between the information a focal firm possesses about its quality and/or intent and that possessed by important stakeholders—either all market participants, a group of stakeholders, or a specific stakeholder such as a client (Mishina et al., 2012). To improve its reputation, the focal firm will strive to reduce information asymmetry by sending signals about quality or intent to stakeholders, aiming to influence their impressions (Salge et al., 2021; Love et al., 2017). Signals are assumed to “shape a firm’s reputation because they enable stakeholders to form impressions and opinions about its ability to create value for them” (Basdeo et al., 2006, p. 1206). While this literature suggests that “reputation formation can be broadly understood as a signaling process” (Basdeo et al., 2006, p. 1205), the process through which organizational reputation develops is still “relatively undertheorized” (Ravasi et al. 2018, p. 574).

While IS outsourcing research acknowledges the role of supplier reputation in winning contracts, it does so in the context of supplier selection before a contract is signed (e.g., Ravindran et al., 2015; Levina & Ross, 2003). Yet, in IT outsourcing, once a supplier is selected, the client and supplier enter a long-term engagement. Over the lifetime of such arrangements, new information asymmetries emerge as the supplier looks for new opportunities to expand their business offerings and gain a more significant share of the client’s business. In recent years, intense competition has pushed IT outsourcing suppliers to focus on higher-value propositions, such as innovation and strategic partnerships (e.g., Gambal et al. 2022; Susarla & Mukhopadhyay, 2019; Oshri et al., 2018; Su et al., 2016; Aubert et al., 2015; Oshri et al., 2015). However, the supplier’s reputation for competency in delivering IT services, which takes years to build, seems insufficient for them to be perceived as an able and trustworthy business partner. Indeed, the following adverse selection and moral hazard concerns remain: (1) Is the supplier an able business partner who can understand business problems and propose innovations that enrich the client’s value proposition? (2) Does the supplier intend to adopt trustworthy behaviors, as reflected by sharing the risks associated with innovation projects and remaining engaged with the client?

Accordingly, we engage in theory building based on a revelatory case study of a large IT supplier, IBM U.K. & Ireland’s Strategic Outsourcing Unit (SOU). The case is particularly relevant to the study of reputation formation by IT suppliers. As a senior executive commented, although IBM had a strong reputation for providing IT services, it was experiencing a reputation deficit as a business partner: “From an IT outsourcing point of view, I would say that we’re kind of still the plumbers, and we have to earn the right to get to their [clients’] business.”

Our study extends the IT outsourcing literature by unpacking the process of reputation formation and proposing a theoretical explanation through the signaling of quality, i.e., ability as a business partner, and intent, i.e., the intention to adopt trustworthy behavior.

## Theoretical Background

## The Reputation of IT Outsourcing Suppliers

Supplier reputation in the IS outsourcing literature is generally understood as being known for the delivery of outsourced IT services (e.g., Ravindran et al., 2015; Levina & Ross, 2003). A strong reputation is imperative for IT suppliers to secure a longterm market position to win future contracts from existing and new clients. Indeed, “most of the larger outsourcing contracts are awarded to the reputed and established players in the industry who can point toward a record of previous achievements or who have been directly recommended” (Hoecht & Trott 2006, p. 676).

Rankings of IT suppliers by consulting groups such as Gartner, IDC, and Forrester are believed to shape supplier reputation at the market level (Levina & Ross, 2003). At the same time, clientlevel reputation can develop during client-supplier interactions. For example, a client’s positive experience with a supplier will improve its reputation with that client (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Levina & Ross, 2003; Banerjee & Duflo, 2000).

While IT suppliers have a strong reputation for outsourced services, the competitive landscape has shifted toward delivering innovation and forming strategic business partnerships (Gambal et al., 2022; Susarla & Mukhopadhyay, 2019; Su et al., 2016; Oshri et al., 2015). The literature suggests that suppliers can become strategic partners when they “significantly enhance the firm’s product or service offerings for existing target customers or enable the [client] firm to enter new markets” (Weeks & Feeny 2008, p.131). However, although the IT outsourcing industry portrays IT suppliers as “world-class strategic partners who will be a source of new ideas and practices” (p. 127), clients are often disappointed with their suppliers’ innovation skills and value-adding capabilities (Su et al., 2016).

Other studies reiterate these concerns. For example, Aubert et al. (2015) argue that the traditional view of IT outsourcing services and the innovation-driven view of outsourcing are paradoxical. Indeed, traditional IT outsourcing draws on a cost-based model, and IT suppliers’ reputation depends on their ability to deliver IT services (Levina & Ross 2003). In contrast, innovation outsourcing requires credible vendor actions that demonstrate their commitment to the client (Susarla & Mukhopadhyay, 2019).

These observations bring to the fore the key challenges that IT suppliers face when seeking to build a new reputation as business partners, particularly in responding to clients’ concerns about adverse selection and moral hazard. Here, adverse selection refers to the risk of selecting a supplier who does not possess the level of ability expected from a business partner, and moral hazard is the risk that a supplier will adopt untrustworthy behaviors. These behaviors include cheating, shirking, free-riding, cost padding, and exploiting their business partner (Aubert et al., 2003). Adverse selection and moral hazard are present in many transactional exchanges (Mishina et al., 2012). However, they are particularly problematic in service-type outsourcing because clients can only determine their supplier’s quality and intent after the service has been performed (Bhattacharya & Singh, 2019). Therefore, reducing the risks associated with adverse selection and moral hazard is key to developing successful outsourcing relationships (Shivendu et al., 2020).

Mitigating the risk of adverse selection often relies on client knowledge about the supplier’s capabilities (Gefen et al., 2008). In particular, clients should “gather information about the supplier, learn about its reputation, its list of other clients or any other signal that only a good supplier could send” (Aubert et al., 2003, p.182). Similarly, increasing the level of information a client possesses about a supplier’s trustworthiness helps reduce the risk of moral hazard (Aubert et al., 2003; Gefen et al., 2008). In economic exchanges, a trustworthy partner is one that will not exploit the exchange vulnerabilities of the other party (Barney & Hansen, 1994, p. 177). IS outsourcing studies recognize trust (and the related concepts of supplier trustworthiness and trustworthy behavior) as an important concern in economic transactions between client and supplier firms. For example, Gefen et al. (2008) refer to trust as “the willingness to rely on a business partner based on its past trustworthy behavior” (p. 533) and state that “trust is the product of beliefs in the trustworthiness of the other party … and is usually defined and measured as a subjective intention or assessment” (p. 536). Studies have shown that supplier reputation in long-term IT outsourcing arrangements requires proven trustworthiness (e.g., Ravindran et al., 2015; Barua & Mani, 2014), which increases the predictability of the supplier’s behavior, implying that clients “expend less information to monitor the provider’s behavior and enforce contractual provisions” (Barua & Mani, 2014, p. 86). Trustworthy supplier behavior is therefore essential to reducing the risk of moral hazard.

Although the literature draws attention to the significance of reputation for IT suppliers and their reputation deficit as able business partners who intend to adopt trustworthy behavior, little is known about how reputation is built. Considering those issues, we turn to the literature on reputation formation.

## Reputation Formation from a Signaling Perspective

Firms seek to form and maintain a favorable reputation to gain competitive advantage and improve their financial performance (Basdeo et al., 2006). Their aim is to become known for something that reflects a particular attribute of interest or value to a specific group of stakeholders (Lange et al., 2011). Stakeholders may be interested in focal firm attributes, such as (1) the quality of products, financial performance, labor relations, and environmental practices (Rindova et al., 2005; Deutsch & Ross, 2003), as well as (2) the firm’s intentions, motives, and goals (Ravasi et al., 2018; Mishina et al., 2012). The former concerns stakeholders reputational assessment of the firm’s quality in terms of its demonstrated ability to deliver value (Mishina et al., 2012; Lange et al., 2011). The latter captures the reputational assessment of the firm’s behavioral intentions (Mishina et al., 2012) and reflects stakeholders’ impressions of the firm’s intent to behave in a specific manner in the future (Lange et al., 2011; Basdeo et al., 2006).

Organizational reputation is an intangible organizational asset (Lange et al., 2011) “reflecting a multidimensional comparative evaluation held among stakeholders” (Ravasi et al., 2018, p. 577). Organizational reputation plays a significant role in cases where the firm’s internal practices and abilities are opaque to external stakeholders, and their experience of the firm’s product or service before purchasing it is limited (Lange et al. 2011). To build their reputation, firms may engage in market actions (i.e., “actions observable to customers, competitors, and other industry watchers”— Basdeo et al., 2006, p. 1213) as well as actions that target specific stakeholders to create a favorable impression (Mishina et al., 2012). Organizational reputation is often studied at the collective level as a stakeholder group-specific assessment of a firm’s quality and intent (Rindova et al., 2005). Some researchers, however, study reputation at the stakeholder-specific level. They assume that individual members of a stakeholder group (e.g., clients of a particular IT supplier) notice the same cues sent by the focal firm, interpret those cues similarly, and form the same impression of the focal firm (Mishina et al., 2012).

Prominent in the organizational reputation literature is the mobilization of signaling theory—whether in its original, game-theory form (Spence, 1973) or as adapted by strategy scholars (e.g., Fombrun & Shanley 1990)—to study reputation formation (Basdeo et al., 2008; Ravasi et al., 2018). Both perspectives place signaling at the heart of reputation formation, wherein firms send signals—information flows— to market stakeholders. For instance, Basdeo et al. (2006) conceptualize reputation formation as a signaling process: a focal firm sends signals to observers who then form their impression of the firm based on those signals.

Signaling theory—first propounded by Spence (1973)— focuses on reducing information asymmetry between two parties. The strategic perspective on signaling theory acknowledges that because receivers are the target of multiple signals by multiple senders—other firms, independent bodies, and the media (Salge et al., 2021), including social media (Etter et al., 2019)—a focal firm cannot control its reputation. Therefore, researchers focus on the firm’s intentions to deliberately influence its reputation (Ravasi et al., 2018). Table 1 presents the basic vocabulary of signaling theory, as mobilized in the reputation-formation literature, and indicates how the terms are applied in this study.

From a reputation formation perspective, focal firms send various signals such as press releases, the hiring of highly regarded executives (Ravasi et al., 2018), and aggressive pricing strategies as market actions (Basdeo et al., 2006) to convey information cues to receivers. These signals aim to reduce information asymmetry about certain aspects of the firm, thereby contributing to the firm’s reputation in a particular area and the generation of valuable outcomes (Ravasi et al., 2018). However, not all signals aimed at reputation formation have the same impact. Firms often use a repertoire of signals to blend substantive signals (e.g., prototypes) and rhetorical signals (e.g., press releases). As pointed out by Steigenberger and Wilhem (2018), because rhetorical signals are language-based, they tend to cost the same for both low- and high-quality signalers. In contrast, substantive signals demonstrate relevant information and are too costly for low-quality signalers. Therefore, substantive signals tend to confer more credibility to the signaler than rhetorical signals and are thus likely to have a more significant impact when applied in reputation-formation contexts.

The literature on organizational reputation highlights many antecedents and outcomes of reputation (Ravasi et al., 2018), yet the “research has stopped short of offering a strong theorization of the processes of reputation formation and change” (Ravasi et al., 2018, p. 575). Given the reputation deficit of IT outsourcing suppliers as business partners and the importance of the role of supplier reputation in an outsourcing context, we undertook a study to unearth the process of building such a reputation. We studied the case of an IT supplier’s efforts to develop its reputation regarding its ability as a business partner and intent to adopt trustworthy behavior.

## Research Methods

We adopted an inductive theory-building research design to study IBM U.K. & Ireland’s Strategic Outsourcing Unit (SOU). Focusing on an SOU operating in one of the world’s largest outsourcing markets presents a powerful setting for theory building. We conducted an embedded case study of three consecutive innovation projects with one major outsourcing client of the SOU (Automotive), which allowed us to incorporate replication logic (Dubé & Paré, 2003; Yin, 2009) into the research design. Two of the three selected innovation projects were completed at the time that data were collected, and the third one was approaching completion. Table 2 provides background on these projects.

Our research is a theory-building case study relying on a grounded theory methodology (Sarker et al., 2018). To ensure methodological rigor, we followed recommendations by Dubé and Paré (2003) on research design, data collection, and analysis, as summarized in Tables 3 and 4.

The second-order themes that emerged from the first-order concepts pertain to: (1) reputation deficit regarding IBM’s ability as a business partner and intent to adopt trustworthy behavior; (2) combinations of the two components of a signal: the message the signal aims to convey (either ability as a business partner or intent to adopt trustworthy behavior), and the type of medium by which the signal is transmitted (rhetorical-event, rhetorical-press release, substantive-demonstration, and substantive-behavioral); (3) targeted receivers: specific client, other existing clients, or market; and (4) client’s responses: short-term and long-term. The corresponding aggregate dimensions emerged: reputation asset, signal of quality (ability as a business partner), signal of intent (to adopt trustworthy behavior), receiver, and response.

## Step 3: Conducting within-case and cross-case analysis

As a strategy for sense-making (Langley, 1999), we created a narrative for each project. This was complemented by visual mapping (Langley, 1999) applied to capture the timeline of reputation building, which commenced with a reputation deficit, followed by signaling (i.e., sequence of signals and client’s responses). Visual mapping also served to illustrate the components of each signal (i.e., medium and message), the target receiver of the signal (specific client, other existing clients, or market), the short- or long-term response, and the effect of a signal on the supplier’s reputation with the client. Here, a positive response from the client—such as showing interest in discussing innovation initiatives with IBM (see Appendix)—was inferred to indicate an increase in IBM’s reputation with this client. This mapping approach portrayed how reputation was built over time with the client through several innovation projects (see Figure 1).

We used these narratives and figures in the cross-case analysis to compare and contrast our findings across levels over time and across projects. Our iterative data analysis allowed us to refine our findings to move to a higher level of abstraction and then to analytical generalization (Langley et al., 2013). We developed a two-level process model of supplier reputation formation through signaling (see Figure 2).

## Outsourcing Unit Background and Case Narratives

## IBM U.K. & Ireland Strategic Outsourcing Unit

The Strategic Outsourcing Unit (SOU) is one of three main business units within IBM Global Technology Services U.K. & Ireland. The SOU’s contracts with its top strategic clients, such as Automotive, are extensive and long term, accounting for revenues ranging from millions to a billion USD and covering major IT and IT-enabled business services. Although SOU executives believed IBM could deliver business solutions beyond outsourcing services, they had concerns about how their clients perceived them in this context, as elaborated in the following section.

## Reputation Deficit: Is IBM an Able and Trustworthy Business Partner?

SOU executives expressed concern that IBM was far from having a reputation as an able and trustworthy business partner for their outsourcing clients. Indeed, the Vice President (VP) of the SOU stated it is “difficult to have the business side of the client accept IBM as businesspeople,” and accordingly, they (SOU) “need to establish credibility that IBM understands business.” He suggested that clients were unaware of what IBM was capable of, emphasizing that IBM “does not want to be known as a hardware and software company.” Rather, it “needs to show clients what technological capabilities IBM can offer for different business areas, and what IBM is doing with other clients.”

The above statements reflect the gap between SOU executives’ belief in their ability to be a business partner and their perception that clients viewed them as lacking such an ability. Other executives expressed similar concerns. As one executive explained, IBM was not perceived as a trustworthy partner when an outsourcing engagement was not yielding returns:

Typically, what happens is somebody [the client] is entering, say, a seven-year deal; the focus on cost at the outset is dominant on the client’s side. And the focus on testing the outsourcer, I suppose, and making sure they get value, tends to inhibit the partnership value, the true partnership value, of the relationship. (Service Line Executive)

We conceptualize these two areas of challenge as a reputation deficit for IBM for being known as an able and trustworthy business partner. The SOU executives attributed this reputation deficit to information asymmetry between them and their clients about quality and intent.

## Building Reputation by Reducing Information Asymmetry

The SOU sought to narrow this asymmetry by signaling IBM’s ability as a business partner and its intent to adopt trustworthy behavior. In the opinion of SOU executives, one market-level substantive signal toward establishing a reputation beyond IT service provision was the acquisition of PWC in 2002. As the VP of SOU commented:

We have to sell the potential of what we’ve got, and we have to prove … that it’s more than anybody else can do on the business side, and this is where PWC becomes effective. The reason that IBM acquired PWC is that we were seen very much as an IT company, a bloody good IT company but an IT company at the end of the day, and we acquired PWC, who basically had access to the Boardrooms. So that was probably the most innovative acquisition IBM’s ever made because all of a sudden, it gave us access to a set of business people that we didn’t even get on their radar because we were just a messy little IT company. … And that coincided with when we woke up to innovation as being something more than just lower-cost IT.

As highlighted by the VP, IBM identified innovation as a way to break out of its reputation as a provider of low-cost IT services. We now describe the innovation environment and the signals transmitted by the SOU, followed by narratives of the SOU-led innovation projects we analyzed.

## IBM SOU Sets up the Innovation Environment

In 2007, the VP of the SOU established a new role, Innovation Program leader, to promote innovation and facilitate engagement with outsourcing clients. In 2010, the position was renamed chief client innovation officer (CCIO) for the U.K. and Ireland. The main tasks became engaging with strategic outsourcing clients, understanding their business aspirations and problems, and initiating innovation projects aimed at addressing problems. The VP of IBM SOU viewed the purpose of the CCIO role as follows:

If I don’t have him [CCIO], I don’t have differentiation. So, there is an investment there that we need to make. My request to him [CCIO] is: “Don’t spread yourself too thin because everybody wants to talk about innovation. Deal with five clients and go and make a difference to those five clients. I don’t care whether they pay for your time. I don’t care about your utilization. I don’t care if the client funds you as an individual because if you can deliver one innovation project, it’s worth more than any self-funding project.”

Establishing the CCIO role sent a signal. The CCIO launched several initiatives to signal IBM’s ability as a business partner to existing and potential clients. He established a joint innovation board that included top executives from clients and appointed innovation leaders to specific client accounts. Table 5 illustrates additional initiatives at the SOU.

## Within-Case Analysis

This section provides brief narratives of three innovation projects wherein the SOU built its reputation by sending a series of signals to reduce information asymmetry, either about its ability as a business partner or its intent to adopt trustworthy behavior. Table 6 presents the narratives using the concepts that emerged during coding (see data structure in the Appendix), and Figure 1 illustrates them. Each signal has two components: the medium, i.e., the carrier of the signal, and the message, i.e., the signal’s information content. We further characterize the medium as being either rhetorical (press release or event) or substantive (demo or behavioral). The message contains either information about IBM’s ability as a business partner or its intent to adopt trustworthy behavior. The project narratives consider the client as the signal receiver, who then provides feedback by responding to the signal, be that a short- or long-term response. Short-term responses ranged from accepting IBM’s offer to engage in an innovation project, to expressing satisfaction with a proof of concept (when the message was one of ability as a business partner), to investing in the project (when the message was one of intent to adopt trustworthy behavior). These short-term responses showed that the client was starting to believe in IBM as an able and trustworthy business partner, indicating IBM’s success in progressively reducing information asymmetry and thereby increasing its reputation with this client. When a project was ongoing, a positive response from the client triggered the SOU to send additional signals. If a client contracted with IBM to provide the newly developed service, this manifested a long-term response, which was inferred as corresponding with an increase in the supplier’s reputation asset with the client.

## Cross-Case Analysis: A Repertoire Of Signals For Reputation Formation

Our cross-case analysis revealed that IBM’s efforts at reputation formation targeted several receivers: all market participants, existing IT outsourcing clients, and specific IT outsourcing clients potentially interested in innovation. The SOU’s top executives felt the business world and even their outsourcing clients perceived them as “plumbers,” in other words as merely a provider of IT services. Furthermore, IBM felt that their clients were unsure of the firm’s trustworthiness and were “testing their outsourcer” and refraining from embarking upon business partnerships.

As shown in Table 7, the SOU developed a repertoire of signals to reduce information asymmetry about the firm’s quality and intent, thereby building its reputation as a business partner. Our analysis led us to conceptualize a signal as having two components—a medium and the message it conveys. We distinguish between four types of mediums—rhetorical-event, rhetorical-press release, substantive-demo, and substantive-behavioral—and two types of messages—one of ability as a business partner and one of intent to adopt trustworthy behavior.

IBM’s signals of quality relied on three types of mediums: rhetorical-event, rhetorical-press release, and substantive demos. The message conveyed by these mediums is the supplier’s ability as a business partner, particularly its ability to understand the client’s business problems and effectively and efficiently come up with innovations to address them.

Interestingly, IBM’s signals of intent used only one type of medium, the substantive-behavioral medium to carry messages of intent to adopt trustworthy behavior. They did this by undertaking innovation projects even if innovation was not part of the outsourcing contract and they were committing resources without any client obligation. This suggests the supplier may believe that telling, i.e., using rhetorical mediums, or showing, i.e., using substantive-demo signals—are not as efficient in conveying the intent of adopting trustworthy behavior as enacting it.

## Theorization: A Two-Level Reputation-Formation Process

We theorize reputation formation by an IT supplier as a process wherein the focal firm possesses a repertoire of signals that it transmits over projects and across levels—the market level and the client level—to reduce its reputation deficit regarding its ability as a business partner and intent to adopt trustworthy behavior. Here, the market level refers to all market participants, including existing IT outsourcing clients. The term client level refers to an individual stakeholder, a specific client the supplier wishes to engage in a business partnership.

Our explanation is bounded by conceptual and contextual assumptions (Rivard, 2014). It is based on two conceptual assumptions from the reputation formation literature. First, a reputation deficit is assumed to be due to information asymmetry between a focal firm and a relevant group of stakeholders, and signals aim to reduce this information asymmetry, thereby decreasing the reputation deficit (Love et al., 2017).

## P#1 Intelligent Vehicle Narrative

To develop its reputation with this important client, the SOU attempted to spark interest from Automotive executives in innovation projects with IBM by conveying a message of ability through Dragon’s Den events held two or three times per year. In 2010, the SOU presented the Extreme Blue concept.

The concept [Extreme Blue] fit Automotive, who were looking at the idea of connected vehicles in the context of vehicles becoming smarter and more intelligent. [We got] a team to look without constraints at the future direction of the automotive industry and where vehicles are heading and to come up with some sort of advanced concept. And in this case, it was around an intelligent vehicle that could learn and could become smarter in terms of understanding who the driver is, what the journey was, and interact with its environment. (Project Leader)

To signal their intent to adopt trustworthy behavior, IBM made it clear that they would be the sponsor.

Whilst the outsourcing service is very focused on the data center and the running of infrastructure and potentially technical innovation within the data center environment, the consulting services from IBM tend to be far more aligned to the business and business around innovation. So, each time we started those projects on an understanding that IBM would invest, and it was up to a tune of about £100,000 in each case to make sure we got the best approach and the right people to move quickly. ## P#2 MIS Dashboard Narrative

The initiation of the MIS Dashboard project was a client response to earlier signals of ability and trustworthiness sent by IBM during P#1. In an informal conversation during a client innovation day organized by the SOU for Automotive, the CIO of Automotive mentioned a business challenge his firm was facing: the firm’s ability to make informed decisions. Automotive’s CEO had asked the CIO for a tool to enhance their decision-making ability by combining business information from multiple sources (within the firm’s environment and from external sources such as newsfeeds, industry forums, and analyst reports) and aggregating the content into an interactive form. The executive board could then use the tool to “feel the pulse of the business” and make decisions based on timely, accurate information. However, the CIO and his group had not succeeded in providing this dashboard.

Automotive’s CIO shared this with IBM and asked for a proof of concept, stressing the urgency. If the initial concept met their basic requirements, Automotive would be interested in seeing a more developed product. No formal contract was created, and Automotive did not commit any financial support. As depicted in Figure 1b, the SOU reinforced its earlier signals of trustworthy behavior by investing in the development of the prototype.

The SOU put significant effort into developing this prototype quickly to signal that its abilities included rapid delivery of solutions. The SOU brought Automotive’s requirements to a specialist team at the Emerging Technologies Services center in Hursley, U.K. This center employed specialists focused on emerging areas of technology and “first-of-akind projects.” Some of the center’s experts had been working on a line of technologies known as “mashups.” Mashups take different sources of data, render them in the required format on different devices, and then combine existing content and functionality in new ways by simply dragging and dropping widgets. The project team reused an existing technology as the core of its mashup to quickly develop a proof of concept (prototype). The initial prototype was shown to Automotive’s CIO, CEO, and other executives using six large interactive screens.

The client responded to this signal by expressing interest in taking the project further to achieve the scope of functionality they wanted on the MIS Dashboard:

Application of the Mashups technology to their [client’s] requirement, which was—available data any place, anywhere, with the ability for it to be quite customizable—met the need of the customer to the extent that they commissioned a piece of work to take back to the phase one. (Project Manager)

Further development for the MIS Dashboard idea involved two phases. The associate partner overseeing the SOU’s relationship with Automotive recalled a discussion with their CIO about the challenges he envisioned:

He [Automotive’s CIO] was going to struggle given the audience here were board directors and very senior people in the company [Automotive], and what he felt very confident of is that by starting with a very small (subset) function demonstrating that it is working. He would then be able to iteratively build on the capability and shape the requirement both with us and also with his peers on the board.

Accordingly, the MIS Dashboard project used an iterative approach that facilitated close involvement with the client:

We went from an investment and a long-term strategic relationship into a paid-for service-based engagement as we started to iteratively build what has become the MIS Dashboard. (Associate Partner)

This careful consideration of the challenges facing the client’s CIO and IBM’s continued investment in the project based on a paid-for service arrangement without any long-term formal commitment from the client signaled trustworthy behavior. In parallel, it was imperative to continue demonstrating IBM’s ability to the client. To reinforce this message, the project manager’s objective was “results that could be seen quickly, could be seen as quality.” As she stressed, “Because we were delivering something into the hands of those people who are at the chief executive level, we had to show delivery excellence.” Elaborating on phase one, the project manager noted:

We needed to get phase one out of the door so that the client saw the benefit and saw that we could innovate quickly. We actually took that initial demonstration [prototype] to deliver phase one in about eight weeks, so that was predominantly bringing the data in, and the only restriction for speed … was the resources we had. We had limited resources to configure and bring in the data. We brought that in, and we demonstrated it to the client to their happiness, and therefore the green light was given to bring in more data and make the structure of the dashboard that we created more useable.

Following a demonstration for Automotive’s executives, who were pleased with the extended functionality evident at the end of phase one, a second phase of development was carried out, still with no commitment from Automotive to take on the product or provide financial support. The second development stage was again followed by a demonstration to Automotive’s executives, which was well received. Automotive then entered a long-term paid-for service contract with IBM for MIS Dashboard. The project, until then informal in structure, became a typical contract project and followed IBM’s product development methodology. At that stage, a diverse project team was assembled from both IBM and Automotive personnel across multiple skill sets, including IT and Emerging Technologies. The multidisciplinary team worked closely with Automotive’s Business Transformation Office to identify stakeholder requirements and iteratively built up the full scope of the dashboard. The MIS Dashboard was developed in three months and successfully launched as a “paid service” within Automotive. As one IBM informant described:

It is available within their [Automotive’s] new boardrooms now at [Automotive’s location] and is a working, interactive service for management information that the board uses and it’s enabled not just in the boardroom setting, but it can be used on iPads or iPhones or on their desktop. (Associate Partner)

Automotive’s executives were pleased with MIS Dashboard, publicly praising the tool and their collaboration, including in presentations to other outsourcing clients at an IBM innovation day,\* sending a signal of ability to the market. This signal was further conveyed to other clients, as IBM took the core technology used in the MIS Dashboard, which remained IBM’s intellectual property, and presented it in a Dragon’s Den format to another major client (Oil&Gas), who decided to pursue it. As one SOU executive commented:

So, a project then was generated out of that Dragon’s Den innovation workshop based on the experience from Automotive and has now gone and been implemented at Oil & Gas. That’s an example of an asset being reapplied with another client whilst clearly preserving the intellectual property of Automotive in terms of what’s been developed specifically for them. (Executive Partner)

## P#3 Virtual Dealership Narrative

Given their positive experience with the MIS Dashboard initiative, Automotive’s CIO approached IBM with another challenge. As the SOU executive partner described:

Interestingly, in our current [outsourcing] contract with Automotive there is nothing specific about innovation. There is a very light-weight reference to continuous improvement, but in the relationship dialogue that’s gone alongside the service, what’s been very clear is that the CIO particularly is very innovative and transformational in his outlook, and one of the things he’s really valued in the relationship with us [SOU] is our ability to respond rapidly to a high-level requirement and then to shape that into some sort of IT solution that can be delivered.

So, the discussion [between Automotive’s CIO and IBM] arose again. This is coming from the chief executive and CIO having a discussion around how to approach this challenge. The concept of a virtual dealership. They [Automotive] already have the capability that customers can go and look at products online, learn more about them, and configure a product to their choice, but this was looking to bring that online digital experience into the physical dealership—to expand effectively that digital experience into the sales and showroom environment.

Automotive planned to bring over 40 new products to the market in the next five years. They had a community of approximately 2,300 dealers and were adding about ten dealers every month across 176 countries as well as experiencing massive growth in the U.K. market. The first challenge was how to get a new product into their dealer communities. The second challenge was how to attract new target audiences. Automotive’s CEO summarized the challenge as: “Can we convince our clients to buy the car without physically touching it or driving it?”

In response, the idea of the Virtual Dealership emerged as a business and marketing tool using highly sophisticated technology to provide a real-time experience of a vehicle. IBM already had the technical capability in the form of CRM-based analytical tools that would allow customers to look at products online, learn more about them, and configure a product to their choice. With the Virtual Dealership, IBM introduced the existing technology into the car dealership context—as a digital showroom. As shown in Figure 1c, IBM once again signaled its intent of adopting trustworthy behavior, putting together a small team comprising people from Emerging Technology Services and Interactive Media. The team spent two weeks in the marketplace to understand what technologies were required, whether it be at motor events, in dealerships, or in the retail arena across other industries. Within a month, the team had developed a proof of concept for the Virtual Dealership tool, signaling IBM’s ability. In response, the client made a partial investment (50/50) in the development of the prototype, which was delivered rapidly by IBM, sending another signal of ability. The executive partner stated:

For the MIS Dashboard, I first put skin in the game to get the prototype. It was my investment 100%. If we move forward just 12 months, the Virtual Dealership experience was a joint investment. It was 50/50 for that prototype.

Following the successful proof of concept, the project moved on to the delivery stage, funded by the client. This involved developing functionality to configure a vehicle. For example, customers could select various features to design a vehicle online. The customers would then receive a code—for instance, on their phone—they could take to a physical dealership where they could have an interactive experience on very large, high-quality screens.

Automotive launched the Virtual Dealership solution in the U.K. market in 2014. Automotive’s CIO praised IBM widely in public press releases, both in terms of its superior ability and trustworthiness as a business partner:

IBM made an outstanding contribution, helping us not only build a technical solution but, more importantly, transform the whole culture and mindset of our business towards a more digitally informed and oriented model.

IBM was the lead integrator and oversaw the project. Many of the Automotive’s teams had not previously collaborated with each other: for the first time, the [Virtual Dealership] project brought together almost every role within the company, including marketing, product design, finance, and dealer operations. IBM inspired the team, showed them what was possible, and ensured that every division was brought fully onside.

The tool proved successful beyond the U.K. market. A signal of ability was sent to the market via press releases. The following excerpt is from January 2017: “[Automotive] will roll out the VR Experience across more than 1,500 retailers in 85 markets, localized into 20 languages.” IBM expanded their service offering to include Dealership Tool Design and other services based on virtual experience and analytics for other clients in the automotive industry. In the long term, Automotive continued working with IBM on new business initiatives that allowed Automotive to use new technologies in their cars, including a partnership with IBM around in-car entertainment.

Second, reputation formation can be theorized at the level of individual members of a particular stakeholder group (such as IT outsourcing clients—Banerjee & Duflo, 2000) because these individual members “notice similar types of cues, react in a similar manner toward those cues, and hence arrive at a similar conclusion” (Mishina et al., 2012, p. 460) in their assessment of the quality or intent of the focal firm. In terms of contextual assumptions, our model is anchored in the experience of a company with an excellent reputation in one domain, the provision of IT services developing a new reputation as a strategic business partner.

Figure 2 illustrates the model. The process commences when a supplier experiences a reputation deficit. This deficit is not caused by a reputation loss but rather because the supplier is not yet known for being more than a provider of IT services. The signaling process conveys information to allow stakeholders— a particular client, existing clients, or all market participants— to assess the supplier’s quality and intent. The supplier focuses on helping clients mitigate the adverse selection and moral hazard risks associated with entering a business partnership (Gefen et al., 2008; Aubert et al., 2003).<sup>3</sup>

Market level—Signal of quality: Continuous broadcasting of ability as a business partner. The supplier broadcasts signals of quality on an ongoing basis. The signals use various rhetorical mediums, such as press releases and talks at industry events, and systematically convey the same message: that the IT supplier is an able business partner. This ongoing transmission of signals of quality is aimed at all market participants, including potential and existing clients, and seeks to develop and maintain the supplier’s market-level reputation as a business partner by reducing market-level information asymmetry in this regard.

Figure 2. Reputation Formation of IT Outsourcing Supplier as Signaling Process

Client level—Signal of quality: Showcasing and demonstrating ability to a specific client. To build a favorable reputation with a client, the supplier seeks to engage the client in an innovation project, sending signals customized to this client’s circumstances and needs. Substantive mediums transmit these client-customized signals from the supplier to showcase and demonstrate their ability to develop innovative business solutions. When this signal of quality produces the desired effect, the client responds by engaging in an innovation project. This response indicates an increase in the value of the supplier’s ability (client-specific) reputational asset.<sup>4</sup> We posit that this increase significantly reduces the risk of adverse selection the client may experience when deciding to engage in a business partnership with this supplier.

Client level—Signal of intent: Letting a client experience the supplier’s trustworthy behavior. At the outset of a project, the supplier broadcasts another signal aimed at reputation building—the intent to adopt trustworthy behavior. Here, the substantive medium is behavioral, with the supplier contributing resources—e.g., time, expertise, and technology. The project itself is an opportunity for the client to experience the supplier’s trustworthy behavior, thereby validating the authenticity of the supplier’s message and informing the client’s assessment of the supplier’s behavioral intentions. A positive response—client satisfaction with the supplier’s behavior—indicates an increase in the value of the (clientspecific) reputational asset associated with the supplier’s intent to adopt trustworthy behavior.<sup>5</sup> This implies that as the client gains more confidence regarding the supplier’s behavioral intentions, the client’s concerns associated with the risk of moral hazard risk are reduced.

Client level—Signals of quality and intent: Ongoing reinforcement of ability and trustworthy behavior. During an innovation project, the supplier reinforces the earlier messages by sending additional signals of quality and intent. The rhetorical mediums conveying a message of ability sent before a project begins are reinforced by a substantivedemonstration medium—such as the rapid delivery of working software—addressing the client’s issue. At the same time, the message of intent to adopt trustworthy behavior carried by a substantive-behavioral medium—such as the supplier investing resources in a project—is reinforced by repeating the message. As the supplier alternates between signals of quality and intent, the client assesses the supplier’s reputation until concluding that the supplier is an able and trustworthy business partner. Specifically, the signals sent by the supplier have overcome its reputation deficit, as indicated by the client engaging in a longterm innovation partnership or paid business service. Because signals transmitted during an innovation project reduce the supplier’s reputation deficit, each innovation project contributes toward building the supplier’s reputation with a particular client. This reputation accumulates as the client engages with the supplier in subsequent innovation projects. Therefore, over time, the supplier does not need to put as much effort into signaling compared to the early stages of reputation building. For example, in the case of Automotive, it took IBM several iterations of signaling quality and intent before the client contracted IBM for the second project (MIS Dashboard). However, only one iteration of transmitting messages of ability as a business partner and intent to adopt trustworthy behavior was required to secure the next project (Intelligent Vehicle).

Market level—Signal of quality: Intermittent broadcasting of ability to the market. At the end of an innovation project, the supplier uses its outcomes to continue building its reputation in the market. These signals rely on rhetorical mediums and aim to bring the supplier’s abilities to the attention of the market, which is essential for building a reputation in an outsourcing context (Hoecht & Trott, 2006).

## Contributions to Research and Practice

Our study makes significant contributions to the IS outsourcing literature and the IS reputation formation research. Our main contribution to the IS outsourcing literature lies in our theoretical explanation of a reputation formation process by IT outsourcing suppliers. We portray reputation formation as a process wherein an IT supplier uses signals of quality and intent from a repertoire, sending them throughout projects and across the client and market levels. The signals are designed to reduce the reputation deficit of the supplier by becoming known to its outsourcing clients for its ability as a business partner and intent to adopt trustworthy behavior.

Until now, the IS outsourcing literature has primarily considered the reputation of suppliers as resting on core competencies in delivering IT services (e.g., Ravindran et al., 2015; Levina & Ross, 2003; Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003). Specifically, the extant research has identified several signals intentionally emitted by suppliers to reduce information asymmetry regarding their capabilities for delivering traditional IT services (Bapna et al., 2016; Gao et al., 2010; Gopal & Gao, 2009). All of these signals—e.g., supplier experience, supplier location, service diversification, CMM certification, cloud services certifications (Lansing et al., 2019), and the presence of advisors (Oshri et al., 2018; Bapna et al., 2016)—typically occur before an IT outsourcing supplier is selected when clients are seeking “to uncover information the seller would prefer not to reveal” (Dawson et al., 2016, p. 78) to mitigate the adverse selection and moral hazard risks associated with the provision of IT services (Shivendu et al., 2020; Gefen et al., 2008; Aubert et al., 2003). Large IT outsourcing contracts, however, are usually longterm arrangements. New information asymmetries may emerge throughout the contract, mainly if the supplier develops higher-value propositions, such as innovation and strategic partnerships (e.g., Gambal et al. 2022; Susarla & Mukhopadhyay, 2019; Oshri et al., 2018; Su et al., 2016; Aubert et al., 2015; Oshri et al., 2015). This may result in a reputation deficit for the supplier regarding its ability and intent as a business partner.

Our study was designed to investigate these aspects by carefully examining how IBM has become a business partner of an automotive company. Our process model suggests that to reduce a reputation deficit in this context, an IT supplier may rely on signals to send messages of ability as a business partner and intent to adopt trustworthy behavior. These messages target different receivers—specific clients and all market participants—gradually building the supplier’s reputation as a business partner. Given that signaling at the market level is aimed at a broad audience, it relies on rhetorical mediums that broadcast a message of the supplier’s ability as a business partner. However, signaling at the client level focuses on a particular client’s business challenges and interests. Therefore, substantive mediums play an essential role at this level, with substantive-demonstration mediums allowing a client to assess the supplier’s ability and substantive-behavioral mediums enabling them to gauge their intent to adopt trustworthy behavior. Our process model also suggests that the outcome sought by the supplier—that is, a long-term business partnership—does not immediately follow the transmission of a signal. It may be necessary for the supplier to engage in multiple rounds of alternating between signals of quality and intent over several innovation projects before securing a long-term partnership. The supplier’s iterative approach gradually reduces the client’s concerns about adverse selection and moral hazard until it has formed a sufficiently favorable assessment of the supplier’s reputation to commit to a new business relationship.

Our second contribution to this literature is to research that focuses on innovation in outsourcing settings, which has largely adopted the client perspective (e.g., Susarla & Mukhopadhyay, 2019; Su et al., 2016; Aubert et al., 2015; Weeks & Feeny, 2008). More specifically, Su et al.’s (2016) portrayal of the client as “in the driver’s seat” underlines the argument that client firms should carefully navigate their outsourcing to obtain innovation from many small innovative suppliers. Small niche suppliers are portrayed as the most capable of innovation in outsourcing settings, along with a few “strategic partners” who continue to compete for outsourcing services. Taking the supplier’s perspective, our study offers an alternative view of innovation in outsourcing. Through a series of signals, a prominent IT outsourcing supplier can instill a perception among its clients that it is an able and trustworthy business partner. Our research therefore extends and complements extant client-focused research on innovation in outsourcing.

Although our main contribution is to the IS outsourcing literature, our work also adds to the broader discussion of reputation formation in IS (e.g., studies by Salge et al., 2021; Polykarpou et al., 2018; Wang, 2010). Specifically, our study improves the understanding of the effects of signals within the reputation-formation process. Indeed, our model suggests that, at least in the context of long-term outsourcing engagements, merely using one type of signal is not sufficient to reduce the reputation deficit to the extent that a client feels ready to make a decision favoring the supplier. Instead, an effective reputation-formation process seems to require the emission of a series of signals of quality and intent. These signals reinforce each other to reduce reputation deficit gradually. Furthermore, the two-level process we propose illustrates and explains how a portfolio of signals serves several purposes. Signals transmitted at the market level convey information about quality and are carried by less costly rhetorical mediums. At the client level, when the supplier interacts with an important client with whom they wish to embark on a long-term partnership, the mediums used are substantive and convey information about quality and intent. We surmise that both messages are essential for motivating clients to engage in long-term business partnerships.

Indeed, our findings highlight that in long-term business partnerships such as in IT outsourcing, reputation formation requires demonstrating intent and quality. The focus of most reputation-formation studies adopting the signaling perspective is on quality (e.g., Connelly et al., 2011), while intent remains relatively understudied. Taking a process perspective, our study illustrates how the focal firm conveys intent through substantive-behavioral mediums. An ongoing engagement between the focal firm and a target receiver is necessary to enact behavioral mediums. Such mediums can only be used in a one-on-one client-supplier relationship that provides suitable conditions for the client to experience trustworthy behavior purposefully enacted by the supplier. On the other hand, quality is conveyed using a variety of rhetorical and substantive-demonstration mediums that all carry the message of ability. Such mediums can target several receivers, including existing and potential clients and all market participants.

Although the concepts of rhetorical and substantive signals have already been discussed (e.g., Steigenberger & Wilhelm, 2018), our study provides a more nuanced understanding of such signals. First, we show that “rhetorical” and “substantive” are mediums (i.e., components of a signal), and we conceptualize a repertoire of signals as medium and message pairings that together constitute signals of quality or intent. Second, our study extends the notion of substantive signals beyond the demonstrations discussed by Steigenberger and Wilhelm (2018). While such substantive signals correspond with the substantive-demonstration mediums reported in our study, we offer a more granular understanding of substantive mediums by distinguishing substantive demonstrations from substantive mediums of a behavioral nature.

Our study also contributes to IT outsourcing practice. While clients’ expectations of benefiting from business partnerships involving supplier-led innovations are rising, concerns remain that suppliers may behave opportunistically (Dawson et al., 2010). For example, the supplier could overcharge the client for innovation services, claiming that innovation requires greater risk on the supplier side. Worse, the supplier could fail to deliver, which may negatively affect the client’s ability to improve its services or competitive edge. Our study shows that having realized that such perceptions were held by their clients, IBM orchestrated a mitigation strategy based on reputation formation by delivering innovative solutions to address the client’s business problems in order to build their confidence regarding IBM’s ability and intent to be a good business partner. Therefore, we posit that suppliers should seek ways to reduce their reputation deficit when offering services beyond the delivery of traditional IT services. While signaling both ability and intent is imperative for reputation formation, overcoming client concerns about moral hazard is particularly challenging. We therefore propose that suppliers seek ways to accommodate acts of trustworthy behavior in their repertoire of signals.

## Conclusion

We explored in this study the process through which an IT supplier developed a reputation as an able and trustworthy business partner. Our analysis of the revelatory case of a prominent IT supplier showed that to reduce its reputation deficit, the IT supplier emitted different messages of ability as a business partner and intent to adopt trustworthy behavior to various receivers, some specific clients, and other market participants. Based on these observations, we developed a process model that theorizes the formation of reputation in the context of long-term business service engagement. Our work contributes to the rather mature IT outsourcing literature by examining the process through which IT suppliers develop a reputation in a particular area of business services.
