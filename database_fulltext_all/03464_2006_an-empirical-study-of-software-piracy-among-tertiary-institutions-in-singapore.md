# An empirical study of software piracy among tertiary institutions in Singapore

Lydia L. Gan <sup>a,\*</sup>, Hian Chye Koh <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Division of Economics, School of Humanities and Social Sciences, Nanyang Technological University, S3-B2B-48, Nanyang Avenue, Singapore 639798, Singapore

<sup>b</sup> School of Business, SIM University (UniSIM), SIM Headquaters 461, Clementi Road, Singapore 599491, Singapore Received 20 August 2005; received in revised form 10 March 2006; accepted 25 March 2006 Available online 24 May 2006

## Abstract

We used a survey technique at Singapore’s three universities to examine perceptions of software piracy and to attempt to discover its underlying factors. About 500 responses were gathered from students and staff. By means of cluster and factor analysis, we were able to identify three groups that had been influenced by attitudes towards software publishers, general acceptance, convenience, and ethics. A decision tree method linked each pirate profile to demographic and computer-related variables. It showed that, while age was negatively related to software piracy, computer experience or computer usage demonstrated an ambiguous relationship to software piracy. Moreover, older respondents who used university software mainly at their workplace tended to pirate less frequently, while students tended to be pirates more often than university employees. Also Malays were the least frequent pirates in all the Singapore ethnic groups.

Keywords: Software piracy; Software policy; Intellectual property rights; Cluster analysis; Factor analysis; Singapore

## 1. Introduction

Software piracy has been described by some as the practice of ‘‘unauthorized copying’’ of a computer program. It is becoming costly to software companies as the business world becomes increasingly global and digital. A survey by the Business Software Alliance (BSA) estimated that illegal software duplication cost the worldwide software industry US\$33 billion in 2004 [6]. In Singapore, revenue losses due to software piracy was estimated to be US\$96 million in 2004, a rise of about 200% from the estimated US\$31.9 million of

2002. The piracy rate for Singapore was estimated at 42% in 2004, as compared to 21% and 27% for the U.S. and U.K., respectively.

Numerous studies have found that the unauthorized copying of software has received increased attention in recent years and has developed into a widespread problem in university, government, and business environments, as well as individuals. Recent study has suggested that software piracy is a bigger problem in the academic community than the business community [33]. Unauthorized software copying by faculty, staff and students has been identified as a key issue. Further, in a study by Shim and Taylor [32], about 90% of business school faculty members believed that their colleagues had duplicated software illegally. A significant number of studies have reported use of pirated software by university students. This applies to universities in the U.S. [16], New Zealand, Denmark [11], Finland [34],

Canada and Sweden [19], Australia [2], Hong Kong [40,25], Singapore [24], and Brunei [30].

In our study, we investigated the claim that software piracy is prevalent in educational institutions by studying the perception of the use of pirated software as well as its contributing factors in three universities in Singapore.

## 2. Literature review

It had been suggested that school is a poor place to inculcate honesty. Using data from Denmark, New Zealand, and the U.S., Fritzche and Becker found that students seemed to be no more ethical than practicing managers. Solomon and O’Brien [35] specifically noted that business students seem to worry less about computing ethics than business executives. Oz [27] observed that software piracy began before young business students became practicing managers. However, Beltramini et al. [4] claimed that students in business schools behave in a more ethical way than those in other disciplines.

A number of forces can explain the implied lack of ethical standards. Hollon and Ulrich [14] blamed it on the emphasis of analytical methods in business programs. Hosmer [15] noted that students were not likely to appreciate the group impact they made on an organization and society if they were not trained in ethical principles. However, Martin [23] found that students who had completed two mandatory university courses in ethics did not respond differently in ethical situations from students who did not take such courses. Boxton and Rarick [5] suggested that the fragmentation of education in universities did not foster moral sensitivity and growth, and that this led to insensitivity about ethical issues.

Sociologists have postulated that moral principles evolve according to the experiences that individuals have over their lifetimes. Some researchers [1,20] have attributed widespread software piracy as a result of individuals’ attitudes toward such behavior and peer norms. A study [28] has shown that students’ attitudes were significantly correlated with their actual software piracy behavior. However, Logsdon et al. [21] did not find a strong link between levels of moral judgment and attitudes towards piracy and argued that there were other factors than moral judgment that influenced moral behavior.

Vitell and Davis [39] felt that software piracy was so prevalent that it had become socially acceptable. It was observed that the most acceptable forms of ‘‘questionable’’ consumer practice was taping a record album or movie or copying computer software. Even in the U.S.

and Canada, some businesses viewed pirate copying as victimless crime, and an easy way to lower their technology spending. Some students genuinely believed it was not illegal to make copies, while some believed that it was justifiable to use ‘free’ trial versions of the actual application for a long time.

One reason for copying might be acceptable cultural norms. According to Swinyard et al. [37], copyright and patent protection are inherent values in the Western world, where property is emphasized over social benefits, whereas Asian cultures have traditionally stressed the sharing of creative works with society. Evidently, software was slow to achieve copyright protection in Japan and the Philippines [13], and it is still only ‘‘partially’’ enforced in Malaysia, Indonesia, and Thailand. Although an attractive market for foreign software firms, the lack of legal protection for software in mainland China had raised major concerns.

Generally, piracy control measures can be classified into: technological solutions and innovative pricing, distribution, and promotion; enforcement of software policy; and legal strategies (monitoring and regulation). Some older examples of technical protection were making laser-burn holes on floppy disks and imbedding counters to keep track of the number of copies made. More recently, technical-based protection has included hard disks and ‘‘dongles’’ and also copy-protection codes that prevent software from being repeatedly copied. Some studies [8] observed that the piracy act had become a ‘rewarding’ experience for those who successfully broke technical protection.

An empirical study conducted by Givon et al. [12] showed that software piracy actually boosted the demand for legal software in the U.K., where pirated software was responsible for generating at least 80% of the legal sales of spreadsheets and word processors. There, a software firm would actually tolerate piracy by choosing minimal protection, because this speeded up software adoption [29].

Chen and Png [7] examined the trade-off between pricing and monitoring by software publishers and suggested that because higher monitoring imposed greater social loss than price reduction, software publishers should focus on cutting pricing rather than spending more to reduce software piracy.

19 statements to measure attitude towards software piracy. All statements were anchored on a Likert-type scale ranging from (1) strongly agree to (5) strongly disagree. A pilot test was first carried out among 20 respondents in October 2003. After careful consideration of the comments and suggestions from the pilot sample, the questionnaire was revised and further testing was carried out and views sought in finalizing the questionnaire before administration. Finally, a survey of attitudes towards software piracy was conducted from November 2003 to May 2004. The data were collected from 566 students and university employees on a voluntary basis from the three universities in Singapore. At the time of the survey, there were only three universities in Singapore, and our survey covered all of them. Fieldwork was conducted in the form of street-intercept interviews at high human traffic locations. A screening question was asked at the start to ensure that the respondent was either a student or university employee (included academic and nonacademic staff). Questionnaires were distributed to respondents who then completed them on site. ## 3.2. Factor analysis results and Cronbach’s alpha coefficients

Based on the literature and content of the statements, the 19 variables were a priori categorized into major groups of similar or related statements. Factor analysis was performed on each of these groups. Two statements had to be removed from further analysis due to low factor loadings and inconsistency in fitting into any of the underlying factors. We are thus left with 17 statements and the results are summarized in Table 2.

To check the reliability of the six resulting factors, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were computed. As can be seen, they are all above 0.70 and hence the constructs can be deemed reliable.

## 3.4. Decision tree results

Finally, a decision tree was constructed using the Chi-square automatic interaction detection algorithm (CHAID) and SPSS software AnswerTree<sup>1</sup> [36] to explore the relationships of the three clusters with the demographic and computer-related characteristics: computer experience [PC-Exp]; computer usage per day [PC-Use]; software use for assignments [Assign]; personal software use [Person]; job-related software use [Job-SW-use]; gender [Gender]; ethnic group [Race]; age [Age]; student/faculty status [Status]; and affiliation/major [Major]. The results are shown in Fig. 2.

Decision trees can handle non-linear and interaction effects and results can be represented visually; hence, they are easier to understand and interpret. The decision tree results indicate that age is the most important demographic variable associated with the three clusters ( p-value = 0.000). In particular, respondents aged 35 and below have a higher probability of belonging to Cluster 1 (frequent pirates) whereas their older counterparts have a higher probability of belonging to Cluster 3 (infrequent pirates) (see nodes 1–3).

Result 1: Age is negatively related to software piracy

In addition, for those aged 36 and above, job-related software use is the next most important variable, with a p-value of 0.064. Respondents who use software primarily for their job have a strong association with Cluster 3 (infrequent pirates) (64.7%) and those who do not use software primarily for their job have a strong association with both Cluster 3 (48.0%) as well as Cluster 2 (occasional pirates) (40.0%) (see nodes 9 and 10). However, respondents who use software primarily for their job compared to those who do not demonstrate a relatively weaker association with cluster 3 (i.e., 48% vs. 64.7%).

Result 2: Among the older respondents, the use of university software primarily for their jobs is negatively related to software piracy

For respondents aged between 26 and 35, student/ faculty status is the next most important variable ( pvalue = 0.071). A relatively greater proportion of respondents who are faculty or staff belong to Cluster 3 (infrequent pirates) (41.5%) whereas a relatively greater proportion of respondents who are students belong to Cluster 1 (frequent pirates) (65.2%) (see nodes 7 and 8). This indicates a positive relationship between students and software piracy, at least for those aged 26–35.

Result 3a: The medium aged students are positively related to software piracy

Result 3b: The medium aged university staff is negatively related to software piracy

For respondents aged 25 and below, computer experience is the next most important variable ( pvalue = 0.004). In particular, respondents with 8–10 years of experience tend to be in Cluster 3 (60%) (see node 6), whereas those with 4 or fewer years of experience tend to be in Cluster 1 (53.3%) (see node 4). The remaining respondents tend to be distributed over the three clusters, with the highest proportion in Cluster 1 (40.4%) (see node 5). This shows that young respondents (<25 years old) who have less than 4 years of PC experience tend to be frequent pirates. However, those with more than 10 years of PC experience also tend to be frequent pirates.

Result 4: Among the younger respondents, computer experience is ambiguously related to software piracy

For the younger respondents with fewer than 4 years of computer experience, computer usage is the next most important variable with a p-value of 0.024. Specifically, respondents with between 4 and 10 h of daily computer usage are associated with all three clusters, especially Cluster 2 (occasional pirates) (40%) (see node 11) while those with low (fewer than 4 h) or high (10 h or more) daily computer usage tend to be in Cluster 1 (frequent pirates) (66.3%) (see node 12). So among the young and less experienced PC users, those with medium PC usage tend to be occasional pirates whereas both the heavy and light PC users tend to be frequent pirates.

## 4. Discussion

Some of the major causes of software piracy at the universities have been the absence of a clear software policy and a lack of understanding of copyright law by students, as well as a lack of vigorous law enforcement by academic officials. A relatively recent survey by Robinson and Reithel [31] indicated that only 64% of the private institutions and 61% of the public institutions had a software policy designed to discourage software piracy. Today, even when universities have no knowledge of an infringement, they might still be held liable if they could have controlled the activity and had some financial interest in the act. Cook [9] said that students tended to pirate software because they were not well informed about issues of hardware/software use, or did not understand the legal definition of piracy and the consequences of noncompliance. This was further supported by Eining and Christensen [10]. All three universities surveyed in Singapore have explicit software policies for their staff and students and at least two of them were found to have diligently enforced it. Other factors contributing to software piracy in Singapore included the availability of pirated software, cultural differences regarding intellectual property rights, and the strength of copyright laws. The popularity of the Internet increased the availability of pirated software. It is common to read in the Singapore newspapers of official raids on residences of piracy syndicates and the confiscation of pirated goods worth millions of dollars in local malls [38]. Furthermore, though copyright and patent protection are inherent values of Western culture, they may be firmly against Asian culture.

The Singaporean government has made great efforts to combat software piracy by providing a stronger legal framework. The signing of U.S. and Singapore Free Trade Agreement and the passing of several related bills in 2004 were aimed at tightening laws covering intellectual property and the manufacture of optical discs. Further, under the Singapore’s Copyright Act of 2004, copyright infringement is treated as a criminal offence with a maximum fine of S\$20,000 and/or 6 months in jail.

The Intellectual Property Office of Singapore (IPOS), the official watchdog agency, has joined forces with the Business Software Alliance (BSA) and local law enforcement units to launch several anti-software piracy campaigns. Measures taken are very similar to the BSA operation in the U.S.: a special hotline for anonymous tip-offs; helping businesses to manage their software assets; distribution of posters with strong anti-piracy messages; the issuing of ‘‘Say No! to Piracy’’ stickers to software vendors for computer software; and the screening of anti-piracy trailer and advertisements in local movie theaters, etc. [17]. IPOS has also been discussing piracy in schools and universities along with organizing seminars to teach businesses software management and auditing.

Recently software publishers shifted their focus to legal protection against copyright infringement. Although this solution has played a role in controlling software piracy, it was not as effective as other solutions. A number of studies [22,26] found that the high price of legitimate software was the main reason that people justified piracy acts. A study by Moores and Dhaliwal comparing piracy behavior of students from Hong Kong and Singapore found that though the piracy level was lower in Singapore, the users were still interested in low cost. Therefore, local software publishers adopted innovative pricing strategies that included negotiated agreements, reasonably priced backups and multiple copies, institutional selling, licensing agreements, and strong customer support. A program was therefore launched by IPOS to offer SMEs a maximum discount of 40% on software products of six software companies: Microsoft, Adobe, Macromedia, Autodesk, Symantec, and Borland.

## 5. Limitations

As with most research, this study has some limitations. First, our survey covered almost four pages and was thus rather long. Respondents tended to get impatient, especially when they came to the last two pages. Hence, there was a tendency for them to answer without giving much thought to the final questions. Second, our main survey questions measured the perception or attitudes of respondents toward software, not the actual piracy acts committed by respondents.

## 6. Conclusion

Three clusters of pirate profiles were identified; they are defined by factors such as: attitudes toward software publishers, software piracy’s acceptance, convenience, ethics, and property rights protection. By further combining the pirate profiles with demographics and computer-related variables of respondents, our research offered a richer analysis to explain piracy behavior than previous work. Six demographic and computer-related variables were found to have significant relationships with software piracy. Age was found to be negatively related to software piracy, while there was no identifiable relationship between computer experience or computer usage and software. Students aged 26–35 years tended to be more frequent pirates than university employees; and the Malays tend to be less frequent pirates than other ethnic groups.

This study could help policy makers in developing better strategies to protect and enforce intellectual property rights in university communities. Our study suggests that policy makers should target students by analyzing and studying the group’s behavior and how they could change students’ inclinations to piracy and imposing heavy penalties on offenders.

On the part of the universities, a clearly stated software policy must be posted with students and staff duly informed of the importance of adhering to the rules and understanding the consequences for noncompliance. Probably students and staff should be asked to sign a consent form before being issued a computer account. An on-screen warning against committing piracy could serve as a reminder when users log on or access any university software. In addition, universities can better monitor activities and work with software publishers while hiring internal auditors to conduct regular software inventory checks.

The universities should continue to treat copyright infringement as a criminal offence and warn or help prosecute offenders. For instance, universities should identify violators [3] and suspend their computer account.
