# Psychological paradox of game software trial

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Game software trial Time restriction Functionality restriction Cognitive absorption Self-control outcomes

## A B S T R A C T

Game software trial versions, as the scaled-down versions of the software through imposing restrictions on functionality and/or usage of a grace period, are often given to users for free evaluation. Such a move is intended to encourage the eventual purchase of the full version software. O<sup>f</sup>ering a trial version could psychologically in<sup>fl</sup>uence an individual (i.e., arousing emotions as joy from playing the game), thereby heightening his or her willingness to pay (WTP) for the full version software, for which the individual s fundamental nature of selfcontrol might avert him or her from doing so the psychological paradox. Considering that a game software trial version is usually restricted through the permitted grace period (time restriction) or the extent of functionality available for use (functionality restriction), this research examines the psychological e<sup>f</sup>ects of these two forms of trial restrictions on users by anchoring on the opponent-process theory. Results from an online study involving 128 users reveal that imposing functionality restriction could lead to strong negative consequences on users’ cognitive absorption but heighten users’ self-control outcomes. Interestingly, imposing time restriction does not have an observable e<sup>f</sup>ect on these two variables. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide insightful theo retical and practical implications.

## 1. Introduction

Game software vendors often provide game software trial versions (i.e., scaled-down versions of the original software with restrictions) to encourage consumers to experience the digital products before purchase [1]. This practice builds on the understanding that game soft ware, which is basically a good experience, can only be assessed in terms of its quality through usage [2]. Through the trial, a user can be attracted to the digital product, dispel any product uncertainty, and make prudent purchase decisions [3,4].

A game software trial version is usually restricted through two approaches, namely, limiting the grace period of the trial (time restric tion) and reducing the set of functionalities in the trial (functionality restriction) [5]. Research on software trial is not abundant. However, the small number of studies looks at the issue of software trial from two perspectives. The <sup>fi</sup>rst perspective focuses on economic purposes, such as increasing monetary gains without jeopardizing sales of the full version, and quantifying the network externality of software trial [6,7,8]. The second perspective is more psychologically oriented, dis cerning the consequential e<sup>f</sup>ects of imposing software restrictions on reducing users’ product uncertainty or enhancing brand belief [1]. The underlying argument of the second perspective is that if a software trial version with particular restrictive settings could yield favorable psychological responses [9], then such an experience would likely heighten the craving for product ownership [1,10].

This research builds on the second perspective by discerning the two restriction approaches on a user’s psychological deliberation of using a software trial version. We formulate the following thesis of psychological paradox that results from the trial: evaluating a game software trial version that uses diferent restrictions could produce a discerned psychological paradox between cognitive absorption and self-con trol willpower from product indulgence. Our investigation of this psy chological paradox builds on the opponent-process theory [11,12] with the idea that software trial users could face two psychologically con<sup>fl</sup>icting forces between seeking hedonic gains (cognitive absorption) and desires for controlling (self-control outcomes), which in turn would lead to di<sup>f</sup>erent perceptual assessments of the software [13 15]. The concept of cognitive absorption, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the state of deep involvement with a particular object or activity [16], is applied to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the e<sup>f</sup>ect of a game trial on a user’s psychological state through personal engagement. The concept of self-control outcomes, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the results of exercising self-control willpower [17], is adopted as the countervailing side of cognitive absorption. Both cognitive absorption and self-control outcomes can re<sup>fl</sup>ect the immediate state of mind at the end of the game trial. For example, a user may be deeply engaged in the trial process, but at the end of the trial, the user may also be able to control oneself from further indulging in the game. This is conceptually di<sup>f</sup>erent from the long-term episodic memory, which depicts an individual’s memory of past events (e.g., time, place, related emotion, or contextual knowledge) that occurred at a time distant from the present (e.g., an event happened during an adult’s childhood) [18]. From this angle, varying trial restrictions can lead to changes in the psychological paradox through speci<sup>fi</sup>c e<sup>f</sup>ects on the two psychological forces. However, the occurrence of such a process is not theoretically or empirically clear. This paper intends to <sup>fi</sup>ll in this gap.

We conducted an empirical investigation involving 128 computer game players to understand their psychological response to the game software trial. The results of the empirical study a<sup>f</sup>ord theoretical advancement in two aspects. First, the theoretical lens of the opponent-process theory is advanced by explicitly emulating the psychological paradox of two forces, namely, the cognitive absorption and the self-control outcomes, in lieu of di<sup>f</sup>erent restriction approaches, namely, time and functionality restrictions. Such investigation allows more re<sup>fi</sup>ned development of the opponent-process theory in terms of the way external interventions could signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly heighten speci<sup>fi</sup>c force(s) in the psychological paradox. Second, by manipulating the game software trial in terms of not only time restriction but also functionality restriction, we gain a more comprehensive under standing of the users’ psychological responses to the restrictions im posed on the trial version.

## 2. Related studies on software trial

Research studies on software trial mainly focus on the design of optimal trial o<sup>f</sup>ers for general types of software, such as the optimal combination of features and trial time for software samples [5]; optimal free-o<sup>f</sup>er policy for a new software product and its successive versions under the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the word-of-mouth e<sup>f</sup>ect and its e<sup>f</sup>ects on product di<sup>f</sup>usion [6]; and various qualities of software trial versions with limited functionality, limited time, or both, depending on the degree of network externality [19,20], among others. Recent studies suggest that o<sup>f</sup>ering free trial versions can increase users’ valuation of the product (i.e., WTP) [21] and purchase propensity [10] by reducing product uncertainties [21], establishing users’ product belief [1] and brand loyalty [2], and enhancing positive network externalities [7]. Increased valuation, thus leads to increased sales of the full version software [6,20,22]. Other studies on digital products, such as those on free movie broadcasting, also illustrate the ways of o<sup>f</sup>ering free samples of information goods to stimulate sales of paid content [23]. The positive e<sup>f</sup>ects of o<sup>f</sup>ering software trial versions on a user’s purchase decision also depend on how the user copes with the enforced trial restrictions including time restriction and functionality restriction [9]. Nevertheless, despite all these studies on software trial, few studies have paid su<sup>fi</sup>cient attention to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of information products, i.e., hedonic software. and the design of its free trial offers. For example, one relevant study compares users’ emotional and cognitive responses to hedonic software trial (such as computer games) and their e<sup>f</sup>ects on users’ evaluation of the product and also the trial experience with those of utilitarian software, such as grammar-checking software [1]. It finds that the arousal felt during hedonic software trial can predict users evaluation of the trial better, and in contrast, brand cognitions and pleasure are significant antecedents of trial evaluations of utilitarian software [1]. However, the psychological forces that underpin the restricted hedonic software trial and drive users’ behavior and decisions are largely unknown. This research gap motivates the current study to investigate users’ experiences and decision-making with regard to engaging in restricted hedonic software trial (e.g., game software), which arouses various countervailing psychological responses of users.

## 3. Theoretical foundation

Game software trial has long been posited as a powerful source of information because of its high level of diagnosticity [2]. The direct experience from a trial can better predict purchase attitude and beha vior than the indirect experience gained from advertisements [10]. The digital nature of game software can enable this strategy by allowing extensive dissemination of free trial copies at no marginal cost. Game software is designed to provide enjoyment-oriented experiences to users [24], with both hedonic and experiential characteristics [25]. First, such products are consumed primarily for sensory grati<sup>fi</sup>cations and a<sup>f</sup>ective purposes [26]. People usually derive fun and enjoyment from game software that can be evaluated in terms of aesthetics or sensory experience (e.g., visual and musical appeal) [24], which can ful<sup>fi</sup>ll individuals’ hedonic desires for social value, playfulness, and accomplishment [24,27,28]. Second, compared with utilitarian software [29], game software requires emotional involvement from users to achieve the unique values it brings. To achieve this goal, an individual must personally experience the game software [24]; in other words, the trial process and its incurred user experiences are critical in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing posttrial game software evaluation.

Moreover, opponent-process theory [11,12], a psychological and neurological theory that accounts for a wide range of behaviors, states that people usually experience cognitions, emotions, or feelings that act in such opposing pairs as desire and self-control. When one aspect of the pair is experienced, the other could be temporarily suppressed. However, the two opposing forces can also be felt simultaneously. For example, the introduction of one type of stimulus (e.g., game software trial) that may induce one side of the subjective feelings (e.g., cognitive absorption) can also activate an inhibitory process (e.g., self-control behavior) to help the individual reduce the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the stimulus and return to the initial equilibrium state [11,12]. Which of the two countervailing forces could prevail depends on their relative strength that is established throughout the interaction process. In turn, the individual’s subsequent behaviors will be a<sup>f</sup>ected by the result of the strength competition between the forces.

Thus, consistent with opponent-process theory, game software trial may a<sup>f</sup>ect a user’s cognitive absorption and decision on the future engagement. By contrast, rational individuals usually hold the inherent propensity to avoid the temptation to indulge [13–15]. Thus, certain countervailing forces (i.e., self-control and its outcomes) are expected to act in the opposite direction of the hedonic goal pursuit behavior. In this process, the presence of trial restrictions and the di<sup>f</sup>erent settings of these restrictions, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally time and functionality restrictions, may lead to di<sup>f</sup>erent user responses and decisions to a game software product. Hence, this study aims to uncover the underneath psychological mechanism in this process. We will de<sup>fi</sup>ne cognitive absorption and self-control outcomes in the following sub-sections.

## 3.1. Cognitive absorption in game software trial

The concept of cognitive absorption is derived from the earlier concept of “<sup>fl</sup>ow” in which people are so involved in an activity that nothing else seems to matter during the period of the activity (). In particular, cognitive absorption denotes a state of deep engagement in the present interaction with certain objects [16,30]. It represents a state of focused and pleasant experience with the object at the moment of interaction. Thus, we posit that cognitive absorption is a consistent subjective experience gained by interacting with a hedonic product such as game software [31]. Furthermore, cognitive absorption is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor of the important outcome of technology use, such as the extent of use [32]. The concept of absorption has been adopted to explain various technology-related behaviors, which include interactions with computer games [33] and web browsing at a time when contemporary IT becomes increasingly riveting and engaging [16].

Cognitive absorption can be decomposed into di<sup>f</sup>erent dimensions, which not only use the <sup>fl</sup>ow experience a basis but also extend it. The process of decomposition stresses on both total concentrations in an activity and enjoyment derived from the activity [24]. In the context of our research, three dimensions of cognitive absorption that cover the most important and essential characteristics of such subjective states are investigated [34]: (1) temporal dissociation, the inability to register the passage of time while fully engaged in the interaction; (2) focused immersion, the experience of total concentration of all the attentional resources on the interaction, which in essence means ignoring the other attentional demands; and (3) heightened enjoyment, the pleasurable aspects of the interaction that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the intrinsic interests toward the activity.

## 3.2. Self-control outcomes in game trial

In addition to the ongoing pursuit of hedonic goals, physical and mental health is also the goal pursued by people when consuming hedonic products [35]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, things that provide people with great pleasure may also cause considerable problems because these can be very costly in terms of money or time [36]. While continuous consumption increases the possibility of deep absorption, which in turn creates mindless behaviors, such as impulsive purchases [37,38], users may feel guilty or ambivalent about the indulgence that causes them to waste resources (e.g., time or money) [17]. According to opponent process theory [11,12], behaviors or psychological manifestations that are con<sup>fl</sup>icting and countervailing toward the same encounter could exist simultaneously and function against each other. Self-conscious ness may become accessible when the desire to continue with the psychological absorptive state increases; hence, self-control activities are activated [39] to avoid temporary or myopic hedonic temptations and to prevent the temptations from jeopardizing the achievement of important current goals or upholding long-term bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [13–15,35].

Moreover, self-control can be triggered by certain situational cues [40] (e.g., end of free trial) that impel individuals to reign over their desires through inner inhibitions [17]. In fact, the same person may or may not be able to resist a particular temptation depending on the current state he or she falls in [41,42]. According to the mere exposure theory [43–46], the extent to which people are exposed to a certain stimulus can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the degree of fondness toward the stimulus. In other words, more exposure to a certain stimulus over time makes people like and depend on the familiar target, and less exposure elicits more self-control and should result in less desire toward the target. For example, users may tend to increasingly favor one particular product if the commercials that display its entertaining features are repeatedly delivered to them and become more appealing over time. The game software is a typical kind of such a stimulus with a relatively high degree of entertaining features.

As a result, the activation of self-control activities results in various self-control outcomes such as refraining from the strong desire to continue the enjoyment [47]. In turn, the decision-making results will be a<sup>f</sup>ected by the relative strength between the hedonic pursuit behavior (i.e., remaining psychologically absorbed) and the outcomes of inhibitory self-control attempts [40].

## 4. Research model and hypothesis development

On the basis of the characteristics of game software trial and users countervailing responses highlighted by opponent-process theory, this study focuses on the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of time or functionality restrictions on users’ WTP for the full version of the software depending on the relative strength between cognitive absorption and self-control outcomes as re<sup>fl</sup>ections of opposing pairs of concurrent processes. ## 4.1. Time/functionality restrictions and cognitive absorption in game software trial

Consistent with the experiential nature of hedonic products [25], the degree of a user’s cognitive absorption throughout the game software trial is expected to be dependent on the continuity and smoothness of the trial process, which can create the <sup>fl</sup>ow experience for a user [16,31]. In the current context of a restricted trial, the restrictions on time or functionality or both exert a terminating or intrusive e<sup>f</sup>ect on the trial process and therefore in<sup>fl</sup>uence the degree of cognitive ab sorption. In particular, time restriction and functionality restriction are expected to be negatively related to cognitive absorption on these three dimensions: temporal dissociation, focused immersion, and heightened enjoyment.

On the one hand, when time restriction is high (e.g., 15-day free trial), a user is less likely to possess ample time to experience the game software and develop a sense of involvement compared with when time restriction is low (e.g., 90-day free trial). Deeply engaging with virtual entertainment necessitates su<sup>fi</sup>cient time to develop familiarity and emotional attachment [16,40]. To reach the state of temporal dissocia tion when a user forgets about the passage of time or the state of focused immersion when a user becomes so focused on the game that he or she ignores all the other attentional demands [34], the user must be allowed to have enough time to develop such level of involvement in the game trial. Therefore, if time restriction is high (e.g., 15-day free trial), the user may not have reached the state of temporal dissociation or focused immersion before the trial is terminated. This painful interruption, in turn, leads to less intrinsic enjoyment perceived by the user because he or she does not have enough time to derive and savor pleasure from the product completely [34]. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H1. The extent of time restriction imposed on a game software trial version is negatively related to a software trial user’s degree of cognitive absorption with the software, which consists of (a) temporal dissociation, (b) focused immersion, and (c) heightened enjoyment.

On the other hand, high functionality restriction $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } } ,$ , only one or two levels of the game are available for trial or critical tools/weapons are disabled) leads to more fractured gaming experience compared with low functionality restriction $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ restricting only a few advanced features, such as online upgrade). Under a high functionality restriction setting, a user’s game-playing process may be frequently disrupted or the game cannot be played anymore after passing certain levels. As a result, it is hard for a user to develop a deep involvement with the game when only a limited part of the game is playable [34]. The user may give up the trial completely or continue to play the game after a while and try to circumvent the obstacles set by the functionality restrictions if he or she is really interested in the game. However, the smoothness of the trial process is disrupted into fractured pieces by the disabled critical functionalities, which prevent the formation of the state of time dissociation and focused immersion [34]. As a consequence, the user can barely enjoy the game continuously wholeheartedly without frustration since the experience is impaired from time to time. Therefore, heightened enjoyment will also be lower when more functionalities are disabled [34]. Hence, we hypothesize the following:

H2. The extent of functionality restriction imposed on a game software trial version is negatively related to a software trial user’s degree of cognitive absorption with the software, which consists of (a) temporal dissociation, (b) focused immersion, and (c) heightened enjoyment.

## 4.2. Time/functionality restrictions and self-control outcomes in game software trial

When building the experience through the game software trial and forming a certain degree of cognitive absorption, unpleasant feelings (e.g., withdrawal symptom) may occur when the trial is disturbed or even ceased because of time restriction or functionality restriction or both [17]. Basically, we argue that a tighter restriction setting, which implies an earlier termination or more frequent disruptions due to the unavailability of key functions, will o<sup>f</sup>er the user more chances to step back and re<sup>fl</sup>ect on himself or herself [39], which in turn results in higher chances for him or her to exercise self-control willpower if the user realizes that he or she is at the edge of indulgence and such a state may do harm to him or her $[ 1 3 - 1 5 , 3 5 ]$ . For example, a limited period for a trial can remind a user of other important personal activities right at the end of the trial: the moment at which the user is still able to escape from the pleasurable gaming experience without too much pain. A longer period of free trial provides the opportunity for a user to be absorbed by the game before the user becomes aware of his or her own situation in real ${ \mathrm { l i f e } } ,$ and the user exercises self-control to refrain from further indulgence. Similarly, self-control activities, such as drawing oneself out of the hedonic consumption state, are more likely to emerge if the trial is interrupted for multiple times due to disabled key com ponents or functionalities $( \mathbf { e . g . }$ , high functionality restriction) [40]. On this basis, high restrictions (time or functionality or both) in the trial demoralize a user for further exploration and provide an opportunity for self-control to prevent excessive use. At the same time, the user may be suspicious of the quality and attractiveness of the game because the trial restrictions have deprived him or her of the opportunity to eval uate it completely. This situation may also contribute to users’ self control and in turn, leads to various self-control outcomes such as re duced desire toward the game. From the opponent-process theory, the two countervailing or competitive forces of cognitive absorption and self-control outcomes can coexist throughout a particular encounter [11,12]. Thus, we assume the existence of an independent relationship between these forces and mainly examine their opposite e<sup>f</sup>ects on decision-making, which may be subject to di<sup>f</sup>erent trial restriction settings. Thus, we hypothesize:

H3. The extent of time restriction imposed on a game software trial version is positively related to a game software trial user’s self-control outcomes.

H4. The extent of functionality restriction imposed on a game software trial version is positively related to a game software trial user’s selfcontrol outcomes.

## 4.3. Willingness to pay as the consequence of conflict

In the context of game software trial, vendors will expect that the trial experience could lead to post-trial purchases. When consuming hedonic products, an individual is likely to develop the desire for or even dependence on the products $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } } ,$ , game software) or related objects $( \mathbf { e . g . }$ ., weapons in a war game or costumes for an avatar) and even has impulsive behaviors, such as imprudent purchases [40]. Therefore, we apply the concept of WTP to measure users’ post-trial product evaluation and purchase propensity. WTP refers to the amount that an individual is willing to pay to acquire goods or services [48]. Higher WTP is associated with higher purchase propensity and vice versa. A user’s WTP for a product re<sup>fl</sup>ects both the perceived value of the product (e.g., playability of the game) and the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce $( \mathbf { e . g . }$ , cessation of enjoyment after free trial) involved in abandoning the product [48]. On the basis of the value judgment, users will decide on the amount of monetary value they will assign to the desired product [49]. Furthermore, the higher the WTP is after a free trial period, the more likely game software vendors can achieve pro<sup>fi</sup>ts through such a marketing campaign. Therefore, we propose that a user’s WTP is an important measure of a game software trial experience [18,50].

Moreover, WTP is a result of the con<sup>fl</sup>ict between the propensity to satisfy the desire and the outcomes of self-control [51]. On the one hand, traditional entertainment theories, such as the mood management theory [51], argue that individuals are hedonically motivated to engage in enjoyable situations to amplify pleasure [52]. When an individual becomes cognitively absorbed, the logical integration of thoughts, feelings, and experiences can be suspended, and consciousness can be reduced [53]. Therefore, deeply absorbed individuals will eagerly pursue hedonic goals (e.g., acquiring the game software at a high price) even when these goals are considered as impulsive and irrational [38,39,54]. From the above discussion, we hypothesize the following:

H5. A software trial user’s degree of cognitive absorption with a game software trial version is positively related to his or her WTP for the full version of the game software.

On the other hand, the self-control e<sup>f</sup>ort that helps an individual to overcome a temptation [35] $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . , } }$ sensory enjoyment from game software engagement) can counteract the e<sup>f</sup>ects of seeking pleasure. In other words, self-control outcomes, which can assist in sophisticated thinking, will compete with cognitive absorption, which raises signi<sup>fi</sup>cant hedonic desires, to in<sup>fl</sup>uence subsequent decision-making. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H6. The outcomes of a software trial user’s self-control toward a game software trial version are negatively related to his or her WTP for the full version of the game software.

Overall, by considering the hypothesized path relationships among the independent variables (IVs; i.e., time and functionality restrictions), mediator variables (cognitive absorption and self-control outcomes), and dependent variable (DV; i.e., WTP), we propose that lower time and functionality restrictions result in the higher WTP of the users through the mediation of both cognitive absorption and self-control outcomes.

## 5. Research methodology

To test the proposed research model, we conducted an online survey through several leading gaming forums. We chose computer games or computer-based games<sup>1</sup> as the target product because their feature of interactivity places a strong emphasis on a user’s participation in the game-playing process to achieve hedonic values [55]. On the basis of various reasons (e.g., desire to change the world and experience fantasy), playing computer games has been recognized as an undertaking that provides self-ful<sup>fi</sup>lling values to the users and encourages prolonged use to obtain more fun [56]. By contrast, the negative e<sup>f</sup>ects of computer game playing have also been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed to be regulated through such measures as self-control [17]. We focused on o<sup>fl</sup>ine computer games that did not require compulsory network connections with peer users to rule out any confounding e<sup>f</sup>ects of social network in<sup>fl</sup>uences.

The survey focused on the game software trial experience of survey respondents to examine their true behaviors. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we recruited survey participants through several major gaming forums using the following requirements:

First, participants should have played the free trial version of any computer game. The free trial version is desktop based and can be evaluated o<sup>fl</sup>ine. Post-trial purchase of the computer game refers to buying the game (e.g., a set of DVDs or game programs available for online download) instead of other types of purchase, such as paying an online subscription fee. Second, the free trial version of the chosen computer game must have been played within the current month only. Most importantly, survey questions must be answered based on the participant’s true experience at a point close to the end of the free trial.

Because the number of users of the online gaming forums can reach a few million, we decided not to send recruitment email or message to each of them but allowed them to opt in for the survey. Participants in these forums, where we posted the survey recruitment notice, could attend the survey if they meet the above criteria. We let each partici pant report their own game trial experience based on the game they have tried instead of forcing them to try the same game to avoid the bias that some participants are not interested in the game we might assign them. To reduce the confounding e<sup>f</sup>ects of examining di<sup>f</sup>erent game products, we requested participants to specify the product name, free trial restrictions, the period of trial, purchase decisions, and product price for further manipulation checks

## 5.1. Measures

In this study, the degrees of time and functionality restrictions were determined by asking each participant to describe the restrictions of their computer game trial. Thereafter, we categorized each description into three levels, namely, absent, low, and high, to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the di<sup>f</sup>erent degrees of time and functionality restrictions. Cognitive absorption was represented by three of its dimensions, which were treated as re<sup>fl</sup>ective variables in this study using veri<sup>fi</sup>ed questions from prior studies. Selfcontrol outcomes were measured by applying the reverse representative items that described the state of the uncontrollable desire of the in: dividual (e.g., depression as the trial ceases) toward the object upon the end of the trial. These outcomes were consistent with our assumption on the close relationship between self-control and the resultant desire. Hence, higher values for the self-control outcome indicated relatively worse self-control outcomes. As we focused on the end state of the game trial, measuring the outcomes of self-control was more relevant than measuring those of self-control behavior or activity itself. WTP was measured by directly asking the participants how much they are willing to pay for the game they have just tried. Considering that we asked participants to report di<sup>f</sup>erent game products that they played with, we use the formula (WTP−Market Price)/Market Price to represent WTP by adjusting the e<sup>f</sup>ect of di<sup>f</sup>erent market prices for di<sup>f</sup>erent game products [57]. In our case, WTP has an exact number of percentage (rather than the categories of 0%, 10%,… to 125%) and can be either negative or positive depending on whether a participant was willing to pay less or more than the market price, which also extended the insights of the measurement used in [57].

To enhance validity, one unlabeled and one labeled sorting sessions were conducted with postgraduate students (eight for each session). Minor modi<sup>fi</sup>cations were implemented to address the concerns raised by the judges and to make the questions easy to understand. Table 1 shows the measurements for each construct.

## 6. Data analysis and results

## 6.1. Manipulation checks

Manipulation checks were performed to verify the self-reported game trial titles, restrictions, and prices. We veri<sup>fi</sup>ed the restrictions and price of each game trial through the o<sup>fi</sup>cial websites. We deleted the data set when the reported game turned out to be a free or online game. The titles of the games reported were highly diverse, and several examples included Age of Empires, Brian Lara Cricket, Burger Rush, Command & Conquer, Diner Dash, Feeding Frenzy, Fish Tycoon, Insaniquarium, Lemonade Tycoon, and Warcraft, to name a few. Survey responses, which indicated that the latest time when the participant played the trial game was more than 1 month ago, were also discarded. Seven graduate students from our department were recruited to screen independently all the restriction descriptions and classify these de scriptions into three categories (absent, low, and high restrictions, re ferred to as 1, 2, and 3, respectively). The most common examples for relatively lower time restriction included 30-day, 60-day, or 90-day trial; relatively higher time restriction is represented by 1-day, 5-day, or 10-day trial. The size of the game also contributed to the variation of time restrictions across di<sup>f</sup>erent games (e.g., simple casual games with short trial period and more complex games with more days for trial). The typical cases for the low functionality restrictions were limited character numbers, limited advanced scenarios, or selected set of re sources (e.g., coins). By contrast, under the high functionality restric tions, the users could only play one or two levels of the entire game or had to contend with a situation where many more important functions were disabled (e.g., weapons, maps, and resources). We decided to discard the data sets that were di<sup>fi</sup>cult to classify, on which the seven judges, after consulting the authors, could not reach an agreement in terms of level. As a result, all the judges agreed with the categorization of the trial restriction levels for the datasets we retained. A total of 128 valid datasets were retained after veri<sup>fi</sup>cation. Seven treatment groups were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in terms of the combination of time and functionality restrictions (Table 3). The missing combinations included the cases where both restrictions were absent, which were theoretically not available for software trials, and were of high level, which may not be reasonable for vendors to provide. From Table 3, we can identify that the top four most popular trial restrictions chosen by game software vendors in our sample were high time and absence of functionality restrictions (participant number = 34), high time and low functionality restrictions (participant number = 24), low time and absence of func tionality restrictions (participant number = 22), and absence of time and high functionality restrictions (participant number = 20). The un even distribution of the sample indicated that several combinations of the restrictions were more common or popular than others, which was a practical phenomenon that re<sup>fl</sup>ected the industry norms. Tables 2 and 4 showed the demographic information and descriptive statistics for the research constructs, respectively.

## 6.2. Testing the measurement model

The measurement model was evaluated by examining the convergent validity and discriminant validity of the research instrument using SPSS and PLS. Before the tests were conducted, we examined the possibility of common method bias because the data were collected from a single source [38]. Given that we only obtained 128 data points, we assessed the adequacy of the sample size using the Kaiser-Meyer Oklin (KMO) measure, which stipulates that a KMO value of 0.70 and higher indicates that factor analysis would be e<sup>f</sup>ective regardless of the sample size. The results indicated that the KMO value of our data was 0.83. From this result, we conducted the Harman’s one-factor test [38]. The resulting principal component factor analysis yielded four factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0, which accounted for 78.4% of the variances. The highest covariance explained by the <sup>fi</sup>rst (largest) factor was 22.5%. This result suggested that the common method e<sup>f</sup>ects were less likely to a<sup>f</sup>ect the results. Hence, we believed that the common method bias was not a serious concern in the current study. We also conducted a multicollinearity test by assessing the variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factors (VIFs) and tolerance values of the constructs. In general, the common rule for the presence of multicollinearity is that VIFs should be higher than 10 or that the tolerance values should be less than 0.1. Ou results indicated that the highest VIF was 1.674, which was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower than the benchmark value of 10, and the lowest tolerance value was 0.543, which was higher than the benchmark value of 0.1. Thus, multicollinearity did not appear to be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant problem.

## 6.3. Hypothesis testing

After establishing the validity of the measures, we assessed the structural paths in the research model by applying the ANOVA tests and regression analysis in the SPSS 16.0 package<sup>2</sup> for hypothesis testing, and we conducted all statistical tests at the 5% signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level.

First, to understand the e<sup>f</sup>ects of trial restrictions on each of the three dimensions of the cognitive absorption construct rather than the aggregated measure, three sets of one-way ANOVA showed that variation in the time restriction level only a<sup>f</sup>ected the degree of temporal dissociation of the participants (F(2, 125) = 4.43, $p < 0 . 0 1 4 )$ Participants who faced low-level time restrictions experienced a higher degree of temporal dissociation than those who faced high-level time restrictions (mean di<sup>f</sup>erence/MD = 0.76026, SE = 0.29317, $\underline { { p } } ~ < ~ 0 . 0 3 4 )$ . By contrast, the variation in the levels of functionality restrictions a<sup>f</sup>ected the degree of temporal dissociation (F(2, 125) = 8.235, $p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 0 )$ , focused immersion (F(2, 125) = 3.218, $p \ : < \ : 0 . 0 5 )$ , and heightened enjoyment (F(2, 125) = 4.384, p < 0.014). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, temporal dissociation level of participants was higher when functionality restriction was absent than when the restriction was low or high (MD = 0.7381, SE = 0.27625, p < 0.026; MD = 1.34921, SE = 0.37158, p < 0.002). Focused immersion of participants was higher when functionality restriction was absent than when the restriction was low (MD = 0.59226, SE = 0.24649, $p \ : < \ : 0 . 0 5 )$ Heightened enjoyment in the game trial was higher when functionality restriction was absent than when the restriction was low (MD = 0.73909, SE = 0.27918, p < 0.028). Overall, the results con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed our hypothesis that the increase in trial restriction levels would lead to a lower degree of cognitive absorption. In general, when time restrictions were low or when functionality restrictions were absent, participants reported a higher degree of cognitive absorption. Functionality restriction also a<sup>f</sup>ected more dimensions of cognitive

absorption than time restriction.

## 7. Discussions

This study advocates the psychological paradox of a game software trial. The focus is on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of both time and functionality restrictions on users’ cognitive absorption and self-control outcomes and the subsequent purchase decision. Table 11 summarizes the hypothesis testing results.

The results revealed that both time and functionality restrictions had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative relationships with several and with all the three dimensions of the cognitive absorption construct (H1 was partially supported and H2 was supported). In particular, time restriction negatively a<sup>f</sup>ected temporal dissociation only. Among the dimensions of cognitive absorption, temporal dissociation is most directly associated with the consecutive usage of the game over a longer period of time. By contrast, functionality restriction exerted a negative e<sup>f</sup>ect on temporal dissociation, focused immersion, and heightened enjoyment. The supported results suggested that time and functionality restrictions could be disadvantageous to the game software trial experience because these restrictions can disrupt a smooth, continuous, engaging, and pleasant game-playing experience. Moreover, the negative e<sup>f</sup>ects of functionality restriction were relatively stronger than those of time restriction because more dimensions of cognitive absorption were in-<sup>fl</sup>uenced. This result implied that functionality restriction posed more frustration to users during the trial because they had to stop the evaluation at times when restrictions of various functions were en countered. Therefore, users were less likely to be in a state of deep cognitive absorption after the trial. By contrast, time restriction had a less negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the formation of cognitive absorption than functionality restriction because users could at least experience a con tinuous trial process during the allowed time period. This result was also consistent with the answer to the question “Which of the two restrictions of the computer game trial is more undesirable to you?” in which the percentage for functionality restriction (44.4%) was slightly higher than that for time restriction (42.5%).

## 7.1. Limitation and future research

Before discussing the implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the limitations that circumscribe their interpretation must be acknowledged. First, both the respondents and the setting must be considered in relation to the external validity of the results [60]. In this study, we targeted a general population comprising both students and working professionals from various industries. The results showed that the student respondents with di<sup>f</sup>erent education levels constituted 51.2% of all the respondents, and the ages of 44.4% of the respondents were below 19 years. However, computer games were more likely to be popular among the younger generations (particularly adolescents) than among the older ones. Although the average age of computer game users was increasing in recent years, we treated responses from the younger participants as valid. We also revised the questionnaire several times before conducting the survey to make it clear and easy to read. The other half of the respondents came from a wide range of industries, which could enhance the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Therefore, the limitation of the student sample as part of the respondents had been minimized. Increasing the proportion of the adult respondents in future studies will be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for verifying the e<sup>f</sup>ects of the age factor.

Second, the setting of this study was restricted to the scope of the free trial versions of computer games, which was perceived as a typical type of game software and possesses a high level of diagnosticity [1]. However, whether the results of this study can be extended to othe types of game software (e.g., products that involve social interactions) was uncertain. Further extensions of this line of research are necessary to identify the boundary conditions of the theoretical model [16].

Third, the survey design of the recalled responses ensured that all the respondents were interested in the respective computer games that they had tried, which enabled us to scrutinize their actual responses and decisions. However, such survey design may have caused biases, such as bad recall and diverse game characteristics. Causalities may be di<sup>fi</sup>cult to infer from the results because of the cross-sectional nature of the survey. Future studies can consider longitudinal experimental approaches to shorten the interval between trial experience and purchase decision, and divide the measurements into di<sup>f</sup>erent time slots during or immediately after the trial activity.

## 7.2. Theoretical implications

This research o<sup>f</sup>ered several implications for theory. First, hedonic consumption has been a popular inquiry concept in multiple research streams (e.g., marketing, consumer behavior, and psychology) ([4,61–64]) but with few works done on game software trial setting despite its commercial prevalence and importance. This study not only <sup>fi</sup>lled in this void but also advanced the psychological response to product trial by proposing the concept of psychological paradox of trial. For the former, this study extended the line of software trial studies from functional to hedonic products $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . , } }$ computer game) [1] and developed theoretical predictions on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of trial restrictions on the subjective experience and purchase decision of users involved in game software trial. For the latter, the results revealed di<sup>f</sup>erentiated e<sup>f</sup>ects of functionality (versus time) restrictions on a user’s psychological paradox of cognitive absorption and self-control outcomes. Such <sup>fi</sup>ndings were in a startling contrast with the bulk of extant works on software trial that either examined the general purposes of having trial versions [1] or the fundamental bene<sup>fi</sup>ts derived from using software trial, such as reducing product uncertainty [21]. To this end, this study highlighted that software trial (as in the case of game software trial) could also induce psychological dilemma (other than the projected economic well-being and reduced product uncertainty) — a consideration that future works need to consider.

Second, by anchoring on the opponent-process theory, this study examined the e<sup>f</sup>ects of trial restrictions on the countervailing factors of the psychological responses (i.e., cognitive absorption and self-control outcomes) of the users. By doing so and through the empirical validation, this research highlighted that contradictory psychological reactions, as suggested by the opponent-process theory [11,12], may di<sup>f</sup>er even within a speci<sup>fi</sup>c setting, as in a game software trial. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the results revealed that while the psychological paradox could be ob served because of the di<sup>f</sup>erence in functionality restrictions entailed in a game software trial version, the same psychological paradox might not be observed because of the di<sup>f</sup>erence in time restrictions. To this end, this research added to the theoretical development of the oppo nent-process theory by delineating a conditional boundary of the theory.

Third, we incorporated the three dimensions of cognitive absorp tion, proposed by Agarwal and Karahanna [16], as re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators. By doing so, we gained a re<sup>fi</sup>ned understanding of cognitive absorption by identifying the relative importance of each of its sub-constructs. This importance could be re<sup>fl</sup>ected in two perspectives: (1) time and functionality restrictions had di<sup>f</sup>erent in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the three dimensions of cognitive absorption and (2) the three dimensions of cognitive absorption had di<sup>f</sup>erent in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the outcome of the game software trial (i.e., WTP). We determined the important roles of temporal dissociation, focused immersion, and heightened enjoyment dimensions of the cognitive absorption as mediating factors between trial restrictions and WTP. Theoretically, this result had identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the most critical cognitive absorption dimensions in this unique scenario and distinguished them from the rest.

Fourth, we found out that trial restrictions in<sup>fl</sup>uenced WTP through cognitive absorption and self-control outcomes; however, their in<sup>fl</sup>uence through self-control outcomes proved contrary to our proposition. The opposite e<sup>f</sup>ects of the trial restrictions on WTP through cognitive absorption and self-control outcomes illustrated the importance of de termining an optimal setting for the trial restrictions. By identifying the relative importance of the various signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationships among the factors, we managed to comprehend the best setting of time and functionality restrictions in terms of maximizing WTP of users. The proposed ideal restriction setting not only corresponded to our theoretical hypotheses but also enriched our understanding of the e<sup>f</sup>ects of the di<sup>f</sup>erent combinations of trial restrictions according to rigorous theoretical derivation and multiple statistical analyses.

## 7.3. Practical implications

The study has practical implications. First, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the importance of free trial and guidelines for designing the restrictions of game software to attract users. Contrary to the common practices in the computer game industry, we suggest that the setting of no time restriction and low functionality restriction performed best in a<sup>f</sup>ecting a user’s cognitive absorption with the game and in turn leading to the success of the free game trial (in terms of WTP). Computer game vendors can utilize this setting to create a most pleasurable and engaging trial experience for the users in the game trial and in turn enhance their WTP for the full version of the game software. Second, self-control is, in fact, bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to game software vendors because it has a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on a user’s purchase decision, and we suggest practitioners determine a balance point between cognitive absorption and self-control by ad justing the design of time and functionality restrictions and providing necessary interventions to enhance a user’s game-playing experience. For example, the absence of time restriction and low functionality restriction conditions can enhance a user’s cognitive absorption with the game trial but reduce the user’s self-control, which may, in turn, reduce the user’s WTP for the game software. The general public may have the concern about the negative e<sup>f</sup>ects of excessive play with the game software and treat the game trial campaigns of software vendors as trying to attract more users to play the games. Software vendors can provide additional assistance or promotional information to the user to remind them of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of limiting their game playing in order not to a<sup>f</sup>ect their normal daily lives. Such a balanced approach of exercising the game trial could help a user make better judgment and evaluation of the product and could further lead to a rational decision, which could bene<sup>fi</sup>t the game software providers as well. The two dimensions of practical implications thus can capture the potential ben e<sup>fi</sup>ts to game software vendors in a sense of both facilitating pleasant game trial play for better sales outcomes and helping a user to prevent excessive gameplay for better personal life management.

## 8. Conclusion

Game software has increasingly become a popular recreational component of the daily lives of people. This study focused on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of the time and function restrictions for the free trial of such digital products on the subsequent responses and purchase decision of users. We expect that this study will contribute to the theoretical development and provide useful practical implications. From the theoretical perspective, a new research model has been established, which can be utilized to explain the underlying user behavior and decision-making in the free trial period of engaging with the game software. The attempt has gone beyond extant studies by emphasizing the psychological process and the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of the free trial restrictions — the psychological paradox. From the practical perspective, we believe that the results of this study can provide insights that could guide computer game vendors in conducting their free trial campaigns more e<sup>f</sup>ectively and in increasing the WTP of the users.
