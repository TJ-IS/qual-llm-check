# Learning and self-disclosure behavior on social networking sites: the case of Facebook users

Rui Chen<sup>1</sup> and Sushil K. Sharma<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Miller College of Business, Ball State University, Muncie, U.S.A.

## Abstract

This paper studies Facebook users’ learning-based attitude formation and the relationship between member attitude and self-disclosure. Through the theoretical lens of learning theories, we recognize the key antecedents to member attitude toward a social networking as stemming from classical conditioning, operant conditioning, and social learning-related factors. In addition, we explore the underlying process through which member attitude affects self-disclosure extent and theorize the mediating role of site usage rate on the relationship between attitude and self-disclosure extent. Analysis of 822 survey data results provides strong support for the role of learning theories in explaining Facebook members’ attitude development. The results also confirm a significant, partial mediating effect of site usage rate. A series of post-hoc analyses on gender difference further reveal that attitude formation mechanisms remain constant between male and female Facebook users; gender difference exists on the association between attitude and self-disclosure extent and the association between site usage rate and self-disclosure extent; and the mediating effect of site usage rate exists in male user group only. Our research, therefore, contributes to the literature on social networking sites, as well as providing behavioral analysis useful to the service providers of these sites. European Journal of Information Systems advance online publication, 12 November 2013; doi:10.1057/ejis.2013.31

Keywords: social networking sites; learning theories; mediation; attitude; self-disclosure; gender difference

## Introduction

Social networking sites allow members to create personal pro<sup>fi</sup>les, list friends and contacts, and retrieve information about others (Boyd & Ellison, 2007). Within the past few years, networking sites have boomed across the globe with many successfully attracting over 100 million users. The latest Pew survey showed that 66% of online adults use social networking sites, as of February 2012 (Brenner, 2012). In March 2013, Facebook attracted 655 million daily active users and 1.11 billion monthly active users (Facebook, 2013). These users come from over 200 countries and they share more than 130 billion connections. Social networking sites serve members on a rich variety of topics. In addition to serving the general public, many networking sites have expanded their businesses and catered to special interest groups on subjects such as business (e.g., LinkedIn), movies (e.g., Flixster), photos (e.g., Flickr), art (e.g., deviantART), travel (e.g., WAYN), music (e.g., Last.fm) and so on. Social networking sites now act as important platforms to connect millions of online users in the world and enable them to interact on a daily basis.

The success of social networking sites has tremendous implications for society. Where business is concerned, networking sites introduce brand new opportunities for companies to reach customers, collect business intelligence, and engage customers for improved service experiences (Medge, 2008). A recent survey by InSites Consulting showed that 80% of U.S. companies now have a presence on Facebook (Ritchie, 2012). Also, over 50% of the U.S. companies use networking sites to interact with consumers and about 61% actively evaluate consumer comments that are posted on networking sites. In addition, government agencies have starting using social networking sites to support their duties. Networking sites help agencies to distribute information and provide services to civilians, recruit employees to <sup>fi</sup>ll openings, and initialize interagency collaborations (Herman, 2012). Examples include GSA’s Twitter presence, EPA’s Facebook page, and the Library of Congress’s Flickr presence. In addition, social networking sites bring in educational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Researchers found that using networking sites helps students in their technology skills, creativity, and communication skills (eNews, 2008). Schools have also increasingly employed social networking sites to support educational purposes. In 2008, more than 136 U.S. universities established educational channels on YouTube (Young, 2008).

As a result of society’s increased social networking site usage, academia has started to examine these sites (Grif<sup>fi</sup>ths & Light, 2008; Lin & Lu, 2011; Roberson & Williamson, 2012; Templeton et al, 2012). To date, there exist several research streams such as self-presentation (Marwick, 2005), impression management (Boyd, 2004), network topology (Golder et al, 2007), friendship development (Lampe et al, 2007), and global difference (Krasnova et al, 2011). However, it is not well known as to what factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence member in developing their attitudes toward networking sites. Attitude refers to one’s positive evaluative effect about using a social networking site. Through the use of social networking services, users develop their attitude toward the service which, over time, results in a positive or negative judgment (Kang & Kim, 2006). In addition, it remains largely unclear as to the motivators of member self-disclosure extent. Self-disclosure extent refers to the amount of disclosure of private information such as identity, state, and disposition into publicity (Moon, 2000). Self-disclosure supports and enables the continuous growth of social networking sites. Unfortunately, self-disclosure extent is not always high for networking services. In their survey among Facebook members, Tow et al (2008) found that 31% of the respondents did not put much personal information on their pro<sup>fi</sup>les. The current study examines the following research questions: ‘How do members learn and develop their attitudes toward a social networking site?’ and ‘How does member attitude in<sup>fl</sup>uence one’s selfdisclosure extent on a networking site?’ New knowledge will allow service vendors to develop effective strategies that improve member attitude and encourage member selfdisclosure on networking sites.

The rest of the paper is organized into six sections. The <sup>fi</sup>rst section offers a brief overview of the prior studies about social networking sites and discusses the theoretical foundation. The next section introduces the research model and explains the research hypotheses. The following two sections detail the research methodology, as well as the results of the data analysis. The <sup>fi</sup>nal sections discuss the paper’s theoretical contribution, managerial implications, venues for future research, and contain its conclusion.

## Literature review and theoretical development

## Prior studies of member attitude and self-disclosure on social networking sites

In this section, we provide a brief review of the existing literature on two important issues: member attitude toward a social networking site and member self-disclosure.

Where member attitude is concerned, prior studies on social networking sites have captured a number of important antecedents. Ke<sup>fi</sup> et al (2010) used extrinsic and intrinsic motivation theories to identify utilitarian outcomes and hedonic outcomes as two important predictors to member attitude among shy people. Survey results of 293 European users found that hedonic outcomes signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly affect member attitude among shy women; results, however, found no impact of utilitarian outcomes and hedonic outcomes among shy men. Shin (2010) borrowed security and privacy literature to suggest the likely impacts of security, trust, and privacy on member attitude development. Survey data collected from 323 student samples supported the hypothesized impacts. Currás-Pérez et al (2013) used the uses and grati<sup>fi</sup>cations theory to consider sociability, fashion/status, and entertainment as pertinent to member attitude. These relationships were supported by survey data from 800 Spanish citizens. Shipps & Phillips (2013) used the technology acceptance model to recognize the potential roles of ease of use and usefulness in member attitude toward social networking sites. Survey data from 164 undergraduate students found support of the projected relationships.

To study member self-disclosure on social networking sites, Posey et al (2010) considered social exchange theory, social penetration theory, and cross-cultural theory of individualism–collectivism. Survey data from 529 French and British users con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that social in<sup>fl</sup>uence, perceived reciprocity, perceived online community trust, privacy risk beliefs, perceived anonymity, perceived collectivism, and perceived individualism affect self-disclosure. Krasnova et al (2009) applied privacy calculus theory and the health belief model to posit that privacy concerns and perceived enjoyment affect self-disclosure. Survey data collected from 237 Germany residents supported the proposed relationships. McKnight et al (2011) also applied privacy calculus theory to suggest privacy concern, information sensitivity, perceived usefulness, enjoyment, and trusting beliefs that determine information disclosure. Survey data collected from 391 student samples lent support for privacy concern, information sensitivity, and trusting beliefs. Pike et al (2009) applied communication privacy management theory to propose a likely relationship between publicness and self-disclosure. Results from an online experiment showed that online self-disclosure is impacted by perceived publicness, which is shaped by mode of entry boundary and ingroup/outgroup boundary.

## Theoretical background

Attitude literature has been widely adopted in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of information systems (Barki & Hartwick, 1994; Gattiker & Kelley, 1999). Prior studies link an individual’s attitude toward objects to actions, where attitude re<sup>fl</sup>ects the extent to which one likes or dislikes an object and an object refers to any aspect of the individual’s world (Friedkin, 2010). Despite the recognition of an imperative role of end-user attitude, little is known about the key sources of attitude formation. Learning theories of attitude change explain the means by which individuals learn attitudes (Lorge, 1936; Hovland et al, 1953). These theories have been a central theoretical framework for behavioral research paradigms and are used in many seminal works, such as theory of reasoned action (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). Learning theories suggest one’s attitude is a result of the learning process, which is shaped by principles of association, reinforcement, and observation (Taylor, 1998). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, learning theories consider classical conditioning, operant conditioning, and social learning as three major mechanisms of attitude formation (Feist & Rosenberg, 2010). Classical conditioning focuses on automatic involuntary behavior and is a learned process where an autonomic response is provoked by external stimuli. Insko (1967) pointed out that objects or events in the environment may carry the power to trigger an emotional response in individual evaluators. For example, frequent displays of a new product along with images of happy customers are likely to affect the attitude of a viewer. When the two stimuli (e.g., new product and satis<sup>fi</sup>ed customers) are repeatedly associated, the viewer learns to respond to the new product with similar emotional responses (e.g., positive attitude). Operant conditioning, also known as instrumental conditioning, employs positive or negative outcomes to reinforce or weaken attitude. It refers to forces that weaken or strengthen voluntary behavior. On the basis of <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the Yale Communication Research Program, Hovland et al (1953) suggested an individual’s opinion persists until he or she encounters a persuasive message, in which incentives are employed to create an acceptance of new beliefs conveyed in the message. In contrast to incentives, negative outcomes (e.g., deterioration of one’s health) may be utilized to elicit fear arousing appeals and lead a person to reject the beliefs expressed in a message (e.g., embarking on habitual smoking). Social learning suggests an individual may form his or her attitude by consulting a reference group (Weiten, 1996). Benedict (1934) pointed out that social systems (e.g., value, norm, and culture) play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant part in shaping the beliefs of a social member. Social learning directly stresses the role of socialization and suggests individuals learn within a social context by observational learning of social models (Ormrod, 1999). Bem (1967) concurred that attitudes are learned as a result of experience with the social surroundings.

The application of learning theories has signi<sup>fi</sup>can implications for the current study. When applied, learning theories suggest classical conditioning, operant conditioning, and social learning may cultivate members’ attitude toward a social networking site. Social networking services are often portrayed by the mass media as a great platform for Internet users to interact (Hampton et al, 2011). Through the principles of classical conditioning, constant associations between social networking services and positive images (e.g., ‘the greatest invention of the 21st century’) in<sup>fl</sup>uence online users’ attitude toward networking services (Atakpa, 2011; Gabriel, 2012). However, one’s susceptibility to classical condi tioning may vary as a result of personality difference. Eysenck (1967) suggested that extroverts are more aroused (i.e., stronger classical conditioning) than intro verts under high stimulation situations, whereas introverts are more aroused than extroverts under low stimulation situations. Therefore, we consider extroversion in this study. Furthermore, learning theories suggest that perceived rewards and penalties may affect the users’ attitude as a result of operant conditioning. Social networking sites offer great assistance to members in networking activities (e.g., relationship maintenance and extension). Through the use of design features, such as pro<sup>fi</sup>le browsing, commenting, and event participation, users are able to make new acquaintances and strengthen existing ties. The value added in networking constitutes an important incentive to encourage users to form a positive attitude toward networking sites. On the contrary, users may be penalized by cyber risks that become prevalent in social networking sites. In the past, numerous incidents of cyber-attacks were reported on networking sites (e.g., frauds, identity thefts, stalking, and malicious codes). Facebook users, for example, experienced the ‘OMG Guy’ attack in 2010 (Schroeder, 2010). As a result, interactions at social networking sites are risky, which is likely to discourage users and drive them to develop adverse attitudes. With regard to operant conditioning, therefore, we consider perceived networking assistance and perceived cyber risks (a subjective assessment of the adverse consequences in accessing social networking sites) in the current study. Lastly, learning theories suggest the potential role of a reference group in shaping users’ networking site attitudes due to social learning. With regard to networking services, users may observe and consult important others (e.g., family members and friends) to acquire their opinions and learn attitudes from them. The attitudinal in<sup>fl</sup>uence of a reference group is an important factor in the process of persuasion (Bella, 2006). Thus, we consider social in<sup>fl</sup>uence in this study to understand its impact on users attitude formation in response to a social networking site. In this paper, we will also explore the relationship between members’ attitudes and their self-disclosure behavior on a social networking site.

## Research model and hypotheses

In Figure 1, we present the research model and hypotheses. The conceptual research model contends that user attitude toward a social networking site affects self-disclosure extent through the mediating role of site usage rate. The model also posits that extroversion, perceived networking assistance, perceived cyber risk, and social in<sup>fl</sup>uence jointly shape member attitude. Extroversion relates to classical conditioning; perceived networking assistance (i.e., assistance to a member in managing networking activities) pertains to the reward aspect of operational conditioning, whereas perceived cyber risk (i.e., the likely cyber-attacks due to the use of a networking site) pertains to the penalty aspect of operational conditioning; and social in<sup>fl</sup>uence relates to social learning.

We contend that the likely impact of attitude on selfdisclosure extent is mediated by site usage rate. Site usage rate is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the time a person spends using a social networking site during a designated period (Theotokis & Doukidis, 2009). When a member is pleased with a networking site, he or she is more likely to use a social networking site. During a visit, a member generates usergenerated content such as logs of personal activities, pictures, audios and videos, and opinions and comments. One may also engage in social interactions, such as wisdom of the crowd, social curation, and social bookmarking. Vasalou et al (2010) found users of Facebook go through three experience stages, which include discovery (e.g., exploration of a site), super<sup>fi</sup>cial involvement (e.g., membership registration), and true commitment (e.g., active engagement). While their usage pattern alters across these three stages, a site member discloses more private information when site use increases. That is, an increased use of a social networking site due to a positive attitude will lead a member to disclose more of his or her privacy. We, therefore, expect that the impact of attitude on self-disclosure is mediated by site usage rate.

## H1: Site usage rate will mediate the association between attitude and self-disclosure extent.

Owing to their innovative designs, social networking sites have been portrayed by many as an amazing place for online users to interact (Sengupta, 2011). For example, social networking sites are considered as ‘the greatest invention of the past decade’ (Atakpa, 2011) and ‘the best invention of the 21st century’ (Gabriel, 2012). Media and celebrities frequently request viewers and fans to follow them on Facebook, Twitter, blogs and so on. By establishing the link between social networking sites and positive labels, classical conditioning functions to shape online users’ attitude. However, the development of a spontaneous response to social networking sites as a result of classical conditioning is not homogenous among online users. Psychological studies have long contended that an individual’s personality trait, such as extroversion, may affect how readily an individual will become conditioned to a particular stimulus (Costello, 1967; Raine & Venables, 1981). Extroversion is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as positive emotion, surgency, and the tendency to seek out stimulation and the company of others (Costa & McCrae, 1992). In his Extraversion-Arousal Hypothesis, Eysenck (1967) suggested that extroverts are more aroused (i.e., stronger classical conditioning) than introverts under high stimulation situations, whereas introverts are more aroused than extroverts under low stimulation situations. Later research has found evidence to support the hypothesis as introverts showed low classical conditioning under conditions conductive to high arousal (Eysenck & Levey, 1972). Nowadays, social networking sites have developed into an overhyped phenomenon and represent a high stimulation situation to average online users. When a member is an extrovert, the effect of classical conditioning on attitude stays strong and leads the member to develop a highly positive attitude toward Facebook. In the case the member is introverted, on the contrary, the impact of classical conditioning on attitude becomes weak and the member develops a less positive attitude. Therefore, the level of extroversion goes hand in hand with a member’s attitude and the two constructs manifest a positive relationship. And thus we posit:

## H2: Extroversion will positively relate to attitude.

Social networking sites offer great tools to help members manage their personal networks. Facebook, for example, allows one to search for old or new friends by location, education, workplace and so on. It also allows an individual to import his or her list of contacts from other sources, such as email systems and instant message services. Once a person makes new connections, he or she is able to view their friends and to further expand own network. Facebook also offers features such as a news feed, which displays the status updates of all friends and allows one to stay informed of their acquaintances. In 2012, Facebook introduced the feature of ‘Find Friends Nearby’, which allows a member to make friends with other Facebook members around own area. Furthermore, the enormous size of leading social networking sites makes it easy and feasible to <sup>fi</sup>nd friends who share common interests or backgrounds. Facebook, for example, has over 1.11 billion monthly active users, which include almost 70% of all U.S. online users. As a result, it becomes a convenient place to search for and manage all contacts. Owing to increasing popularity, social networking sites have become a preferred attack vector by hackers to sabotage online users. ## Research methodology

To increase measurement validity, we developed the survey measurement items by borrowing from the existing literature. Detailed measurements of the key constructs and their sources are presented in Appendix A. The items were all measured on the 7-likert scale. The survey instruments were pretested with 60 users of a social networking site to check the psychometric properties of the measurement scales (Churchill, 1979). We tested the research model by using empirical survey data. Survey responses were collected from 822 college students who took undergraduate or M.B.A. courses at a large institution in the Midwestern United States. Two rounds of survey data collection were performed and a series of t-tests showed no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between early and late responses on research subject attributes such as gender, computer usage experience, and Internet usage experience. College students are a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant segment of social networking site users. A recent Quintly analysis released on March 2013 showed that 18–24-year-olds presented the largest user group on Facebook (Nierhoff, 2013). Therefore, the student sample is appropriate for the current research. The research subjects consisted of business and non-business major undergraduates; as a result, the samples therefore present a degree of heterogeneity. Respondents were guaranteed both con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality and access to the aggregated survey results. Participation in the survey was voluntary. ## Analysis and result

We tested the research model using structural equation modeling analysis. We employed partial least squares (PLS), which uses a component-based approach for estimation and places minimal restrictions on sample size and residual distributions. PLS is best suited for testing complex relationships by avoiding inadmissible solutions and factor indeterminacy (Chin, 1998). PLS also supports exploratory research. Our paper investigates member attitude formation through the lens of learning theories and it also studies the impact of attitude on member selfdisclosure, which has received limited validation in the current research context. In addition, PLS is useful with multiple dependent variables and when one dependent variable becomes an independent variable (Gefen et al, 2000). PLS is, therefore, considered appropriate for the current study.

## Measurement mode

Table 2 reports the correlation matrix, the average variance extracted (AVE), and the descriptive statistics of the principal constructs. Measurement reliability was assessed using composite reliability (Werts et al, 1974) and Cronbach’s α (Cronbach, 1971). Fornell & Larcker (1981) suggested that a composite reliability of 0.70 or greater is considered acceptable for research. Nunnally suggested a Cronbach’s α of 0.70 or greater is considered acceptable (Nunnally, 1976; Gefen et al, 2000). As in Table 2, the internal consistencies of all variables are considered acceptable, thus signifying satisfactory reliability.

Convergent and discriminant validity are inferred when (1) the square root of each construct is larger than its correlations with the other constructs (i.e., the AVE shared between the construct and its indicators is larger than the AVE shared between the construct and the other items); (2) all AVEs are greater than 0.50; and (3) the PLS indicators load much higher on their hypothesized construct than on other constructs (i.e., own loadings are higher than crossloadings) (Chin, 1998). As shown in Table 2, the square roots of the AVE are all greater than 0.5 and greater than all other cross-correlations, indicating that the variance explained by each construct is much larger than the measurement error variance. As in Appendix B, all items load high on their own constructs. These tests validate the measurement properties of principal constructs.

The research data was collected from a single survey; therefore, we checked for the extent of common method bias. First, the Harman’s one-factor test was performed by including all the variables in a principal components factor analysis (Podsakoff et al, 2003). Common method bias exists when one single factor emerges or when one factor accounts for the majority of the covariance among the variables. The results showed that none of the emergent factors explained the majority of the covariance. Second, the correlation matrix was examined for highly correlated factors. The common method bias exists when there exist extremely high correlations (r > 0.9). Table 2 does not reveal such evidence.

## Structural model

The PLS results show the structural model explained 28% of variance in attitude and 27% of the variance in selfdisclosure extent (Model 4 in Table 3). Regarding the control variables, age was found to negatively associate with site usage rate (b = 0.10, P<0.01), whereas Website use experience was found to associate with site usage rate (b = 0.08, P<0.01). The two control variables, however, were not found to associate with attitude or self-disclosure extent in a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant manner.

To test H1, we followed the Baron and Kenny test (Baron & Kenney, 1986; MacKinnon, 1994). Evidence of mediation effort is fourfold: (1) there exists a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between the independent variable and dependent variable; (2) there exists a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between the independent variable and mediating variable; (3) there exists a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between the mediating variable and dependent variable; and (4) there exists a relationship between the mediating variable and the dependent variable when controlling for the independent variable. In case the effect of independent variable on dependent variable becomes zero when the mediating variable is included, there is evidence for full mediation. If the effect of independent variable on dependent variable is reduced when the mediator is included, then the direct effect is considered partially mediated. To verify the mediation effect, we tested four PLS models (see Table 3).

## Discussion

Our paper makes a twofold contribution to the literature. First, we uncover the underlying mechanisms of member attitude formation through the lens of learning theories. The existing literature on social networking sites has adopted alternative frameworks (e.g., motivation theories and the uses and grati<sup>fi</sup>cations theory) to explain member attitude. Our paper is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to recognize the relevance and signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of learning theories on the research of social networking sites. Following the three key mechanisms of learning (i.e., classical conditioning, operant conditioning, and social learning), we theorize extroversion, perceived networking assistance, perceived cyber risk, and social in<sup>fl</sup>uence as important antecedents to member attitude. Survey results of a large sample lend support to these hypothesized relationships. Principal constructs such as perceived cyber risk is new to the literature on social networking sites, suggesting that the fear of cyber-attacks plays a critical role in one’s assessment of Facebook. In addition, the use of learning theories offers alternative theoretical reasoning to explain interesting research phenomenon. For example, prior studies have debated over the likely impact of extroversion on member perception and behavior on a social networking site. While Social Compensation Hypothesis argues that introverts will favor networking sites more than extroverts will (Gross et al, 2002; Valkenburg et al, 2005), Social Enhancement Hypothesis (rich-get-richer) contends the opposite (Walther, 1996; Kraut et al, 2002). In this paper, we drew from the literature on classical conditioning to recognize the differential in<sup>fl</sup>uences of extroversion on the classical conditioning process. Social networking sites nowadays represent a highly stimulating situation to an average online user. Internet users, for example, have become captivated by social networking sites and they vigorously search for related information. Since 2009, the phrase ‘social networking sites’ has been one of the top search interests on Google and it became the top search interest in September 2011 (Google, 2013). The current study suggests the complex relationships between extroversion and user perception and behavior may be, in part, determined by classical conditioning. That is, extroversion in<sup>fl</sup>uences how successfully a member internalizes the external stimulus of Facebook as a high sensation. Analysis results of a large survey lend strong support to the research hypothesis. Our study, therefore, offers a new perspective on the study of extroversion/introversion within the context of social networking sites and lays the foundation for future research on a related topic. Posthoc analyses show the formation mechanisms of member attitude remain identical between male and female Facebook users. Results as such further demonstrate the value of learning theories in explaining social networking site phenomenon.

Second, the current research uncovers the underlying mechanisms through which attitude affects self-disclosure extent. Previous studies of networking sites have generated limited <sup>fi</sup>ndings about the relationship between attitude and self-disclosure (Salehan & Kim, 2012; Chen, 2013). On the basis of literature, we contend that positive attitude encourages a member to increase his or her site usage on a social networking site, which in turn leads one to widen his or her disclosure of personal data. While site usage rate does not fully mediate the association between member attitude and self-disclosure extent, its impact proves to be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, partial mediating effect. To the best of our knowledge, we are the <sup>fi</sup>rst to reveal the interesting role of site usage rate within the context of a social networking site. Post-hoc analyses show that site usage rate mediates the relationship between attitude and self-disclosure extent among male users, but not female users. Furthermore, post-hoc analyses con<sup>fi</sup>rm the association between attitude and self-disclosure and the association between site usage rate and self-disclosure signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly differ between male and female members of Facebook. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are brand new to the literature on social networking sites. Overall, the current study makes noticeable contributions to the existing literature on self-disclosure, such as Posey et al (2010), as it explores and validates important antecedents and contingencies that prior studies did not capture.

This study informs practice. Self-disclosure adds vital support to the sustainable growth of networking sites, so service vendors may stimulate them by cultivating users’ positive attitude. To improve user attitude, service providers may alter users’ levels of extroversion. Psychology literature pointed out that personality is malleable and may be reformed, given the proper motivations from the task environment (Markus & Nurius, 1986). To this end, site-wide campaigns that promote an open, social, and interactive culture are likely to encourage users to turn more extroverted toward the online community. Meanwhile, vendors of networking services may consider adding new site features to facilitate users in managing personal networks. The newly introduced ‘Timeline’ feature of Facebook represents one of the new designs, which may be deployed to help relationship management. It is also imperative that networking service providers alleviate users’ perceived cyber risk. Among others, vendors may employ advanced information security safeguards. In 2010, Facebook, for example, developed and rolled out new security features to protect its 500 million members against stolen passwords, malicious attacks, phishing scams, and spam (Rickman, 2010). Moreover, site vendors may resort to social in<sup>fl</sup>uence in recruiting new members. For example, Websites may offer convenient tools to help users invite new members among own their own clique. Convincing messages from an inviter will effectively drive followers to participate in a social networking site. Finally, our results suggest that more site usage gives rise to a greater amount of member privacy disclosure among male users. To utilize this encouraging <sup>fi</sup>nding, vendors of networking services may organize more site activities and attract male members to participate. In this way, members are likely to reveal more of their private lives during their interactions on the Website. Where female users are concerned, however, service vendors may rely more on the improvement of member attitude, in order to promote a greater level of selfdisclosure extent.

This paper is limited in certain ways. First, the current study recruits college students as the research sample. The results must be treated with caution in terms of its generalizability to other user populations of social networking sites. Following recent studies such as Miltgen & Peyrat-Guillard (2013), future research may analyze the general public to understand the patterns in their perceptions and behaviors on social networking sites. Second, the current study employs a cross-sectional design and does not allow us to observe the longitudinal impact. Hence, the results should be treated with caution in that causality cannot be inferred with cross-sectional data. While a longitudinal analysis is preferred, solid cross-sectional models must <sup>fi</sup>rst be established before future research can examine their viability over time (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006). Third, the current study utilizes self-reported survey data to assess online use behavior at a networking site. Although prior studies have found a positive correlation between self-reported and actual data of members’ use of Facebook (Junco, 2013), future research may employ actual site usage data to assess the actual user behaviors of the research samples. Fourth, our use of survey as the main research method for data collection is subject to certain limitations. While it reduces interviewer bias and reaches a large population, precision and control are accordingly lost in a survey when it focuses solely on realism (McGrath, 1982; Dennis & Valacich, 2001). While each methodological approach is somewhat <sup>fl</sup>awed and there is no perfect research, future study may consider adopting multiple methodologies (e.g., experiments) to retest the model. Fifth, future studies may consider other constructs that relate to the learning theories. For example, perceived hedonic value may be studied within the dimension of ‘operant conditioning’. This is because hedonic value (e.g., perceived enjoyment) brings in positive reinforcement to members of a social networking site and subsequently creates learned experience. Future studies will help complete our understanding of key drivers of member attitude within the theoretical framework of learning theories.
