# Understanding the effect of knowledge management strategies on knowledge management performance: A contingency perspective

## Abstract

The universalistic perspective research on employing a unidimensional knowledge management (KM) strategy has yielded conflicting findings and recommendations under different contexts. This study proposes a contingency model for investigating the effects of KM strategies on KM performance to compromise these contradictions. Drawing on knowledge-based view of the firm, which identifies knowledge type and origin as two key KM dimensions, this study first defines four KM strategies: external codification, internal codification, external personalization, and internal personalization. A multiple contingency model of KM strategy is then developed based on the technology-organizationenvironment framework. This study proposes that the effectiveness of each KM strategy depends on both external and internal contextual conditions, namely, environmental knowledge intensity and organizational IS maturity. To test and validate the contingency model, we analyze data from 141 firms to explain the effects of KM strategies on KM performance. Results reveal that aside from the internal personalization strategy, the other three KM strategies have a significant association with KM performance in their hypothesized contexts. This study expands KM strategy research by theoretically developing an advanced contingency model aligned with external and internal contexts and by providing valuable practical suggestions to managers in selecting KM strategy based on multiple contingencies with the external and internal conditions of a firm.

Keywords: Knowledge management, Knowledge management strategy, Knowledge management performance, Contingency perspective, Technology-organization-environment framework, Environmental knowledge intensity, Organizational IS maturity

## 1. Introduction

Developing a knowledge management (KM) strategy is important in effective KM. An appropriate KM strategy enables a firm to create, acquire, access, and leverage knowledge in a timely manner, thereby resulting in better performance [1]. Considering this KM strategy impact, the knowledge-based view (KBV) of the firm has extended the resource-based view (RBV) of the firm, which contends that organizational resources should be valuable, rare, and appropriable to generate a competitive advantage and be sustained over time because of their low substitutability, low mobility, and low imitability. That is, the KBV contends that organizational knowledge is the primary resource for creating and sustaining competitive advantage [2].

While prior studies on KM have improved our understanding of KM strategy, its roles and impacts are fragmented for several reasons. First, studies on KM strategy have mainly adopted the universalistic perspective with the assumption that certain KM strategies are consistently effective regardless of the given organizational contexts [3, 4]. However, the oversight of contextual factors is vulnerable to the contingency under certain conditions because the effects of different KM strategies on knowledge management performance (KMP) are affected by the external and internal contexts of a firm. Nonetheless, the alignment of KM strategy with organizational contexts has not been fully addressed in KM literature [5, 6]. Second, the KBV has devoted substantial attention to KM strategy analysis by identifying two major dimensions at the firm level: (1) the extent to which knowledge is accumulated by a person or a system (knowledge type) [7, 8] and (2) whether knowledge originates from within or outside the firm (knowledge origin) [9, 10]. However, previous studies that examined the effects of KM strategies on KMP have only considered a single KM dimension—either knowledge type (system/person) or origin (external/internal)—and have neglected the possible combinations of these two dimensions [e.g., 4, 6]. Therefore, these studies failed to elucidate the effect of KM strategies because of lack of investigation on interactions between different strategic dimensions of KM.

To explore these gaps, this study uses the KBV to define individual KM strategies that indicate the two dimensions of strategic KM approaches: i.e., knowledge type and origin [11]. Combining both

#

dimensions, this study proposes four KM strategies: external codification (external system-oriented), internal codification (internal system-oriented), external personalization (external person-oriented), and internal personalization (internal person-oriented). To suggest the optimal choice of KM initiatives upon multiple contingencies, we propose a contingency model based on the technology-organizationenvironment (TOE) framework [12], which identifies environmental knowledge intensity and organizational IS maturity as two key contextual factors that interact with KM strategy. As proposed by Sambamurthy and Zmud [13], our multiple key contingencies given to organizations with the four KM strategies posit that KMP is determined by a firm’s fit formation of KM strategies with external information-processing needs from its environment and its technology-oriented capabilities achieved internally [14]. The major premise underlying this study is that the effectiveness of KM strategies depends on external and internal contexts [15, 16].

Specifically, this study aims to answer the following question: How does the effect of KM strategies on KMP differ depending on the external and internal contexts, i.e., the degree of environmental knowledge intensity and the level of organizational IS maturity? This study attempts to answer the question using data collected from 141 Korean firms that have implemented enterprise-wide KM initiatives. This study expands KM strategy research by theoretically developing an advanced contingency model aligned with external and internal contexts and by providing valuable practical suggestions to managers in selecting the KM strategy that will be successful in different external and internal contexts. We also believe that our two factor-contingencies fill another gap in the KM literature, which faces difficulty in integrating the effect of KM strategy into multiple contingencies, such as business-related environmental and information system (IS)-oriented organizational contexts [14], in an empirical analysis. The gap is primarily due to the tradeoff between the omitted variable bias among multiple existing contingencies and a parsimonious research design for robust empirical evidence [17]. The findings of the study may be added to those of existing North American research [4, 18, 19] and European studies [5, 6, 8] to provide a more international and comprehensive perspective on KM strategies.

## 2. Knowledge management strategy

Research on the effect of KM strategies on KMP has yielded conflicting findings across different contexts [e.g., 8, 18, 19, 20]. For example, certain studies [8, 19] propose that the internally systemoriented KM strategy provides a competitive advantage in a firm because people can easily access and acquire codified knowledge from internal rather than external sources. However, other studies [18, 20] indicate the opposite effect of the same strategy. Their ground is that overreliance on codified knowledgeoriented strategy results in the loss of integrity of internal knowledge and of causal connections between organizational knowledge and firm-specific contexts in which knowledge is applied because codified knowledge in the electronic forms mainly contains basic and general information rather than new insights or creative ideas. Thus, prior studies have not fully compromised such conflicts, as summarized Table 1.

The KBV indicates that conceptualizing the type and origin of organizational knowledge is important to simultaneously explain organizational learning [19, 20]. However, little is known about the combined functions of knowledge type and origin, despite their interrelationship. This insufficient consideration has resulted in a serious limitation on KBV because the functions and effects of different knowledge aspects have not been understood in an integrated manner. Although KM researchers have emphasized the necessity to simultaneously consider external and internal contexts, prior KBV-based studies have not integrated the multiple contexts in a single study [e.g., 5, 6]. In prior KM studies, the relationship of a firm’s strategic effort with its environment was not a key concern in explaining how the firm improves KMP [5]. Therefore, the KBV needs to be advanced by considering strategic KM alignment with external and internal contexts.

To fill these research gaps, this study uses the KBV to define KM strategies based on two major dimensions of KM—knowledge type (person- or system-oriented) and knowledge origin (internal- or external-oriented). The KBV suggests that implementing a KM strategy requires not only firm-specific accumulated knowledge assets but also knowledge flows within or channeled into a focal firm, which is assimilated and developed into its accumulated knowledge [11]. Therefore, we define KM strategy as a logical plan with regard to decisions of firms on knowledge type and origin to create and sustain a competitive advantage. The KM strategy does not need to be a consciously made unidimensional decision but rather a manifestation of multiple decisions.

The effects of KM strategies on KMP, the degree to which a firm achieves knowledge-oriented benefits by adopting and implanting KM [21], have been analyzed from various theories, such as integrative capability view [10], knowledge sourcing theory [1], organizational learning theory [22], and transaction cost theory [4]. Particularly, the KBV posits that organizational knowledge is the most significant resource that leads to long-term sustainable competitive advantage [2]. The main focus of KBV is on value creation using knowledge. Thus, its core issue is to understand how KM should be pursued to improve a firm’s capability that leads to better performance.

A critical contribution of the KBV is the recognition of two KM dimensions based on knowledge type: system-oriented (codification) and person-oriented (personalization) [3, 7]. Although several studies dimensions of knowledge, the most enduring distinctions are explicit and tacit [8]. Codification and personalization provide underlying mechanisms on how explicit and tacit knowledge are created, accessed, and acquired. Codification relies on simple and explicit knowledge, and attempts to improve firm performance by using KM systems [23]. Personalization deals with complex and tacit knowledge, and applies personal contacts and socialization processes to increase KM effectiveness [3].

Another contribution of the KBV is identifying two distinct KM choices based on knowledge origin: internal-oriented and external-oriented [9]. Thus, the forces that motivate a firm toward internal knowledge sourcing may not be the same as those motivating it away from external knowledge sourcing [24]. The internal-oriented approach attempts to increase firm performance by integrating knowledge within the boundary of the firm [18]. Knowledge generated within the firm is unique and specific. Thus, competitors may find imitating this knowledge difficult, yielding considerable value for the firm. By contrast, the external-oriented approach attempts to bring in knowledge from outside sources via acquisition or imitation and then transfer that knowledge within the organization [25]. Thus, firms can obtain fresh ideas to complement their knowledge base, thereby leading to higher KMP [4].

Individual KM approaches can improve KMP. However, generating synergies among the four KM dimensions can be more beneficial to firms. Given the existing synergies between the approaches based on knowledge type and origin, the KBV suggests that firms should consider both dimensions in launching KM strategies for improving KMP, thereby deriving four KM strategies, namely, external codification, internal codification, external personalization, and internal personalization. ## 3. Contingency approach for knowledge management strategy

A contingency approach shows fit relationships among multiple factors and their resultant influence on relevant criteria not only by identifying ideal configurations that generate optimal outcomes but also by indicating deviation from such fit configurations that cause low performance [15]. The contingency approach has been taken by prior studies [13, 15, 16] to reveal such fit or misfit among multiple factors. In research on strategy-structure-performance relationship, however, the contingency theory has been severely criticized because of its lack of clarity and failure to specify the form of interaction between factors of interest [26]. In addition, the contingency perspective is limited by its difficulty to exhaustively define, measure, and test full contingencies without the omitted variable bias among potential contingencies [17] because the fit between factors depends on the certain contingencies that are considered. In this sense, our contingency approach addresses such theoretical and empirical issues via the TOE-based identification of multiple contingencies [13], which are exhaustively (both internally and externally) relevant to KM in organizations [27].

Based on the contingency approach, this study suggests that a successful KM requires mixed strategies exerted by a firm in its given situations [5, 6]. A few recent studies have analyzed the effects of KM strategies with organizational contexts, but their contingency perspectives have focused only on either environmental factors [e.g., 6, 18] or internal contexts [e.g., 5, 19]. Lack of simultaneous consideration of external and internal contingencies results in inconclusive findings and inappropriate practical guidelines that prevent organizations from developing KM strategies suitable for their situations [27].

To overcome the limitation, this study proposes an advanced contingency model that reflects both

#

environmental and internal fits of the best KM strategy based on the TOE framework [12], which identifies multiple factors related to organizational innovations. Following Sambamurthy and Zmud [13], we are specifically interested in the multiple contingencies of environmental knowledge intensity and organizational IS maturity as key external and internal contexts [22]. As shown in Table 3, KBV posits that environmental knowledge intensity is a key external context because organizational knowledge is the most significant resource of strategic advantages [22]. A knowledge-oriented economy describes business environments as industrial trends toward greater dependence on organizational knowledge [28]. Knowledge-oriented logic implies that firms are exposed to analytic or synthetic knowledge bases in their environments [29, 30]. By contrast, RBV emphasizes IS as a set of key internal resources for sustainable competitive advantage [31]. IS literature suggests that each firm has different endowments of IS resources at its discretional disposal to identify, assemble, deploy, and use technologies because given IS resource barriers cause a firm to achieve a certain level of IS maturity by exploiting its idiosyncratic set of technologies [32]. As such, a firm establishes its IS resources more (or less) than its competitors because it depends more (or less) on IS resources [33, 34]. In this sense, the best KM strategy is contingent on the IS maturity discretionally decided by a firm’s dependency on IS assets and IS resources [35].

This study investigates the contingent effects of different KM strategies on KMP by simultaneously considering (1) environmental knowledge intensity, the extent to which a firm relies on knowledge inherent in its activities and outputs for a competitive advantage to survive in the market [6, 36], and (2) organizational IS maturity, the degree of IS effectiveness in supporting organizational decisions and the contribution of IS to a firm’s desired outcomes [37, 38]. The TOE framework suggests that changes in organizations are caused by the confluence of multiple sources [12]. Thus, this study integrates a firm’s strategic choices (i.e., organizational decision on KM strategies), evolutionary forces from the outside of the firm (i.e., environmental knowledge intensity), and IS competence-oriented forces within the firm (i.e., organizational IS maturity) in a single research model. Such representations of knowledge intensity and IS maturity are explained in detail in the following sections.

## 3.1. Environmental knowledge intensity

Environmental knowledge intensity reflects knowledge-oriented requirements from external circumstances in which a firm differently manages and organizes its tasks and businesses [36], thereby forming external context-driven propensities for KM strategy [6]. Environmental knowledge intensity has been considered as an important key context because the extent of organizational knowledge required to preserve valuable heritage, learn new techniques, solve problems, create core competences, and identify new business opportunities differs according to industry-specific businesses and tasks [28]. The innovation of a firm is influenced by its knowledge-oriented environment [39]. Thus, the firm must confront different innovation logics to be superior to its competitors.

In this sense, a widely accepted assumption is that the innovation required for firms depends on their environmental necessities regarding key knowledge sources, function of codified and tacit knowledge, and types of knowledge links with others [40], which distinguish between analytic and synthetic knowledge bases for successful businesses [29]. A low knowledge-intensive environment of a synthetic knowledge base, which is dominant in some industries exposed to the competition based on the logic of incremental innovations (e.g., food products and beverages), is principally characterized by the application or novel combination of existing knowledge, relatively low level of R&D, and strong orientation to solve specific problems articulated by customers [30]. By contrast, firms in a high knowledge-intensive environment compete against each other according to a different logic, that is, an analytic knowledge base, which requires the firms to realize their radical innovations through the effective interplay between their codified and tacit knowledge in other industries (e.g., pharmaceuticals and electrical machinery and apparatus) [30]. Thus, this study characterizes the knowledge-oriented dynamic environment as high knowledge intensity (i.e., analytic knowledge bases) and the knowledge-oriented stagnant environment as low knowledge intensity (i.e., synthetic knowledge bases).

## 3.2. Organizational IS maturity

Organizational IS maturity has been addressed in different terms, including IS success [41], IS maturity [42], IS effectiveness [43], and IS evaluation [44]. This comprehensive concept embraces the internal development of information resources, proper integration of computer-based systems, and the capability of users to utilize the systems in organizations. Based on RBV, organizational capability implies that firms create new knowledge internally and leverage existing knowledge, thereby favorably positioning in their own external contexts [45]. In this sense, IS maturity is crucial to improving a firm’s ability to use prior knowledge. IS maturity also helps firms recognize the value of new information, assimilate it, and apply it to create new knowledge. Therefore, IS maturity not only affects a firm’s effectiveness of the design of knowledge type and origin to meet its internal needs [37] but also the quality of key functions that channel and utilize knowledge within and outside the firm [38]. IS maturity is a decisive factor in determining how a firm leverages its knowledge effectively; hence, the firm’s KM strategy choice should adequately align with its IS maturity to form the internal fit for a successful KM [41].

However, firms do not necessarily need high IS maturity to be successful [7]. On the one hand, high organizational IS maturity provides high-quality, reliable, and fast KM systems by encouraging organizational workers to rely on codified knowledge in a “reuse economy,” in which investing once in a knowledge asset and recursively using it are required. On the other hand, low organizational IS maturity generates competitive advantages by reducing unnecessary investments in KM systems and facilitating conversations and exchanges of tacit knowledge. Regardless of the level of organizational IS maturity, individual expertise channels can provide a firm with creative, analytically rigorous advices on and highly customized solutions to its unique tasks, thereby yielding substantial profits with competitive advantages. Our consideration of IS maturity as a key internal context is to reflect the contingent relationship between a firm’s KM strategy choices and IS resource endowments. That is, a successful KM is not fully but only partially dependent on IS maturity, which indicates IS-oriented resources and capabilities [41]. This implies that a firm’s best KM strategy for KMP improvement depends on its IS maturity level.

## 4. Contingency hypothesis development

Our contingency perspective toward the best choice of KM strategy theoretically relies on the TOE framework, which explains the successful adoption of KM systems and practices in organizations [12].

Simultaneously dealing with knowledge intensity from environments and achieving an appropriate level of IS maturity is important for firms to be competitive in KM. Both contexts need to be considered in developing a contingency model to suggest the best KM strategies across internally and externally different situations. The research model proposed in this study is represented with contingent expectations for each of the four KM strategies with different levels of environmental knowledge intensity and organizational IS maturity, as depicted in Fig. 1.

## 4.1. High knowledge intensity and high IS maturity: Cell 1

External codification strategy refers to the endeavor of a firm to access specialized and standardized external codified learning sources, such as technical reports, trade journals, patents, and other sources [25]. This strategy provides opportunities for a firm to improve its competitiveness by benchmarking other successful firms and conveying stories about best practices to its employees [46]. Such standardized codified knowledge is usually less sensitive to space than tacit knowledge embedded in a person. Thus, a firm can easily adopt external codified knowledge and assimilate it with other activities and processes to create new knowledge.

An organization with high knowledge intensity relies heavily on external knowledge sources [30]. Such an organization is more likely to continuously monitor other firms to identify and imitate superior solutions as well as combine them with its own knowledge [47]. The strategy also facilitates creation and sharing of valuable organizational knowledge, thereby making the firm more innovative. Therefore, accessing external knowledge might have important functions in enhancing knowledge creation and sharing and achieving organizational innovation at a fast pace. In addition, a system-oriented approach focuses on codifying and storing knowledge via IS orientation [48, 49]. A firm with high IS maturity accumulates specific knowledge of its experts and transfers such codified knowledge to other members through such technologies as video conferencing, groupware, chatting features of the intranet, virtual reality systems, and online communities for efficient and timely communications [50]. Therefore, firms with high IS maturity effectively codify organizational knowledge by their IS-oriented tools and channels.

We suggest that external codification is the most suitable strategy for firms with high organizational IS maturity and high environmental knowledge intensity.

H1: When the organizational IS maturity and environmental knowledge intensity of a firm are both high, the external codification strategy is the most effective way to improve its KMP.

## 4.2. Low knowledge intensity and high IS maturity: Cell 2

Internal codification strategy refers to the effort of a firm to manage knowledge residing in internal IS or documents (e.g., sourcing knowledge by a manufacturing firm from its enterprise knowledge portal for the convenience of internal users and sharing valuable contents among internal users). This strategy improves the ability of a firm to access, update, and utilize standardized internal codified knowledge. As internal computerized knowledge provides ease of access and comprehension [51], this strategy facilitates knowledge sharing [19] and enables a firm to achieve economics of reuse and to decrease searching cost by reconfiguring internal computerized knowledge to fit new situations [52], thereby improving KMP.

An organization with low knowledge intensity is characterized by synthetic knowledge bases rather than analytic ones [29, 53]. Environmental requirements for synthetic knowledge bases mainly include the application of existing knowledge, the importance of problem-related knowledge derived through inductive processes, and a strong orientation on solving specific but routine problems. Learning to acquire practical skills is also crucial for a firm with low knowledge intensity, resulting in not radical innovations but incremental ones. Given the key features of synthetic knowledge bases, a firm should focus on developing its internal knowledge with high IS maturity. Therefore, we suggest that internal codification is the most suitable strategy for improving KMP, in which a firm has high organizational IS maturity and low environmental intensity.

H2: When the organizational IS maturity of a firm is high and its environmental knowledge intensity is low, the internal codification strategy is the most effective way to improve its KMP.

## 4.3. High knowledge intensity and low IS maturity: Cell 3

External personalization strategy refers to a firm’s orientation towards the use of external personal learning (e.g., a consulting firm’s requesting internal consultants to attend various seminars, conferences and workshops). For example, users share their innovative ideas with outsiders to solve their own and shared problems in an open-source software development environment. This situation provides an opportunity for firms to co-create products with the participation of external customers [54]. Suppliers can also improve competitive advantage by expanding the knowledge base of the firm [25].

External personalization strategy yields higher KMP because direct contact with external people opens a firm to different viewpoints and expands its knowledge base [25]. As discussed above, the externalization of organizational knowledge is appropriate for a firm with high knowledge intensity. For a firm with low IS maturity, creating and sharing organizational knowledge through knowledge personalization is more effective than knowledge codification. Human-oriented networks enable organizational members to identify and share knowledge via inter-personal interactions with external sources and professional groups, and knowledge transfer via communications with external experts [48]. Then, individuals personalize knowledge by sharing it with coworkers through personal contacts. A firm in which intellectuals share knowledge through human-oriented networks can establish knowledge dialogues with external experts rather than knowledge objects from databases [7]. Personalized knowledge, which is not yet or cannot be codified, can be transferred through brainstorming sessions and one-on-one conversations even in organizations with low IS maturity. Thus, external personalization might be the best strategy for a firm with low organizational IS maturity and high environmental knowledge intensity to encourage its members to personalize knowledge via human-based external networks.

H3: When the organizational IS maturity of a firm is low and its environmental knowledge intensity is high, the external personalization strategy is the most effective way to improve its KMP.

## 4.4. Low knowledge intensity and low IS maturity: Cell 4

Internal personalization strategy refers to the ability of a firm to access, acquire, and leverage knowledge from internal personnel. Its examples are valuable experience, know-hows, and networks of internal experts sourced by a firm via communities of practice (COPs) in which collective joint sensemaking and collaborative problem solving form strong interpersonal ties among members and encourage direct reciprocity within a community with common interests [55]. Unique knowledge, such as the skills and experiences of internal personnel, provides a firm with competitive advantage [19]. Knowledge resulting from this combination cannot be built in a day because this knowledge has firm-specific and tacit characteristics, causing difficulty imitating and copying this knowledge [22].

Internal personalization strategy helps firms increase their performance by focusing on an internal and person-oriented approach, in that they do not only develop their own core competencies and appropriate benefits, but also control and understand tacit knowledge in the KM process effectively and efficiently [10]. The characteristics of synthetic knowledge bases suggested in H2 indicate that a firm with low knowledge intensity is more likely to cultivate and utilize its internal knowledge to improve KMP. Moreover, as discussed in H3, a firm with low IS maturity can be expected to improve its KMP by relying on human-oriented networks. By internally personalizing knowledge, the firm attempts to informally acquire and share its valuable internal knowledge [20] through one-to-one mentoring, face-to-face help by internal experts, and informal dialogues for knowledge sharing within organizational boundaries [49]. Therefore, internal personalization is proposed to be the best strategy for firms with low environmental knowledge intensity and low organizational IS maturity.

H4: When the organizational IS maturity and environmental knowledge intensity of a firm are both low, the internal personalization strategy is the most effective way to improve its KMP.

## 5. Research methodology

## 5.1. Sample and procedure

Data for empirical examination were obtained through a survey conducted in Korea. For the representativeness of the study sample and the generalizability of the results, we first referred to the ninth edition of the Korean Standard Industrial Classification [56] to select an initial sample of 154 firms whose industrial composition was proportional to that in Korea. In this sampling phase, we ensured that the selected firms had implemented KM initiatives because it is impossible to examine the contextual relationships between KM strategies and KMP by sampling firms that had never initiated any KM attempt and thus had no records of KMP. We communicated with the managers in charge of KM in the 154 firms. We explained the purpose of this study and the contents of the questionnaires, and then asked the managers to select 5 to 10 employees with backgrounds that make them eligible to answer the survey questions. We asked the managers to choose multiple respondents because KM strategies and KMP are firm-level phenomena that are better assessed by multiple raters across different ranks, functions, ages, gender, organizational tenures, and years in focal industries [57]. We then visited the headquarters of these firms, personally distributed 738 survey questionnaires, and collected data on site. To ensure confidentiality and minimize socially desirable responses, enclosed with each questionnaire was a joint researcher-company cover letter stating that respondents should correspond only with researchers for survey return, their firms would not have access to individual ratings, and aggregated results without firm identification would be reported.

Of the 738 survey questionnaires collected from 154 firms, we filtered out 78 responses from 13 firms, which included unreasonable numbers of missing values for key study variables or failure to meet the adequate level of index for interrater reliability within firm, $r _ { \mathrm { w g ( j ) } }$ [58], yielding a total of 660 survey sets from 141 firms for hypothesis testing (raters per firm: $\scriptstyle M = 4 . 6 8 ,$ SD=1.09, $\scriptstyle m i n = 2$ , max=10). KM strategies and KMP are firm-wide decisions, practices, and outcomes. Thus, the unit of analysis for examining KM strategy-KMP relationship in specific contexts was held at the firm level. In addition, multiple individual ratings on a study variable in each firm were aggregated to obtain an average score to represent firm-level property. Details on size and age of the 141 firms and demographic information of the 660 respondents are presented in Table 4. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed no significant differences in study variables based on the number of raters in each firm nor across respondents’ rank, age, gender, organizational tenure, and years in the industry. All final data were pooled for analyses.

## 5.2. Measurement

An initial version of the survey instrument was vetted through a series of personal interviews with five academic experts in KM. The instrument was then administered for a pilot test to 56 graduate students in the Master of Business Administration programs at two top-tier universities in Korea. The respondents had at least three years of KM-related work experience. The multi-phase development of the instrument resulted in a number of refinement and restructuring. The process helped establish the initial face and internal validity of the measures, which confirmed the suitability of the questionnaire for studying realworld phenomena [59]. The Korean version of all measures adapted from prior studies was created by following Brislin’s [60] translation-back-translation procedure. Unless otherwise indicated, each measure was assessed on a seven-point response scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The structure of all measures used in this study is shown in Appendix.

## 5.2.1. Knowledge management strategies

Given the absence of an established measure for the four types of KM strategies, we developed single-item measures for KM strategies by combining two KM dimensions: knowledge type [7] and knowledge origin [9]. For example, sample items state “My company relies on KM strategy that aims at both codifying organizational knowledge through system and accessing external sources for knowledge” for external codification and “My company relies on KM strategy that aims at both personalizing organizational knowledge through human networks and developing knowledge from internal sources” for internal personalization.

Klein and Rai [61] identified conditions wherein single-item measures are acceptable in the IS research domain: (a) when the concentration of the respondents needs to be maintained by shortening the length of the survey, (b) when additional questions cause unnecessary redundancies, and (c) when constructs are considered as unambiguous and narrowly focused. In the phase of survey administration, respondents in each firm were required to attend an explanation and Q&A session before answering the survey. We provided in-depth explanations and examples of how organizational knowledge is accumulated through system or personal network (knowledge type) and how it is internally or externally regulated (knowledge origin). This explanation was followed by a Q&A session to further ensure that the respondents have an accurate understanding of KM strategies. This procedure also helped respondents focus on the survey by enhancing their precise understanding of the subject and preventing boredom resulting from inter-item redundancy. In addition, we presented the four single-item measures in parallel in the survey to help respondents differentiate one KM strategy from the others and to determine the relative dominance of certain KM strategies to the others in a firm [62]. Thus, respondents in a firm indicated the extent to which their firm uses each of the four KM strategies. Using single-item measures for the four types of KM strategies seemed acceptable when such procedures and questionnaire design are to be

#

considered. Moreover, empirical evidence for the reliability of single-item measures was estimated as detailed below.

We used various within-firm interrater reliability indices, namely, $r _ { \mathrm { w g ( j ) } } , \eta ^ { 2 } ;$ , and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs: ICC(1) and ICC(2)), for three reasons. First, because single-item measures were used to assess KM strategies, we could not estimate within-rater, internal consistency reliability coefficients, such as Cronbach’s alpha. Instead, we used interrater reliability indices for the single-item measures to determine whether multiple responses to a single-item measure within a firm are consistent and reliable. Nunnally [59, p. 191] stated that measurement reliability represents the extent to which the instrument for a construct is intended to be repeatable and stable, and to generate the same results over different conditions (e.g., either across different raters or with multiple items, or both). Thus, using interrater reliability indices for single-item measures is appropriate to ensure reliability of measures in this context. Second, we estimated the within-firm interrater reliability indices to support the aggregation of multiple ratings of a KM strategy and its performance within a firm for producing their firm-level average scores and testing their relationship at the firm level. Third, we estimated all interrater reliability indices based on the recommendation of Klein and Kozlowski [57] because each index has its own strengths and weaknesses and there is no single best way.

$r _ { \mathrm { w g ( j ) } }$ is an index of within-unit interrater agreement [58]. Thus, the $r _ { \mathrm { w g ( j ) } }$ index was calculated separately for each unit. An average $r _ { \mathrm { w g ( j ) } }$ value across units is typically reported [57]. The criterion for $r _ { \mathrm { w g ( j ) } }$ for supporting within-unit agreement is above 0.70 [58]. Because within-unit agreement does not necessarily represent between-units variance, other indices comparing within- and between-variance, such as $\eta ^ { 2 }$ , ICC(1), and ICC(2), with F-test significance levels derived from ANOVA, are also used supplementarily [57].

$\eta ^ { 2 }$ is an estimation of the relative amount of between-unit vs. within-unit variance across an entire sample of units. The greater between-unit variance relative to within-unit variance is, the higher $\eta ^ { 2 }$ will be. Bliese [63] noted that $\eta ^ { 2 }$ is influenced by unit size (the number of raters in the unit); the smaller the unit is, the higher $\eta ^ { 2 }$ value can be obtained. ICC(1) provides an estimate of the proportion of the total variance of a measure accounted for by unit membership. Unlike $\eta ^ { 2 } ,$ , this index is not influenced by unit size [63]. A significant F-test and the suggested acceptable range of 0.05 to 0.50 are often used to determine whether unit-level property exists in individual responses [57]. ICC(2) estimates the reliability of unit mean values and is a function of ICC(1) and unit size [63]. The greater ICC(1) or unit size is, the higher ICC(2) value is obtained. Like other reliability measures, ICC(2) values are commonly considered acceptable for aggregation if they are equal to or exceed 0.70 [57].

Next, we operationalized organizational IS maturity as a dichotomous variable. ## 7. Discussion and implications

This study sought to apply a contingency perspective to the impacts of KM strategy on KMP. Drawing on the TOE framework, we identified two major contextual factors (i.e., organizational IS maturity and environmental knowledge intensity) and proposed multiple contingencies with four KM strategies (i.e., external codification, internal codification, external personalization, and internal personalization strategies), based on the KBV, to hypothesize the distinctive advantage of each strategy in a given situation. This study aims to answer how the effect of KM strategies on KMP differ depending on the degree of environmental knowledge intensity and the level of organizational IS maturity. On the one hand, knowledge-intensive firms are much more susceptible to the logic of knowledge economy than traditional firms. In this study, the KBV was supplemented by external characteristics. On the other hand, organizational IS maturity has been commonly recognized as a key enabler. This is because organizational capabilities and KM-related infrastructures and processes are dependent on it. Thus, this study theoretically extended the existing KBV by including multiple contextual factors, such as environmental knowledge intensity and organizational IS maturity, based on the TOE framework.

We interpreted the results from the contingency perspective. First, as hypothesized, external codification strategy led to the best KMP for firms with high knowledge intensity and IS maturity, while other KM strategies did not have any effect on KMP in the same context. Results indicated that the expected benefit of external codification strategy can be realized more effectively when organizations had both high IS maturity and high knowledge intensity. Firms in this situation were able to identify and absorb external codified knowledge, combine it with internal knowledge, transform tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge, and then transfer the accumulated knowledge to organization members through their system-oriented channels [48, 50]. Second, test results offered clear evidence regarding internal codification as the most appropriate strategy for organizations with high IS maturity but low knowledge

#

intensity. As noted earlier, firms in this category generally deal with routine activities and prefer incremental innovations to radical ones [29, 53]. Thus, they tend to develop and accumulate their internal knowledge using high organizational IS capabilities. Third, the results supported our expectation that firms with low IS maturity and high knowledge intensity would be more successful with external personalization strategy. Firms with lower IS capabilities are likely to use human-based networks as main knowledge sources [24]. Moreover, as these firms are working in a knowledge intensive environment, they attempt to bring in knowledge from outside sources through face-to-face interactions and transfer this knowledge across organizations [25]. Finally, the result for internal personalization strategy did not support the last hypothesis from the contingency perspective, as shown in Table 8. A possible explanation for the insignificant effect is that its role is an underlying strategy in realizing effects of the other three strategies, regardless of environmental knowledge intensity and organizational IS maturity [7]. In other words, internal personalization strategy can be suggested as the first initiative in effectively implementing other KM strategies, rather than as an independent strategy. This is because emerging firms must focus on the internal personalization strategy as they initiate and facilitate creation and sharing at their KM status, which facilitates the development of internally codified knowledge base via technology or the establishment of reliable inter-firm alliances for external knowledge sources [7]. Another possibility for the unexpected finding lies in understanding the organizational climate in Korea, which might be explained by the concept of “stickiness.” This concept refers to the difficulty involved in transferring internal knowledge within a firm [72]. The cost of stickiness is relatively low when organizational members share common values and engage in similar practices, as in the case of Korean firms with high collectivism orientation [73]. In fact, employees in Korean organizations are believed to be even stickier than those in Japanese firms [74]. These Korean firms are good at person-based internal sourcing of knowledge without spending significant amount of resources. Thus, the internal personalization strategy in the collectivistic context may not be considered an independent KM strategy. Instead, this internal personalization strategy may be a natural process that provides the foundation for implementing KM strategies [23].

## 7.1. Implications for research

Considering the inconsistent findings of prior studies regarding the effect of KM strategies on KMP, this study emphasized the importance of multiple contingencies in developing or selecting an effective KM strategy. The support found for the contingency perspective was entirely consistent with theories on strategy, human resource management, and inter-organizational relationships. Given the interdependence perspective is more meaningful than a universalistic perspective, which has been adopted by prior studies on KM strategy [3, 4]. Our findings indicated that the contingency perspective clearly explained the KM phenomenon with regard to KM strategies and their effect on KMP. Therefore, a contingency explanation is required when the effects of strategy dimensions on outcomes are dependent on environmental situations.

Strategic alignment between business environments and IS-oriented capabilities has been considered as a critical issue in literature on organizational strategy, particularly the strategic fit with environmental factors in the organizational research [75, 76] and the strategic fit with IS resources-oriented capabilities in IS research [77, 78]. In this study, we theoretically reply on the TOE framework to explain the alignment of KM strategy from the contingency perspective, using key external and internal contextual factors that bridge the missing link in strategic KM research [79].

We further elaborated the TOE framework through theoretical synthesis. This means that the theoretical incorporation of KBV into the TOE framework in the current study helped specify the TOE contexts, thus restraining the freedom to differentiate among organizational context factors. This integration generated theoretical advancement that evolves and appropriates the TOE framework in the strategic KM research, while maintaining the simplicity of classification of the framework. In addition, this study further extended the TOE framework by including a performance-based outcome from an organizational innovation, that is, KMP. In predicting the improvement of KMP, the organizational value created by successfully adopting KM helped extend the application of the TOE framework, instead of only explaining organizational KM adoption. We believe that this theoretical attempt might advance our understanding of a contingency perspective toward strategic KM alignment. This can be achieved by predicting KMP that involves something more complex than isolating specific KM strategy factors, such as a more holistic configuration view to fill the gap caused by the missing link in strategic KM research [79].

Previous research on organizational effectiveness focused on the effects of different organizational contexts, such as firm size, industry type, firm choices, and performance [80]. The legacy of this early approach is still apparent in recent strategy research, wherein such variables are treated as “controls.” Two distinct perspectives later emerged in the intellectual tradition of strategy research, namely, universalistic and contingency perspectives. The former suggests that a single best approach to managing knowledge adopted by all firms in all circumstances exists. Accordingly, researchers have attempted to identify “best practices” or those processes that positively affect firm performance [81]. The latter suggests that neither structural features nor firm choices directly affect performance; instead, contextual features moderate the effectiveness of choices or work practices [82]. Therefore, we took multiple contingencies by integrating environmental knowledge intensity and organizational IS maturity, grounding on the TOE paradigm, in order to explain contingent effects of KM strategies on KMP according to different hypothesized contexts of firms. Therefore, this study contributes to the contingency framework in KM literature by considering key external and internal contexts that a firm should consider in developing or selecting the best KM strategy.

## 7.2. Implications for practice

KM strategies are linked to the practical implications for managers who make decisions related to the selection of KM strategies. Their KM strategy choices represent alternate ways by which firms can increase the value of KM in meeting corporate objectives. This study offers contingent guidance to managers on how to best use different KM strategies in different organizational contexts. Our findings indicate that managers must understand the effect of each KM strategy on KMP from the contingency perspective, warning against a firm’s focus on all four KM strategies, which might be an exhausting approach for KM. Firms should be more efficient and realistic in implementing KM and in selecting an appropriate KM strategy, which can be achieved by considering their environmental knowledge intensity

#

and organizational IS maturity. In other words, managers must simultaneously consider knowledge type and origin with external and internal contextual situations in order to reflect the knowledge economy in the business environment under a limited resource condition. The findings encourage managers to train their focus on a KM strategy not as a decisional island but as a critical link to business environments and resource barriers in organizations.

Another implication is that the contingent KM strategies identified in this study provide organizations with a benchmark against which they can compare their own KM strategies. Unlike prior KM strategy studies that focused on knowledge-intensive industries/organizations and excluded traditional industries/organizations, this study relied on an alternative sector-specific categorization of the environmental knowledge intensity of firms [28]. This approach provided more legitimate contingent guidelines to both knowledge-intensive and traditional firms for their best choice of KM strategy. In investigating the effects of KM strategies, we also recognized organizational IS maturity not as an explainer but as an internal contextual factor in relation to the contradictions on IS effectiveness in managing organizational knowledge. These contradictions could explain why many IS-focused KM efforts face difficulties in building effective KM environments. Therefore, this study offers firms a meaningful lesson, which states that the effectiveness of KM strategy in managing organizational knowledge depends on both environmental knowledge intensity and organizational IS maturity for external and internal fits [27] of KM success.

The final implication for practitioners is that internal personalization strategy does not have any significant impact on KMP. Such finding suggests that this KM strategy may not be directly effective as an independent strategy, unlike the other three strategies, and that the former should be considered as an underlying requirement to realize the expected benefits of other KM strategies in the firm [7] across different contexts hypothesized in this study. For example, a firm can generate valuable knowledge, experience, and networks of internal experts through a KM practice of internal personalization strategy, that is, COPs of which reciprocal members share similar interests in their organizational tasks and mutually interact by meeting face-to-face, and by directly negotiating, communicating, and coordinating with one another [55]. This situation can cause practical application of innovative ideas to overcome timespace limitations through constant learning activities of COP. Firm members can conveniently discover appropriate knowledge, which is customized for each worker and task. Eventually, a “COP culture” can become a means for organizational innovations. As a result, created knowledge and workers’ experiences are efficiently codified and standardized to improve KM process of organizations. In such a way, internal personalization strategy might facilitate the exchange of knowledge among firms’ internal experts specializing in different fields, thus serving as a cornerstone for the effectiveness of other strategies. In addition, this unexpected finding might compromise the conflicting findings between prior studies. Some studies suggested that firms accumulating and leveraging internal tacit knowledge tend to outperform competitors pursing other KM strategies [19, 20], whereas the other studies argue that external knowledge sourcing [18] or explicit knowledge creation [4, 8] is the key enabler for a competitive advantage. ## 7.3. Limitations and future research directions

We now turn to the limitations of this study, some of which offer opportunities for future research. First, the metrics of KM strategy and KMP clearly require further development. Stronger metrics may evince clearer distinctions across strategies, because the instruments suggested by earlier studies provide a starting point for assessing strategy dimensions and the performance of KM strategies. For example, expectations regarding internal personalization strategy were not supported by data, thus suggesting the need for stronger metrics to assess KMP, alternate theory, or the study of our proposed model within an alternate institutional environment. As KM becomes more complex, researchers need to develop more sophisticated metrics to assess the success of KM. Nonetheless, the nature of the metrics alone may not completely account for the perceived relationships among the dimensions of KM strategy and those between KM strategies and their contextual situations. Different KM strategies can eventually enhance firm performance in different ways, although appropriate contextual factors are considered. Longitudinal research on KM strategies and their effects on KMP can tease apart such cumulative effects.

Second, it is possible that the strategy dimensions in this study drawn from the KBV are inadequate in completely specifying a KM strategy. Furthermore, in addition to the two contextual factors identified in this study, other conditions may have contingent effects on the relationship between KM strategies and their performance. Further theoretical development and organizational practice can thus stimulate the exploration of such new contingent variables. A deeper revelation of the interaction between KM strategies and their situational factors would also be valuable in enhancing the understandability of KM practice in organizations.

Finally, the findings of this study are limited by its cross-sectional design, in which the ratings of both independent and dependent variables have been collected from a single source. However, the likelihood of a common source bias might be reduced in this study, because testing the hypothesized relationships at the organizational level by aggregating individual ratings would cause most individuallevel random errors and sources of bias to cancel each other [83]. Nonetheless, replications and extensions of our findings using longitudinal designs with different rating sources are needed. These longitudinal studies can also benefit from including financial measures of firm performance to examine the causal relationships among KM strategies, KMP, and firm performance. Results of this study also include regional biases because data have been collected solely in Korea. Thus, the results require careful interpretation and replication in other industries and countries in order to improve the generalizability of our findings.

## 8. Conclusions

With the increasing attention focused on KM strategy, organizations must effectively recognize the importance of the contingency perspective on their actions. This study expands KM strategy research by theoretically developing an advanced multiple contingency model, which is aligned with both external and internal contexts. At the same time, this study contributes to this research area by providing valuable practical suggestions to managers in selecting KM strategies that can be successfully applied in different external and internal contexts. The results of this study highlight the importance of the fact that organizations must consider external and internal contextual factors in developing their KM strategies, in order to fully realize the benefits of KM strategies. Results highlighted distinctive KM strategies accruing from two KM dimensions (i.e., knowledge type and origin) and comprise two contingent contextual situations (i.e., environmental knowledge intensity and organizational IS maturity). KM strategy choices represent alternate ways through which organizations increase the value of KM in meeting corporate objectives.

## Research Highlights

z A contingency model is proposed to investigate the effects of four KM strategies on KM performance.

z The effectiveness of KM strategies depends on external and internal contextual conditions.

z KM strategies have a significant association with KM performance in their hypothesized contexts.

z This study offers contingent guidance to managers on how to best use different KM strategies in different organizational contexts.

z The contingent KM strategies provide organizations with a benchmark against which they can compare their own KM strategies.
