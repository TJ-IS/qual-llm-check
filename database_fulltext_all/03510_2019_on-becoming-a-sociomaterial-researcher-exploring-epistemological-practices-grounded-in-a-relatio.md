# On becoming a sociomaterial researcher: Exploring epistemological practices grounded in a relational, performative ontology

![](/api/attachments/FHFGNQHF/fulltext/images/68410858bfc4747ebf61b4377db2c0038d9fbc279e732c25a56661bc92d8563a.jpg)

Lotta Hultin

## A R T IC L E IN E O

Keywords: Sociomateriality Post-humanist methodology Epistemology Performativity Ontology of becoming Entanglement Ethnography Practice of research

## A B S T R A C T

What is the role of the researcher in a world that is continuously enacted and recon<sup>fi</sup>gured in sociomaterial practices, a world in which subject and object, structure and agency, body and mind, knower and known, are assumed to be ontologically inseparable? In this article, I explore this question by drawing on my own experiences of reconsidering essentialist and re presentationalist assumptions, and becoming a sociomaterial researcher. My exploration draws on my experiences of conducting a qualitative longitudinal case study at the Swedish Migration Board. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, I show what it can mean to invite materiality into interviews, examine the conditions of possibility to become in certain ways by tracing the genealogy of practices, and engage with data relationally rather than categorically. By accounting for my experience of working through these practices, I aim to develop and articulate an understanding of what the ontological position underlying a sociomaterial approach implies for epistemology, and of how we can act (or, rather, intra-act) more creatively and responsibly as sociomaterial researchers. Moreover, I highlight di<sup>f</sup>erences in the kinds of knowledge that a sociomaterial approach grounded in relational and performative onto-epistemologies, as opposed to a socio-material approach, grounded in critical realism, produce about the unfolding of organizational practi ces speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the practices unfolding in the reception area of the Swedish Migration Board. The paper contributes to the current debate on sociomaterial approaches, and in particular to the development of practices available to draw upon for researchers taking a sociomaterial approach.

## 1. Introduction

Grounded in a long tradition, the assumption that the human and the social are separate from the material or the technical” has permeated, and still permeates, the majority of organizational and information systems research (Lorino, Tricard, & Clot, 2011; Manning, 2015; Vannini, 2015). Another way to put this is to say that the majority of research operates with a being ontology in which the world is constituted by, or comprised of, ontologically distinct entities (social and technical) that interact or connect in order to produce organizational phenomena. A relational and performative sociomaterial approach operates with a di<sup>f</sup>erent ontology. Its underlying assumption is that there are no beings, social or material, no subjects and objects, no research and researched. Rather, all assumed actors, entities and categories are understood as relational enactments or material con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of the world's becoming (Barad, 2007).

While studies assuming this approach have made signi<sup>fi</sup>cant theoretical advancements, showing how we can conceptualize the social and the material as ontologically entangled, and organizational phenomena as enactments in material-discursive practices, the development of research methods grounded in a relational ontology has failed to keep up with these advancements (Cecez-

Kecmanovic, Galliers, Henfridsson, Newell, & Vidgen, 2014). While studies assuming a sociomaterial approach closely attend to materiality and thus, take material (or sociomaterial) agency more seriously than in much of the previous IS literature—the materiality, nevertheless, is often fairly mute, typically only represented by human spokespersons. As pointed out in previous reviews of the literature on sociomateriality (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014: Jones, 2014), this may be because authors have tended to use much the same methods as we have used for examining IS phenomena from an interpretivist (but not relational) perspective.

In this paper, I explore what the ontological position underlying a sociomaterial approach (Barad, 2007; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008, 2015) implies for epistemology. In other words, what does this position imply for the researcher going out in the <sup>fi</sup>eld or working to formulate her insights in a journal article? How can the e<sup>f</sup>ects of agency be understood when it cannot be ascribed to speci<sup>fi</sup>c actors? How can the meaning of the acts of the material be understood when it cannot express them in language? How can words be imposed on a process that does not conform to researchers' practical needs for entities (nouns) with attributes (adjectives) and that consequently calls for the analysis of relationships among distinct entities?

I explore these questions by drawing on my own experiences of becoming a sociomaterial researcher. These experiences include beginning my doctoral studies by accepting essentialist assumptions and learning how to create representative accounts of more or less causal relations, interactions, connections between actors and entities in a reality ‘out there’. It includes my experiences of gradually becoming attracted to and convinced by the assumptions and potential of the sociomaterial approach, but simultaneously, realizing the many challenges that the shift from an ontology of being to an ontology of becoming implies in terms of developing new conceptual vocabularies, new epistemological practices, and new positions from which we can evaluate what ‘good’ research is. Finally, it includes my experiences of meeting these challenges, and exploring what it means to become in entanglement with my research, rather than being positioned outside it.

My exploration draws specifically on my experiences of conducting a qualitative longitudinal case study at the Swedish Migration Board. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, I'm going to show what it can mean to invite materiality into interviews (Aagard & Matthiesen, 2016; Bodén, 2014; Nordstrom, 2013; Simonsen Abildgaard, 2018; Riach, Rumens, & Tyler, 2016), to examine the conditions of possibility to become in certain ways by tracing the genealogy of practices (Butler, 1997a, b; Foucault, 1991), and to engage with data relationally rather than categorically (Nordstrom, 2017). By accounting for my experimentation with these practices, I aim to develop and articulate an understanding of what the ontological position underlying a sociomaterial approach implies for epistemology and to show how the onto-epistemological research practices that I have enacted have come to produce di<sup>f</sup>erent kinds of knowledge. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, I aim to highlight the di<sup>f</sup>erences in the kinds of knowledge that a sociomaterial approach grounded in a relational or becoming ontology (Introna, 2013; Orlikowski, 2007, 2010; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008; Scott & Orlikowski, 2014), as opposed to a socio-material approach, grounded in critical realism (Leonardi, 2013; Mutch, 2013; Tunçalp, 2016), produce about the unfolding of organizational practices—speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the practices unfolding in the reception area in a unit of the Swedish Migration Board. Im portantly, my aim is not to provide clear guidelines for how to conduct ‘sociomaterial research’, but to share a confessional account (Schultze, 2000) of my journey of becoming a sociomaterial researcher. In assuming that the production of knowledge is an ongoing creative process of co-construction (Bright, 2018; Nordstrom & Happel-Parkins, 2016; Nordstrom, 2013, 2017; Clark/Keefe & Haines, 2019; Vannini, 2015 Law & Urry, 2004; Shotter, 2006), I argue that we need to, and show how we can, develop practices that allow researchers to be more sensitive to how organizational realities, and their experience of them, are sociomaterially con<sup>fi</sup>gured and to act (or, rather, intra-act) more creatively and responsibly as sociomaterial researchers.

Before I present the setting for, and the methodological challenges involved in, my <sup>fi</sup>eldwork, I will say a few more words about the position underlying a sociomaterial approach grounded in a relational or becoming ontology (in particular agential realism) and how it di<sup>f</sup>ers from a socio-material approach grounded in critical realism.

## 2. Review of the literature

## 2.1. Sociomateriality and the sociotechnical paradigm

Research assuming a socio-material approach grounded in critical realism work within the socio-technical paradigm of IS studies (Bijker, 1997; Mumford, 1983, 2006; Trist & Bamforth, 1951). Studies within this paradigm are grounded in a being (or essentialist) ontology and committed to the idea that the social and the technical are ontologically distinct and separate phenomena, entities, or objects that interact or connect in order to produce organizational phenomena. They also share a commitment to representationalism and claim to produce knowledge by representing assumed pre-existing beings. From this perspective, the quality of a study is measured by the accuracy of the account in representing a certain phenomenon, and its generalizability to explain similar phenomena.

Sociomaterial studies grounded in a relational or becoming ontology (e.g. agential realism) on the other hand, aim to move away from a view of materiality as something distinct, bounded, and separate from human agency and intentionality, to an understanding of it as entangled with and thus, deeply co-constitutive of, agentic action and organizational realities (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014: Hultin & Introna, 2018: Hultin & Mähring, 2017: Introna & Haves, 2011: Nvberg, 2009: Orlikowski, 2007: Orlikowski & Scott, 2008. 2013; Scott & Orlikowski, 2014). From this perspective, the human and the material are not pre-formed substances and their relationship not limited to a dualistic interaction. Rather, the relationship is one of entanglement, in which the human and the material are an ensemble of continuously performed intra-relations (Barad, 2007; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008). In this view, agency is not given in advance of action in a cause-and-e<sup>f</sup>ect relationship, nor does it belong to any one actor. Rather, agency is constantly forming and transforming from within the action itself (Barad, 2007; Ingold, 2016). Contrasting studies within the sociotechnical paradigm, which consider agency as spatial phenomena exercised in the relationships between distinct actors, sociomaterial studies consider how agency emerges, transforms, and enacts as a temporal and performative <sup>fl</sup>ow of practices (Barad, 2007; Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2016; Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014; Hultin & Introna, 2018; Ingold, 2016; Introna, 2018).

The key to understanding how sociomaterial studies di<sup>f</sup>er from socio-material studies, and other studies produced within the socio-technical paradigm, is the notion of performativity. Butler (1993) suggests that performativity cannot be understood outside of a process of iterability, a regularized and constrained repetition of norms. And this repetition is not performed by a subject” (Butler, 1993, p. 95). Thus, through the notion of performativity, it is possible to understand how a subject neither precedes nor follows the process of subjection, but how she rather becomes through the iterative enactment of subject positions that presuppose the subject and the practices that they seek to describe (Borgerson, 2005; Butler, 1997a, b). In other words, the performative <sup>fl</sup>ow of practice is what “enables a subject and constitutes the temporal condition for the subject” (Butler, 1993, p. 95). Drawing on Butler's conception of performativity, Barad (2007) attempts to consider with greater care the intimate entanglement of non-human and human elements in the construction of sociomaterial realities (both subject and object positions). In her agential realist account of performativity, she “calls into question the givenness of the di<sup>f</sup>erential categories of ‘human’ and ‘nonhuman,’ examining the practices through which these di<sup>f</sup>erential boundaries are stabilized and destabilized” (Barad, 2007, p. 66). This means that, in a sociomaterial account of performativity, attention is not primarily paid to what actors do, think, or say, but rather to that which provides them with their actions and intentionality, namely ways of acting that are already assumed to be appropriate and legitimate by the circulating <sup>fl</sup>ow of agency through material-discursive practices (Barad, 2007; Gergen & Gergen, 2014; Hultin & Introna, 2018). Thus, in this view, subjects and objects emerge as positions that are both the e<sup>f</sup>ect of prior conditions of possibility for agency, and subsequently conditions the form agency can take—these conditions of possibility are that which is taken up and reiterated in the subject's or object's own acting (Butler, 1997b).

## 2.2. How to study the sociomaterial? Critique, challenges and opportunities

The sociomaterial perspective grounded in a relational or becoming ontology not only implies a new way of understanding the subject-object relationship, it also implies a new understanding of how to explore and account for this relationship—i.e. of our epistemological practices. Because from this perspective, ‘practices of knowing and being are not isolable; they are mutually implicated’ (Barad, 2007; p. 185). This means that we don't obtain knowledge by creating representations at a distance, but rather from a direct material engagement with the world (Barad, 2007; Shotter, 2014). In contrast with a Cartesian metaphysics that retains a duality between mind and body and social and material, viewing knowledge as something attainable using human agency, a performative view of the world acknowledges practices of thinking, observing, experiencing, measuring, theorizing, and knowing as material practices of intra-acting within and as part of the world (Barad, 2007; Introna, 2013; Thiele, 2014). In this view, knowledge is a process that cannot be separated from the practices performed to enact it (Bergson, 2002; Ingold, 2015; Koro-Ljungberg & Mazzei, 2012; Lorino et al., 2011; Sørensen, 2013; Styhre, 2004). This means that as a researcher, I am neither an outside objective observer of the phenomenon of study, nor am I merely a situated subjective onlooker. Instead, I am always and already part of this phenomenon (Barad, 2003). I am productive and constitutive of it. In short: I as a researcher, and my research practices do not represent reality, they enact it as such. Consequently, a relational sociomaterial perspective grounded in for example agential realism does not acknowledge the separation of epistemology from ontology. Rather it thinks of these as already entangled, as onto-epistem-ology (Barad, 2007).

As this view di<sup>f</sup>ers, not only from the assumptions underlying the sociotechnical paradigm, but from the assumptions upon which most research is based, a shift to this onto-epistem-ology constitutes a challenge for researchers adopting it. Researchers attempting to explore the empirical world by adopting a sociomaterial ontology (grounded in agential realism) have reported di<sup>fi</sup>culties in doing so (Faulkner & Runde, 2012; Wagner, Newell, & Piccoli, 2010; Mueller, Renken, & van Den Heuvel, 2016). For example, Wagner et al. (2010) report: “We found it quite challenging to keep the material in the storyline without falling from one side to another — either leaving the material realm unexamined, or emphasizing the agency of the material to the detriment of understanding the entangled practice” (p. 292–293). The challenge of developing and adopting epistemological practices consistent with the relational ontology underlying sociomateriality is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the scarce number of studies doing it. Although many studies explicitly position their sociomaterial approach in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c ontology (e.g., agential realism), most studies adopt the same methods we use for examining IS phenomena from an interpretivist (but not relational) perspective (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014).

The sociomaterial onto-epistemology grounded in agential realism has also received criticism by scholars arguing that the ontology is not only di<sup>fi</sup>cult to apply in practice, but also is less powerful than other approaches within the sociotechnical paradigm (e.g. a<sup>f</sup>ordances) in explaining the constitution of organizations (Leonardi, 2013; Mutch, 2013; Tunçalp, 2016). While Leonardi (2013) and other scholars such as Mutch (2013), Mueller et al. (2016), and Tunçalp (2016) argue that other ontological and epistemological positions, such as critical realism or a structural-functionalist perspective (Mueller et al., 2016; Parsons & Shils, 1951), make us better positioned to explain organizational processes and phenomenon, I join the group of scholars suggesting that the organizational research community and, overall, the production of knowledge, gain from developing and experimenting with a diverse set of ontological and epistemological positions and practices (Koro-Ljungberg & Mazzei, 2012; Law, 2004; Law & Urry, 2004; Scott & Orlikowski, 2013). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, I argue, in line with other scholars (Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2016; Elbanna, 2016; Scott & Orlikowski, 2013), that the methodological uncertainty surrounding the sociomaterial approach grounded in a relational or becoming ontology cannot be resolved by simply departing from its underlying ontological assumptions and by adopting epistemo logical practices rooted in a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent (mostly representationalist) sociological and philosophical thinking. Rather, I argue that we need to practically experiment with approaches, tools and vocabularies in order to develop ways of doing/being research/er that do not reproduce essentialist and dualistic worldviews and representational assumptions of what is interesting and good research. The continuation of this article is an attempt to do exactly this.

In what follows, I present the context of my exploration of, and experimentation with, the sociomaterial onto-epistemology that challenges dualistic (and representational) assumptions of organizational reality and research. I will not present “solutions,” nor is it my aim to <sup>fi</sup>nd the “right approach.” Rather, I hope to be able to increase awareness of what assuming a relational or becoming ontology can imply for our epistemological practices, and the production of knowledge, and to inspire greater diversity in the epistemological practices of researchers.

## 3. Criminal or customer? From representing to sensing the person behind the window

My exploration is based on my experiences conducting a qualitative, longitudinal case study as part of my doctoral project. This study, which was conducted between November 2012 and April 2014, investigates the development of Lean management (referred to simply as Lean) at the Swedish Migration Board (MB). The MB is the central authority for the implementation of migration policy in Sweden, managing asylum applications, making asylum decisions, and defending those decisions in appeals court. It is also responsible for managing integration and settlement for those granted asylum. The MB has approximately 7000 employees (the number as of November 2015) who handle, in a given year, anywhere from approximately 20,000 to over 80,000 individual asylum seekers.

Since 2009, the MB has worked to implement and continuously develop the Lean management philosophy (Womack & Jones, 2010) in order to shorten the processing and waiting time for asylum applicants and to increase quality and <sup>fl</sup>exibility throughout the asylum process. This paper draws on my experiences from conducting <sup>fi</sup>eldwork at the reception unit in Stockholm. The primary purpose was to explore how the new Lean work model had changed the everyday work of the case o<sup>fi</sup>cers, their meetings and relationships with the applicants, and their overall professional sense of selves. In other words, I was interested in how the subjects of the case o<sup>fi</sup>cers (and, implicitly, of the asylum applicants) “came to <sup>fi</sup>gure” (Suchman, 2007) in the new Lean operational practices.

Before the Stockholm reception unit relocated to its current facilities in 2010, the reception unit was (since 1998) housed in a former police station, to which its operations had been transferred because the property in which they previously resided had been sold and converted into housing. The reception area was equipped with eight separate counters, each counter responsible for handling cases from a speci<sup>fi</sup>c region, and each counter using a distinct queue ticket system, designated by a speci<sup>fi</sup>c color, for the applicants to take their turns.

The photograph in Fig. 1 depicts a family visiting the former reception area in 2009. Visible in the photograph are the separate reception counters equipped with thick glass windows designed to protect persons behind the counter from physical attack. Ac cording to accounts of the sta<sup>f</sup> who worked there, the glass windows were not supposed to be opened during conversations with the applicants.

Shortly after Lean was introduced at the reception unit, the operations moved to new o<sup>fi</sup>ce facilities in a nearby suburb of Stockholm, and this is where I conducted my <sup>fi</sup>eldwork. We will return to this reception area later in the paper.

In the interviews of case o<sup>fi</sup>cers for my study, the contrast between the practices and the atmosphere in the former and the current reception areas is often highlighted as a way to illustrate the change in the orientation of the operational sta<sup>f</sup> over time. In the current reception area, the goal is described as providing good service to the customers (applicants for asylum) through teamwork and continuous, incremental improvement in work routines. This is in sharp contrast with the work practices in the former reception area, which are described as internally oriented and imbued with silo thinking and to some extent with stereotypical ideas regarding the asylum seekers. I sought to explore how these various experiences were con<sup>fi</sup>gured and their di<sup>f</sup>erences in terms of case o<sup>fi</sup>cers ways of relating to the applicants and to themselves as professional subjects. In what follows, I will examine how the various methods that I used to collect and analyze data in my <sup>fi</sup>eldwork have participated in the production of answers to these questions.

## 3.1. Listening to the agentic human and searching the meaning behind the codes

In the course of my <sup>fi</sup>eld work, I conducted in total 67 interviews with case o<sup>fi</sup>cers, team leaders, unit managers, and other executives at the MB and approximately 115 h of observations. Most of the interviews took place during the <sup>fi</sup>rst half part of my <sup>fi</sup>eld work and most of the observation toward the end. This was not an intentional strategy. In the beginning, interviews simply seemed like an e<sup>f</sup>ective method for gaining an understanding of sta<sup>f</sup> members' experiences of Lean, their perceptions of themselves as professional subjects, and the meanings that they ascribed to their everyday work practices. My approach also seemed consistent with the literature that was in<sup>fl</sup>uencing me at the time, which suggests that knowledge is gained through discovery, interpretation, judgment, and ultimate representation of the world (e.g., Glaser & Strauss, 2009; Miles & Huberman, 1994). This approach presupposes agentic subjects “who speak for themselves; subjects capable of knowing others; and subjects in charge of their desires and identi<sup>fi</sup>cations” (Lather, 2009, p. 17). Inspired by this literature, I assumed my role as a qualitative researcher was to assimilate knowledge by interviewing the agentic subjects of study about the objects of study and to analyze the resulting accounts, the words and interpretations, as representations of the world produced by unique, essentialist subjects. Observations, I thought, would then allow me to verify the accounts of the interviewees and to augment them with details and richness by adding an extra (and somewhat more objective) perspective. Alternatively, I thought, observations would allow me to identify inconsistencies between the accounts of the interviewers and the real practices so that I could reveal the cognitive and communicative strategies used by the interviewees in forming, repairing, maintaining, strengthening, or revising their understandings of themselves and the organizational realities in which they were situated (Sveningsson & Alvesson, 2003).

Acknowledging that there might be multiple—overlapping and sometimes con<sup>fl</sup>icting—interpretations of the world of the MB, I saw it as my task as a researcher to provide as versatile and ‘neutral’ representation as possible of the phenomena that I was studying. Thus, I made sure to assemble a representative sample of interviewees and to analyze accounts from informants with di<sup>f</sup>erent experiences and from di<sup>f</sup>erent professional and social categories.

I conducted my interviews based on a semi-structured protocol that developed over time. The questions were designed to capture o<sup>fi</sup>cers' experiences working in the reception area and their ways of making sense of their work. In my initial observations, I focused mainly on the verbal conversations between the o<sup>fi</sup>cers and the applicants and among the o<sup>fi</sup>cers as a team. I recorded numerous internal meetings in order to capture how meanings were constructed and negotiated among team members. The following quotes are extracted from case o<sup>fi</sup>cers' interview accounts and include descriptions of the organization of operational work and the o<sup>fi</sup>cers experiences working in the former reception area.

We were very internally oriented back then. We thought of and talked about how we would be able to perform our work in the best and most convenient way, not what would be best for the applicants.

Instead of viewing the applicants as our common customers, you thought, “no, you don't belong to this unit, we don't talk to you,”

If you met a Congolese who had been a child soldier, it felt like a special and important thing to work at the migration board. If you met a Russian who had been caught by the police, stealing boat engines in the Archipelago, you had somehow a di<sup>f</sup>erent approach to that person.

I remember once when I tried to open the window to hear the applicant better – colleagues told me to close it immediately. Talking to the applicants was perceived as a safety risk.

My analysis of these accounts was highly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the techniques taught in PhD courses on qualitative methods. In these forums. a method that was commonly presented as legitimate and effective for the analysis of interview material was the coding and categorizing of interview transcripts (e.g., Glaser & Strauss, 2009; Miles & Huberman, 1994). Its purpose is to assist researchers in using their cognitive ability to categorize and bring order through the use of concepts and other forms of representations (such as diagrams, tables, etc.). Certain methods, such as those inspired by grounded theory (Corbin & Strauss, 2008; Glaser & Strauss, 2009), assume as their starting point codes originating in the words of the interviewees. Through the practice of so-called “open coding,” the researcher is expected “to break open the data to consider all possible meanings" (Corbin & Strauss. 2008, p. 59). During the second stage, the task of the researcher is to understand how the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order categories <sup>fi</sup>t together so as to suggest more abstract theoretica categories. Finally, in the third stage, the researcher iterates between data and theory more frequently to inform the patterns observed initially and to identify underlying dimensions through which identi<sup>fi</sup>ed categories <sup>fi</sup>t together.

In following this approach, I tried to identify patterns across the accounts, commonalities that indicated some underlying meaning or discourse that would explain the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed pattern. I suggested that, for example, the rather skeptical and sometimes hostile attitudes toward the asylum seekers indicated by the quotes above could be a consequence of the discourse, characterized by an increased criminalization of immigrants and asylum seekers, that was noticeable in media and public debates during this period (the early twenty-<sup>fi</sup>rst century) (Khosravi, 2009, 2010; Schierup & Ålund, 2011) and of the discourse of Lean management (including a customer-oriented view of operations) assumed to permeate the mindset of the interviewees and their interpretations of the past. I could also describe the o<sup>fi</sup>cers' internally-oriented mindset and the practice of keeping the glass windows closed when talking to the applicants as professional and subjective biases, that is, as expressions of the innate agency of subjects making choices that are willed rather than conditioned.

What kind of knowledge about the organizational realities of the reception area, then, did I produce in adopting these analytica methods? First, it is clear that I perpetuated the tendency to understand the interviewee as an autonomous subject acting according to her own intentions and interests or conforming to the dominant order in a workplace that had been developed by managers, operational sta<sup>f</sup>, and (unexpressed) organizational or institutional norms and discourses. As the individual mind and verbal expressions of the more or less autonomous subject became positioned as the primary element of action and analysis, I came to view agency as essentially human, <sup>fl</sup>owing vertically up and down from individual human action and verbal discourse to broader organizational action and institutional discourses.

Importantly, this was not because I did not inquire about materiality in the interviews. My semi-structured interview protoco included questions about the design of the reception space, the purposes and functionality of various technologies and objects, and how they were used in daily operational practices. However, including these materialities through the accounts of the interviewees only acknowledged them as structures or tools exterior to the interviewee, her intentions, agency and experience. For example, the windows separating the case o<sup>fi</sup>cer and the asylum seeker in the old reception do not count for anything substantial, but are rather viewed as a kind of projection screen for socially constructed meanings, a resource or a tool that the o<sup>fi</sup>cer use in operational practices and in the interview to show himself as a particular kind of o<sup>fi</sup>cer, for example one who takes proper safety precautions. When focusing on the words describing the glass windows as a tool, the ontological di<sup>f</sup>erences between subject and object, structure and agency are reproduced and the human subject is positioned as the primary agent capable of transforming discourses and practices.

Second, in following the process of coding as prescribed in grounded theory, I made sense of my data, the operational work practices, the meetings taking place in the reception area, the asylum applicants and the case o<sup>fi</sup>cers as functions of something other than themselves, as symbols or representation of that which some practices or events have in common with others. This analytical method is described by Bergson as the process through which an object is reduced to what is already known, to its “resemblance” to other objects, a categorical and reductionist mode of knowledge (Bergson, 1999; Grosz, 2005). By this, he means that, rather than attempting to excavate the concrete speci<sup>fi</sup>city and particularity of each experience, researchers <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves trying to generalize and impose a common framework on the experiences, thus subsuming the embodied sensations in each as instances of generalized recognizable social categories (Brown, Cromby, Harper, Johnson, & Reavey, 2011). In this process, the researcher emerges as an autonomous, intentional subject, stretching herself toward the material world (the data) and digging behind or beyond or beneath it to identify higher-order meanings, themes, or categories (Barad, 2007; Jones & Jenkins, 2008). With this approach, quality becomes bound to the methodological pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency and accountability of the researcher, which entail convincing an audience that every e<sup>f</sup>ort has been made to <sup>fi</sup>nd a true and accurate re<sup>fl</sup>ection (mirror representation) of the research setting.

## 3.2. Tracing the genealogy of practices by taking the silent ‘material’ actors seriously

From the above description we could be a little too quick to assume that data generated through interviews is built on essentialist and representationalist assumptions and thus, that interviews per se are incompatible with a sociomaterial approach grounded in agential realism. This is not what I, based on my experience and review of the literature, would argue. However, what I realized in the iterative process of collecting and analyzing data, and deepening my reading of the literature is that, if I seek to move beyond representational accounts, I have to expand my understanding of what an interview is and what kind of data it can produce. I need to approach the interview. not as a meeting in which the subiect of study gives an account of the obiect of study. to me as a researcher positioned outside the phenomena of study and the production of data. Rather, I need to view the interview as a relational practice through which the researcher and the researched, subjects and objects, simultaneously become in a relationship of entanglement. Building on insights from previous studies exploring and experimenting with onto-epistemological practices grounded in post-humanist ontologies (Bodén, 2014; Bright, 2018; Fenwick, 2010; Nordstrom, 2013, 2017; Nordstrom & Happel-Parkins, 2016; Simonsen Abildgaard, 2018; Clark/Keefe & Clark/Keefe & Haines, 2019; Taguchi, 2012; Vannini, 2015), I attempted, toward the end of my <sup>fi</sup>eldwork, to conduct interviews in ways that would decenter the agentic human actor and foreground the practices through which this assumed separate human actor became positioned and thus, conditioned to act in certain ways. In other words, I tried to develop techniques that enabled me to better foreground the performativity of sociomaterial practices. Inspired by studies experimenting with the interview from a sociomaterial or a posthumanist position (Bodén, 2013: Nordstrom, 2013: Simonsen Abildgaard. 2018: Riach et al., 2016), I began to incorporate materialities such as photographs and whiteboards into the interviews.

My primary purpose in inviting material artifacts into the interviews was to help the interviewees to provide richer accounts of everyday sociomaterial work practices that they often regarded as insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant and thus, not worth talking about. In the words of Star (1999), my aim was to include the study of ‘boring things’, to show how these, brought alive in the <sup>fl</sup>ow of everyday practice, performatively enact conditions of possibilities to think, act, and become in certain ways. In this regard, the technique was e<sup>f</sup>ective. Especially, when I asked interviewees to account for their experiences of working in the old reception, a picture of the reception counter in this space (see Fig. 1) enabled the interviewees to access memories and to give more detailed descriptions of how the meetings with the asylum applicants unfolded across the counter and how they experienced it. It enabled them to recall the sensations of talking through that glass window and to shed light on details that otherwise would have been regarded as too mundane and insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant to account for. Similarly, talking about the whiteboard (a ‘Lean-tool’ implemented in purpose to facilitate team-based problem-solving) with the board present in the room, prompted interviewees to become more concrete and speci<sup>fi</sup>c about the functionalities, oddities, and frustrations of working, or having worked, with it.

Yeah right, the glass window, it seems quite strange today, talking through those windows, but we thought it was necessary for security reasons back then. …I don't remember any violent incidents, but hiding behind that window somehow made it feel as if we were in need of protection.

(Case O<sup>fi</sup>cer)

The whiteboard makes you see both your and the team's performance clearer. When you stand here with your colleagues around you, it is black on white. It's hard to hide behind the numbers. You can feel quite exposed and uncomfortable.

(Case O<sup>fi</sup>cer)

The incorporation of material objects in the interviews allowed me to shift focus: from an inspection of objects from the outside, to a situated material engagement with them (Barad, 2007). This shift was important in my work to understand the performativity of sociomaterial practices in two ways. First, as the accounts elicited by the engagement with the whiteboard or the photograph from the old reception were often infused with emotions, they enabled me to develop a better sense of how everyday mundane sociomaterial practices mattered and matter in the conditioning of possibilities for subject and object positions to become (re)produced. For example, standing in front of the whiteboard together with the interviewees, and listening to their accounts, I could feel what it was like having your weekly production rate (number of handled asylum cases) displayed on that board. I could feel the expectations, the competition, the stress the uncertainty and the inadequacy of my own performance. In other words, I could sense the becoming of the case o<sup>fi</sup>cer in that speci<sup>fi</sup>c sociomaterial practice (Nordstrom, 2013). This practice can be likened to what other researchers have pointed out as central in our attempts to decenter the human in the production of data, namely to ‘dwell in the sociomaterial world’ so that we can get su<sup>fi</sup>ciently close to the practice at hand and recognize its material and embodied nature (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014; Gheradi, 2012; Nicolini, 2013). Importantly, this material engagement did not necessarily create a more representative account of the team meetings around the whiteboard or of what actually happened in the old reception, but it allowed me to get a situated, embodied and nuanced sense of operational practices, in other words, it allowed me to sense the 'materially felt consequences of entangled patterns and how such consequences ripple across circumstances to enact transformation or variation' (Pomerantz & Raby, 2018, p. 6).

Second, inviting material objects into the interview enabled me to do what Foucault (1991) and Butler (1997a, b) refer to as a genealogy, that is, to account for how certain practices and categories, such as identities, or the management idea of Lean, have become enacted as appropriate and legitimate, and ultimately taken for granted over time (Butler, 1997a, b). The speci<sup>fi</sup>city of practices accounted for by materially engaging with objects in the interviews, enabled me to see and account for how possibilities of transformation were enacted in the arbitrary relational enactments in mundane, every day work practices. For example, it was by noticing the specific situated sociomaterial conditions under which the meetings in the old and in the new reception took place that ] understood how di<sup>f</sup>erences in practices and conceptions emerged, not only through the managers' intentions and decisions to, for example, leanify the business, but through the ways in which historical contingencies, such as being located in a former police station, imparted certain ways of doing and thinking in conditioning the becomings of the case o<sup>fi</sup>cer and the asylum seeker. This genealogical tracing enabled me to understand the meeting between the o<sup>fi</sup>cer and asylum seeker through the window in the old reception, not primarily as an interaction between an authority in need of protection, and a potentially violent person, but as a historically contingent and performative practice enacting those particular subject positions as available and legitimate. A practice that inherited its conditions from the time the building served as a police station and the glass window protected the policeman from the criminal. More generally, the speci<sup>fi</sup>city of practices accounted for through the material engagement with objects, enabled me to see how change in a practice does not necessarily begin with an assumed human intention. but in the situated (and thereby already conditioned) sociomaterial practices, which create the conditionality that made certain practices repeatable (and others not)—often, more or less contingently

This genealogical understanding of change di<sup>f</sup>ers from accounts assuming ontological separation between subject and object, the social and the material (e.g. Leonardi, 2011; Paring, Pezé, & Huault, 2017; Volko<sup>f</sup> & Strong, 2013). While approaches grounded in for example critical realism consider “structures” as exterior constraints or a<sup>f</sup>ordances for the individual to act in relation to, a genealogical account enacts “structures” as more or less enduring iterative repetitions of particular practices. This genealogical account enables us to move beyond the dichotomous view of structure and agency and recognizes how categories such as identities are radically unstable and open to modi<sup>fi</sup>cation and alteration. The sociomaterial perspective has been criticized for not ‘being able to explain why certain actions occur when they do because it focuses so much on how certain actions are performed in practice. Consequently, it becomes di<sup>fi</sup>cult for the analyst to understand what role the sociomaterial plays in the constitution and perpetuation of organizations.’ (Leonardi, 2013, p. 71). Leonardi (2013, p. 71) further argues that ‘The problem with grounding analysis of sociomateriality solely in practice (the realm of action), is that it lacks any consideration of the structural or material precursors to that action’. By assuming a genealogical approach and by working to situate the interview in ways that enable a material engagement, we can create a sociomaterial account of organizing that foregrounds the kind of ‘structure’ that a sociomaterial perspective acknowledges, that is, the performative iterations of practices conditioning possibilities for subjects and objects to become in certain ways. In this account, material-discursive practices ‘not only act “on” the subjects but also, in a transitive sense, enact the subjects into being (Butler, 1997b, p. 13). This means that the assumed subject (and object) emerges as a relational enactment not, however, between the ‘I’ and the ‘other’, in the interaction between two actors modifying each other, nor as causal consequence of agents drawing upon some form of pre-existing structures (such as critical realist accounts would suggest) (Fleetwood, 2014), but as the momentary and historical outcome of an ensemble of speci<sup>fi</sup>c and ongoing – always concrete and mattering – material-discursive practices (Hultin & Introna, 2018). Signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, such ongoing subject (and object) positionings do not transcend their contingent and situated enactment.

## 3.3. Searching the who in the how by following the temporal flow of agency

As I became increasingly intrigued by the sociomaterial perspective and engaged in the discussion of how an approach grounded in agential realism could enable us to decenter the human actor and foreground the performativity of sociomaterial practices, the notion of time and temporality became increasingly important. In deepening my reading of work grounded in a process ontology (Bergson, 2002; Latour, 1988, 2005; Styhre, 2004; Tsoukas & Chia, 2002), I realized the shared roots of, and the clear overlaps between a relational ontology (assumed as the basis for sociomaterial studies) and process metaphysics. As other scholars have pointed out, (e.g. Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2016; Hultin & Introna, 2018; Introna, 2018; Pentland, Pentland, & Calantone, 2017; Shotter, 2006), I could see how the assumptions of a process ontology, and a process oriented vocabulary, could help researchers to account for the entangled relationship between the social and the material in a way that would further bring forth the unique value of a performative sociomaterial perspective. As a result of this, I began to shift focus in my observations, from the sayings, doings, and interactions of primarily human actors, to how the temporal <sup>fl</sup>ow of practice enacted conditions of possibility to speak, act, and interact in certain ways. I will provide an example from my <sup>fi</sup>eldwork in the current reception area. As seen in Fig. 2, this reception, unlike the former reception area, consists of four open counters for general inquiries and three additional glassed counters for handling cash (as is typical in banks). One of the counters (number 7, on the right in Fig. 2) is height-adjustable so that case o<sup>fi</sup>cers can adjust the space between themselves and the applicants. All working desks are adjustable. There is a single ticket system which means that applicants are not sorted into categories based on their origin

During my observations, I sat or stood next to the case o<sup>fi</sup>cers and observed (and, from a sociomaterial perspective, therefore also participated in) the interactions between the o<sup>fi</sup>cers on the one hand and the applicants and interpreters on the other and took notes regarding how the meetings evolved, how such materialities as computers and post-it notes were used, and also incidents that occurred. It sometimes happened that applicants entered the main entrance and, instead of taking a ticket, came straight to the counter, approaching the case o<sup>fi</sup>cers with utterances such as excuse me, could you help me or I just have one question. They did not seem to consider the possibility that they might be disturbing the o<sup>fi</sup>cers working by the open counter, but rather approached it casually, as if checking into a hotel and expecting to be served as a guest or a customer. Why did this particular behavior on the part of the applicant seem appropriate and self-evident? Only <sup>fi</sup>ve years earlier, the applicant standing in front of the o<sup>fi</sup>cer was on the other side of a glass window and <sup>fi</sup>gured, not as a customer with the right to initiate contact and express her interests and needs, but rather as a potential safety risk, as someone who needed to be kept at a distance and separated from the o<sup>fi</sup>cers by a glass barrier.

How can we understand this change in how they understood themselves as subjects (pleading asylum seeker versus customer) and the resulting change in behavior?

Of course, some of the reasons for this change in the ways the actors related to each other may be ascribable to the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the Lean discourse, in which citizens, patients, or immigrants are commonly referred to as customers. It is likewise possible that some particularly visionary and capable manager saw the need to make the operations more e<sup>fi</sup>cient and service-oriented. However, by beginning the inquiry with these pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned human actors, there is a risk of losing sight of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways in which these actors are already performatively con<sup>fi</sup>gured to think and act in certain ways in the temporal and performative <sup>fl</sup>ow of practice (Hultin & Introna, 2018). As Barad (2011) puts it, to start the analysis by taking them (the actors) as granted, is to start the analysis too late.

In the previous section, I described how material engagement with objects enabled interviewees to recall and account for the speci<sup>fi</sup>city of practices, and me as a researcher to more carefully trace the genealogical lines through which certain ways of thinking, doing, and saying have become enacted as appropriate and legitimate in every day practices. I tried to adopt this genealogical approach also in my observations. Here however, my focus was not to study how practices inherited and imparted certain ways of doing and thinking over years, but rather, to attend to how actions conditioned other actions from the moment an asylum seeker entered the reception area and looked around the room to orient herself, until the meeting with the case o<sup>fi</sup>cer was <sup>fi</sup>nished. With this approach, my primary focus was not what happened “between" the reception counter and the applicant (i.e., what they are doing to each other). Rather, I focused on how possibilities for action were conditioned temporally (Butler, 1990; Ingold, 2015) in the <sup>fl</sup>ow of practice in which the reception counter (with or without thick glass windows) is already enacted as appropriate and legitimate. This practice can be likened to the practice that previous research (Bruni, 2005; Bruni, Gherardi, & Parolin, 2007) has referred to as shadowing objects and that requires the researcher to plot the connections among di<sup>f</sup>erent courses of action, and to determine how actions and subjects de<sup>fi</sup>ne each other in relations (Bruni, 2005). However, instead of focusing on subjects and objects and how their actions and interactions conditioned each other spatially, I focused on temporal relations, that is, how situated and contingent material-discursive practices inherited and imparted certain ways of doing and being temporally. This approach enabled me to see how, already before the asylum applicant had interacted with any assumed social or material actor in the reception area, she is positioned and thus conditioned to act in certain ways. For example, as the asylum applicant enters the current reception area and, instead of, as in the old reception, becomes sorted by the queue ticket system and watching case o<sup>fi</sup>cers sit behind the thick glass windows, enters a room with open reception counters similar to any hotel, she is positioned as a subject with the right to initiate contact and express her interests and needs with the expectation of being served. That is, already before any verbal or physica interaction, before any technology has been used, before the applicant has been imbricated (Leonardi, 2011) and has drawn upon any assumed structure, she is positioned to be taken as—and to take herself to be—a certain type of subject.

Previous research has suggested that one way to move beyond the tendency to enact a static picture of the sociomaterial world, that is, of seemingly bounded actors acting and interacting in situated practices, and to instead, study the ‘dynamics of emergence (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014), is to explore practice as a material-discursive <sup>fi</sup>eld of action by zooming-in and zooming-out iteratively (Nicolini, 2009, 2013). This practice begins as an in-depth inquiry in one location (zooming in) and then expends to other locations by following emerging relations (zooming out); zooming in then continues in new locations (Bruni et al., 2007; Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014; Nicolini, 2013). Cecez-Kecmanovic et al. (2014, p. 821) describe that the objective of this practice is to: ‘develop an appreciation and articulate the dynamics of practice by observing and experiencing from di<sup>f</sup>erent angles and perspectives how entities, people and technologies, their boundaries, properties and identities, are continuously performed, what the consequences are and for whom’. My genealogical approach of tracing the conditioning <sup>fl</sup>ow of practice temporally, shares the ambition of this approach and aims to contribute to developing a practice based method (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014; Gheradi, 2012; Nicolini, 2013) by directing our focus to the temporal, rather than spatial enactment of relations. This means that rather than, or in addition to, following or shadowing heterogeneous entities across di<sup>f</sup>erent locations or sites, we follow the practice as it <sup>fl</sup>ows temporally, and how each repetition, and subversive act, conditions the possibilities for subjects and objects to become enacted in certain ways.

Critics of a sociomaterial approach grounded in agential realism have argued that this approach ignores time (Leonardi, 2013; Mutch, 2013). They argue this is problematic, ‘because a lack of temporal <sup>fl</sup>ow in a theory of constitution reduces considerations of structure and institution’ (Leonardi, 2013, p. 67). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Mutch (2013) argues that ‘[agential realism] ignores the role of time in producing particular constellations of position-practices that emerge from the activity of persons, but are not reducible to that activity. Most crucially, the conditions in which such practice occurs were not produced by those now here in the present’ (p. 32). Based on my experience of experimenting with epistemological practices grounded in a relational, performative ontology, I believe this critique is largely built on a misconception (likely caused by an actor centric language that constrain sociomaterial accounts from foregrounding relational becoming (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014; Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2016; Jones, 2014; Kautz and Jensen, 2013; Mazmanian et al., 2014)) of the ontological foundation of sociomateriality and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the notion of performativity. Because with a sociomaterial approach grounded in a relational or becoming ontology such as agential realism, temporal <sup>fl</sup>ow is not lacking. Quite the opposite, it is all there is. There are no subjects or objects or social and material actors that can be studied over time, only performative relational enactments within the temporal <sup>fl</sup>ow of practice. In other words, there are no beings, only becomings. So temporal <sup>fl</sup>ow is certainly there, however, in contrast to a socio-material approach grounded in critical realism, time is not perceived as a linear timeline along which emergent properties, created through past actions, continuously produce the structure that causally shape present actions. Structure , in this view are the norms and assumptions about how to behave in certain situations that are continuously and contingently enacted within the temporal flow of practice (Butler. 1997a. b: Barad. 2007: Hultin & Introna. 2018) This means that asylum seekers and o<sup>fi</sup>cers do not behave di<sup>f</sup>erently in the former police station and the current reception area because historically emergent structures such as the material-discursive properties of the reception desks, glass windows, queue tickets, signs, gestures and so forth cause them to behave di<sup>f</sup>erently, collectively. Rather, in our agential realist account, they act in certain ways (rather than in other ways) because the ongoing situated enactment of material-discursive practices already enact such ways of speaking and doing as appropriate and meaningful ways to be. It is true as the critics say, that a sociomaterial perspective cannot show how practices become sociomaterial (Leonardi, 2013). This is because the ontological assumption is that all practices are always and everywhere already sociomaterial. However, what a sociomaterial perspective can show, when it does not have to bother with drawing lines de<sup>fi</sup>ning and categorizing social and material realms and actors and connecting these with arrows, is the temporal conditions of possibilities for these assumed categories or actors to become and behave in certain ways.

## 3.4. Difraction, attention and intuition: allowing our genealogies to matter

If it is the conditioning <sup>fl</sup>ow of practice that matters, how do we observe and analyze this <sup>fl</sup>ow? How do we understand which practices, subject and object positions are enacted as appropriate and legitimate in the temporal performative <sup>fl</sup>ow of everyday work, if they only exist in their ongoing enactment?

The <sup>fi</sup>eld notes from my observations are full of personal associations, comments, ideas, questions, and expressions of emotion. The observed reality captured in these notes is far from being a distant description of the comings and goings of applicants and the unfolding of routines in the reception; it is a reality in which my previous experiences, memories, ambitions, and feelings have merged to form expressions and descriptions that are constitutive of the data and of the operations of the reception. Most qualitative researchers would recognize that, as subjects already located in their situated bodies, minds and worlds of practice, they cannot escape their already situated and subjected gaze when trying to account for organizational realities. A common suggestion for avoiding or mitigating these situated (subiective') biases is to use a reflexive approach (Alvesson & Sköldberg. 2009). From a performative posthumanist perspective, however, the notions of re<sup>fl</sup>ection and re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, as methods for taking account of theoretical and subjective biases on the investigation, become problematic, since they foster the illusion that there is a correct, mostly <sup>fi</sup>xed position from which the world of practice can be observed (Barad, 2007; Haraway, 1997). In their critique of re<sup>fl</sup>ection, Barad and Haraway suggest di<sup>f</sup>raction as a useful counterpoint to re<sup>fl</sup>ection: both are optical phenomena, but whereas re<sup>fl</sup>ection is about mirroring and sameness, di<sup>f</sup>raction trains our more subtle vision and to attend to the small but consequential di<sup>f</sup>erences (Barad, 2007). While representational analysis (including re<sup>fl</sup>ection) begins from a position outside the observed phenomena and seeks to understand it through a methodological structured interpretation, a di<sup>f</sup>ractive analysis aims at accounting for the materially felt consequences of entangled patterns and how such consequences ripple across circumstances to enact transformation or variation (Barad, 2007; Pomerantz & Raby, 2018). This thinking is similar to that of other scholars grounding their work in a relational or process ontology such as Bergson (1999, 2002), Ingold (2009, 2011, 2015), and Heidegger (1993, 2009). For example, in Bergson's critique against analysis, (including re<sup>fl</sup>ection), he introduces the notion of intuition as a way for the researcher to insert herself in the phenomena, or, as Bergson puts it, being (or rather becoming) “in sympathy with” the object “by an e<sup>f</sup>ort of imagination” so as to know the movement of an object through space “from within” (Bergson, 1999; Coleman, 2008). Similarly, Heidegger (1993), uses the notion of zuhandenheit ( ready-to-hand ) to describe the knowledge gained through practical doing with rather than analyticall thinking about’, a thing such as a tool. Ingold (2011, 2015), in turn, uses the notion of attentionality to describe the experience of, and the knowledge produced through, the engagement in any habitualised practice (such as walking, talking, writing, etc.). Contrasting the notion of intention, attention is not consciously directed by a subject, but is rather emergent in the event, ‘activated by the force of the directionality the event calls forth’ (Manning, 2016, p. 154).

What the notions of di<sup>f</sup>raction, intuition, zuhandenheit, and attentionality have in common is that they encourage us, and work as means, to intra-act with and sense that which is di<sup>f</sup>erent (or di<sup>f</sup>ering) in a practice (Coleman, 2008). To position ourselves as researchers as something other than merely always already subjects, and our participants and their participants' material conditions as something other than merely always already objects. In other words, to engage with data relationally instead of categorically (Nordstrom, 2017). When I reread my <sup>fi</sup>eld notes in the light of this perspective, I realized that the shift from a representational to a performative methodology would not necessarily imply a big shift in the way I was practically conducting my <sup>fi</sup>eldwork and analyzing my data. I realized that I was already, unconsciously, engaging with my data di<sup>f</sup>ractively, intuitively, attentively, in practica doing with’. However, being trained in using representational techniques, I was also trying to limit and reduce this kind of ‘relationa data . Or perhaps more correctly, I had learnt that my thoughts, feelings, imagination and sensory experiences was not data, but something that risked distorting data. I realized that conducting <sup>fi</sup>eldwork and ‘analysis’ grounded in a relational performative ontology would not necessarily imply doing research di<sup>f</sup>erently, but to acknowledge what the research practices and settings were already doing to me. I realized that I have to let go of the assumed researcher ‘behind the study’ and instead, try to understand how the research created me as I tried to create it (Nordstrom, 2017).

In the context of the present study, I realized that this could entail conducting the same form of material engagement as I had asked my interviewees to do. For example, returning again to the photograph in Fig. 1 and this time with Barad's words, ‘di<sup>f</sup>ractively engaging’ with it, or, in Bergson's words ‘imaginatively inserting myself’ into the practice of meeting with someone through the glass window above the reception counter, I am reminded of my own experiences waiting in line for public authorities, such as at a police station to obtain a new passport, an embassy to obtain a visa, or the immigration o<sup>fi</sup>ce when arriving as a visiting scholar. I recall the glass windows through which I have asked for help and under which I have slid documents. As part of these recollections I feel the irritation over long waiting times, over the negligent attitude of a tired o<sup>fi</sup>cer for whom I am just another administrative case. I fee the discomfort of having to stand within marked areas and respond to the wary questions. Feeling moves my initial experience from the objectivity of data to the subjectivity of the actual occasion (Manning, 2015). Importantly, this subjectivity is not that of my preexisting human subject, and my feelings are not to be understood as located in my person but rather in the occasions themselves. Just as thought cannot be placed outside experience (Whitehead, 1929), feeling is a key aspect of the creativity of an occasion in its coming to expression (Manning, 2015). From this perspective, my ‘analysis’ of the asylum applicant approaching the glass window in the old reception, becoming positioned and conditioned to become enacted as a potentially violent person, can be understood as a response created as my genealogy corresponded with the genealogy of the asylum applicant.

Rather than positioning myself as a <sup>fi</sup>xed point in time from which I observe, connect and categorize data, this practice implies allowing my own genealogy to matter. Just as the case o<sup>fi</sup>cer, asylum seeker, and the reception counters, through my genealogical analysis become enacted as entangled genealogical lines (Ingold, 2011; 2015), continuously conditioning each other's possibilities for action, so can my position as a researcher be understood as a genealogical line (rather than a dot) entangled with and conditioning what ‘data’ can become. In this position, analysis implies engaging with data by attending to all of the details in our experiences of becoming within sociomaterial practices, including past experiences, expectations, and emotions (Nordstrom, 2013). It implies attending to the subtle sensations and practices constituting my experiences that are not yet categorized but that makes a di<sup>f</sup>erence for how I and my research becomes. As MacLure (2013, p. 228) explains, data exist in a wondrous state between ‘knowing and unknowing,’ and it is this liminality ‘that prevents wonder from being wholly contained or recuperated as knowledge and thus a<sup>f</sup>ords an opening into the new.’ A di<sup>f</sup>ractive analysis is focused on what newness might emerge in such an experimental space along the line (see Allen, 2016; Davies 2014; MacLure, 2013; Ringrose & Renold, 2014).

Let me be clear: I am not arguing that we should necessarily abandon our analytical work of coding and categorizing all together. These epistemological practices might still be useful but must be understood in genealogical terms—that is to understand why certain categories became seen as more obvious than others, and so forth. I would suggest that a di<sup>f</sup>ractive analysis can complement this work. While analytic work of coding and categorizing makes use of human intellectual qualities (and history, if understood genealogically) in order to impose a certain type of order through concepts and other forms of representational practices; di<sup>f</sup>ractive methods can help restore the many complex, politicized, and messy subject and object biographies to the analytical simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation that our coding practices brings to the reality of organizational life (Grosz, 2005).

## 4. Discussion: corresponding responsibly and creatively as researchers

If we are to be faithful to the ontological assumptions of the sociomaterial perspective grounded in a relational or becoming ontology (e.g. agential realism), we cannot adopt methods based on a view of the researcher as a separate entity owning her agency and the ability to represent a reality ‘out there’. We need to develop new ways of positioning ourselves as researchers in the (re) production of research (and realities), and new epistemological tools, practices, and conceptual vocabularies to investigate and account for these realities. This paper is a result of an, for me, ongoing process of experimenting with my position, my thinking, and my writing as a sociomaterial researcher.

I have argued for and shown that a sociomaterial approach does not necessarily mean inventing completely new epistemological practices, but that it can imply understanding a practice, and our position as a researcher in it, in new ways. I argued and showed how the data collected through interviews cannot, from a sociomaterial perspective, be equated with, and reduced to, the words spoken by participants and then transcribed into words in interview transcripts. From a sociomaterial perspective, there is no unique, essentialist subject who can produce voice , nor can voice be separated from the enactment in which it is produced, an enactment among researcher-data-participants-theory-analysis. Consequently, conducting interviews as a sociomaterial researcher means to listen to the ‘voice' that is produced in the flow of material-discursive practice (Aagard & Matthiesen. 2016: Bodén. 2014: Nordstrom 2013: Simonsen Abildgaard. 2018). It implies inserting oneself into these practices, to feel the movement of practices from within, for example by inviting material artifacts into the interview or conducting the interview in a material work space of the interviewee. It implies sensing how the speci<sup>fi</sup>city of practices makes a di<sup>f</sup>erence, practically and emotionally, in the experience of becoming positioned in certain material-discursive practices (Clark/Keefe & Haines, 2019; Nordstrom, 2013). The aim is to develop a sensitivity to and a contact with phenomena that are not trapped in categories and measurement but instead are richer and more intimate. In other words, to sense an event, subject, or object, not in terms of what it has in common with others, but in its unique becoming (Bergson, 1999; Ingold, 2015). We can perform these practices, not to create a more representative account of what actually is or was’, but to produce e<sup>f</sup>ects of being a<sup>f</sup>ected, in which thinking and imagining exceed the data and ourselves as researchers (Taguchi, 2012). In this context, triangulation might be useful, not because this practice reveals or pinpoints the supposed actual prior processes or situation, but because it includes more possibilities to become a<sup>f</sup>ected and for ‘voice’ to become enacted.

Moreover, I have suggested that becoming as a sociomaterial researcher implies a shift from focusing on individual actors and their doings, to the <sup>fl</sup>ow of sociomaterial practices enacting such doings as legitimate and appropriate, a shift from tracing the constituting interaction between actors or elements, to tracing the genealogy of practices (Butler, 1997a, b; Foucault, 1991). Such an inquiry does not begin with an interest in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c end, nor involve an analysis aimed at unraveling the interests that might cause certain actors to act in speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways or to wish for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c outcome. A productive inquiry in these terms contests the tendency for dualism by instead beginning “in the middle” of the <sup>fl</sup>ow of practice and focusing on how the temporal performative <sup>fl</sup>ow of agency establishes a set of constitutive conditions that render certain modes of being and acting (rather than others) to be taken, or accepted, as meaningful, obvious, and legitimate (Hultin & Introna, 2018; Introna, 2018). This shift can be summarized as a shift from assuming and focusing on spatial movement, to attending to how positions and categories are enacted in the temporal <sup>fl</sup>ow of practices, on that which practices inherit from previous practices and that constitutes the temporal condition for subject and object to become (Butler, 1993).

Within the <sup>fi</sup>eld of information systems, a few studies have demonstrated the ontological kinship and shared roots of a relationa ontology and process metaphysics (Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2016; Hultin & Introna, 2018; Hultin & Mähring, 2017; Introna, 2018;

Shotter, 2006) and argued that a process perspective, and a process oriented vocabulary, can help us move away from a dualistic enactment of subject and object, structure and agency, by enabling us to attend to the temporal conditioning of certain practices, subject and object positions. This paper builds on and extends this work by showing how a focus on temporal movement, for example by tracing the genealogy of practices, enables researchers to account for the entangled relationship between the social and the material in a way that constitutes a radical departure from an ontology of being and that, moreover, in fact makes a di<sup>f</sup>erence in terms of how organizational realities are understood.

What constitutes this di<sup>f</sup>erence in how organizational realities are understood? Just like accounts grounded in a being ontology, for example critical realism, my account of the practices unfolding in the di<sup>f</sup>erent reception spaces at the MB includes asylum seekers, case o<sup>fi</sup>cers, consultants, managers, change agents, and team leaders. It also includes reception desks, queue ticket machines, and glass windows. In other words, it includes the same labels as a critical realist analysis would use to refer to these actors and the same labels that the asylum seekers, case o<sup>fi</sup>cers, consultants etc. would use to describe themselves. However, as I focus my attention on the temporal and conditioning <sup>fl</sup>ow of practice, I don't see these labels ascribed to actors, but to doings and becomings, rendered possible within the conditionality of the <sup>fl</sup>ow of practice. I see how the <sup>fl</sup>ow of mundane and seemingly insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant practices of everyday work, conditions and transforms not just the interactions, roles, perceptions, or cognitive frames of subjects, but the very conditions of possibilities to become as certain kinds of subjects. Thus, attending to how positions and categories are enacted in the temporal <sup>fl</sup>ow of practices, rather than assuming and focusing on spatial movement, brings a di<sup>f</sup>erent ontological understanding of the subjects and objects involved in the meetings in the receptions. In this account, the constitutive enactment of materialdiscursive practices does not necessarily re<sup>fl</sup>ect the intentions of the individuals working in the reception area; rather, it (re)produces the material-discursive possibilities of their positioning in which ‘the doer’ becomes an e<sup>f</sup>ect of these practices. Similarly, in the temporal, performative <sup>fl</sup>ow of material-discursive practices, an object's properties (or a<sup>f</sup>ordances) cannot be found in its ‘nature’, in its materiality. In this view, attending to materiality does not mean that we imbue materiality with agency, or in Barad's words, that we ‘thingify’ it (Barad, 2003). Rather, attending to materiality is a matter of listening to their silence, telling their stories, tracing their genealogy (Foucault, 1991) by starting in the seemingly self-evident present everyday work practices and showing how they are enactments of a historically contingent performative <sup>fl</sup>ow of material-discursive practices

That subjects become as enactments in material-discursive practices does not mean that they are determined by these materialdiscursive practices. On the contrary, it is precisely because subjects are constantly reproduced (through reiteration), they are never fully constituted, that there is always space for reworking and resisting (Jackson, 2004). Thus, the shift from identifying spatial connections to tracing temporal conditionings enables us to see possibilities for (and the power to) change, not just enacted in the intentional actions and interactions of actors, but in each moment as the partial, fragmented and unstable nature of repetition continuously produces new possibilities for being and acting. This shift also implies a di<sup>f</sup>erent understanding of responsibility. Assuming an ontology of becoming, responsibility does not begin and end with an intentional subiect destined to reap the con: sequences of her actions (Barad, 2007). In the performative <sup>fl</sup>ow of material-discursive practices, responsibility cannot be ascribed only to a manager, case o<sup>fi</sup>cer, or researcher. This is not to say that persons functioning in such capacities cannot be held accountable for their actions. On the contrary, it means that their responsibility exceeds their own actions. In this view, responsibility is the ability to be responsive to the possibilities of becoming in each moment. The practices by which the ‘I’ is continually rediscovering itself through its continual responsiveness to the <sup>fl</sup>ow of material-discursive practices. It is a practice in which the key quality lies in the ‘I's resonance with the movements of this <sup>fl</sup>ow, its ability to see, listen, feel, join, and participate as it <sup>fl</sup>ows along (Ingold, 2017). It is a process that involves hesitation, uncertainty, and the impossibility to say, in con<sup>fi</sup>dence, I do this or I did that . One has rather to ask. 'Is this what I am doing?', or ‘Did I do that?' (Manning. 2016). It implies thinking critically about the boundaries, constraints, and exclusions that operate through particular sociomaterial practices (Barad, 2007; Schultze, 2017) and of experiencing, accounting, and taking responsibility for, not just what we do with our methods and theories, but what they do to us, to our thinking, action, and the realities we enact.
