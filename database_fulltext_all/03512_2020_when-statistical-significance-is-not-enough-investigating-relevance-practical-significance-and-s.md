# WHEN STATISTICAL SIGNIFICANCE IS NOT ENOUGH: INVESTIGATING RELEVANCE, PRACTICAL SIGNIFICANCE, AND STATISTICAL SIGNIFICANCE<sup>1</sup>

Kaveh Mohajeri IESEG School of Management, 3 rue de la Digue, 59000 Lille, FRANCE {k.mohajeri@ieseg.fr}

Mostafa Mesgari College of Business Administration, Loyola Marymount University, 1 LMU Drive, Los Angeles, CA 90045-2659 U.S.A. {mostafa.mesgari@lmu.edu}

Allen S. Lee School of Business, Virginia Commonwealth University, 301 West Main Street, Richmond, VA 23208-4000 U.S.A. {allenslee@alum.mit.edu}

The notions of significance and relevance have provoked much controversy and confusion among those who conduct and those who are intended to be informed by quantitative research in the information systems (IS) field. The history of quantitative research in the IS field and beyond reveals not only disputes over the adequacy of statistical significance to warrant the scientific merits of research, but also pleas for drawing attention to practical significance, as well as a lack of distinction between relevance and practical significance. This essay offers a remedial, overarching account. We establish the position that statistical significance, practical significance, and relevance are distinct qualities, where the latter two transcend mere statistical concerns and respectively refer to the distinct matters of research impressiveness and real-world usefulness. Furthermore, we draw attention to the importance of proper communication of quantitative/statistical analyses through a detailed examination of published IS research. Our examination gives rise to three major issues. The three issues are concerned with the proper communication of (1) research rigor, (2) practical significance, and (3) research relevance. We express our opinions with respect to the three issues and provide a number of recommendations.

Keywords: Research communication, rigor, relevance, nonacademic stakeholders, practical significance, statistical significance

## Introduction

Quantitative research has a long history in the information systems (IS) field. The methods employed by quantitative IS researchers have significantly evolved from simple regression/factor analysis, to structural equation modeling (SEM), and nowadays, to advanced data analytics. The practice of quantitative research, however, has remained vastly unchanged over the course of the field’s history in terms of at least one particular aspect: the quest for statistical significance. Quantitative IS researchers, as with their peers in many other social science fields, have been used to considering statistical significance a cornerstone of “scientific” inference (e.g., Boudreau et al. 2001; Gefen et al. 2000;

Straub 2009; Straub et al. 2004). Nevertheless, the debates over the notion of statistical significance have long established that statistical significance has little, or nothing, to do with scientific inference, and researchers need to change their perspective toward what statistical significance is and what it really indicates (e.g., Daniel 1977; Kirk 1998; Nickerson 2000; Selvin 1957). In the IS field, the mere reliance on statistical significance and p-values has been seriously questioned as well, particularly over the past few years with the increasing popularity of data analytics research, where the issue described as “the deflated p-value problem” (Lin et al. 2013, p. 907) has drawn much attention. The issue is that large datasets, which are common in data analytics research, almost always lead to statistically significant p-values for research results (e.g., Abbasi et al. 2016; George et al. 2014; Lin et al. 2013; Müller et al. 2016).

There have been remedies suggested to address the situation portrayed above. In particular, drawing on the concept of practical significance (in contrast to statistical significance), quantitative researchers have long been encouraged in many disciplines (e.g., Chatfield 1995; Cohen 1977; Daniel 1977; Fisher 1925; Kerlinger and Pedhazur 1973; Kirk 1996; Nickerson 2000; Pearson 1900; Selvin 1957) to supplement, if not replace, their reporting of statistical significance with interpretations or judgments of the magnitude (and corresponding confidence intervals) of their obtained research results. In the IS field, this turn toward practical significance is much more evident, again, over the past few years with the prevalence of data analytics research. Based on longestablished lessons from other disciplines, data analytics IS researchers are now strongly advised to convey the scientific merits of their research studies through giving, as much as possible, objective and clear presentations and interpretations of their obtained effect sizes, reporting confidence intervals, and employing graphical charts (see Abbasi et al. 2016; Lin et al. 2013; Müller et al. 2016).

Despite the seemingly positive developments in the IS literature with respect to encouraging quantitative researchers to go beyond statistical significance, the observations we share in the current essay indicate that the status-quo research practice, widespread among quantitative IS researchers, is still far from satisfactory. We observe that, in either the more traditional statistical research involving comparatively small samples or the recent data analytics research utilizing large samples, the majority of IS research studies still tend to only focus on reporting the statistical significance of the results. In other words, the majority of IS research studies still delegate the task of interpreting the magnitude of results to their academic or nonacademic audience. In our view, this unpleasant situation in quantitative IS research can be partially attributed to conceptual confusion concerning the three concepts of statistical significance, practical significance and relevance. For instance, we searched for the usage of the term practical significance throughout the history of MIS Quarterly and found that, out of 24 papers and editorials mentioning the term, only 10 papers<sup>2</sup> use it in the context of statistical analysis and in contrast to statistical significance, and the rest mostly use the term to imply the real-world usefulness of research in contrast to its theoretical importance. On this basis, we believe there is still considerable misunderstanding in the IS community with respect to such fundamental questions as: What does statistical significance have to do with scientific inference? What is practical significance? Why is reporting on practical significance a necessary practice in all forms of statistically conducted research (not merely data analytics)? What should appropriate reporting on practical significance in a research study look like? And, why does obtaining and reporting research results bearing practical significance not necessarily mean that a research study bears relevance to the real world?

Against the outlined backdrop, the contribution that the current essay makes is twofold. On the one hand, this essay offers illustrative, yet critical, material concerning the three notions of statistical significance, practical significance and relevance, all in one place, in order to clarify the conceptual distinctions among them for IS researchers. On the other hand, the current essay advances a coherent account on the proper communication of practical significance and, especially, research relevance, in the context of quantitative IS research. In the next two sections, we make distinctions among the three concepts of statistical significance, practical significance and relevance in quantitative research, so that IS researchers may consider this essay a reference point for their understanding of the three concepts—including the distinction between relevance and practical significance. Next, we examine the extent to which practical significance and relevance have been considered in the published IS research. Finally, in the fifth section, we discuss three major issues arising from our review of published IS research and highlight additional considerations and tasks that IS researchers must take into account as a part of modern, rigorous quantitative research practice.

## Some Background on Significance and Relevance

The work in this essay essentially revolves around two general notions we consider to be pertinent to statistically conducted IS research: significance<sup>3</sup> and relevance.<sup>4</sup> These two notions, however, have developed in two separate intellectual traditions. On the one hand, the notion of significance (covering both statistical significance and practical significance) has developed in the statistical school of thought exclusively concerning the quantitative results of statistical analyses. On the other hand, the notion of relevance, as far as the management-school disciplines are concerned, has developed in connection with much broader concerns regarding the real-world usefulness of management research (including research models, constructs, variables, and results)—where research employing statistical analysis is only one form—both in practice and for society in general. We review the background of these two notions in the following paragraphs.

One major issue that led to the emergence of the notion of significance was to address the issue that researchers conducting inferential statistical analysis had to make sure that results induced from only a sample of a population are sufficiently reliable to show that hypothesized effects/relationships actually exist in the population. Although the idea of statistical significance tests dates back to the 1700s (see Hacking 1965), most of the developments happened in the early 1900s. Fisher (1921, 1925) and Neyman and Pearson (1928) are considered pioneers who worked out the underlying ideas, concepts, and methods in order to enable researchers to show whether their reported results can be considered sufficiently discernible<sup>5</sup> (i.e., significant) in statistical terms. On this basis, one may tend to say that, in the history of statistics, the notion of significance was initially and mainly perceived as statistical significance.

Controversy around the notion of statistical significance is almost as old as the notion itself (see Berkson 1938). Statistical significance has long been shown to be an inadequate measure for scientific reasoning and credibility due to a variety of reasons. However, our aim here is not to bring in all the prolonged and detailed debates on the notions of statistical significance and null hypothesis significance testing (for a coherent account see Nickerson 2000). Yet, it is critical for our purpose in this essay to emphasize a very fundamental criticism of statistical significance. As explained by Kirk (1996), a major criticism is that statistical significance, and the procedure to establish it in statistically conducted research, do not “tell researchers what they want to know” (p. 747). According to Kirk (p. 747):

[N]ull hypothesis significance testing and scientific inference address different questions. In scientific inference, what we want to know is the probability that the null hypothesis (H ) is true given that we have obtained a set of data (D); that is, p(H |D). What null hypothesis significance testing tells us is the probability of obtaining these data or more extreme data if the null hypothesis is true, p(D|H ).

As Falk (1998) argues, p(H<sub>0</sub>|D) and p(D|H<sub>0</sub>) can be equal, but that happens only under rare mathematical conditions. Therefore, as Kirk (p. 747) explains:

Researchers reason incorrectly that if the p value associated with a test statistic is suitably small, say, less than .05, the null hypothesis is probably false. This form of deductive reasoning has been referred to by Falk and Greenbaum (1995) as the “illusion of probabilistic proof by contradiction.”

In the IS field, reactions to the inadequacy of statistical significance have emerged much more recently (e.g., Abbasi et al. 2016; Lin et al. 2008, 2013; Müller et al. 2016; Shmueli and Koppius, 2011). What has triggered IS researchers to attend to the matter seems to stem chiefly from one of the classic criticisms of statistical significance, namely, the issue that research results nearly always turn out to be statistically significant when the sample size is large enough (see Chatfield 1995; Nickerson 2000). This classic criticism has drawn much attention in the IS field particularly because of the increasing prevalence of what is known as data analytics research. In data analytics research, IS researchers usually exploit datasets—drawn from the web, social media, mobile devices, or sensor networks—that contain a huge number of observations. Relying on very large sample sizes bears many advantages, but comes at the high price of encountering what is termed the deflated p-value problem (see Lin et al. 2013). In other words, in research studies that employ large datasets, the p-values associated with research results can go quickly to zero, creating the illusion that the research results are scientifically supported, while the magnitude of the research results may have no particular meaning or significance in research or real-world terms.

Most of the remedies offered in the IS field and beyond with respect to the inadequacy of statistical significance revolve around another form of the notion of significance (i.e., practical significance). Historically speaking, along with placing emphasis on the statistical discernibility of results, statisticians were also aware of another demanding issue of a different nature: the research impressiveness<sup>6</sup> of results. In other words, researchers conducting inferential statistical analysis also had to make sure that the magnitude of their reported results are sufficiently impressive so that they could consider their hypothesized effects/relationships to actually matter in the research area they are concerned about. Kirk (1996) explains that Pearson (1900) and then Fisher (1925) were among the first to react to the issue of practical significance. In particular,

Fisher (1925) proposed that researchers supplement the [statistical] significance test in analysis of variance with the correlation ratio or eta, which measures the strength of the association between the independent and dependent variables (Kirk 1996, p. 748).

Later reactions to this issue can also be identified in the behavioral and educational literatures (e.g., Cohen 1977; Daniel 1977; Kerlinger and Pedhazur 1973), and the sociological literature (e.g., Selvin 1957), among others.

In the IS field, as with many other disciplines, the effort to encourage researchers to address practical significance is mainly focused on promoting the practice of reporting and interpreting effect sizes (and their corresponding confidence intervals)<sup>7</sup> (e.g., Abbasi et al. 2016; Aguirre-Urreta and Rönkkö 2018; Lee and Mohajeri 2012; Lin et al. 2013). This was once described as “the effect size movement” (Robinson et al. 2003, p. 51). For instance, in the case of large-sample studies in IS, Lin et al. (2013, p. 909)

suggest that in addition to the traditional statistical tests, researchers should, as much as possible, be objective and clear in helping readers understand the meaning of the coefficient estimates [i.e., effect size values] within the study context.

To do so, the authors discuss the utility of what they call “marginal analysis,” through which the sensitivity of a dependent variable to changes in an independent variable may be reported by researchers. The same kind of analysis is advocated by Lee and Mohajeri (2012). Furthermore, along the same lines, Aguirre-Urreta and Rönkkö (2018) argue that IS researchers should offer interpretations of their obtained research results (i.e., effect sizes and their corresponding confidence intervals) in terms of what those results mean for the literature under examination. However, as stated earlier, we, in this essay, make a contribution by distinguishing between practical significance (i.e., research impressiveness of statistical results) and relevance (i.e., real-world usefulness of research and its results). In fact, despite what seems to be a major turn toward practical significance in the IS literature, it appears that what is referred to as practical significance in our field is vastly conflated with the notion of relevance (as indicated in the “Introduction” section).

Reviewing the background of the notion of relevance suggests that Koontz (1961) is perhaps one of the earliest works reacting to the issue of usefulness of management research. Koontz, arguing for the clarification of management theory, emphasized the usefulness and understandability that such theory should have in the eyes of practitioners. Thirty two years later, Hambrick (1994), in his Academy of Management presidential address,<sup>8</sup> voiced substantially similar concerns, criticized the minimal innovation, visibility, and impact of management research, and called for making “a significant contribution to the solution of major problems facing our society and its value-creating enterprises” (p. 15). Concerns for the usefulness or impact of management research are still raised in the 21<sup>st</sup> century, where reports, such as one from the AACSB International (2008), draw our attention again to the criticality of enhancing the value and visibility of management scholarship and research. In the IS field, the most notable works on the notion of relevance, in our view, are the 1999 MIS Quarterly essays on “rigor vs. relevance” where IS scholars provide their own definitions of the concept of relevance, argue for or against the lack of relevance in IS research, and recommend ways by which IS research relevance could be improved. Prior to, and following, the 1999 MIS Quarterly discussion, we also have some invaluable works and commentaries such as the ones by Dennis (2001), Desouza et al. (2006), Hassan (2014), Robey and Markus (1998), Rosemann and Vessey (2008), and Straub and Ang (2008, 2011). Although our review of the notion of relevance in the IS literature shows maturation in the perspectives developed on the notion over time (see Mohajeri and Leidner 2017), we did not find any coherent account particularly addressing relevance in the context of statistically conducted research.

The foregoing review supports our position that there exist major distinctions among the concepts of statistical significance, practical significance and relevance. To better illustrate our contention, we highlight all the key distinctions among the three concepts in Table 1.

As demonstrated in Table 1, besides the fact that statistical significance, practical significance and relevance each refer to a different quality, it is clear based on our review that whereas the first two concepts are only concerned with the quantitative results of a particular form of research (i.e., statistically conducted research), the third concept is concerned with research as a whole not just its results, whether that research is statistically conducted or not.

To develop a more nuanced view on the distinction between practical significance and relevance, another point we wish to emphasize is the difference in the entities making judgments about practical significance and relevance. As shown in Table 1, making judgments about practical significance falls upon the shoulders of researchers. This can be inferred from the effort statisticians have been historically making to scale practical significance (e.g., certain thresholds specified for small, medium, and large effects; see Cohen 1992; Kirk 1996). Kirk (2001, p. 214) has a well-expressed statement in this regard:

I believe that researchers have an obligation to make this kind of judgment. No one is in a better position than the researcher who collected and analyzed the data to decide whether the effects are trivial or not. It is a curious anomaly that researchers are trusted to make a variety of complex decisions in the design and execution of an experiment, but in the name of objectivity they are not expected to nor even encouraged to decide whether the effects are practically significant.

On the other hand, in accordance with our review presented above, we attribute judgments about research relevance to nonacademic research stakeholders. Nevertheless, there seems to be a common perspective in the literature that does not recognize the distinction between the two different types of entities (i.e., academics vs. nonacademics) in relation to making judgments about practical significance and relevance. For instance, Aguinis et al. (2010), drawing on Vaske et al. (2002), point out that “practical significance involves a value judgment made by the consumer of research” (p. 530). It is clear from Aguinis et al. that “the consumer of research” in fact refers to “practitioners” (see p. 530). Another example, from the IS literature, is Lin et al. (2013), where the authors indicate that the practical significance of research results depends on the point of view of the general readership (i.e., nonacademics) of a research study (see p. 909). As mentioned before, we see this as an opportunity for us to make a contribution by making explicit the difference between relevance and practical significance.

The last point, again on the distinction between practical significance and relevance, is that there exists a tendency in the literature (e.g., Kelley and Preacher 2012; Kirk 1996) to equate the research impressiveness of quantitative results with the real-world usefulness of research or its results, and hence not to make a full distinction between practical significance and relevance. One might speculate this occurs, in part, due to the fact that the relevance of the conceptual aspects (e.g., research models, hypotheses, variables, etc.) of statistically conducted research is often a taken-for-granted quality among statisticians. On this basis, statisticians mostly presume that the only remaining condition to achieve relevance for the entire research is to obtain impressive results. In the next section, by illustrating the distinction between relevance and practical significance, we indicate that such a presumption can be questionable.

The seven different regions in Figure 1 using two research models illustrate the differences among the concepts of statistical significance, practical significance and relevance. Two points are worthy of some elaboration.

The first point is that, across the seven regions, any determination of statistical significance, practical significance, and/or relevance requires human judgment; there is no purely objective rule that can render the determination by itself. Even in the case of statistical significance, the reliance on a level of significance of .05 or .01 is only a convention and, therefore, is itself only a judgment. As for practical significance, interpretation of obtained effect sizes is a matter of human judgment and also relies on convention. “Researchers study varied phenomena using a plethora of paradigms and methodologies” (Cortina and Landis, 2009, p. 306). Consequently, depending on the context and research domain/design, there can certainly be situations where a small effect size can denote a large practical impact and vice versa (e.g., see Hsieh et al. 2012; Lewandowsky and Maybery 1998; Prentice and Miller 1992; Rosenthal 1990; Rosenthal and Rubin 1979). On this basis, factors such as population and sample size, the particular effect size measures involved, and the nature of the phenomenon in question, are in fact determinants of how the magnitude of effect sizes obtained in a given research study should be interpreted.<sup>11</sup> Therefore, as Grissom and Kim (2005) state, “knowledge of a result’s effect size can [only] inform a judgment about practical significance” (p. 4). Moreover, not a convention but nonetheless requiring judgment across the seven regions are decisions made by the nonacademic stakeholder about relevance, namely, whether the dependent variable is understandable and noteworthy, whether the independent variables are understandable and actionable, and whether the magnitudes of the associations between the latter and the former are satisfactory to make a difference to their question of interest.

The second point is that the seven regions are sufficient for illustrating the concepts of practical significance and relevance, but do not necessarily exhaust all possible cases or scenarios. For instance, the seven regions illustrate just one form of relevance, which we name “action lever” relevance. We are referring to Robey and Markus (1998) who define “action levers” as “factors that decision-makers can influence through direct action, example, or policy” (p. 13)—where the action levers are the independent variables. In addition, there is at least one other form of relevance, which we name “general” relevance; it is a quality of research that makes a difference to the nonacademic stakeholder’s question of interest even without any independent variables that the nonacademic stakeholder can use as action levers. For example, a statistical study establishing that telemedicine is beneficial to patients can be considered relevant, even though the nonacademic stakeholder (a patient) has no control over the presence or absence of the independent variable (telemedicine).

## The Consideration of Practical Significance and Relevance in Published IS Research

In the previous sections, we examined the concepts of sta tistical significance, practical significance and relevance from a theoretical standpoint in an effort to shed light on the distinctions among them, as well as to explicate situations under which research may bear any one, any two or all three of them. However, when thinking of published research, a dis tinction needs to be made between whether a research study bears statistical significance, practical significance and relevance, on the one hand, and how the same research study actually reports or communicates the statistical significance, practical significance and relevance, on the other hand. For instance, a research study could have yielded very large statistical results that are of practical significance, but the paper reporting that research might not consider and discuss the practical significance of the research’s results at all. In this light, we take an empirical stance in the current section in order to investigate the extent to which published IS research considers and communicates the two critical qualities of practical significance and relevance. Our investigation is primarily focused on all statistically conducted research published in MIS Quarterly in 2015, where published IS research is well represented. The protocol we use to review the MIS Quarterly papers involves two steps. In the first step, we present a bird’s-eye view of a total of 27 MIS Quarterly papers published in 2015. The outcome of the first step is presented in Table 5 below. Then, in the second step, we pick three papers (the ones shown with a gray background in Table 5) reviewed during the first step in order to offer an in-depth review of them, and to highlight pivotal lessons we have learned from our review. In addition, we supplement, and provide further support for the results of, our investigation using randomly picked examples of research published in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and Journal of the Association for Information Systems in 2016. Finally, we provide extra evidence from the only identified review of the communication of practical significance in published IS research (i.e., Lin et al. 2013).

As shown in Table 5, the 27 MIS Quarterly papers are evaluated in terms of seven aspects (i.e., seven columns) which we believe are pertinent to the issues of practical significance and relevance. These seven aspects along with the results of our review are discussed in the following paragraphs.

1. What are the reported effect sizes (RES)? In order for a paper to consider the issue of practical significance, one or more effect sizes must be stated in the first place. Following Kirk (1996) and Kelley and Preacher (2012), we consider a broad range of statistical measures as effect size measures. This includes path/regression coefficient, correlation coefficient, fit indices, measures of variance explained and so forth.<sup>12</sup> According to Table 5, all of the papers have reported effect sizes of at least two different types.

2. Does the paper offer any judgment, with supporting rationale, indicating whether the RES are practically significant? To merely report effect sizes, as captured by the first aspect, however, does not suffice if a paper intends to consider the issue of practical significance. As explained in the previous sections, researchers should offer judgments on the magnitude of effect sizes as well. A quick look at Table 5 shows that, in fact, no paper offers this kind of judgment with respect to all the effect sizes that it reports. The table also shows that while 81% of papers give no consideration to the issue of practical significance at all, most of the remaining papers consider and discuss the issue of practical significance with regard to only a small portion of their reported effect sizes.

3. Who are the identified nonacademic stakeholders? In order for a paper to consider the issue of relevance, one or more nonacademic research stakeholders should be identified in the paper in the first place. In our review of MIS Quarterly papers, we noticed that the vast majority of papers (i.e., 96%) identify, and target, at least one group of nonacademic stakeholders.

4. Are there separate paper section(s) on implications for nonacademic stakeholders? The fact that almost all of the papers we reviewed identify at least one nonacademic stakeholder should be viewed as a result of the editorial policies in effect at MIS Quarterly (and also some other leading IS academic journals) which have established the expectation that IS research studies, including statistically conducted ones, should identify one or more nonacademic stakeholders and provide some implications for them (e.g., see Straub and Ang, 2008, 2011; Te’eni et al. 2015). These implications can be presented in separate paper section(s), or can be part of a general discussion section or implications section. Based on curiosity, we reviewed the MIS Quarterly papers to find out the percentage of papers which devote separate paper section(s) to implications for nonacademic stakeholders. The result of our review indicates that 65% (17 out of 26) of articles contain separate paper sections covering the nonacademic implications.

5. Does the paper indicate how its dependent variables are relevant in the eyes of the identified nonacademic stakeholders? We reviewed the MIS Quarterly papers to find out the percentage of papers that offer content (e.g., statements from interviews with nonacademic stakeholders) on how nonacademic stakeholders find the papers’ dependent variables (as operationally defined measured) relevant. Consistent with the conceptualization offered in the section with the Venn diagram illustration, we consider the relevance of a dependent variable to be concerned with the understandability and noteworthiness of the variable in the eyes of nonacademic research stakeholders. As seen in Table 5, despite the fact that almost all of the papers target at least one nonacademic stakeholder group and the majority of them devote separate paper sections to implications for nonacademic stakeholders, our review shows that 92% (24 out of 26) of papers do not indicate how their dependent variables are relevant in the eyes of the identified nonacademic stakeholders.

6. Does the paper indicate how its independent (including moderating) and mediating variables are relevant in the eyes of the identified nonacademic stakeholders? We included this aspect in order to find out the percentage of MIS Quarterly papers that offer content (e.g., statements from interviews with nonacademic stakeholders) on how nonacademic stakeholders find the papers’ independent (including moderating) and mediating variables (as operationally defined/measured) relevant. Consistent with the conceptualization offered in the section with the Venn diagram illustration, we consider the relevance of an independent/mediating variable to be concerned with the understandability, and when appropriate, actionability, of the variable in the eyes of nonacademic stakeholders. Actionability, as mentioned, was a contingent criterion in our review of the MIS Quarterly papers due to the possibility of invoking a “general” relevance, as opposed to an “action lever” relevance (see the section with the Venn diagram). Table 5 shows that, despite the fact that almost all of the papers target at least one nonacademic stakeholder group and the majority of them devote separate paper sections to implications for nonacademic stakeholders, 81% (21 out of 26) of papers do not indicate how their independent/mediating variables are relevant in the eyes of the identified nonacademic stakeholders.

7. Does the paper indicate how the magnitude of the RES would make a difference in the eyes of the identified nonacademic stakeholders? Aspect 7 was another review aspect we employed to examine the consideration of the issue of relevance in the MIS Quarterly papers. What motivated us to include this aspect was the recognition, as explained in all three preceding sections of this essay, that nonacademic stakeholders may have different judgments compared to researchers on whether the magnitudes of effect sizes reported in a research study make any difference in real-world terms. Nonacademic stakeholders’ judgments can be different in part because they might consider other criteria, which may not be of interest to researchers. For instance, nonacademic stakeholders might apply a financial cost-benefit criterion, which may often have no place in the researchers’ interpretation of the practical significance of effect size measurements. Our review of the MIS Quarterly papers suggests that, despite the fact that almost all of the papers target at least one nonacademic stakeholder group and the majority of them devote separate paper sections to implications for nonacademic stakeholders, 25 out of 26 papers (i.e., 96%) do not provide any content on the relevance of the magnitude of their reported effect sizes. Only one paper provides such content, albeit only for one of the effect size measurements that the paper reports.

What is presented so far is concerned with the overall profile of the MIS Quarterly papers in terms of the consideration of the issues of practical significance and relevance. A more indepth view however is needed to reveal the nuances of this kind of consideration in the published IS research. For that purpose, we selected the three papers shown with a gray background in Table 5. This essay, however, is not intended to be, and should not be taken as, a negative criticism of the three excellent articles. In this essay, we are synthesizing ideas that were not synthesized at the publication time of those papers; therefore, it would not be reasonable to hold the authors of the three articles to the standards or criteria implied in a framework that was not even proposed at the time of their research. Rather, we regard these papers as providing a solid foundation upon which to provide more details on the underlying logic we used to review the 27 MIS Quarterly papers, as well as to highlight some critical observations we have made.

## The Case of Park, Sharman, and Rao

The study by Park, Sharman, and Rao (2015) investigates the effect of an individual’s “perceptions of sociotechnical safety factors” on the individual’s “perceived usefulness of hospital information systems (HIS).” The authors also posit that the aforementioned effect is moderated by the “individual’s disaster experience” (i.e., whether a person has had an experience of natural or human-made disasters in the past when working with an HIS). The individual’s “perceptions of sociotechnical safety factors” in fact is an umbrella term for the three distinct independent variables that the study incorporates “perceived system risk,” “perceived resilience” and “perceived information assurance.”

Park et al. administer three surveys: a survey distributed among hospital personnel who have had an experience of a snowstorm in the past when working with an HIS (i.e., the experience group), a second survey distributed among the same group of people but after showing them a message that stimulated the personnel to recall the snowstorm they had experienced (i.e., the recall group), and finally, a survey among hospital personnel who had not experienced the snowstorm (i.e., the no-experience group). Using the survey data, Park et al. report many different types of effect sizes (as shown in Table 5). Primary among these effect sizes are path coefficients estimated using PLS, R<sup>2</sup> measurements, and varying types of statistical fit measurements.

We will not summarize Park et al.’s entire analysis here, but instead focus on some particular observations we have made in relation to the consideration of the issues of practical significance and relevance.

It is clear that Park et al. offer a comprehensive and appropriately conducted statistical analysis. Nevertheless, we argue that the authors could have made a much greater contribution to their research area of interest by addressing the practical significance of the results they have obtained. We found that, beyond simply reporting numerous effect sizes, the authors give no additional consideration to the issue of practical significance. This is particularly evident when Park et al. discuss their obtained path coefficients. The authors provide statements such as “systems risk had negative effects on perceived usefulness (â = -0.240, p < 0.001 and -0.160, p < 0.01)” (p. 331), or “the effect of perceived information assurance (H2) was also significant for the perceived resilience $( \beta = 0 . 2 4 9 , \rho < 0 . 0 0 1 \mathrm { ~ a n d } 0 . 3 1 8 , \rho < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ in both the experience and recall groups” (p. 331). In other words, the authors merely draw attention to the sign and statistical significance of the path coefficients, without providing any interpretation of their own regarding the magnitude of those coefficients. This of course does not necessarily mean that the path coefficients obtained by Park et al. are not of practical significance. The point we make here only is that the authors, in their writing, could have addressed the issue of practical significance as well.<sup>13</sup> Moreover, we notice that Park et al. (2015) in many places, including the second statement quoted above, use the term “significant” in an ambiguous way, insofar as the term can connote an effect with considerable magnitude, whereas what the authors actually mean is a statistically significant effect.

With respect to the issue of relevance, our review suggests that Park et al. target a highly-regarded nonacademic stakeholder group (i.e., hospital managers). They well elaborate on the “practical implications” of their study for hospital managers in more than a full page. In the “practical implications” section, the authors legitimately argue that the findings of their study “suggest that hospitals seeking to increase their successful HIS implementation may pursue” (p. 338) some strategies such as focusing “on managing employees’ attitudes toward and perceptions of risk and resilience in a normal context” (p. 338), or adopting “one process to enhance resilience, and a separate process to reduce perceived system risk” (p. 338). Nevertheless, the authors could have provided a much stronger basis for their recommendations by indicating how the independent variables (i.e., “perceived system risk,” “perceived resilience,” and “perceived information assurance”), upon which hospital managers are encouraged to act, are understandable and actionable in their eyes in the first place. More importantly, the authors could have indicated the empirical basis on which they presume that their dependent variable of interest (i.e., the research construct, “perceived usefulness of HIS”) is a matter of interest or importance to, or even understandable by, hospital managers. On a side note, we argue that the relevance of the study by Park et al. would have been better established if the authors could have shown how, and at what level, the magnitude of the effect sizes they obtained can actually support assertions such as the following ones and make them important in the eyes of hospital managers. On page 338, the authors state that:

The findings of our studies suggest that employees perceptions of risks and information assurance are important not only for maintaining resilience and effective operation of HIS, but also for facilitating employees’ HIS related work.

Or, on the same page, the authors argue that:

As the results of the present study show, perception of the strength of information assurance increases belief in resilience.

The above quotations are examples highlighting the fact that there was an opportunity for Park et al. to make references to the magnitude of their obtained effect sizes in order to precisely specify why the factors they are concerned about are actually important in the practice of managing hospitals, or why levels of increase/decrease in such factors can actually make a difference in the eyes of hospital managers. Again, our intention here is not to imply that the study by Park et al. is not relevant. The point is that, in order for the nonacademic stakeholders (i.e., hospital managers) to acknowledge the novelty and importance of Park et al.’s paper, the authors could have considered and discussed the relevance of their work in greater depth and specificity.<sup>14</sup>

The above observations regarding the issues of practical significance and relevance, however, are not unique to the Park et al.’s paper. We have made many similar observations of other MIS Quarterly papers also appearing in Table 5.

## The Case of Armstrong, Brooks, and Riemenschneider

The second article we examine is by Armstrong, Brooks, and Riemenschneider (2015). In their article, they draw attention to what they call an individual’s “exhaustion from IS career experience (EISCE)” and investigate its effect on what is called the individual’s “intention to turn away from an IS career” (i.e., turn-away intention or TAI). Furthermore, Armstrong et al. incorporate in their study four other independent variables as antecedents to EISCE. The variables are “perceived workload” (PW), “career–family conflict” (CFC), “fairness” (FAIR), and “control of career” (CTRL). The authors also posit that the relationship between EISCE and TAI can be mediated by another variable called “affective commitment to the IS profession” (ACISP).

In our view, Armstrong et al. present a very well-conducted study. As shown in Table 5, the authors estimate and report many types of effect sizes. The case of Armstrong et al. shows considerable similarity to the case of Park et al., where the authors merely draw attention to the sign and/or statistical significance of their obtained effect sizes. The case of Armstrong et al. is, however, useful for highlighting another practice we found common in our reviewed MIS Quarterly papers. The practice is to offer interpretations, without solid supporting rationales, of the magnitude of obtained effect sizes. In our review of the MIS Quarterly papers, we did not count such interpretations as adequate discussions of practical significance. For instance, Armstrong et al. offer an interpretation of the magnitude of one $\mathrm { R } ^ { \overline { { 2 } } }$ measurement, which corresponds to the TAI variable and has the magnitude of 0.555. Referring to this $\mathtt { R } ^ { 2 }$ measurement, Armstrong et al. state that “the model had reasonable explanatory power accounting for just over half of the variance in TAI” (p. 720). Strictly speaking, this statement constitutes only a partial treatment of the issue of practical significance with respect to the concerned $\mathtt { R } ^ { 2 }$ measurement. Although Armstrong et al. offer a judgment on the magnitude of the $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ measurement by using the term “reasonable,” which may connote “medium in size,” the authors do not provide any supporting rationale on why 0.555 might indicate a “reasonable explanatory power” within the authors’ research domain/context.

With regard to the issue of relevance, we notice that, different from Park et al., Armstrong et al. do not devote a separate paper section to implications for nonacademic stakeholders. However, we believe Armstrong et al. provide a better treatment of the issue of relevance. The reason is that the authors are concerned about the relevance of their variables of interest, at least in the eyes of one of the nonacademic stakeholder groups they target, namely, IS professionals. Armstrong et al. report on focus group interviews with 29 IS professionals, conducted with the aim of determining the applicability of their research model to the particular context of an IS career. Armstrong et al. point out (p. 715):

Based on the comments from the focus group interviews, we realized the importance of considering an individual’s career instead of a specific job when looking at TAI. Individuals in the focus groups highlighted the importance of exhaustion and turn-away for IS professionals and identified several of Ahuja et al.’s [2007] constructs [the main literature’s constructs on which Armstrong et al. draw] as relevant across their ISCE [IS career experience]. The exploratory focus group sessions revealed the need to study the concepts and helped identify key constructs that were then complemented with our own understanding of the literature.

Nevertheless, we believe the authors could have avoided the same pitfall, as we encountered in Park et al.’s paper, when it comes to the discussion of implications for nonacademic stakeholders. Precisely speaking, Armstrong et al. could have drawn attention to the magnitudes of obtained effect sizes when making assertions with respect to the nonacademic implications of their research. In the “Discussion and Recommendations” section, we demonstrate how this could have been achieved in Armstrong et al.’s paper.

## The Case of Liu, Brass, Lu, and Chen

The study by Liu, Brass, Lu, and Chen (2015) examines the effect of online/offline friendship relationships on lending decisions in a large peer-to-peer (P2P) lending website. The authors’ main argument is that whether or not to offer a loan on P2P lending websites “is affected by the friendship between the potential lender and the borrower (the pipe effect), by a bid from the borrower’s friend (the prism effect), and by a bid from the potential lender’s friend (the relational herding effect)” (p. 730). The study by Liu et al. is distinct from the previous two cases as it belongs to the growing body of data analytics research in the IS field. The authors obtained a large dataset from PPDai, one of the largest P2P lending platforms in China. The dataset includes all the bidding activities of more than 2,100 lenders, 7,800 borrowers, and 12,500 listings, and incorporates a total of 2,546,799 lending decisions.

Liu et al. present a solid statistical analysis. They estimate a total of eight different conditional logit models and conduct Wald tests, using nearly 50% of their obtained dataset (N = 1,250,426 lending decisions). As shown in Table 5, the authors report various types of effect sizes including odds ratio coefficients, log-likelihood, Pseudo $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } ,$ Wald $\chi ^ { 2 } ,$ Adjusted $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } ,$ , and correlation coefficients. Unsurprisingly (considering the volume of data), almost all of the statistical results reported by the authors are statistically significant. However, the audience of data analytics studies (like the one conducted by Liu et al.) often look for qualities far beyond mere statistical significance. Data analytics studies are generally expected to leverage large amounts of data in order to “detect smaller, subtler, and more complex effects” (Lin et al. 2013, p. 906), as well as generate novel understandings of real-world phenomena (see Lycett 2013; Sharma et al. 2014) on a broad empirical landscape, which is usually not available to the classic, survey-based quantitative studies. On this basis, the proper communication of results in data analytics research demands that researchers supply, among other material, sound interpretations of the magnitudes of estimated effects as well as justified explanations on the importance of those magnitudes.

Contrary to our expectation from a data analytics study, our review of Liu et al. shows that the research communication language adopted by the authors predominantly relies on the issue of statistical significance. This is in fact similar to what we observed previously in the two rather classic cases of statistically conducted IS research (i.e., the papers by Park et al. and Armstrong et al.). Liu et al. discuss their obtained odds ratio coefficients (reported in the paper’s Table 3), for the most part, based on the two criteria of statistical significance and whether the coefficients are greater or less than 1. The authors, for instance, make the following inferences (p. 739):

As shown in Table 3, the lending probability increases with the number of days passed, the percentage funded, the number of prior bids, and colocation of the borrower and the lender in the same city. The lending probability decreases with 100 percent funding status, gender (female), education, and the number of friends the lender has.…The number of large bids has a [statistically] significant negative effect on subsequent lending probabilities.

What the authors conclude based on their reported results is statistically appropriate. However, we argue that the paper could have yielded a much greater contribution if, instead of using such terms as “increase,” “decrease,” or “[statistically] significant negative effect” and so forth, the authors could have explicitly mentioned the magnitude of the effects they estimated and offered their own judgments (along with corresponding rationale) on those magnitudes.

The authors also provide three relatively short paragraphs (on page 739) to address the results associated with the three hypothesized effects in the study (i.e., the pipe effect, the prism effect, and the relational herding effect). A constructive practice the authors pursue here though is that they convert the obtained odds ratio coefficients (reported in the paper’s Table 3) to percentages. For example, the authors point out (p. 739):

Each prior bid increases the likelihood of lending by 2.1 percent (Model 3), suggesting an anonymous herding effect among potential lenders (H4 is supported).

To mention the magnitude of statistical results in the form of percentages is certainly helpful because it makes the results more comprehensible and transparent in the eyes of the audience of the research. However, the authors could have contributed to greater appreciation of the statistical results by the audience if they could have offered, in addition, an assessment (with supporting rationale) of the magnitude of the estimated effect sizes such as “2.1 percent” in the research area concerning P2P lending platforms. As indicated before, to address the magnitude of estimated effect sizes (and hence to address practical significance) is particularly important in the case of data analytics studies due to the possibility of the existence of the deflated p-value problem.

With respect to the relevance issue, the paper correctly identifies P2P lending platforms and the borrowers who use them as major groups of a nonacademic audience. Moreover, the authors raise interesting implications and “suggest that … [P2P platforms] can increase bidding activities by making it easy for lenders to follow their offline friends,” or state that “P2P lending platforms may prioritize loan and friend-bid notifications by friendship types” (p. 740). However, the paper’s recommendations could have had more impact if the authors could have shown that the variables they studied are in fact understandable, noteworthy and/or actionable in the eyes of the nonacademic stakeholders they identify for their paper. The authors could have demonstrated the relevance of the study by providing quotations or other evidence from practitioners to report their reactions to the employed dependent and independent variables. Additionally, as just recommended for the other two papers, in order to present a much more objective and convincing case as to why Liu et al.’s study might be important for P2P lending platforms and the borrowers who use them, the authors could have made reference to, and discussed, the significance of the magnitude of the results they obtained. Indeed, it is particularly important for data analytics studies (due to the highly sophisticated analytics models and algorithms they often employ) to discuss their findings in a way that is conveniently interpretable for the nonacademic, nonexpert audience. As a matter of fact, the interpretability of research results has regularly been brought up as a critical aspect of communicating data analytics research in a number of recent commentaries in the IS literature (e.g., see Abbasi et al. 2016; Lycett 2013; Müller et al. 2016; Sharma et al. 2014).

## Additional Cases

Apparently, what we have observed in the case of papers published in MIS Quarterly in 2015 is not exceptional at all. In other words, the lack of attention to the communication of practical significance and relevance in quantitative IS research seems to be an issue spanning a range of different outlets and a series of years. The support for this contention follows.

In addition to our review of 2015 MIS Quarterly papers, we did an extra investigation of published IS research. We randomly selected papers published in 2016, two each from the journals of MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and Journal of the Association for Information Systems.<sup>15</sup> These randomly selected papers present outstanding statistical analyses. However, our review suggests that there still are major overlooked opportunities for the papers to address practical significance and relevance. Overall, despite the fact that a large number of various effect sizes such as regression coefficients, correlation coefficients, $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } ,$ adjusted R<sup>2</sup>, AIC, and BIC are reported in the papers, the practical significance of research results receives partial, minimal, or no attention. In our view, out of the six papers, only one (i.e., Greenstein and Zhu 2016) offers a partial treatment of the issue of the practical significance of research results. The rest of the papers, as with the 2015 MIS Quarterly papers, primarily rely on statistical significance and the sign of obtained effect sizes for the matter of discussing research results. On the other hand, as for the issue of relevance, we found that all of the studies identify at least one nonacademic stakeholder group as their audience, and each offers statements on how the identified nonacademic stakeholder group(s) may benefit from the research study. Still, our review suggests that none of the papers draw attention in a meaningful way to the relevance of the employed variables and the magnitude of reported results in the eyes of the identified nonacademic stakeholder groups.

As a matter of fact, the findings from the above review of published IS research (i.e., the 2015 MIS Quarterly papers as well as the six additional papers), particularly with respect to the issue of practical significance, are in accordance with the indications made in the only relevant review of published IS research we found in the literature (i.e., Lin et al. 2013). Lin et al. (2013) conduct a survey of large-sample papers (n > 10,000) published in leading IS journals and conferences from 2004 to 2010. Based on the survey, the “overall conclusion is that information systems research that is based on large samples might be over-relying on p-values to interpret findings” (p. 908). Lin et al. also indicate that “about half of the recent papers with large samples rely almost exclusively on low p-values and the sign of the coefficient” (p. 908). Furthermore, as shown by Lin et al., only one out of four reviewed MIS Quarterly papers, and only two out of eight reviewed Information Systems Research papers, offer conclusions that rely on practical significance.

## Discussion and Recommendations

There have been three interrelated themes in this essay. The first theme pertains to the position that there are unquestionably distinctions among statistical significance, practical significance and relevance. These distinctions proved to be completely recognizable and valid both when one examines the literature concerning the notions of significance and relevance (as shown in our review of the background of these notions), as well as when one ponders some hypothetical examples/scenarios where research may bear any one, any two, or all three of the qualities of statistical significance, practical significance and relevance (as shown in the section with the Venn diagram). The second theme relates to the position, which we developed in the preceding section, where we argued that it is not sufficient for research to bear statistical significance, practical significance and relevance, but it is also critical for research to address another issue of a different nature, namely, to explicitly communicate the status of its statistical significance, practical significance and relevance. Finally, the third theme, regarding our findings from reviewing published IS research, is that the issues of practical significance and relevance have been considered, and reported on, far less than the issue of statistical significance.

The third theme concerns us the most in that it shows, in our view, the need for raising awareness about some critical issues concerning statistical practice in IS research. In the following paragraphs, and in line with our purpose in the current essay, we will enumerate three issues we believe are of high importance followed by our opinions on how future IS research can deal with the issues and communicate their significance and relevance in a much more adequate manner.

Issue 1: Communicating rigor transcends merely reporting statistical significance and also requires addressing practical significance.

The predominant exclusive reliance on statistical significance, as our review of high quality published IS research suggests, can imply that there is still a widespread notion-in-use in our community presuming that reporting statistical significance alone is sufficient to establish and communicate research rigor. However, reviewing the literature and history of statistics indicates otherwise. Statistical significance and null hypothesis significance testing have been criticized for many decades and from several different angles as misunderstood and misplaced concepts incapable of satisfying the requirements of scientific practice in general (e.g., see Kirk 1996; Nickerson 2000, 2011).

Our opinion is that a shift in perspective should occur with regard to how IS researchers perceive and practice quantitative research. The long history of controversy around the notion of statistical significance, and the respective remedies offered in the literature, offer a clear message for IS researchers. The message is that if IS researchers want to pursue rigorous quantitative research that matters to their own self-chosen questions of interest, whether the research is of a classic statistical type or data analytics type, they should view practical significance not as an optional notion to be taken into account, or as an antithesis to statistical significance, but as a necessary and complementary quality for their research to address. Practical significance is as equally important a measure as statistical significance when it comes to rigorous quantitative research. Thus, addressing practical significance can be regarded a major step toward broadening the notion-inuse of “rigor” in statistically/computationally conducted IS research. In other words, we posit that rigor should be perceived as composed of at least the two equally important measures of statistical significance and practical significance among those who practice statistically/computationally conducted IS research.

Nonetheless, our position by no means should be interpreted as a call for obtaining the largest possible statistical results with regard to practical significance. We once again emphasize the fact that the matter of practical significance, and the matters of statistical significance and relevance, refer to qualities heavily dependent on human judgment, research context and conventions. What we do want to promote through this essay however is the practice of communicating practical significance, however large or small the obtained research results are.

Issue 2: Communicating practical significance transcends merely presenting effect magnitudes and also requires their interpretation in context.

We acknowledge that many researchers in IS do recognize the importance of the notion of practical significance and its role in conducting sound statistical analysis. However, there seems to be a prevailing issue about how one can, or should, communicate the practical significance of research results. With the advent of statistical/computational software packages that provide advanced functionalities, researchers nowadays, particularly in the data analytics domain, can conveniently and effortlessly generate and report many different types of effect sizes. A dubious belief, however, is that as long as numbers known as “effect size” are reported in the text or tables of a research paper, the task of communicating the practical significance of research results is accomplished.

Scientific research goes much beyond simply reporting numbers. Researchers are also expected to deliver interpretations and insights on the basis of quantitative research results. This is an essential task given the fact that one’s interpretations of the magnitude of the same effect size measure can dramatically change from one research study to another depending on the particular circumstances under which the research is conducted. Therefore, to communicate the practical significance of research results is more a matter of offering interpretations about the magnitude of research results, rather than merely reporting them. The proper communication of the practical significance of findings is even of much higher importance in data analytics research. As stated earlier, whereas large datasets common in data analytics research can comfortably turn findings statistically significant (i.e., the deflated p-value problem), the practical significance of the findings, as a distinct matter from their statistical significance, cannot be taken for granted and certainly demands an explicit and justifiable treatment (see George et al. 2014; Lin et al. 2013).

## Recommendations on the Proper Communication of Practical Significance

In general, we recommend that IS researchers offer clear interpretations about the magnitude of research results and bring those interpretations to the fore, making them explicit in their research articles. This in our view is essential to make sure that the findings and conclusions drawn from our quantitative IS scholarship are transparently interpretable. In practice, we believe that the proper communication of the practical significance of results (particularly, those that are directly associated with research hypotheses) should incorporate the following four elements: effect magnitudes, confidence intervals, interpretations of magnitude, and rationales supporting the interpretations of magnitude.

(I) Effect Magnitudes: The proper communication of practical significance begins with an explicit presentation of effect size measurements. We particularly emphasize this for all effect sizes (e.g., path coefficients) directly related to research hypotheses. The measurements can be presented in a variety of different ways such as in the text, in tables, or on charts graphs. The important point is that the quantitative magnitude of effect sizes should be stated clearly. Authors should avoid using such vague terms as “positive impact” or “non-zero effect.” Our review of published IS research suggests that all of the papers pursue our recommendation and provide quantitative magnitudes of all effect sizes they deal with.

(II) Confidence Intervals: Whenever the data and statistical analysis warrant the calculation of confidence intervals, we emphasize the need for IS researchers to present confidence intervals in their papers. In fact, within the context of communicating practical significance, the reporting of confidence intervals “has been advocated as an alternative, or adjunct, to NHST [null hypothesis significance testing] by many writers, including both critics and proponents of NHST” (Nickerson 2000, p. 278). A confidence interval delivers all the same information provided by NHST (Kirk 1996, 2001). A confidence interval (surrounding the point estimate of an effect size) that includes 0 (where 0 is the point corresponding to the null hypothesis) suggests that the null hypothesis cannot be rejected. In other words, “the smaller the confidence interval that includes 0, the stronger the evidence for the null hypothesis” (Nickerson 2000, p. 261). However, a confidence interval provides more important information than what is afforded by NHST. A confidence interval shows “a range of values within which the effect parameter is likely to lie” (Kirk 2001, p. 214), with the same unit of measurement as the data (Kirk 1996, 2001). This means that calculating and reporting confidence intervals can significantly boost researchers ability to offer meaningful interpretations of magnitude (see element III). On the other hand, the practice of reporting confidence intervals yields a means to develop a common thread through which researchers in a community become able to conduct purposeful synthesis and meta-analysis of findings across different studies that investigate certain variables/phenomena of interest (Cumming and Finch 2001; Lin et al. 2013). Additionally, the frequent reporting of confidence intervals in a research community allows researchers to compare the magnitude, and the lower and upper bounds, of their own estimated effect sizes with the ones reported in similar studies. This therefore can aptly pave the way to establish the required rationale(s) for the interpretations of magnitude (see element IV).

The move toward reporting confidence intervals is quite widespread across research domains and communities. We for instance know that “medicine has moved away from sole reliance on NHSTs” and almost “all medical studies now state confidence intervals” (Schwab et al. 2011, p. 1111). In addition, a number of other disciplines have already announced the mandate to report confidence intervals in research studies (e.g., see American Educational Research Association 2006; American Psychological Association, 2010). In the IS field, we found some examples of published research where confidence intervals have been reported. For instance, Ludwig et al. (2014) present a research study on user communities where two mixed multilevel Poisson regression models have been utilized to conduct text mining on users’ posts in 37 communities. The authors show the 95% confidence intervals for an estimated effect size called incidence rate ratio (IRR). The confidence intervals are presented along with the actual magnitudes for the IRR effect size in a table (see Ludwig et al. 2014, Table 3). In another IS research study, Saboo et al. (2016) investigate the issue of marketing resource allocation using a computational model called the time-varying effect model (TVEM). The authors use “transaction data from a Fortune 500 retailer [along] with demographic information obtained from Acxiom Corp for over a quarter million customers” (p. 911) to develop and test their proposed model. The authors present 95% confidence intervals for parameter estimates of their independent variables of interest. These confidence intervals along with the magnitude of estimated parameters are presented in three tables (see Saboo et al. 2016, Tables 3, 4, and 6). According to the authors (p. 925), the confidence intervals are in fact reported to display the authors’ conservative approach in presenting and interpreting research results in reaction to “the deflated p-value problem” concerning data analytics studies.

Different computational/statistical software packages such as R, SPSS, STATA or even Excel are available to calculate confidence intervals. As shown in the cases of Ludwig et al. and Saboo et al., it appears a common practice to report 95% confidence intervals, but it should be noted that this is as arbitrary a practice as setting 0.05 or 0.01 thresholds for pvalues. Moreover, the manner in which researchers may present confidence intervals in their papers depends on the particular type of research they conduct and/or the readership they target. The simplest way to present confidence intervals is perhaps the way we observed in the cases of Ludwig et al. and Saboo et al., where confidence intervals are presented along with effect magnitudes in the text or in tables. However, there are other, more sophisticated, suggestions on how to present and take advantage of confidence intervals in research studies. We offer one example here. In the case of large-sample studies, Lin et al. (2013) argue for the use of a chart called the confidence interval chart. The chart “displays the confidence interval as a function of the sample size, ranging from very small to the maximal large-sample size itself” (p. 910). According to Lin et al., the chart is useful to show the effect magnitude and its decreasing standard error.<sup>16</sup>

(III) Interpretations of Magnitude: Whereas the two preceding elements of the proper communication of practical significance are, so to speak, “hard” elements, conveniently achievable nowadays with the help of statistical computer programs, the current element and the next one (i.e., rationales supporting the interpretations of magnitude) are “soft” elements, which are often challenging to deliver because they highly depend on researchers’ depth of understanding concerning both statistics as well as the research area they are dealing with. That is perhaps why the latter two “soft” elements are often missing in some published research. Notwithstanding, our review of published IS research suggests positive examples of presenting interpretations of magnitude. For instance, Salge et al. (2015), in their study of “behavioral and institutional search mechanisms underpinning hospitals IS investment decisions,” state the following regarding an obtained regression coefficient (p. 76):

According to hypothesis 3, we expected the coefficient of the lagged dependent variable IS investment intensity in model 3 to be between 0 and 1 and statistically significant. In line with our theoretical arguments for institutionalized search, our analyses yield a [statistically] significant estimate of 0.536, indicating moderately high levels of temporal persistence in hospitals’ IS investments.

In other words, Salge et al. consider 0.536, on a scale of 0 to 1, to constitute a moderately high effect size value. However, the academic audience of the paper might still argue that the number does not represent a moderately high effect size value. This all goes back to the presence, or lack thereof, of convincing rationales supporting the interpretations of magnitude—a crucial issue that will be discussed later under element IV.

The study by Greenstein and Zhu (2016) represents another example of published IS research where interpretations of the magnitude of obtained effect sizes are proffered to some extent. The authors’ investigation of “whether the open-content production model helps achieve a neutral point of view (NPOV)” (p. 618) uses data from Wikipedia’s articles on U.S. politics. In one particular case, the regression analysis run by Greenstein and Zhu yields estimated coefficients associated with the two independent variables of “unique contributors” and “total revisions to date” and a dependent variable, which is the probability of Wikipedia articles having certain “code words” related to U.S. politics. The authors interpret the results this way (p. 630):

We find that having more unique contributors reduces the probability of having code words, while having more revisions works in the opposite direction, increasing the probability of having code words. However, the value of the coefficients suggests a small overall effect: a 10% increase in the number of unique contributors reduces the chance of having code words by 0.2%, and a 10% increase in the number of revisions increases the chance by 0.1%.

It is worth noting that although Greenstein and Zhu offer their own interpretation (i.e., small overall effect) of the magnitude of the two coefficients, the exact basis on which the authors offered such an interpretation may still remain an open question for some segments of the paper’s audience. As stated earlier, we discuss the matter of rationales supporting the interpretations of magnitude under element IV.

The third example we draw here is Vance et al. (2015). The authors propose a new solution to address the issue of computer systems access-policy violations. Essentially, Vance et al. offer “four user-interface (UI) design artifacts that raise users’ accountability perceptions within systems and in turn decrease access-policy violations” (p. 345). In their statistical analysis of the matter, the authors investigate the relationship between an independent variable (i.e., Äaccountability) and the main dependent variable (i.e., Intention to Violate the Access Policy), among others. In a footnote, the authors present their interpretation of the magnitude of the obtained coefficient for Äaccountability (p. 357):

The size of the effect of Äaccountability can be understood in terms of the scale of the dependent variable, the range of the difference of the change in accountability, and the parameter estimate (-.187) in the model. For every unit increase in Äaccountability, intention was reduced by -.187. Because the values for the change in accountability had a range of 32, intention could be reduced by up to -5.76 (-.187 C 32) [-5.98 is correct]. Because intention was measured on a 0 to 10 scale, this represents a very large effect size.

We should emphasize that, as far as element III alone is concerned, the three IS studies examined above exemplify what we consider adequacy in the practice of offering interpretations of magnitude. However, these studies do not necessarily satisfy all the requirements we propose in this essay as for the proper communication of practical significance. For instance, only one of the three studies (i.e., Vance et al.), albeit partially, reports confidence intervals.

With regard to advice to facilitate the task of offering interpretations of magnitude, we wish to draw attention to two particular tactics here. The first tactic deals with utilizing a type of analysis known as “marginal analysis,” whenever possible. Indeed, the preceding quote from Vance et al. vividly demonstrates this type of analysis. As stated earlier in this essay, through marginal analysis, the sensitivity of a dependent variable to changes in an independent variable may be measured and reported by researchers (see Lee and Mohajeri 2012; Lin et al. 2013; Vittinghoff et al. 2005). Indeed, in the case of nonlinear models,

which are quite common in IS research, marginal analysis is a more robust way—and sometimes the only way—to interpret effect size, compared to looking at the p-value or magnitude of the coefficient (Lin et al. 2013, p. 909).

Marginal analysis can therefore be instrumental to not only deliver an interpretation of magnitude (i.e., element III), but also to articulate and present a sound rationale (i.e., element IV) for such an interpretation.

The other recommended tactic is to calculate and report unstandardized regression/path coefficients when possible. In fact, there can be different reasons as to why interpretations of the magnitude of obtained effect sizes are often missing in published research. In the case of path coefficients in SEM, we believe a major reason is that statistical software packages usually generate the coefficients in standardized form, which hardly lends itself to being meaningfully interpreted. Kirk and other statisticians suggest a general remedy, which is to “use the same unit of measurement as the data” (1996, p. 754) when calculating research results in order to facilitate the task of interpreting them. Accordingly, we recommend that IS researchers consider calculating and reporting unstandardized path/regression coefficients, particularly when the units of measurement of the data can be familiar to the outside world (e.g., Likert scale,<sup>17</sup> financial indicators/ratios, stock market indices, etc.). In SEM, this can be achieved by estimating path coefficients using the covariance matrix, rather than the correlation matrix (Kelley and Preacher 2012, p. 144). Also, in regression tables, which are often reported in papers, separate columns can be used to communicate both standardized and unstandardized coefficients. We know that “this is the way in which SPSS reports the output for linear regression analysis, whereas with SAS the option STB in PROC REG yields the same result” (Kelley and Preacher 2012, p. 148). Even if standardized path/regression coefficients are already obtained, there is a fairly straightforward formula to transform them to unstandardized ones when one conducts multiple regression or PLS analysis—which is “conceptually and practically…similar to using multiple regression analysis” (Hair et al. 2011, p. 140). The formula can be demonstrated in the following way:

$$
\text { Unstandardized   Coefficient } = \frac {\text { Standardized   Coefficient } \cdot S D _ {\text { dependent   variable }}}{S D _ {\text { independent   variable }}}
$$

“An unstandardized effect size is one whose interpretation is dependent on the units of the measurement instrument” (Kelley and Preacher 2012, p. 144). With unstandardized coefficients, authors are often in a better position to communicate the substantive importance of their obtained results to both the reviewers/editors and the readership. Employing the unstandardized magnitudes, authors can, for instance, conduct a marginal analysis and show the actual change in a dependent variable along the same scale as the collected data when one unit of change happens for any independent variable of interest, again along the same scale as the collected data. This often looks more sensible in the eyes of the reviewers, and particularly the readership, compared to an analysis that draws on standardized coefficients, where the entire narrative concerning the amount of change in the variables has to be stated in units of standard deviations.

(IV) Rationales Supporting the Interpretations of Magnitude: To offer interpretations of magnitude (element III) is a necessary, but insufficient, condition in order to ensure the proper communication of practical significance. Authors should also clearly disclose the rationales with which they make certain interpretations about the magnitude of their obtained effect sizes. Nevertheless, we found that the IS articles pointing to the issue of practical significance—in particular, those which promote the practice of addressing practical significance in data analytics research (e.g., Abbasi et al. 2016; Lin et al. 2013; Müller et al. 2016)—draw little or no attention to this critical aspect. Essentially, the question is: How may researchers establish sound rationales for their interpretations of magnitude? As stated before, the classic statistics literature has long suggested some conventional rationales to support the interpretation of practical significance (i.e., to distinguish among small, medium and large magnitudes) in the cases of certain types of effect sizes such as Cohen’s ƒ<sup>2</sup> or Cohen’s d (Cohen 1992; Kirk 1996). However, to draw on such conventions has been severely criticized, particularly over the past decade (e.g., Aguinis et al. 2010, pp. 529-530; Cortina and Landis 2009; Ellis, 2010). The reason is that any assessment of the magnitude of obtained effect sizes in a research study— even for some well-known effect size measures such as R<sup>2</sup>, Cohen’s ƒ<sup>2</sup> or Cohen’s d—must take into account the particular contextual circumstances under which the research study has been conducted. As stated earlier in this essay, depending on the research context, we can certainly envisage situations where a small effect magnitude can in fact denote a high substantive importance and vice versa (Lewandowsky and Maybery 1998; Prentice and Miller 1992; Rosenthal 1990; Rosenthal and Rubin 1979).

In our review of published IS research, we found examples displaying varying degrees of adherence to the, so to speak, “principle of interpretation in context” described in the preceding paragraph. For instance, Tian and Xu (2015), in their interpretation of obtained magnitudes for the Cohen’s ƒ<sup>2</sup> effect size, state the following (p. 51):

By convention, effect sizes of 0.02, 0.15, and 0.35 are termed small, medium, and large, respectively (Cohen 1988). We chose R&D spending, which has been generally considered to be a major risk factor, as a benchmark (e.g., Kothari et al. 2002). Based on the regression results reported in Column (3) of Table 4, the effect size of R&D spending and the effect size of ERP systems (including its primary effect and interactions with uncertainty) were estimated to be 0.0385 and 0.0161, respectively. Based on the regression results reported in Column (2) of Table 5, R&D spending and ERP system scope variables had effect sizes of 0.0344 and 0.0376, respectively. Hence, regarding their effect on firm risk, ERP systems and R&D spending had comparable effect sizes, between a small and medium effect size (Cohen 1988).

Evidently, Tian and Xu offer the least adherence to the principle of interpretation in context and state a rationale that is completely drawn on the classic conventions communicated in the statistics literature (i.e., Cohen 1988). In contrast, drawing another example from published IS research, we believe the marginal analysis quoted earlier (under element III) from Vance et al. largely satisfies the principle of interpretation in context. The authors show an adequate articulation of their logic for why they consider the parameter estimate of -0.187 for the Äaccountability variable to constitute a very large effect size. Another interesting example in the IS literature, where adherence to the principle of interpretation in context is implied, is Hsieh et al. (2012). In fact, Hsieh et al. employ very appropriate language to convey the message that their research findings are of substantive importance, although the magnitude of findings may be deemed small (p. 1075):

Importantly, while the marginal contribution of UserSat to ESQ is small in absolute terms (the correlation between UserSat and ESQ = 0.19, see Table 3; ÄR² = 4.2% for the direct effect and ÄR² = 3.5% for the interaction effect, see Table 4), methodologists have noted that small effects in the social sciences must be evaluated in the context of the significance of the practical problem and the theoretical gaps, the nature of the research design, and the measures (Ellis 2010; Rosnow and Rosenthal 2003). In our context (1) the problem domain is of significant practical value, (2) the research design involves multisourced data with a time lag, (3) the independent variables and/or dependent variables are cumulative in nature, and (4) the relationships are theoretically important. As such, the identified effects, though small in size, are indeed of considerable theoretical and practical value.

The rationale offered by Hsieh et al. could have been presented with much more elaboration regarding the four aspects indicated in the quote above, but does hint at an appropriate practice through which researchers may establish a rationale to support their interpretations of magnitude. The practice is to draw attention to the literature in a certain research domain. Ideally speaking, researchers may juxtapose the findings of their own study with the ones reported by comparable research studies in order to establish a sound rationale for their judgment of practical significance. As stated before, a pertinent facilitator in this type of practice is to draw on confidence intervals. Assuming that confidence intervals associated with a researcher’s variables of interest are already reported by some comparable studies in the literature, the researcher will be able to draw and show comparisons in terms of not only the magnitude of estimations, but also the uncertainty surrounding, or the precision of, the estimations (Cumming and Finch 2001; Schwab et al. 2011).

It is completely valid to say that not all IS research studies want or are conducted to directly inform, or offer implications to, nonacademics. Part of the reason is that nonacademics are neither the primary readership of IS academic journals nor often involved in the review process at those journals. However, we believe, as long as these journals require authors to address relevance to nonacademics (e.g., Straub and Ang 2008, 2011; Te’eni et al. 2015), there is a significant need to raise awareness among IS researchers that simply mentioning the name of one or more nonacademic stakeholder groups and embedding an implications for practice section in a research paper are not sufficient for the task of communicating research relevance. In fact, the lesson from our review of the published IS research is that communicating research relevance is not only a matter of form, but also, and more importantly, a matter of content. The content should be offered in a way that could appropriately enable nonacademic stakeholders/readers (if they happen to read IS academic journals) to make judgments about the impact and importance of a research study.<sup>19</sup> In particular, in data analytics research, it is of high importance for nonacademic decision makers to be able to comprehend the results of applying sophisticated models and algorithms to large datasets (see Müller et al. 2016, p. 294). “[Real-world] insights do not emerge automatically out of mechanically applying analytical tools to data” (Sharma et al. 2014, p. 435), in part, because “managerial decisions are typically based on economic return, rather than a statistically significant analytical model, and that is where analytics can generate mistrust and thus fall short” (Baesens et al. 2016, p. 815).

## Recommendations on the Proper Communication of Research Relevance

The proper communication of research relevance is largely absent in published quantitative IS research. Perhaps a major reason, as indicated before, is that there is no coherent account in the literature particularly characterizing the notion of relevance in the context of quantitative research. We, in this essay, have offered a remedy. The characterization of research relevance we presented in the Venn diagram section (see Table 2), as well as in our review of the 27 MIS Quarterly papers published in 2015 (see Table 5), indicates three major elements in the notion of relevance in quantitative research: the relevance of dependent variables, the relevance of independent/mediating variables, and the relevance of the magnitude of findings.<sup>20</sup> Accordingly, we recommend that if IS research papers are intended to provide nonacademics with lessons and implications (and our review of published IS research suggests that this is often the case), authors should provide adequate content on each of the three aforementioned elements in order to ensure the proper communication of research relevance.

(I) The Relevance of Dependent Variables: The dependent variables/constructs in quantitative research often represent the principal phenomena of interest researchers are dealing with. It is therefore crucial, regarding the communication of relevance, to offer content on the significance of dependent variables in the eyes of nonacademics. Based on the perspective proffered in this essay so far, it is clear that nonacademics may not necessarily find subjects or phenomena, which researchers choose to study, relevant. The manner in which we characterized the relevance of dependent variables in this essay (see the Venn diagram section) emphasizes two aspects: understandability and noteworthiness. In other words, in an ideal situation, authors in a research paper are to clearly establish that the nonacademic audience of their research not only understand, but also acknowledge the noteworthiness of, their dependent variable(s) of interest.

We envisage that, in practice, authors may find themselves in at least two distinct types of situations when striving for establishing the relevance of their dependent variables of interest. The first type of situation, which is quite common, has to do with research studies that employ dependent variables/constructs derived from pure academic literature. In this type of situation there is a high chance that the dependent variables/constructs will not be recognized at all, or not fully acknowledged, in the everyday life of nonacademic stakeholders. One practice we recommend in this type of situation is to collect empirical evidence from samples of the targeted nonacademic stakeholders through methods such as interviews or questionnaire surveys. The evidence should incorporate the stakeholders’ views about the understandability and noteworthiness of the researchers’ dependent variables of interest. It is also expected that researchers draw on the collected evidence (say, through triangulation with the related academic literature) to build/refine their proposed research model(s)/hypotheses. We found a compelling example of this practice in the study by Armstrong et al. (2015). As discussed earlier in our detailed review of Armstrong et al., the authors report on focus group interviews with IS professionals as one of the major nonacademic stakeholders they target. The authors conducted the interviews during the theory development phase with the aim of determining the applicability of their proposed research model to the particular context of an IS career. Particularly with respect to the relevance of their two major dependent variables of interest (i.e., Exhaustion from IS Career Experience and Turn-Away Intention), Armstrong et al. point out that “individuals in the focus groups highlighted the importance of exhaustion and turnaway for IS professionals” (p. 715). The authors also provide details on how they adjusted their research model based on comments from the IS professionals. Notwithstanding the example drawn from the IS literature, we understand that, in general, the task of conducting fieldwork to collect evidence in order to establish the relevance of dependent variables might be too demanding a requirement for IS researchers. Therefore, another appropriate practice we can recommend is to look for evidence reported by other studies in the literature, where the desired nonacademic stakeholder group(s) are shown to be understanding and acknowledging the importance of the researchers’ dependent variables/constructs of interest.

The second type of situation is concerned with research studies that incorporate constructs/variables that are directly, in one way or another, derived from industry reports, practitioner outlets, or online user-generated data. In this type of situation, the researchers’ dependent variables/constructs of interest are very likely to be already in use, or fully acknowledged, by nonacademic stakeholders. In that case, we recommend that authors provide clear evidence from the respective industry/online sources to communicate that their dependent constructs/variables of interest are actually the same, or directly related to the ones, already acknowledged among their targeted nonacademic stakeholders. An exemplar study is Gao et al. (2015). The authors provide plain evidence right from the beginning of their paper that their research variables directly reflect “population measures of physician quality as rated by patients, constructed using rigorous standards set by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) of the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services” (p. 566).

(II) The Relevance of Independent/Mediating Variables: Apart from dependent variables, the relevance of independent/ mediating variables should not also be taken for granted. As illustrated in the Venn diagram section, nonacademics may not find certain factors—which are research-wise believed to be contributing to, or associated with, a phenomenon— relevant (see Table 4). This is of particular issue in data analytics research, where, even from the standpoint of research rigor and methodology, there are increasing “construct validity/credibility concerns pertaining to variables derived from user-generated, non-survey-based data sources, [which are] often characterized by low veracity” (Abbasi et al. 2016, p. xxiii).

Our characterization of the relevance of independent/medi ating variables (see the Venn diagram section) specifies the two aspects of understandability and actionability. As mentioned before, actionability, in our view, is a contingent criterion—due to the possibility that a research study may merely invoke a “general” relevance, as opposed to an “action lever” relevance (see the Venn diagram section). Therefore, our recommendation is for authors to clearly establish and communicate that their targeted nonacademic stakeholders consider the independent/mediating variables at least understandable, if not actionable. The practicalities of establishing and communicating the relevance of independent/mediating variables though remain similar to that of dependent variables. Authors in fact may face the very same two different types of situations as described in the case of dependent variables. And again, the studies by Armstrong et al. and Gao et al. can definitely serve as exemplars of communicating the relevance of research variables (whether dependent or independent/ mediating). However, we also wish to draw attention here to the interesting case of Wang et al. (2015), where the rele vance of independent variables/constructs is established during the operationalization of the research model—as opposed to the cases of Armstrong et al. and Gao et al., where the relevance of research variables was mainly established during the development of the research model or even earlier. Wang et al. develop hypotheses on how five theoretical constructs—derived from the routine activity theory (RAT) in the criminology literature—concerning the characteristics of computer applications are associated with the dependent risk of unauthorized access attempts. In order to operationalize their research model, the authors solicit input from their main targeted nonacademic stakeholder group (i.e., security managers). In particular, the authors “interviewed application owners and security managers for relevant information regarding the [computer] applications” (p. 97). In addition, Wang et al. report on a Delphi-type exercise with “13 application owners who were ultimately responsible for the security and risk management of their applications” (p. A2). The aim of the exercise was to “confirm the mapping and mea surement relationship between [the theoretical] constructs and [the real-world computer] application characteristics” (p. 97).

To conclude our discussion on how to communicate the relevance of research variables/constructs, it is important to emphasize that, as shown through the exemplar studies, the evidence on the relevance of research variables/constructs might, and sometimes should, not merely be presented in an “implications for practice” section of a paper. On many occasions, authors may, and in fact need to, initiate communicating the relevance of research variables/constructs much earlier—for instance, in the theory development, measurement, or introduction sections.

(III) The Relevance of the Magnitude of Findings: To establish and communicate the relevance of research variables constructs is a crucial task. However, the bottom-line aspect of research relevance often has to do with the real-world impact associated with research findings in the eyes of the nonacademic audience. When it comes down to writing up the section on practical implications, IS researchers should establish and communicate research relevance in an objective manner—with clear mentions, and comprehensible interpretations, of the magnitude of research findings, rather than with subjective, imprecise statements, which may often have no readily apparent connection with those research findings. Our emphasis on providing nonacademics with objective and comprehensible interpretations of quantitative results also turns out to be completely in line with some emerging concerns in the data analytics domain—namely, that analytical models and algorithms often yield “measures that can be difficult to understand for end-users or nonexperts” (Baesens et al. 2016, p. 814). As addressed in a number of recent commentaries and editorials (e.g., Abbasi et al. 2016; Baesens et al. 2016; Lycett 2013; Müller et al. 2016; Sharma et al. 2014), the issue with the interpretability and accountability of the outputs of analytical algorithms seems to be a serious bottleneck in the way of realizing (business) value from data analytics. Although advanced analytics models are obviously superior in terms of capturing complex relationships and producing accurate results and predictions, there is clear evidence from such areas as healthcare or finance (e.g., Breiman 2001; Martens et al. 2007), where the stakes are high, that practitioners might still strongly prefer simpler, parsimonious regression models with a limited number of well-understood variables—“even though these models could be easily outperformed by non-linear models working on high-dimensional data sets” (Müller et al. 2016, p. 294). Therefore, strictly speaking, in a choice between interpretability and accuracy/comprehensiveness, it is not surprising at all that nonacademics would prefer the former.

In a nutshell, our recommendation is for authors to write the “implications for practice” section differently in the future. Authors should clearly exhibit how the real-world insights, advice, viewpoints or interpretations offered in that section are grounded in the particular magnitudes of research findings. Therefore, it is necessary to present the quantitative magnitude of pertinent findings again in the “implications for practice” section. To refer the readers back to a sophisticated table or chart, where many analysis results are often reported, to look for a particular research finding is not an appropriate practice. The magnitude of research findings along with the corresponding real-world insights or interpretations should be as accessible as possible to the nonacademic readership. It is also obvious that the use of language is a key. The language employed to communicate research findings should be as simple and straightforward as possible. That being said, other appropriate practices to facilitate the comprehension of research findings are to convert and present results in such easily digestible forms as percentages (when possible), and to visualize research findings using charts/graphs/plots. In a broader perspective on how to present findings in an “implications for practice” section, we also envision that the nonacademic audience would often conveniently relate to the cost-benefit type of analyses, where authors may present alternative manipulations of factors studied in their research in order to demonstrate possible varying returns in terms of comprehensible real-world costs/benefits.

According to our review of published IS research, the study by Wang et al. (2015) is the only one that shows a fair amount of adherence to the practices outlined in the preceding paragraph, albeit with respect to only one research finding. In their discussion of practical implications, the authors refer to one obtained hazard ratio and conclude the following (p. 108):

The study showed that one level of increase in CSTR [i.e., control strength] decreased the hazard rate of an application by 28 percent. This finding becomes pertinent in practice owing to the fact that each application works within several controls; enhancing an existing or implementing a new (exclusive or compensatory) control can have a farreaching impact on improving the overall security of an application.

The above quote from Wang et al. in fact denotes that the authors aim to pursue a form of cost-benefit analysis in order to establish a sound rationale on why a 28% decrease in the hazard rate of an application is significant in the world of practice. The authors could also pursue other complementary practices to further strengthen their rationale. One practice we recommend is to solicit, and present, views and comments from samples of targeted nonacademic stakeholders (in the case of Wang et al., security managers) on the significance of research findings (in the case of Wang et al., the 28% decrease in the hazard rate of an application). Another suitable approach is to draw comparisons with findings reported in practitioner outlets, where similar effect sizes, ratios or measures have been reported for the concerned phenomena/ variables. Additionally, in some cases, the comparisons may be made, in one way or another, between research findings and certain industry-wide/global standard rates, market averages, benchmarks, and so forth.

## Generalizing Relevance,Practical Significance, and Statistical Significance

To conclude our recommendations on how to properly communicate practical significance and research relevance, we raise a point as to how to address the issue of generalizability. May the interpretations made by a research study in a given setting, or with a certain population, concerning the matters of relevance, practical significance, and statistical significance be generalized to a new setting or population? For instance, for a particular research study, there could be the findings of no relevance, medium practical significance, and high statistical significance. May these interpretations be generalized to a new setting or population? According to Lee and Baskerville (2003), in their statement on the generalizability of a theory, “[t]he only way in which a researcher (or practitioner) may properly claim that the theory [which has been tested] is indeed generalizable to the new setting would be for the theory to be actually tested and confirmed in the new setting” (p. 237). Therefore, in a research study that, for instance, involves SEM, the “theory” would refer to its operationali zation in the form of equations, which are fitted, using data from a sample, to the population. Thus, the communication of practical significance, relevance, and even statistical significance, of results in the research study should also include the caution that the interpretations associated with these matters may not necessarily generalize to a new setting or population—and that valid interpretations would require generating new results by refitting the statistical equations to the new setting or population.

## Conclusion

To conduct statistical analysis merely to obtain statistically significant results is too limited a goal. Statistical significance simply has to do with statistical concerns as to whether an obtained result may be considered discernible or not. Returning to the question posed earlier (i.e., toward the end of the “Introduction” section) on the relationship between statistical significance and scientific inference, it is also clear that statistical significance does not do any good under most circumstances because, mathematically speaking, statistical significance is concerned with $p ( \mathrm { D } | \mathrm { H } _ { 0 } )$ , whereas through scientific inference researchers are looking for $p ( \mathrm { H } _ { 0 } | \mathrm { D } )$ .

In addition to statistical significance, there are the matters of practical significance and relevance. Practical significance, although a well-established concept in statistics and some social science areas, is largely misunderstood in the IS field. Concerning the question “What is practical significance?” (posed toward the end of the “Introduction” section), both the theoretical (see Table 1) and illustrative (see Table 2) material in this essay indicate that practical significance refers to the impressiveness of the magnitude of results—obtained through statistical/computational analyses—in the research world. Research results could well be significant in the statistical sense but still may be of no or little substantive importance among researchers. This long-established contrast between statistical significance and practical significance, therefore, clearly suggests—in response to the question (posed toward the end of the “Introduction” section) on the necessity of reporting on practical significance—that researchers need to address, and report on, not only p-values, but also the practical significance of obtained effect sizes, no matter if those effect sizes are reported in the more recent “data analytics” type of research, or in the classic types such as ANOVA, linear regression or SEM. Furthermore, a firm communication structure has to be in place for reporting practical significance. To only present effect size measurements in some bulky tables or charts would not suffice. At least three other elements (see Table 6), in addition to effect magnitudes, have to be communicated in IS research studies in order to accomplish an appropriate reporting on practical significance.

Nonetheless, a research study, where statistically and practically significant results are obtained and properly communicated, may not necessarily bear relevance to the real world. Relevance is a distinct matter. As conceptualized in this essay, relevance pertains to the situation in which a nonacademic stakeholder finds the phenomenon under study, the factors associated with the phenomenon, and the reported magnitude of findings, to make a difference to the stakeholder’s own real-world concerns or interests. Therefore, in response to the last question posed in the “Introduction” section, it is completely conceivable to think of situations where research findings would be of considerable practical significance among researchers, but the research as a whole would not interest the targeted nonacademic audience (see Table 4).

In principle, it is possible for a study to bear any or all three of the qualities of statistical significance, practical significance and relevance. A pertinent question, therefore, is: “Might IS researchers deliver more impactful research if their studies would bear all the three qualities?” Whereas achieving all three qualities in a research study would be desirable, we believe there still might be occasions where a research study may or may not bear one or more of these three qualities, but could still tell an interesting and impactful story. Thus, our major motivation behind this essay is not to call on producing more IS research bearing all the three qualities; rather, we are motivated by establishing the lesson that all three qualities should be appropriately addressed and reported on by researchers. Therefore, this essay is not primarily meant to address a problem of research methodology per se, but to draw attention to an equally important problem, namely, that of research communication.

Being concerned with the issue of research communication, our review of published IS research suggests that statistical significance (as expected) is always reported, but practical significance and relevance are seldom fully reported in IS research studies. This, in our view, is indicative of the lack of attention to the development and promotion of proper research communication practices in our community. Consequently, our effort in this essay has been to put forth a body of recommendations concerning how researchers can better communicate the value of their research to both academic and nonacademic stakeholders. This is a critical effort considering the fact that quantitative research has a major role in shaping the profile and character of IS research as a whole. Interestingly to note, our review of MIS Quarterly papers published in 2015 established that 64% (27 out of 42) of the articles involved statistical/computational analyses.

In summary, our recommendations for future quantitative IS research are (1) to view practical significance as an essential part of rigorous statistical practice and a necessary companion to statistical significance; (2) to communicate the practical significance of research results by offering explicit, contextsensitive interpretations of magnitude and supporting rationales; (3) to provide evidence on the relevance of variables incorporated into the research; and (4) to discuss implications of the research for nonacademics by making reference to the magnitude of research findings, rather than merely the sign or the statistical significance of those findings. Our recommendations are readily implementable and do not place any additional burden on researchers. Indeed, as exemplified throughout this essay, there have been some past IS research studies that have, in effect, followed our recommendations to some extent. Furthermore, while not primarily intended to serve as procedures or evaluative standards as to how one may achieve or assess practical significance or relevance in IS research studies, our recommendations can still give rise to ideas with respect to such procedures or standards—a research opportunity worthy to be pursued much further in the future.
