# Economics of shareware: How do uncertainty and piracy affect shareware quality and brand premium?

Wendy Hui <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Byungjoon Yoo <sup>b</sup>, Kar Yan Tam <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Information Technology, Zayed University, Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates

<sup>b</sup> College of Business Administration, Seoul National University San 56-1, Shinlim-Dong, Kwanak-Gu, Seoul, Korea

<sup>c</sup> Department of Information and Systems Management, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, Hong Kong

Received 5 May 2006; received in revised form 29 March 2007; accepted 26 July 2007 Available online 3 August 2007

## Abstract

Increased network speed has opened up new opportunities for developers to distribute their products as shareware through the Internet. This paper analyzes the interrelationships among key issues that are central to the software industry, including uncertainty, piracy, shareware quality and full version price. We show that customers' aversion to uncertainty of software quality tends to increase shareware quality while piracy tends to decrease it. Counter-intuitively, the perceived quality of the full version software or the trust towards the software developer does not affect the full version price and the profit of the firm when piracy is prevalent. © 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Shareware economics; Piracy; Uncertainty; Pricing; Versioning

## 1. Introduction

The existence of freeware has been a puzzling phenomenon to economists as freeware developers do not receive any monetary rewards from their time and effort in developing software that is shared by the general public. Shareware is a closely related concept, motivated by “sharing” good software with friends. The first piece of shareware was initially distributed free, but as maintenance and administrative costs increased, the author asked users to donate a small amount of money on a voluntary basis [27], and thus, the shareware distribution model was born. Although monetary compensation is expected in shareware, payment based on the honor system is still not a phenomenon well-explained by classical economics because a classical rational consumer would never pay for the shareware. As it turned out, however, the owner of this very first piece of shareware actually earned a fortune based on the honor system [27].

The shareware model has one obvious advantage: it allows software developers to bypass intermediaries and keep most of their revenue as their profit. This advantage especially appeals to individual programmers and small developers who normally do not have the financial resources to adopt the traditional on-the-shelf strategy. In fact, in the early days, shareware products were usually small and simple utilities or games written by individual programmers who started off developing these programs as a hobby. As of mid-1990s, shareware was only a \$300 million industry, a very humble figure compared to that of the commercial software [15]. In recent years, broadband connection has changed the situation significantly. Increased network speed that facilitates transfer of large data files has made it a common practice to deliver shareware and full version software over the Internet. In October 2004, the market was surprised to find that even Microsoft began to adopt the shareware marketing model through the Internet [17]. Shareware is now generally considered different from freeware, with the understanding that the user may pay for it later, if he finds it useful and decides to continue using it. The honor system is still in use today, but most developers now add various use restrictions in shareware to make sure that their efforts are rewarded. This paper focuses only on liteware (i.e., the disabling of certain features of the software).

Many users have lamented the “death of shareware” and complained about developers' excessive disabling of software functionalities in shareware [1]. And as the sharing spirit of shareware continues to evaporate, more and more often, marketing strategists regard it as a variation of the try-before-use distribution model [21], which aims to reduce uncertainty faced by consumers when they make their purchase decisions about new products. According to Nelson [30], some attributes of products can only be collected from experience. This is especially true for software, the quality of which, as most would agree, can only be observed by experience. It is this very characteristic of being an experience good that makes the shareware strategy a particularly attractive marketing tool for software.

Because the emergence of shareware has not been driven by economic concerns, there are limited analytical studies on it. And yet, as shareware gains popularity in the software and gaming industry as a marketing tool, it is of interest to both researchers and practitioners to better understand the economic implications of its use. Since piracy is common in the software industry, and since shareware opens up new opportunities for pirates (as we shall explain shortly), an analysis of shareware would not be complete without considering how piracy affects shareware decisions. In this study, we analyze the interrelationships between consumers' uncertainty about quality, piracy, shareware (liteware) quality and the full version price.

This paper is organized as follows: The following section provides background information of shareware and clearly states our research questions. Section 3 reviews the literature of shareware economics, piracy and other marketing strategies that serve to eliminate consumer uncertainty. Section 4 presents and analyzes the base model. Section 5 analyzes the uncertainty aversion model and Section 6 analyzes the piracy model.

Our results suggest that aversion to uncertainty and piracy have opposite effects on shareware quality: aversion to uncertainty increases shareware quality, while piracy decreases it. Section 7 discusses the business implications of our findings with real life examples and observations. Finally, Section 8 concludes the paper with suggestions on future research.

## 2. Background

As mentioned earlier, some shareware programs are distributed based on the honor system. However, it is more common nowadays to find shareware applications that are programmed with a built-in expiration date. Some simply add annoyance in the program once the trial period is passed. One example is NJStar Communicator, a program that writes and views Chinese, Korean and Japanese characters. If the user continues to use the shareware after expiry, there will be a one second delay per day after the expiry date before the software launches successfully, as shown in Fig. 1.

Some software developers distribute demonstration versions that would work for only a certain period of time. Afterwards, the user is required to pay a registration fee. Examples include MathType (an equation editor that works in Microsoft Office) and Windows Live OneCare (an all-in-one application for personal computing security, functionalities of which include virus scanning, firewall and file backup). When a user installs the shareware, he would receive the dialog box such as that as shown in Fig. 2. To obtain the right to use the full version, the user has to input a registration code that he receives upon successful registration. However, the user may pirate the software with the use of software key generators (or “keygens”), which are small executable files that generate registration numbers.

Other developers offer software with limited functionalities as shareware (also called liteware). It serves to lure users to buy the full version of the software. Examples include Ultimate ZIP Cracker (a password recovery application) (http://www.vdgsoftware.com), My Screen Recorder (http://www.deskshare.com), and #1 DVD Ripper (http://www.dvdtox.com). Many PC and Mac games are also offered this way. Examples are the Escape Velocity series (http://www.ambrosiasw. com), Airburst (http://www.strangeflavor.com) and Noiz2sa (http://victoly.com/\~adam/noiz.html). Users may become satisfied with the liteware and decide to continue to use it instead of buying the full version [37]. Alternatively, “unethical” users may also use “crack” programs to modify the code in the shareware and unlock the full version capability.

This paper focuses on liteware and the reader should apply the results only to this type of shareware. The results can also be applied to “time consuming” or “attention consuming” shareware that punishes the user who continues to use the shareware after the expiry date by annoying them with subscription reminders (e.g., NJ Star), or shareware that shows users advertisements unless they pay for the full version (e.g., Eudora). However, the concept of “shareware quality” in the model will need to be interpreted differently; we shall discuss how the interpretation needs to be altered when we present the results. It is important to note that results in this paper cannot be applied to shareware that becomes completely unusable to the user after the expiry date, e.g., MathType, Windows Live OneCare.

The business motivation of shareware is to market software to consumers directly. It allows consumers to try a program before they buy it. Many software products are considered experience goods because users would have no idea whether they satisfy their needs or tastes without having tried them [31]. Allowing consumers to evaluate whether the software fulfills their needs and suits their tastes is important because it reduces uncertainty on the consumer side. For example, video and online games are typical experience goods and frequently employ this marketing strategy through trial versions uploaded on the web or free trials at games counters in department stores and shopping malls.

When deciding whether or not to adopt the shareware strategy, the firm faces a dilemma: If he disables functionalities too much, the user may find that the product is not interesting or that there is too much uncertainty in making the purchase decision; however, if the shareware quality is too good, the user may find the shareware adequate and never buy the full version. Shareware is also particularly vulnerable to piracy because they rely on the honor system, or can be cracked easily. In this study, we seek to answer three research questions: (1) how far should the firm disable full version functionalities in the shareware? (2) How does aversion to uncertainty affect the optimal shareware quality, and (3) how would the shareware and pricing decisions be affected by piracy?<sup>1</sup>

## 3. Literature review

## 3.1. Shareware economics

There has been limited effort in academic research on shareware. Heiman and Muller [23] look at the effect of competition on the length of the trial period and usage restrictions and show that competition tends to increase the duration and quality of the trial version. Chellappa and Shivendu [9] investigate the possibility of using shareware to mitigate piracy in the context of versioning and identifies the conditions under which the offering of shareware is optimal. However, the managerial insights sought in the current paper are fundamentally different; and our model setup is also notably different from theirs: (1) instead of using shareware to mitigate piracy, we investigate the optimal shareware strategy in the presence of piracy, which is assumed to be a given market condition, i.e., out of the control of the firm. (2) While we do not assume that the firm offers different versions of the legal software, we study other important issues including brand premium, piracy costs, and pirated software quality, and their implications to marketing practices of software developers. Gopal et al. [19] investigated the economic impacts of online sharing of music. Although the context of application is different, music shared over P2P networks are usually in the mp3 format and are generally considered of lower quality than music CDs; online music sharing may be considered as a channel for sampling music, and the mp3 downloads would be analogous to trial versions of software. Driven by the differences in the research questions studied, there are three key differences in of model setup of the current study from that in Gopal et al. [19]: (1) the quality of the trial version is under the control of the firm, i.e., a different firm may set a different quality level for its shareware; whereas in Gopal et al. [19], the reduction in the consumption value of the pirated software compared to CD music is a given market condition. (2) As in Chellappa and Shivendu [9], Gopal et al. [19] investigate how piracy can be affected by “trial version”, but the current study takes piracy as a given market condition. (3) In Gopal et al. [19], consumers demonstrate one-dimensional heterogeneity (demand for music), while in the current study, consumers differ in two dimensions (demand for quality and taste). The current study serves to complement Chellappa and Shivendu [9] and Gopal et al. [19] and promote better understanding of the different economic forces at work when using the shareware marketing strategy.

## 3.2. Aversion to uncertainty

People are generally averse to uncertainty. Grossman [20] shows that consumers with rational expectations will assume that monopolist offers the worst possible quality consistent with his disclosure when he makes less than full disclosure. Thus, it is sometimes of interest to the firm to disclose information about their products to the consumers prior to their purchase decisions. Firms employ various marketing activities to eliminate uncertainty faced by consumers, e.g., brokerage houses provide seminars and reports to potential buyers, free samples of food items can be found at promotional counters in supermarkets, and new releases of video games can be tried at department stores. Firms invest in these strategies in hope that they can help generate longer term profits.

There are quite a number of analytical studies on marketing strategies for reducing uncertainty. When disclosure of information is used to facilitate price discrimination, Lewis and Sappington [29] show that in a variety of settings, it is best that either buyers are supplied with best available knowledge, or no information is supplied at all. Cremer's study [14] on discounts on repeated buying shows that the practice of this marketing strategy can be explained by a model in which a monopolist sells a good, and the buyers are uncertain of their taste for the product but not of the quality of the product per se. Che [8] studies the rationale for customer return policies by focusing on the uncertainty faced by consumers and shows that the firm adopts the return policy when consumers are highly averse to uncertainty. In this study, we look at how the quality of shareware can be used to control the consumer's uncertainty level to maximize the firm's profit.

## 3.3. Piracy

Due to the ease of digital duplication, piracy is prevalent in the software industry. In 2005, the software industry lost about US\$34 billion in revenue to piracy [2]. About half comes from Asia, where Vietnam, Indonesia, Pakistan and China are the biggest offenders. Piracy losses in Western Europe are around \$11.8 billion, while losses in North America are around US\$7.6 billion in 2005 [2].

There has been substantial research on piracy in Information Systems, Business Ethics and Organizational Management. A number of potential predictors of piracy have been found, including demographics [34], perceptions of authority figures' approval or disapproval [12], software prices [11,22], and presence of profit motive [28,38]. Hinduja [24] shows that pirates rationalize for illegal behavior by blaming the relative high prices of software or faulting the greedy profiteering motives of software corporations. Perceiving the situation and circumstances this way unbridles the individual from any ethical harnesses, freeing him or her to participate in the activity. Other proponents of piracy would argue that software creators who fail to implement copy protection schemes actually deserve to have those vulnerabilities exploited or cracked. Finally, culture also plays a role in piracy behaviors [18].

Analytical research on piracy can also be found in the literature. From the social planner's perspective, Banerjee [4] shows that welfare maximization may or may not result in monitoring as the socially optimal outcome. Chen and Png [10] show that increases in detection affect welfare more negatively than price cuts and that tax is welfare superior to fines in copyright enforcement. Gopal and Sanders [18] show that the government's incentives to enact and enforce copyright laws are closely related to the size of the domestic software industry. From the firm's perspective, Takeyama [36], Slive and Bernhardt [33], and Shy and Thisse [32] suggest that it might be profitable for the firm to allow limited piracy to exist in the market. The general argument is that piracy increases the network size which has a positive effect on the value of the software. Chaves and Deroian [7] explain the benefits of piracy to the firm by learning costs. Gopal and Sanders [18] analyze the use of strategic alliances between local and foreign software publishers to combat piracy. Bae and Choi [3] consider two different types of piracy costs: the reproduction cost and the degradation cost, and show that the effects of piracy depend crucially on the nature of the piracy costs.

More recently, Sundararajan [35] analyze the optimal pricing scheme and digital rights protection in the presence of piracy. He shows that vertical price discrimination can lead to lower level of digital rights protection, which in turn increases the total surplus. Jaisingh [25] studies the effect of digital rights protection on the profitability of recording companies when consumers have the choice of pirating from file sharing networks. According to him, the disutility associated with digital rights protection on legal copies would decrease revenue in the absence of network effects. Interestingly, in the presence of network effects, none-zero protection become optimal. His results are opposite to what was found in previous research e.g., [13], and highlight the importance of considering consumer disutility resulted from digital rights protection.

Although existing literature has provided us with an understanding and intuition about the relationship between piracy and full version software, little is known about its effect on shareware. This study analyzes this relationship and provides important business implications to marketing strategists in the software and computer games industry.

## 4. Model and analysis

In this study, consumers have different tastes located on the Hotelling model as shown in Fig. 3. The firm's product is fixed and is assumed to be located at 0, but the consumers do not know about this before they have experienced the product. “Taste” measures consumer's heterogeneity in preference for product characteristics that are not directly related to product quality, e.g., the look-and-feel, organization of presented information, interaction style, etc. It is for simplicity that the Hotelling model is used instead of other location models for horizontal differentiation.

Beside differences in taste, consumers also have different demand for quality. In the literature, the marginal utility of quality is typically assumed to be continuous and decreasing. For simplicity, we assume that the utility from quality is piecewise linear as shown in Fig. 4. A consumer has a demand for the quality of the product $q _ { \mathrm { c } } .$ Below this desired level of quality, every unit of improvement in quality is valued equally. When the need is met, further improvement in quality is not valued. Therefore, mathematically, we assume that utility is given by

$$
v (q, q _ {\mathrm{c}}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} q & q \leq q _ {\mathrm{c}} \\ q _ {\mathrm{c}} & q > q _ {\mathrm{c}} \end{array} \right.
$$

The assumption that there is no additional utility beyond some quality level is not very unrealistic or restrictive. For example, from a psychological perspective, a user's utility is influenced by the perceived usefulness of the software, which is in turn affected by the functionalities needed by the users; once these core needs are met, the perceived usefulness is unlikely to be enhanced by additional features. Mathematically, the only difference between a typical continuous and concave utility function and our piecewise linear utility function is that the indifference curves shown in Figs. 7 and 9, instead of being straight lines, would be curved. The curved indifference curves would in turn affect the exact value of the objective functions and the resultant solutions slightly. However, since our piecewise linear utility function resembles a concave function, our propositions (which focus on the directional relationship between various factors instead of a specific optimal value) should remain the same.

It is true that there is a lot of competition in many software applications and a monopolistic model is in general not applicable in these markets. However, there are markets in the software industry that are not very competitive, $e . g .$ , Photoshop (which is the de facto industry standard for editing images in the business world), and results of this paper would be relevant to these industries. Furthermore, from a theoretical perspective, we believe that it is important to analyze a monopolistic model which results can serve as a baseline for comparison with subsequent studies on competitive markets.

## 5. Aversion to uncertainty

In Gaudeul's [16] model, it is assumed that people value the product less favorably when they do not know much about the product. To incorporate this into our model, we assume that, in Stage 1, the shareware allows consumers to find out the fit of the software in taste, but since some capabilities have been disabled in the shareware, the consumers can only guess the full version quality. This assumption is consistent with Cremer [14]. We further assume that consumers discount the full version quality according to the level of uncertainty they face. This assumption is applicable to liteware for which limited functions do not prevent the consumer from accurately evaluating the fitness of taste (thus, no discounting along the taste dimension). For example, the trial version of a DVD ripper may only allow the user to rip 50% of a DVD. By using the trial version, the user can have perfect understanding of the style of interaction with the software (the taste dimension); but the user may be not be certain about the speed of ripping the entire DVD, the ease of conversion to different video formats, the level of the technical support provided by the firm (including notification of software updates), documentation, and other factors that determine the quality of a software product [26].

We model uncertainty to be $\scriptstyle u = k ( 1 - q _ { \mathrm { s } } )$ , as shown in Fig. 6. We interpret k as the negative of trust or distrust towards the software developer. A small k indicates that the market generally trusts the quality of the products produced by the software developer, while a large k indicates a general distrust. For the sake of convenience and ease of interpretation, we define trust $t \equiv 1 - k ,$ , and so we now have $\boldsymbol { u } { = } ( 1 { - } t ) ( 1 { - } q _ { \mathrm { s } } )$ . We could have used the notion of “distrust” (which is more akin to the notion of uncertainty) all the way through our analysis, however, we introduce “trust” because, compared to “distrust”, it is more readily interpretable as a property or characteristic of the firm, and thus it gives us a convenient way to state a proposition in business terms. Henceforth, we shall refer only to “trust” instead of “distrust” in our analysis.

\begin{array}{l} \Pi_ {2} = a _ {2} \times \text { Area(Full   Version   Users) } \\ \quad = a _ {2} \times \left[ \frac {1}{2} (q _ {\mathrm{s}} + 1 - a _ {2}) (1 - q _ {\mathrm{s}} - a _ {2}) - \frac {1}{2} (1 - \widetilde {Q}) (1 - a _ {2} - \widetilde {Q} + a _ {2}) \right] \\ \quad = \frac {a _ {2}}{2} [ (1 - a _ {2}) ^ {2} - q _ {\mathrm{s}} ^ {2} - (1 - t) ^ {2} (1 - q _ {\mathrm{s}}) ^ {2} ] \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Now, the optimal value for $q _ { \mathrm { s } }$ is not obvious. We maximize profit with respect to $q _ { \mathrm { s } }$ and $a _ { 2 }$ to find

$$
q _ {\mathrm{s}} ^ {*} = \frac {(1 - t ^ {2})}{1 + (1 - t) ^ {2}}
$$

We can see that $q _ { \mathrm { s } } ^ { \ast }$ decreases in t:

Proposition 2. The optimal quality of the shareware decreases with consumer's trust towards the software developer, and it decreases in an increasing rate.

The optimal full version price and profit are found to be

$$
a _ {2} ^ {*} = \frac {2 + 2 (1 - t) ^ {2} - \sqrt {1 + 5 (1 - t) ^ {2} + 4 (1 - t) ^ {4}}}{3 (1 + (1 - t) ^ {2})}
$$

and

$$
\Pi_ {2} ^ {*} = \frac {(2 + 2 (1 - t) ^ {2} - \sqrt {1 + 5 (1 - t) ^ {2} + 4 (1 - t) ^ {4}}) (1 - 2 (1 - t) ^ {2} + \sqrt {1 + 5 (1 - t) ^ {2} + 4 (1 - t) ^ {4}})}{2 7 (1 + (1 - t) ^ {2}) ^ {2}}
$$

It is easy to prove that

Proposition 3. When there is no piracy, the optimal full version price, $^ { a _ { 2 } ^ { * } , }$ , and profit, $\varPi _ { 2 } ^ { * }$ , both increase with the trustworthiness of the software developer or publisher.

Fig. 8 graphically shows the equilibrium quantities with respect to trust (t). Proposition 2 (shown in Fig. 8a) can find application in massively multiplayer online games and PC games. For example, popular games such as Disney's Toontown Online and Linden Lab's Second Life provide only limited functions for their trial versions, whereas less popular games provide full capabilities in their trial versions. Similarly, Chess Rally, which is a popular chess game, has a shareware version that deliberately slows down the response time of the computer when the user plays chess against the computer. From the author's experience, the computer actually plays a lot slower than an average chess player in the Chess Rally shareware. On the other hand, Fantasy Chess, a newer and less established game, only disables some aesthetic features in their shareware. Interested readers can find both games under Download.com. For Proposition 3 (Fig. 8b and c), the results are intuitive, as it is well understood that branded goods are more expensive and profitable than no frills goods.

To apply these results to “time consuming” or “attention consuming” shareware, we need to first note annoyance hurts the overall user experience, which in turns affects the perceived usefulness of the software. Therefore, beside the trustworthiness of the software developer, the expected quality of the full version software is also negatively related to the shareware quality $( q _ { \mathrm { s } } )$ (which is the full version quality discounted by the level built-in annoyance in the shareware in this context). Hence, the formulation of uncertainty (u) is still valid and the results in this section can be directly applied to “time consuming” and “attention consuming” shareware; and except for $q _ { \mathrm { s } } ,$ , interpretation of all variables needs not be changed. Applied in this context, Proposition 2 suggests that a more trustworthy software developer cannot afford to introduce more annoyance in their shareware.

## 6. Piracy

Piracy is common in the software industry and shareware is particularly vulnerable to piracy because it is possible for the user to unlock full version capabilities using crackers. In this section, we look at how piracy affects shareware decisions.

Jaisingh [25] describes in detail why pirated software is of lower quality than the official software. Pirates may not be able to receive technical support from the software developer. They are also deprived of the right to receive patches and upgrades for the software. We assume here that the expected pirated software quality is discounted by a factor of r compared to the expected full version quality, $i . e . ,$ , its quality is expected to be $r \tilde { \mathcal { Q } }$ Jaisingh [25] also describes the cost of pirating, which may include the cost of searching for crackers and key generators in the context of shareware piracy. It is denoted as $g$ and it is a one time cost. We also assume that $g < a _ { 2 }$ . In this piracy model, all consumers are allowed to pirate. Although individuals have to pay ethical cost when they pirate, for simplicity, we ignore this effect. We take a pessimistic view that all consumers are “unethical” software users if pirating provides them the highest positive surplus. The surplus an individual receives from pirating is

$$
v (r \widetilde {Q}, q _ {\mathrm{c}}) - x - g
$$

In Stage 2, the indifference curves are as shown in Table 4 (Please refer to Appendix A3 for the derivations). They are graphically presented in Fig. 9.

The assumption that all individuals are unethical may at first glance seem unrealistic. However, it is intended that the piracy model (in this section) be interpreted together with the uncertainty aversion model (in the previous section). The uncertainty aversion model portrays a world where all users are “ethical,” and the piracy model portrays a world where all users are “unethical”. The two models together provide an interesting contrast to situations at two extremes. The real world is probably somewhere in between; and by contrasting the solutions to the two models, the reader can obtain a good idea of the directions to which different variables move when there are more or less piracy in a market.

Proposition 4. Both the optimal full version price, $^ { a _ { 2 } ^ { * } } ,$ , and profit, $\pi ^ { * }$ , are NOT dependent on consumers' trust towards the software developer.

Proposition 4 might seem counter-intuitive. Mathematically, if we look at the objective functions for the cases with and without piracy, $i . e .$ , Eqs. (2) and $( 3 )$ we find that Eq. (3) can be expressed entirely in terms of $\tilde { \mathcal { Q } }$ alone, while Eq. (2) cannot. Therefore, the solution to the maximization of Eq. (3) gives $\tilde { Q } ^ { * }$ and $a _ { 2 } ^ { * }$ expressed in terms of r and g only. Intuitively, this result is driven by the fact that the official full version is now competing with the pirated version, which is in generally only slightly inferior but much cheaper. The firm now cannot charge consumers based on the expected quality of the full version alone; instead, it has to charge relative to the utility provided by the pirated software. But since the brand value of the firm goes to both the legitimate full version as well as the pirated version, brand premium evaporates in the presence of privacy.

Given that $r > g ,$ , it is not hard to show that both the full version price, $a _ { 2 } ^ { * }$ , and profit, $\varPi ^ { * }$ , increase with the cost of pirating, $^ { g , }$ and decrease with the expected quality of the pirated software $r \tilde { \mathcal { Q } } ^ { * }$ . This result is intuitive: if the firm makes it difficult for the consumers to pirate, the firm can raise the full version price and earn more profit because the pirated software is now less competitive. By similar argument, if the expected quality of the pirated software is high, it becomes more competitive, and the firm has to lower the full version price and at the same time loses some business to the pirated software.

As in Section $5 ,$ results here can be directly applied to shareware that uses annoyance or third-party advertisements as the price for not paying for the full version software. $q _ { \mathrm { s } } ,$ however, should be interpreted as the full version quality discounted by the built-in annoyance in the shareware.

## 7. Business implications

7.1. Do not offer shareware if the consumers are certain about the quality of the software product

Proposition 1 shows that when there is no uncertainty, the firm is always better off not to offer shareware. The result implies that if the consumers are already familiar with the product, the firm should not offer shareware. Therefore, the liteware of the latest version of the software should be directed towards non-users of the software instead of existing users.

7.2. Established firms can afford to offer lower quality shareware

In a world without piracy, when a firm offers a completely new software or game, the optimal shareware quality is negatively related to trustworthiness of the software developer. A software developer that consumers find trustworthy can afford to produce lower quality shareware. For example, the value of massively multiplayer online games (MMOG) comes mainly from interaction with other gamers online, instead of the software itself. In order to play on one of the game servers, the user has to pay the software developer or publisher a subscription fee regularly. The issue of piracy is less relevant. (In fact, a lot of online game developers distribute the game software freely.) In the MMOG market, we can find that popular games such as Disney's Toontown Online and Linden Lab's Second Life provide only limited functions for their trial versions, whereas less popular games provide full capabilities in their trial versions.

## 7.3. Invest in digital rights management in shareware

Although [25] has shown that it is not optimal to add digital protection in the official full version software in the absence of network externalities because such protection introduces disutility to the ethical users.

However, adding protection to the shareware will not introduce such disutility to ethical users who actually use the full version. Our results suggest that firms should invest in digital rights management for shareware to increase the cost of pirating. This way, the firm not only earns more profit but can also increase the quality of the shareware and allow consumers to evaluate the product more completely.

## 7.4. Increase relative quality of the official full version compared to pirated software

We found that profit increases with the relative quality of the official full version compared to pirated software. One way to achieve this is provide additional value added services to the official full version users. Perhaps Microsoft does not regularly supply updates and patches of Windows to official users with this motivation; however, it is achieving the same purpose. With so many Microsoft conspiracy theories circulating on the Internet,<sup>4</sup> offering free Windows updates is clearly delivering a business value for Microsoft.

## 7.5. Piracy eliminates brand premium

We also found that without piracy, more trustworthy developers can charge a higher price and earn more profit. This result is intuitive. On the other hand, in a world where every potential user is unethical, trustworthy developers can still afford to offer lower shareware quality, but they can no longer charge a brand premium, nor can they be more profitable than less known developers. While the real world probably lies between our no-piracy model and our piracy model, the fact that piracy undermines brand premium should still hold true. It is not surprising to see that established firms are the ones who are the most keen to fight piracy.

## 8. Limitations, future direction and conclusion

Software is an experience good and consumers generally have very diverse preferences. Those who are not familiar to a new software product often exhibit uncertainty aversion in their adoption decisions. As broadband services penetrate the market, there is a growing interest in using shareware strategically. Because software piracy is common and because the design of a shareware version is often vulnerable to piracy through the use of crackers, it is important to understand how piracy affects shareware decisions. This study looks at the interrelationships among uncertainty, piracy, shareware quality and pricing.

We find that, when piracy is not severe, a developer's trustworthiness decreases the optimal quality, increases the full version price and profit. However, when piracy is prevalent, even though trustworthiness continues to allow the firm to lower shareware quality, it no longer provides any advantage in pricing or profitability. Our results make good contribution in theorizing shareware economics, an area that has not been much examined. These results are also very relevant to the software industry and provide useful guidelines in devising shareware-related strategies.

We identify here a number of future research directions. First, closely related to liteware quality is the length of the trial period. Future study should look at how length of the trial period is affected by uncertainty and piracy. A combined strategy of liteware, trial period, and other shareware restrictions, should also be analyzed. Second, this paper studies how a firm can adjust liteware quality in general to maximize profit in a market where experience is required to determine “fit” and where there is software piracy. It is unable to provide design guidelines to specific software products as to which features should be disabled and which features should be kept in the liteware. From a practitioner's point of view, this is important to examine the problem down to the level of specific software functionalities. Third, our study ignores competitive effects in the industry. Competition is especially more intense in games and entertainment software, where software standards are not as important as in application software. Fourth, we should in the future investigate the effect of network externalities on the optimal shareware strategy given its close relationship with piracy. Other factors that are relevant to the online gaming industry and are worth investigating include connection speed, subscription pricing schemes, and marginal cost. Fifth, Bester [5] shows how switching cost and quality uncertainty together affect trading rules in the market, and Bester [6] shows that quality uncertainty mitigates product differentiation. Future research should look at how these relationships are affected when marketing tools such as shareware are available to reduce quality uncertainty.

Finally, there are a lot of future research opportunities to empirically test the propositions developed in this paper or otherwise, because shareware is a new area of research and has not been well understood by academia and practitioners. A good example of a study in this area that provides analytical modeling supported by empirical evidence is Gopal et al. [19]. Shareware data can be collected from centralized shareware sites such as the Association of

Shareware Professionals (ASP) website and Download. com. These websites provide information about relevant variables in this paper, including full version price, shareware restrictions, consumer ratings, brand, and number of competitors, among others. Data collected from these websites can be used to validate the findings in the current study and provide empirical support for future analytical studies on shareware economics. In short, shareware as a new form of marketing strategy for information goods opens up enormous opportunity for marketers as well as researchers.

\begin{array}{c} v (\widetilde {Q}, q _ {\mathrm{c}}) - x - a _ {2} \geq 0 \\ x \leq \widetilde {Q} - a _ {2} \end{array}
$$

A.4. Analysis of the base model using a different willingness-to-pay function

Here, we use a quadratic function instead of a linear function for the disutility that arises from the mismatch in taste, $i . e .$ , the willingness-to-pay function is now given by $\nu ( Q , \ q _ { \mathrm { c } } ) = q _ { \mathrm { c } } - x ^ { 2 }$ . The indifference curves for this model are shown in Table A1 and Fig. A1. The profit function is given by

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \Pi_ {2} & = a _ {2} \times \int_ {q _ {\mathrm{s}} + a} ^ {1} \sqrt {q _ {\mathrm{c}} - a _ {2}} \mathrm{d} q _ {\mathrm{c}} \\ & = a _ {2} \left[ \frac {2}{3} (1 - a _ {2}) ^ {\frac {3}{2}} - \frac {2}{3} q _ {\mathrm{s}} ^ {\frac {3}{2}} \right] \end{array}
$$

Again, the profit function suggests that it is best to set $q _ { \mathrm { s } } { = } 0$ , consistent with Proposition 1. The solution obviously should change and it can be found to be $a ^ { * } { = } 2 / 5$ and $\Pi _ { 2 } ^ { * } = 4 \sqrt { 1 5 } / 1 2 5$ . Note that propositions in this study only describe the directional relationship between variables.
