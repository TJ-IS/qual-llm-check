# An exploratory study of the selection of communication media: The relationship between <sup>fl</sup>ow and communication outcomes

## a b s t r a c t

This study examined how two communication media (e-mail and instant messaging) affected communication outcomes; and, more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, how these two media in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the relationship between <sup>fl</sup>ow experience and communication outcomes. An experiment was conducted on a college campus using 94 student subjects. Communication outcomes were collected using a questionnaire. Data were analyzed using MANCOVA (multivariate analysis of covariance) and discriminant analysis. Playfulness was used as a covariate. The analysis showed that the e-mail group appeared to have higher communication quality and effectiveness. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship was found to exist between <sup>fl</sup>ow and communication outcomes when the communication medium was e-mail; but no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship was found to exist when the communication medium was instant messaging. Playfulness, a covariate, affected the relationship between the media type and communication outcomes.

## 1. Introduction

Previous studies on computer-mediated communications (CMC) focused primarily on comparing CMC to face-to-face communication [23,26,29,32,36,43,47,50]. Among these studies (conducted in the last two decades), e-mail was the communication medium most widely discussed, compared, and contrasted with other channels of communication [9,11,17,18,26,29,31,37]. As with e-mail, instant messaging is also an important communication medium, but it has not received the same amount of attention, and has not been studied extensively. This may be due in part to the fact that instant messaging is a relatively new application, newer than e-mail, and to the fact that most early adaptors have been younger users. As a result, instant messaging has not received adequate attention in literature.

Although they share many characteristics, there are some fundamental differences between e-mail and instant messaging. For instance, a frequently cited difference between email and face-to-face communication is the reliance of e-mail on textual information, which lacks both visual and social cues during use [46]. But such a de<sup>fi</sup>ciency no longer exists in the case of instant messaging because instant messaging applications are equipped with a number of features that mimic visual and non-verbal cues, such as emotional icons, presence awareness, presence noti<sup>fi</sup>cation, voice chat, video conferencing, <sup>fi</sup>le exchange, drawing pads, application sharing, and others. These additional features and functions give instant messaging capabilities that signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly exceed those of traditional text-based e-mail previously studied. For example, the physical presence of participants is required for face-to-face communications, because the participants are necessarily aware of each other's presence due to proximity. Such awareness information is not available when using email, but is available in most instant messaging applications, because instant messaging programs can notify users when someone they know becomes available online by changing the icon representing the user or even by sounding an alert.

However, presence does not always indicate availability to start a communication. For example, a person facing you may not be available to make conversation if he or she is reading a document quietly with the head down. Such cues are, again, unavailable to the user of traditional telecommunication media. To overcome this obstacle, instant messaging applications use additional icons to represent a user's availability status while online. Common availability status indicators include “away,” “do-not-disturb,” “call-me,” or “busy.” Such presence and availability status information is not available on e-mail applications. E-mail users need to wait for a response from the other party before knowing if that party is available. Other non-verbal cues such as hand waving, may attract the attention of persons involved in face-to-face communication, and instant messaging can also provide this effect. For example, some instant messaging applications allow a user to “shake” another user's application window to attract attention. At present, few studies have empirically examined the effects of such features offered by CMC media to provide the type of surrogate functionality that would be comparable to verbal and non-verbal cues in face-to-face communications [3].

As instant messaging technology plunges ahead in its rapid evolution, it sometimes creates obstacles to smooth communication. Because features in certain instant messaging programs are proprietary, they set up incompatibilities between applications, and so offer less consistency in user experience. Now, however, standardization of instant messaging protocols is underway through the Internet Engineering Task Force's (IETF) proposal of Session Initiation Protocol for Instant Messaging and Presence Leveraging Extensions (SIMPLE). This standardization effort will serve as a foundation for enhancing compatibility across instant messaging programs because it will not limit the ability of vendors to add features to enhance interactivity or to improve communication quality. (Vendors could “borrow” features from other vendors without fear of copyright infringement.) Although standardization of instant messaging technology is not yet a reality, more interactive features, such as emotional icons, presence awareness, presence noti<sup>fi</sup>cation, voice chat, video conferencing, <sup>fi</sup>le exchange, drawing pads, application sharing, and others, are gradually becoming available across multiple proprietary instant messaging programs.

With these added capabilities, instant messaging will not only provide a new way to communicate, but will also offer a wealth of opportunities to change communication outcomes. As a result, some of the early theories and <sup>fi</sup>ndings on computer-based communications may need to be revisited to assess their applicability to these new applications [29,36].

Due to the many differences between e-mail and instant messaging described above, existing theories and research outcomes applicable to e-mail may not be applicable to instant messaging; and using instant messaging instead of email for task communication may lead to different research outcomes. The purpose of this study is to examine how two electronic media, e-mail and instant messaging, may affect communication outcomes differently, to examine the relationship between the <sup>fl</sup>ow construct and communication outcomes, and to determine how the choice of media may affect that relationship.

## 2. Literature review

Two areas of study that are directly related to this research are media choices and the concept of <sup>fl</sup>ow. A considerable amount of research has focused on the choice, use, and consequences of communication media [19,24]. This issue has become even more important recently due to the proliferation of new media that have changed CMC in fundamental ways (such as videoconferencing and instant messaging). Flow is a construct that captures a person's inner state of feeling and perception when deciding to carry out a task to its completion. In human–computer interaction, <sup>fl</sup>ow is characterized as being either a playful or exploratory experience. Flow is also used as a frequent construct in the study of electronic media and computing tools [49]. In the following section, the two areas of study are brie<sup>fl</sup>y reviewed.

## 2.1. Media choice

A medium is a material substance capable of carrying waves or energy. Communication media are carriers of signals or information. Choices of communication media have increased signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in the last two decades with the proliferation of computing and networking technologies. Before the 1980s, conventional communication channels were limited to face-toface conversation and the use of telephones, telegraphs, and paper documents. In the 1990's, a great deal of attention was shifted to e-mail and its potential impact on business organizations and society in general [9,11,17,26,29,31,37]. In recent years, the use of instant messaging and its related applications has stimulated another wave of change.

Managers' selection of media to <sup>fi</sup>ll different communication needs has always been an important subject of study [3,15,21,35,38,41]. Throughout this existing literature, a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant theory upon which to base media selection has been media richness theory (MRT). MRT argues that media characteristics affect media richness, which in turn affects media choice. Media characteristics that affect media richness include feedback capability, availability of non-verbal cues, language variety, and level of personal focus [2,9,10,11,25].

An example of rich media is face-to-face communication. Face-to-face communication provides immediate feedback; conveys non-verbal cues such as gestures, tones, and eye movement; and gives both parties increased personal attention. E-mail is an example of lean media. It provides feedback, but the response time is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to control; non-verbal cues are usually absent; and because e-mail may be addressed to a large number of recipients, it usually lacks personal attention.

However, rich media are not intrinsically superior to lean media. The ideal medium for any task depends on the type of information to be processed and the purpose of the communication. In previous studies, two main purposes of information processing were uncertainty reduction and equivocality reduction [9]. For reducing uncertainty, a medium simply needs to facilitate the exchange of a large amount of objective and numeric data. On the other hand, media for reducing equivocality need to support a number of communication cues to clarify and explain issues, rather than merely providing large volumes of data. Consequently, lean media are better for reducing uncertainty, while rich media are more appropriate for resolving equivocal situations [6,9,11].

EI-Shinnawy and Markus [15,16] applied MRT in a study of managers' selections of voice mail and e-mail as their electronic media. Their results showed that e-mail was preferred over voice mail for information exchange for reducing uncertainty. In other words, a leaner medium (in this case, e-mail) was used in situations requiring uncertainty reduction. However, their <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicated that managers also preferred e-mail over voicemail for the resolution of equivocality. Voicemail's capability to convey multiple cues was unimportant in the managers' decisions to select a medium, which result was contrary to the predictions of MRT. Elshinnawy and Markus therefore concluded that the MRT might not be applicable to new electronic media. The study also raised doubts as to the value of media richness as the only factor in determining a medium's ability to reduce equivocality.

## 2.2. The flow experience

The construct of <sup>fl</sup>ow is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the state occurring during network navigation which is: 1) characterized by a seamless sequence of responses facilitated by machine interactivity, 2) intrinsically enjoyable, 3) accompanied by a loss of self-consciousness, and 4) self-reinforcing” [22, p. 23]. Computer users may experience the state of <sup>fl</sup>ow when they feel in control of their actions and master of their own fate. They also feel “a sense of exhilaration and a deep sense of enjoyment.” Flow is the “holistic sensations that people feel when they act with total involvement” [8, p. 36].

In human–computer interaction research, the <sup>fl</sup>ow construct is represented and measured using four different factors: control, attention focus, curiosity, and intrinsic interest in the interaction [7,12,44,49]. The control dimension of <sup>fl</sup>ow refers to a user's ability to dominate over his interaction with the technology. Attention focus is the extent to which the user's attention is absorbed by the activity in which he/she is engaged [8]. An individual's level of curiosity is heightened in the <sup>fl</sup>ow state [27,28]. And, <sup>fi</sup>nally, users <sup>fi</sup>nd their activities intrinsically more interesting when they experience the state of <sup>fl</sup>ow. Flow has also been found to correlate with work outcomes and effectiveness and is positively correlated with perceptions of <sup>fl</sup>exibility, experimentation, and expected use of the technology [8,14,49].

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Fig. 1 shows the conceptual model for this study. The three primary constructs in the model are media type, <sup>fl</sup>ow, and communication outcomes. A fourth construct, playfulness, is included in the model as a covariate—its purpose will be described in the following section. Three sets of hypotheses are listed below.

Hypothesis 1. Playfulness will have no effect on the relationship between media type and communication outcomes.

Most current instant messaging applications contain features that users regard as entertaining. Because these features may or may not be directly related to the primary communication function, it is desirable that the effects of these features be isolated in the data analysis. To achieve this purpose, the study included the playfulness construct as a covariate in the research model. The <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis tested the correlation between playfulness and the relationship between media type and communication outcomes. The playfulness construct would be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant covariate if the hypothesis were to be rejected. In other words, playfulness would be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor if media type does affect communication outcomes.

Playfulness describes a user's tendency to interact with computers spontaneously, inventively, and imaginatively [48]. Playfulness is an intrinsic motivation that drives the intention to use [12]. Previous studies have shown that the potential advantages of playfulness include increased involvement, positive mood, and satisfaction [7,30,40,48]. Playfulness also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the use of the World Wide Web for entertainment purposes; while perceived usefulness had no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on the same use intention [1]. Previous studies also suggested that perceived playfulness not only had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on attitude, but also on the eventual use intention of mobile Internet applications [5,34]. Because of its correlation with usage, playfulness affects user experience and helps shape user perception of the value of the target system [20]. Potential negative effects of playfulness include longer time to completion [39] and over-involvement [7].

This set of hypotheses was proposed based on the MRT described above. Due to the differences in feature sets of email and instant messaging, it was logical to hypothesize differences in the communication outcomes. The three subhypotheses (a, b, and c) were established based on the three factors of the communication outcomes: quality, effectiveness, and volume. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant outcome (rejection of the hypothesis) would indicate a correlation between media type (e-mail vs. instant messaging) and the corresponding factor.

Hypothesis 3. Flow experience will have no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on communication outcomes.

Hypothesis 3a: The <sup>fl</sup>ow experience will have no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on communication outcomes for the IM group.

Hypothesis 3b: The <sup>fl</sup>ow experience will have no effect on communication outcomes for the e-mail group.

A great deal of research effort has focused on theorizing and using the <sup>fl</sup>ow construct as an intrinsic motivational construct. The results showed that the <sup>fl</sup>ow experience is positively related to the tendency to revisit Web sites [4,13], attitudes towards and use of media [45], perceived communication quality, and communication effectiveness [49]. In a later study, Trevino, Webster, and Stein [44] found that the state of <sup>fl</sup>ow was the most important in<sup>fl</sup>uence on attitudes toward e-mail, but it was not associated with use. A recent study constructed two models (re<sup>fl</sup>ective vs. formative models) for the concept of <sup>fl</sup>ow, and studied the relationships with purchases and revisit intentions. The results con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that <sup>fl</sup>ow does affect purchases and revisits [42].

The third set of hypotheses tested the potential relationship between <sup>fl</sup>ow and communication outcomes. The purpose was to see if the same outcome (as suggested in previous studies) would also apply to newer media such as instant messaging.

## 4. Research methods

The study was conducted at a large private university in the Mid-Western United States. Subjects were 94 students who volunteered to participate. The researchers neither coerced the subjects into participation nor provided <sup>fi</sup>nancial compensation to encourage participation. All participants were Internet users, and had prior e-mail and instant messaging experience. The majority of subjects (78%) were male students. Of the 94 participants, 74 were undergraduate students (79%), 16 were graduate students (17%), and 4 were unclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed subjects. The majority of the participants, 86 out of 94, were under 30 years old (92%), 2 students were between 31 and 40 years old (2%), 2 students were between 41 and 50 years old (2%), and the remainder of the students did not report their ages (4%).

During the experiment, the participants were divided into two groups: the instant messaging (IM) group and the e-mail (EM) group. The ratio of male to female subjects was approximately equal in both groups. Each group was subdivided into small task teams of two people each. The IM group contained 27 teams and the EM group contained 20 teams.

Each team was assigned the task of solving a business problem (see Appendix A). This problem required the teammates to interact with each other through several rounds of brainstorming sessions. Teammates were geographically separated and were asked to communicate using only the assigned communication media during the entire problemsolving process. The EM teams used only e-mail for communication, and the IM teams used only instant messaging for communication. Teammates were asked not to contact each other using any other means of communication, and not to meet face-to-face for the duration of the experiment. Each team was required to keep a communication log.

After the experiment, each participant <sup>fi</sup>lled out a questionnaire (see Appendix B) to report his or her experiences and perceptions using the assigned medium to solve the problem. The questionnaire was developed using items from several existing studies. Participants indicated their responses on a seven-point Likert Scale, where a 1 indicated “strongly agree” and a 7 indicated “strongly disagree.” Negatively worded items were reverse scored for analysis.

## 5. Data analysis and results

Data analysis consisted of several steps. First, the reliability of the collected data and the validity of the instrument were analyzed. Second, selected descriptive statistics were applied to provide an overview of the results. Third, hypotheses were tested using various techniques.

An effort was made to ensure reliability and to create validity through carefully designed research processes and the adoption of existing validated instruments. The content validity was improved by having an expert panel review and modify the questions, and by having the samples piloted with representative responses. Factor analysis was employed to verify predicted associations of variables for construct validity. With the VARIMAX rotation and factor extraction based on the Eigen-value criterion, we con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that each construct was uni-dimensional as identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in prior research. The psychometric properties of the scales were evaluated in terms of reliability coef<sup>fi</sup>cient and validity. The reliability of variables and covariates was suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high (Cronbach's alphaN0.81), greater than Nunnally's suggested criterion of $0 . 7 ,$ and was consistent with past research that used these items [33]. ## 5.2. The playfulness construct

The playfulness construct was adapted from Webster and Martocchio's Computer Playfulness Scale (CPS) [48], in which the construct was measured with seven variables on a sevenpoint scale. An exploratory factor analysis was performed with the VARIMAX rotation to extract the playfulness factors. The result showed a three-factor solution with the last factor having only one variable (the “unimaginative” variable in CPS) with a high factor loading. Since this variable was less congruent with the concept of playfulness, and has a low correction with other variables in the playfulness construct, the factor analysis was rerun by excluding the “unimaginative” variable. The result showed that two factors (referred to as Type-1 Playfulness and Type-2 Playfulness) explained 67.639% of the variance (see Table 2). The Cronbach's alphas for the two factors were 0.732 and 0.808 respectively.

To <sup>fi</sup>lter the potential effects of such features, playfulness was used as the covariate. The covariate also statistically validated that the two groups did not initially differ, thereby achieving control of the pre-existing differences among the variables. ## 5.4. Discriminant analysis

Since the effect of Type-1 Playfulness was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, it was included in the discriminant analysis to examine the difference between the two user groups. In the discriminant analysis, the variable arrangement was different from the arrangement for MANCOVA. The dependent variable was the media type, i.e., the two user groups. The independent variables were the communication outcomes and Type-1

Playfulness. Table 4 shows that only the communication effectiveness and communication quality exceeded the threshold of 0.30, which indicates that the two groups differed only on these two variables.

As re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the mean values of variables and the discriminant loadings, the difference resulted from the EM group communicating more effectively and better for the assigned task than the IM group. Interestingly, the volume of communication did not seem to vary between the two user groups (IM: mean=4.61, SD=.79; EM: mean=4.64, SD=.97). The classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy was 0.674. Based on Proportional Chance Criterion, the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy of 0.674 exceeded the proportional chance for this case $( C _ { \mathrm { p r o } } { = } 0 . 4 3 ^ { 2 * } 0 . 5 7 ^ { 2 } { = } 0 . 5 1 )$ by approximately 0.322 (32.2%).

## 6. Discussions

The data analysis result suggested that the choice between e-mail and instant messaging made a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in communication outcomes. The two user groups differed on two variables: effectiveness and quality. The e-mail group performed better than the instant messaging group on both variables. In other words, e-mail was a more effective medium for the assigned task in this experiment. Instant messaging, which included additional features that seemed to mimic face-to-face communication, did not provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

The task in the experiment was to solve a hypothetical problem (see Appendix A). The information provided was limited. Participants also found it impossible to obtain information from external sources because the scenario was hypothetical. As a result, this was not a situation where additional information could have been exchanged to reduce uncertainty. Due to these information constraints, team members could easily have had con<sup>fl</sup>icting interpretations of the situation and therefore could have had different opinions about the solution, which made the exercise more of an equivocal situation. Team members needed to discuss and argue their views and positions with each other and eventually come to an agreement as to the best solution. In other words, the task was to resolve an equivocal situation.

Based on MRT, rich media were shown to be better for resolving equivocal situations. In the study, instant messaging was a richer medium due to its personal focus, instant feedback, and additional cues. Yet, the study results contradicted the prediction of MRT. The results showed that the email group (using a leaner medium) actually reported higher communication quality and effectiveness than the instant messaging group (using a rich medium).

In the study by El-Shinnawy and Markus [15], the researchers speculated that MRT was not general enough to explain the preferences and usage of new media (e-mail) because of differences between traditional media and new electronic media. Combining their conclusion and the result of this study, MRT not only failed to explain user preferences and intended usage, but also failed to predict communication outcomes using new media.

The study also examined the relationship between <sup>fl</sup>ow experience and communication outcomes. Three <sup>fl</sup>ow experience variables (control, attention focus, and curiosity/interest) were examined. The last <sup>fl</sup>ow variable was a combination of two original dimensions — curiosity and interest [44]. A factor analysis con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the simpler three-dimensional view of the <sup>fl</sup>ow construct.

The <sup>fl</sup>ow experience encouraged users to engage in exploratory behavior and to perform an activity repeatedly to see whether, as hypothesized, the <sup>fl</sup>ow construct might correlate with communication outcome variables such as communication volume. In this study, the relationships between <sup>fl</sup>ow and communication outcomes were con<sup>fi</sup>rmed for the e-mail group, but surprisingly not for the instant messaging group. In other words, the <sup>fl</sup>ow experience produced an impact on communication outcomes for e-mail users, but not for instant messaging users. An explanation for this outcome is that the media type had an effect on the relationship between <sup>fl</sup>ow experience and communication outcomes. Also, the effects of <sup>fl</sup>ow did not apply to all media types.

Although the <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest a positive relationship between <sup>fl</sup>ow experience and communication outcomes for the EM group, not all dimensions of the <sup>fl</sup>ow experience were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly or positively correlated with communication outcomes. One dimension was the relationship between attention focus and communication quality. Another dimension was the relationship between attention focus and communication volume. These two dimensions were not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. The effect of attention focus on communication effectiveness was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, but in a negative way (β=−0.427).

Overall, the attention focus dimension did not seem to produce a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive effect on communication outcomes. A possible explanation for this counter-intuitive result was the asynchronous nature of e-mail. Users who used e-mail did not need as much attention focus as users of other media such as face-to-face conversation or instant messaging. They were able to take a longer time to respond and they did not need to devote complete focus to the task. In other words, they could multitask while working on the assigned task. This explanation is speculative. This study did not collect enough information to support this rationalization, so more evidence is needed to provide a more reliable explanation.

The most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor of the <sup>fl</sup>ow construct was the control dimension. Its effects were especially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant on communication effectiveness. This result was not surprising: The more control the users felt, the more effectiveness they perceived. When they perceived that something was out of control, they also felt ineffective.

Unlike the EM group, none of the three <sup>fl</sup>ow dimensions had any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on communication outcome variables for the IM users. This disparity may be explained by the fact that too many interactive features may drive a user's attention toward the media and away from the task at hand. That is, users may perceive the instant messaging device as easy to control and attention-grabbing, but these advantages did not lead to more effective communication — the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts were lost somewhere in the process. Some evidence of decreased quality of communication with the use of IM is supported in Kock's study [24], where he found that a pseudoinstant messaging program (i.e., a hand-crafted system that carries multiple IM features) resulted in a 12% increase of cognitive effort, 19% increase of communication ambiguity, and 90% decrease of communication <sup>fl</sup>uency. In a study into virtual communities, Chiu et al. [6] reported that excitements generated through social interaction ties, reciprocity and an individual's sense of belonging to the community increased the volume of communication but not necessarily the quality of communication. Although both studies cited above researched into pseudo-IM programs (a hand-crafted dynamic discussion board and an online community), they nonetheless offer some evidence that the cognitive excitement through which IM users experience could be more of a hindrance for the task at hand than an aid. This is an issue that may deserve a dedicated focus for future studies.

In the data analysis, the playfulness construct was included as a covariate. The results showed that a subset of the original playfulness construct had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on the relationship between media types and communication outcomes, but because the actual effects were unclear based on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study, further studies are needed on this issue as well.

## 7. Limitations

The study had several limitations. First, the sample was not a representative group of the general population. The subjects were all college graduate and undergraduate students who were early adopters of new media. So the use of student subjects may have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the effects of the playfulness construct. Playfulness is more likely to be a factor for new applications. For college students who use both email and instant messaging daily, the lack of effect seemed to be predictable. If the subjects had been a different group of people, it is likely that the result would have been different. The gap between the sample group and computer users in the general population may become smaller as more people adopt instant messaging applications. Secondly, the experiment was conducted using assigned media and tasks. The results may be different if subjects had been free to choose which communication medium to use to best accomplish their assigned tasks. Finally, the sample size was not large. Therefore, this study may be viewed as a preliminary effort for future large-scale research on the same topic. In summary, this study used a tightly controlled environment to enhance the reliability and validity of the results, but these limitations caused somewhat generalized results. It would be interesting to see the experiment replicated in a different setting, with different tasks, and with different user groups.

## 8. Conclusion

This study used two electronic media, e-mail and instant messaging, to solve a problem in a controlled setting. Using student subjects, data were collected to assess the effects of media types and <sup>fl</sup>ow experience on communication outcomes. Subjects were divided into two groups, e-mail and instant messaging groups, and were assigned a problemsolving task. After the experiment, the student subjects <sup>fi</sup>lled out a questionnaire to collect the data. The results showed that the two media types produced different communication outcomes. The e-mail group reported better communication outcomes, despite the additional communication capabilities available to the instant messaging group. Playfulness proved to be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor in the relationship between media and communication outcomes. Two dimensions of the <sup>fl</sup>ow construct had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on communication outcomes for e-mail users. The two groups also reported differing relationships between <sup>fl</sup>ow experience and communication outcomes. Although the study mostly con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the <sup>fl</sup>ow theory, it nevertheless showed that the <sup>fl</sup>ow experience may have an impact only in certain combinations of communication media and tasks. Brie<sup>fl</sup>y, the study produced some interesting results, but additional data are needed for external validity and clari<sup>fi</sup>cation of the results.
