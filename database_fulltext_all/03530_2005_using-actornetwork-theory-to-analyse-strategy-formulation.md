# Using actor-network theory to analyse strategy formulation

Ping Gao

Department of Informatics, Copenhagen Business School, Howitzvej 60, DK-2000 Fredriksberg, Denmark, email: pg.inf@cbs.dk

Abstract. Drawing upon actor-network theory, this article analyses the sociotechnological construction of China’s strategy for the telecommunications market transformation. We define the telecommunications market as the non-human actor. The public and society, the state, and the operators constitute three groups of human actors representing the social interests in the telecommunications industry. We have observed that these actors’ interests are influenced by the situation of technology advance, the telecommunications development level, the macro reform progress and the national policies concerning the political and economic systems, and the international trend in telecommunications reforms. Owing to the dynamics of these contextual elements and the struggle of actors to inscribe their interests into the national strategy, China has transformed the telecommunications market by four stages, each stage with specific foci. Our case study demonstrates that the applications of actor-network theory can be extended to investigate the formulation of a national strategy. The research design in which the social and technological contexts are dynamically connected with strategy formulation can be drawn upon by other actor-network studies.

Keywords: actor-network theory, China, socio-technological construction, strategy formulation, telecommunications

## INTRODUCTION

In recent decades, the telecommunications market has undergone dramatic changes on a global scale attracting a broad variety of research interests. A large number of publications address the telecommunications transformation in Europe and the USA (see e.g. Noam & Kramer, 1994; Snow, 1995; Thatcher, 1999), while others have focused on developing countries (Petrazzini, 1995). This article relates the experience of China.

In an international perspective, China presents an interesting case as the largest market for telecommunications services (MII, 1999). The numerous publications on China’s telecommunications industry are affected by two problems. First, they focus on specific aspects of it, for example, the manufacturing sector (Chismar et al., 1996) and internet development (Martinsons, 2002). Research on the national telecommunications market is rare (Zhang, 2002), especially with focus on national strategy. Second, the research methodology is fact-finding and descriptive, lacking theoretical arguments.

National strategy presents an important research focus. From an institutional perspective (King et al., 1994), considerable research examines the role that government promotion and national strategy play in stimulating technology diffusion and market development. Although these studies facilitate understanding of how governments influence these processes, they fail to explain why governments adopt specific methods. Drawing upon actor-network theory, this article relates the socio-technological construction of China’s strategy for telecommunications reform (Callon, 1991; Latour, 1996). Key research questions are: what the strategy is that China has adopted, and which social and technological elements have influenced the strategy formulation and how.

The actor-network theory has gained increased attention with researchers trying to extend its applications beyond technology adoption and design to broader areas (Walsham, 1997). Our case study attempts to make substantial contributions to literature, demonstrating how the actor-network theory can be used to analyse the strategy formulation process and resulting in wider applications from our research design in which the social and technological contexts are dynamically connected with strategy formulation.

This article has five sections. The second section introduces the concept of national strategy for telecommunications transformation and outlines the framework and methodology of our analysis. The third section presents the analytical results. The fourth section discusses the theoretical and practical implications of our findings. The last section reviews the contributions of this case study to actor-network research.

## RESEARCH APPROACH

## Theory and analytical framework

Historically, the telecommunications market was largely regarded as a natural monopoly with the PTT<sup>1</sup> monopolizing the market (Director, 1992). In the 1970s, the natural monopoly concept started the dismantling process. There are three approaches pertaining to the telecommunications market change: liberalization, restructuring (including devolution and consolidation) and reregulation. Liberalization denotes the introduction of new entrants into the monopolized market. Restructuring assumes the incumbents as targets of change. Specifically, devolution defines the approach of splitting the monopoly structure into separate units; consolidation is the opposite approach to devolution, creating larger units in the market. Reregulation refers to

<sup>1</sup>PTT (Post, Telegraph and Telephone) has traditionally been a state network operator. Since the regulatory functions were split up, it has been referred to as PTO (Public Telecommunications Operator).

changes in contents and patterns of regulation and therefore covers cases like deregulation and asymmetric regulation, etc., in addition to changes in the institutional set-up. Usually, countries will adopt a mixture of these approaches at specific stages of market change. For a country in a specific period, the configuration of these three approaches over time constitutes a national strategy for telecommunications transformation (Gao & Lyytinen, 2005).

Based on the above concepts, we consider China’s telecommunications reform over time as a continued process of choosing transformation approaches. Our consequent interest lies in the social interaction around the telecommunications market, providing insights into the strategy formulation. The actor-network theory suits our initiative, and hence we will employ it as an analytical tool.

A key feature of actor-network theory is that the actors include both human beings and nonhuman actors (such as a technology), together composing a network to be studied (Callon, 1991; Latour, 1996). The theory examines the motivations and actions of human actors that align their interests around the requirements of non-human actors. As such, it differs from the theories of both the technology determinism discipline and the institutional school that focus on technologies and social elements respectively (Kling, 1980).

One potential application of the actor-network theory is to investigate the process whereby the respective interests of different human and non-human elements are aligned into a social and technological arrangement or artifact. An artifact is a ‘fact’ created by human beings and usually refers to a technological design in literature – in our case to the national strategy for telecommunications transformation (Walsham, 1997). ‘Inscription’ and ‘translation’ are key concepts for understanding the interest alignment to form an actor-network. An inscription is a form of anticipated characteristics which actors, in one way or another, try to build into an artifact. Furthermore, there are several aspects to inscription: What is inscribed? Who or which type of actor tries to inscribe it? How or in which form is the interest inscribed? What is its strength, or how difficult is it for an actor to realize the inscription (Callon, 1991)? Translation implies that an actor reinterprets or appropriates the interests of other human actors and the interests embedded in non-human actors according to one’s own, and has these interests represented in the inscription. The actors’ interests are flexible and can be translated, enabling the interest alignment and the maintenance of an actor-network (Monteiro & Hanseth, 1996).

‘Successful networks of aligned interests are created through the enrolment of sufficient body of allies and the translation of their interests so that they are willing to participate in particular ways of thinking and acting that maintain the network’ (Walsham & Sahay, 1999, p. 42). The actors have been broadly defined in the applications of actor-network theory to include people, organizations, and technologies such as software, computer, and communications infrastructure. In this article, we define the telecommunications market as the non-human actor. In identifying the human actors, it is necessary to consider whose interests the candidates represent (Walsham, 1997). This article defines the public, the state and the operators as three groups of human actors that represent the social interests in the telecommunications market. In the end, the state stands for the interests of the public and the operators owned by the state. For the sake of distinction, the interests of the state are here restricted to political and macro economic concerns.

The human interpretation of the interests embedded in telecommunications technology and market is flexible, just as human actor interests are adaptable (Bijker et al., 1989). To capture this flexibility and adaptability, the researchers must allow multiple inscriptions and representations of the contexts (Boland & Schultz, 1996; Walsham, 1997; Langley, 1999). We classify the contexts into social and technological aspects related to human and non-human actors respectively.

Figure 1 shows our model of the actor-network analysis. The relationships between the human and non-human actors exemplify the ongoing struggles in formulating the national strategy for telecommunications transformation (Bloomfield et al., 1992). With time, the contextual elements will vary and the interests of both human and non-human actors, as well as their alignment, will alter, which may result in changes to the approaches of market transformation. Hence, we view the telecommunications market change as a process of several stages with varied foci. Our task is to define the contextual elements, determine their influence on the actors’ interests, and trace the design of the national strategy for telecommunications reform as a process of interest inscription and translation.

## Methodology and data analysis

The actor-network theory requires that researchers examine the process of interest alignment to form a network (Monteiro & Hanseth, 1996). Accordingly, this article presents a longitudinal case study. Using the concept of national strategy for telecommunications transformation and the terminology of actor-network theory as the unifying lexicons of analysis, we develop a context-based, process-oriented account of the telecommunications reform in China (Orlikowski, 1993; Walsham, 1995). We start with a contextual analysis and then identify the interests of the actors as determined by the contexts. Finally, we examine how the actors struggle to inscribe their interests into China’s strategy for telecommunications transformation.

We explain the market transformation and strategy formulation through a narrative that reveals how events, activities and choices are ordered over time. According to critical events, the whole process of market transformation is divided into four distinct stages: the years up to 1993, 1994–97, 1998–2000, and from 2001. Before 1994, change only took place in valueadded services (VAS). From 1994 to 1997, Unicom was set up and the PTT system was changed. During 1998 and 2000, the Ministry of Information Industry (MII) was formed as the neutral regulator, and the mobile sector of China Telecom was divested to form an independent company, China Mobile. After 2000, China published the schedule to open its market to foreign competitors, and China Telecom was split into two parts by areas. To categorize the social and technological contexts of the market change, we execute iterative interplay between the data and the categories. Additional concepts are generated by comparison and contrast between different phases of market transformation. This inquiry process is halted when the derived categories are able to account for, not only outcomes of a specific stage, but also that of China’s telecommunications market change as a whole (Orlikowski, 1993).

## Data collection

Carrying out a longitudinal study on the telecommunications transformation in China requires capturing changes in telecommunications technology and the market, analysing the dynamic contexts within which the transformation takes place, and exploring key stakeholders’ perceptions of the market change (Walsham, 1995). The author of this article first engaged in field observations (Yin, 1994), working for several years (until the end of 1997) as a consultant for the Ministry of Posts and Telecommunications (MPT) of China and personally experiencing the early phases of telecommunications reforms in China. Second, from 1998 to 2001, a substantial number of interviews were conducted with the main players in the Chinese telecommunications market: major manufacturers, research institutions, authorities, and operators (see Table 1 for a summary of interviews with names of interviewees ambiguous by request). In this period, the author visited China at least once a year. For example, at the end of 1998 when China was discussing its third round of reforms, the author visited China for 1 month to interview people who were working in three panels to draft reference plans of telecommunications market change for the State Council. Finally, to identify the order of critical events within the telecommunications reforms in China, the author used archival documents, including news reports in official newspapers, speeches of ministers and national leaders, and the annual reports of telecommunications development in China published by MPT and MII. The data collected by these different means were cross-validated before use.

## Up to 1993: competition in VAS

In the past, the public switched telephone network (PSTN) was generally operated as a monopoly because of technology requirement (Gong & Srinagesh, 1996). The traditional telephone transmission and switching technology had low capacity and did not facilitate efficient network interconnections. Therefore, network construction and operation involved high costs and had to be centrally coordinated. Managing such technologies through a monopoly was a pragmatic solution for network extension and service development. From the late 1970s, as the computer, microwave and fibre technologies advanced and digitalization increased, the natural monopoly of the telecommunications industry began to be challenged, with the USA and the UK setting the pace. In the USA, the telecommunications market was liberalized in 1978 when MCI’s dedicated network was allowed to provide long-distance services using microwave technology. Furthermore, in 1984 the federal government mandated AT&T to divest itself of the local service sector (Snow, 1995). In the UK, in 1984 a new Telecommunications Act abolished the exclusive rights of British Telecom in public services. Mercury was allowed to provide longdistance services (Thatcher, 1999). Apart from the market change progresses in the USA and the UK, the telecommunications monopolies were generally accepted in other countries in the 1980s (Melody, 1999).

Historically, the state controlled the Chinese national economy. Although a reform of the socialist economic system started at the end of the 1970s, progress was slow with the focus on the less important sectors of the national economy. The socialist ideology stipulated that the state had to run basic industries like power, transportation, telecommunications, banking, etc. (Ma & Ling, 1998). Moreover, China’s telecommunications market was underdeveloped and the state monopoly was unable to handle the competition. For example, in 1980 the Chinese mainland only had 4.12 million telephones, i.e. the telephone penetration rate was merely 0.4% per capita. In contrast, at the same time Hong Kong had 2.8 million subscribers out of 5 million residents (MII, 1999). From an economic, political and ideological viewpoint, the state had no interests in implementing a sweeping telecommunications reform.

In line with the state, MPT had an interest in taking advantage of the market monopoly instead of supporting liberalization. To prevent China’s telecommunications industry from foreign influence regarding reforms, in 1989 MPT, representing the central government, issued the decree no. 216, which declared that the telecommunications network was a national infrastructure and therefore had to be under the absolute control of the state to guarantee nationa security and sovereignty (MPT, 1992). Furthermore, MPT persuaded the State Council to grant preferential policies to the telecommunications sector to improve the poor status of China’s telecommunications infrastructure. MPT claimed that this was necessary as low network efficiency and high costs went hand in hand in the starting period of large-scale network construction (MII, 1999). This initiative was supported by the state that was interested in removing the bottleneck position of telecommunications industry in its efforts to build a strong national economy. Consequently, in 1991, the State Council enacted the decree no. 56 allowing MPT to charge an installation fee from every subscriber equalling the construction cost of one line. In addition, MPT enjoyed a privilege in tax policies (MPT, 1992). Until 1994, more than half of MPT’s investments came from these preferential policies (MII, 1999).

The preferential policies enjoyed by the national telecommunications industry were also supported by the society. In an interview, a policy researcher from MII noted:

At this time when China adopted a central planning system in the national economy, it was normal that the central government used preferential policies instead of the national budget to support a state industry. As the situation for the national economy was bad and the national budget was small, such a national policy support appeared to be an efficient and feasible solution to improve the telecommunications services.

Termed value-added services (VAS), new telecommunications services like paging emerged during the mid-1980s. Unlike basic services, VAS did not need large investments for the provision (Gong & Srinagesh, 1996). In terms of Porter (1979), the potential competitors would not meet significant barriers of capital requirement to enter into the VAS market. Hence, a broad range of institutions could be interested in providing the VAS. Moreover, VAS competition would not threat the dominance of incumbent network operators in the whole telecommunications market and, hence, not meet strong objections from them (Melody, 1999). Consequently, the VAS market was liberalized ahead of basic sectors in most countries (Collins & Murroni, 1997; Baliga & Santalainen, 1999). These practices proved to be a success as they promoted the fast development of the VAS market (Council of Economic Advisers, 1999). In this context, in the late 1980s, China liberalized the VAS market to all domestic non-private institutions (MPT, 1996). This initiative was in the interests of society enjoying good services and participating in the telecommunications market. Meanwhile, it did not jeopardize the interest of the state regarding control of the telecommunications infrastructure. Recognizing its incapability of monopolizing the whole telecommunications market and meeting the growing market demand alone, MPT translated its interests and aligned them with those of the public and the state (Chismar et al., 1996). A CEO from a local paging operator provided the following explanation:

To operate paging services you needed to have two things in place: you had a license to use a specific band of radio frequency, and you were allowed to connect your paging base stations to PSTN. The access line was controlled by the local branch of MPT, but the frequency allocation had been managed by the Radio Administration Office, which was a functional division of the local government. Hence, there must be a trade-off between the telecommunications operator and the local government in arranging the paging market. It was natural that the local government supported local capitals to benefit from participating in this lucrative market. In fact the local government itself, as a big investor, was also eager to enter into this market. As a result, the paging market was shared by MPT and local operators.

## From 1994 to 1997: change in PTT regime

In the mid-1990s, the telecommunications monopolies started to collapse on a global scale. In the USA, the 1996 Telecommunications Act removed all remaining restrictions on operators’ services and enacted a full liberalization on the US market (Baliga & Santalainen, 1999). The push towards stronger competition was driven by fast technological innovations marked by the emergence of the internet. Since its birth, the internet market has never been controlled by the traditional telecommunications incumbents. The internet allows the interconnection between diverse networks, providing telecommunications services. Hence, technologically, the appearance of the internet removed the foundation for the incumbents to monopolize the market (Abbate, 1999). Moreover, the liberalization of VAS globally and the basic telecommunications services in the USA and the UK were viewed positively by most policy makers (OECD, 1995; Council of Economic Advisers, 1999). Consequently, competition became a point of interest for the international telecommunications world and formed the basis for a country to design its reform (Melody, 1999). This influenced the progress of the telecommunications reform in China.

In China, the domestic environment of telecommunications reform also changed. As the national economy developed and the living standards improved, telecommunications services became an important part of business and public life (MII, 1999). The preferential policies and the strong market demand promoted fast telecommunications growth. By 1994, the level of the telecommunications development was already equivalent to the general growth level of the national economy (Yang, 1997). However, the service quality was still inferior. An interviewee from MII recalled that in the late 1990s the customer complaints on service quality peaked and the telecommunications reform was one of the topics of most concern in society. According to him, although MPT argued that it was normal to have service quality problems in a period with fast development, the Chinese society made a strong appeal to the government to remove MPT’s monopoly. The public had been encouraged by the successful international implementation of reforms, demonstrating that service quality and price reductions could be realized through enforcement of a competitive mechanism (OECD, 1995; Council of Economic Advisers, 1999). They attributed the poor services in China to the monopoly supported by the national preferential policies, and claimed that both encouraged the operator to focus on infrastructure construction but neglected users’ concern about service quality.

The strong appeal from society received the support of the state. As the telecommunications sector changed from being a bottleneck to becoming a leading industry in the national economy, there was no longer any reason to retain the strong preferential policies (MII, 1999). As a result, the interests of the state of using preferential policies to support telecommunications development were translated. The state began to consider restructuring the PTT system and placing MPT in a competitive market where it could realize development by providing good services.

Competition could push MPT, the incumbent operator, to improve services and hence attract more users. Hence it was in the interest of MPT in the long run (MII, 1999). Moreover, MPT was politically incapable of obstructing reforms that had been promoted by the public and other industries and supported by the state, even though it might hope to sustain its monopoly for the near benefits. As the authority in charge of China’s electronic manufacturing, the Ministry of Electronic Industry (MEI) played a leading role in challenging the monopoly. This was because of the strong support from its minister Hu Qili, a keen reform proponent. Mr Hu had been a member of the Standing Committee of the Political Bureau in the Communist Party of China. Although he was relegated to the minister position in MEI in 1990 after the Tian An Men Square Event, he remained influential at the Chinese political stage (Chismar et al., 1996). In such a situation, MPT’s interests were translated as indicating willingness for market changes.

In 1994, China launched the national reform on its governmental system (Ma & Ling, 1998). As a part of this macro initiative, the telecommunications market was transformed based on the interest alignment above. Consequently, registered as China Telecom, the operating sector of MPT was changed from a ‘functional’ department of MPT to a PTO. At the same time, the market was liberalized: MEI and other state institutions formed Jitong dedicated to competition in internet network and services, Unicom competed with China Telecom in all telecommunications services<sup>2</sup>, and Sino Satellite Communications and China Orient participated in the satellite communications market monopolized by ChinaSat of MPT (MPT, 1996; MII, 1999).

At the outset, these changes followed most international telecommunications reforms, aimed at restructuring PTT and separating the functions of market regulation and operation, and implementing liberalization (Noam & Kramer, 1994; Melody, 1999). But, in fact, China’s reform was mostly cosmetic. China Telecom and Unicom had a great disparity in market strength and capital assets. For example, in 1998 Unicom’s income was only about 1% of that of China Telecom (Li, 1998; MII, 1999). It was an unbalanced market situation. But even more importantly, China Telecom was directly under MPT’s control without any independent rights in matters of finance, investment and personnel. Hence, China Telecom was not a real enterprise, and the MPT not a ‘pure’ regulatory authority. Consequently, even if the central government required an asymmetric regulation to support Unicom, MPT might not implement the regulation actively owing to its close affiliation with China Telecom.

Meanwhile, MPT did not have enough power to intervene in the domains of other ministries such as MEI that now participated in the telecommunications market. To coordinate regulatory functions distributed among multiple state institutions, the State Council set up the National Information Infrastructure Steering Committee (NIISC). The NIISC was chaired by a vice premier minister of the State Council. The members were vice ministers from authorities including MPT, MEI, etc. It had a working office located within MEI. But NIISC only acted as an interim institution. It lacked legislative status and administrative power to exercise efficient regulations. In overseeing the policies and regulations, it had to negotiate and cooperate with other powerful government agencies as the main stakeholders of the telecommunications market (Zhang, 2002).

The telecommunications reform followed the process of the whole national reform, in which China adopted an ‘act after trials’ policy of transformation in order to prevent political disorder. As the first macro reform move, the state really had no interest in changing the current system designed for the socialist economy. Instead, it was implemented as a ‘pilot’ test for future moves, while the ministries retained a tight control on enterprises (Ma & Ling, 1998).

## From 1998 to 2000: liberalization and PTO restructuring

In the late 1990s, competition was accepted globally. In the European Union (EU), January 1, 1998 was the deadline for full liberalization, excluding Greece, Ireland, Portugal and Spain, which had been granted derogation (European Commission, 1997). To adapt to the technology advances that required network convergence, a tide of enterprise mergers took place crossing national boundaries and spanning several industries, including telecommunications, computing and media (Melody, 1999).

The dynamics of the international telecommunications market pushed China to intensify its reforms in the interests of the operators, the public and the state. First, as the market structure and regulatory regime did not alter the situation that a singular PTO controlled the market, the new entrants called for devolution (Zhang, 2002). At the same time, China Telecom as the PTO criticized the market structure. China Telecom called it unfair that the State Council mandated it to provide universal service, which meant high costs, but allowed Unicom to focus on lucrative urban areas. It also wanted to end its affiliation relationship with MPT, and complained of the lack of management freedom which led to a slow response to market requirements and low operating efficiency (Li, 1998).

Second, service quality remained unsatisfactory; hence, the appeal from the society intensified demanding that China should follow the international trend and strengthen the competition. As follows, two interviewees from MII and China Telecom illustrated the strong push from the society for a deep telecommunications reform:

In the parliament congresses held in 1994 and 1998, the public concerns about the telecommunications service quality and requests for intensifying market competition were documented as formal proposals to the government. In this situation, the government had to take public interests into serious account and plan a thorough transformation of the telecommunications sector.

In 1996, a citizen in Shandong province sued MPT demanding that the installation fee he had paid was returned. He claimed that such a charge was against the constitution, which stipulates that civilian property cannot be confiscated. Although the lawsuit was dropped in the end due to political interference, the event raised the concern of public media. It turned out that there was a strong public appeal in thoroughly reforming the telecommunications sector.

Third, a deep telecommunications reform was part of China’s plan to join the World Trade Organization (WTO), which was in the fundamental interest of the state (Ma & Ling, 1998). To foster a fair competition as required by WTO and to improve the domestic competence in the forthcoming international competition, a profound change on the telecommunications market was required (Zhang, 2002). Moreover, in the late 1990s, China’s telecommunications development entered a new phase marked by the formation of advanced, cross-country networks, which provided a solid basis for intensive market competition (MII, 1999).

The opportunity to further telecommunications reform came in 1998 when a new round of governmental reforms started, which resulted in deep changes in the governmental system (Ma & Ling, 1998). As part of this macro move, the reregulation was executed on the telecommunications industry, resulting in the establishment of MII based on MPT, MEI and NIISC. MI became a coherent regulator and was authorized with exclusive power in administrating the whole information industry<sup>3</sup> (People’s P&T, 11 July 1998). Based on the principles of ‘breaking monopoly, securing fair competition, optimizing national resources, and enforcing regulation set by the primer minister, MII began to restructure and liberalize the telecommunications market (MII, 1999). To consolidate Unicom, the paging sector of China Telecom (with a turnover of five times larger than Unicom’s), and the mobile network of Great Wall as an army-run communications company, were appropriated to Unicom (People’s P&T, 21 May 1999). As a result of liberalization, a number of state institutions formed China Netcom in August 1999 to offer internet network and public data services (People’s Daily, 5 January 2000). The government also permitted the telecommunications networks of the national railways to enter into the public market, and established China Railcom<sup>4</sup> in December 2000 (People’s Daily, 26 December 2000).

The key problem was how to restructure China Telecom. To consider the interests of all stakeholders comprehensively, at the end of 1998, the State Council required the State Planning Commission, MII and China Telecom to draft reference schemes for it. We had the opportunity to interview participants from these institutions, who reported on the process of interest alignment. We have observed that, although they all recognized the need to transform the telecommunications market, every institution emphasized different objectives in their proposals. The State Planning Commission focused on ensuring fair competition and proposed two plans, both of which recommended a strong devolution of China Telecom. The preferred plan aimed to separate the service operation from China Telecom. According to it, China Telecom would become a network operator, and each province would have one service operator. The second plan aimed at separating the mobile sector from China Telecom and dividing the remaining fixed network and operation into two parts according to geographical areas. MII proposed a relatively ‘conservative’ plan, which only required the devolution of the mobile sector from China Telecom. In contrast, in the beginning China Telecom insisted that the market structure should be improved by consolidating Unicom instead of dividing the incumbent. Being aware that this motion had little chance of being accepted, China Telecom later agreed to a spin-off of its mobile sector on the condition that it would control a major share. This was an imitation of the Japanese experience. In April 1992, the prior wireless arm of the formerly state-owned Japanese telecommunications monopoly NTT (Nippon Telegraph and Telephone Corporation)

was spun-off to form NTT DoCoMo, while the parent NTT remained in control of 62% of the outstanding shares (Vogel, 1996).

The interest translation and alignment were achieved through fierce debates between the dominant operator China Telecom on the one side and the State Planning Commission and MII on the other side, representing the interests of the state and society, and the negotiations of China Telecom with the central government. Finally, in April of 2000, the State Council decided to accept the devolution proposal of MII and mandated to spin-off the mobile sector of China Telecom to form China Mobile as an independent company<sup>5</sup> (People’s P&T, 21 April 2000).

## From 2001: ready for foreign competition

The fourth stage of reform commenced when China was officially accepted as a member of WTO at the beginning of 2001. In this process, the state played a key role and translated other actors’ interests into a liberalization initiative. As a condition to enter WTO, China agreed to accept 49% foreign ownership in mobile communications within 5 years, 49% foreign ownership in international and domestic services within 6 years, and 50% foreign ownership in VAS within 2 years (Zhang, 2002). The limitations on foreign share were the focus of the negotiations. The above agreement reflects China’s interest that foreigners must not control the telecommunications industry. In the socialist economy, it is an ideological requirement that in infrastructure the state has a dominant position, while private capital serves as a supplement (Ma & Ling, 1998).

After three stages of reforms, fair competition was not yet a reality in the telecommunications market. Comparatively, Unicom was still too weak to effectively compete with China Telecom. Hence, changes were required within the telecommunications industry. As foreign companies would enter China within a few years, it was vital for the state to prepare by restructuring the market. The state had set the policy called ‘strategic restructuring’ and ‘optimizing national resources’, which aimed at forming several large-scale state-controlled enterprises with the ability to win in the international competition (MII, 1999). The interest alignment led the State Council at the end of 2001 to mandate the formation of a working group in charge of the reform plan and personnel arrangements. The working group was headed by a vice-premier minister. Its members included ministers from MII and the State Planning Commission, etc.<sup>6</sup> As a result of rounds of discussions and negotiations, a consolidation approach was applied to the satellite sector. In December 2001, Sino Satellite Communications and China Orient Satellite were merged into ChinaSat (People’s Daily, 29 December 2001). Moreover, in May 2002, China Telecom was restructured into two sections by areas, mostly following the second plan proposed by the State Planning Commission 2 years previously. The northern section combined 11 northern provincial networks, China Netcom and Jitong and adopted the name China Netcom. Keeping the name China Telecom, the southern section retained other provincial net works (People’s Daily, 26 May 2002). Consequently, China had finally formed a ‘national fleet of operators composed of six ‘carriers’: China Telecom, China Netcom, Unicom, China Mobile, ChinaSat and China Railcom.

## DISCUSSION

## Socio-technological construction of China’s telecommunications transformation strategy

Two layers of social and technological elements have influenced the formulation of China’s telecommunications transformation strategy (Figure 1). The internal layer is composed by the actors. In this layer, the public and society, the operators, and the state struggle to shape the market transformation. We have observed that the strength of the actors’ interests, their specific positions in the market, their political power and their efforts to influence market change determine the strategy selection. Anticipating good services and low prices from competition, the public and society have been keen to see the implementation of a thorough reform. The consideration of the state has been comprehensive. It had to evaluate the impact of the telecommunications reform on the national economy and the political goals. It also needed to treat the telecommunications reform as part of the macro reform and consider its effect on other national policies. The immediate interests of the incumbent operators tended to be against any reforms that might challenge their established positions in market. But reforms might also lead them to improve operation efficiency and service quality, and hence win more customers. Therefore it was in their ultimate interests to support a transformation to competition. They also understood that the reforms were a trend required by the technology advance, and that their power was limited in a bargain over the reform strategy. So it was possible to translate the interests of the incumbents and keep them in the actor-network. Meanwhile, because of the important positions of the incumbents in the market and their strong objections to a radical reform, the interests of the state and society were also translated and aligned to a strategy suggesting a stepwise transformation. For example, execution of the plan to divide China Telecom by areas was delayed from the third to the fourth stage of reform (Table 2).

The external layer defines the contextual conditions for market change. Particularly, different social and technological contextual elements have had various effects on the interest alignment of the human and non-human actors and therefore on the national strategy (Table 3). Generally, the technological characteristics determined the interests embedded in the telecommunications market (Gong & Srinagesh, 1996). Consequently, China’s VAS market was liberalized soon after the services were available, because of its low dependence on network and small requirement on investment. Competition in basic telecommunications market was intensified gradually. Partly, it was promoted by the maturing of the transmission and switching technology that enabled efficient interconnections between networks, and the internet development that required network competition (MII, 1999).

The development in telecommunication market and infrastructure was a necessary condition for market change. Specifically, when the monopolized market was underdeveloped and the national telecommunications infrastructure was weak, liberalization especially when it was related to international companies was unacceptable, as state-owned PTT could not cope with competition. In this situation, the interests were aligned as the Chinese government used preferential policies to support the PTT in constructing a strong national infrastructure. China began to introduce competition as its telecommunications market and infrastructure had been substantially developed.

China’s telecommunications market change has been part of the national economic reform, and strongly influenced by other national policies such as joining the WTO (Zhang, 2002). China’s economic and political environment determined the characteristics of its strategy for telecommunications transformation, which we will illustrate by two examples. First, in most other countries, market transformations have been enacted through legislation such as the Telecommunications Act in the USA and the UK (Snow, 1995; Thatcher, 1999). In China, the telecommunications reform has been carried out by simple governmental orders. Although the reforms in China’s telecommunications market have lasted about two decades and a full competition market is now approaching, China still lacks a ‘Telecommunications Law’ (MPT, 1992; 1996). This has been a result of the slow progress of China’s national reform of the socialist planning system, which excludes the role of economic laws in running the economy (Ma & Ling, 1998). Second, the socialist ideology dominating China’s economic system favours that the state controls the telecommunications industry. Hence, in China the telecommunications competition has been among the state-owned operators. Furthermore, the state competition enabled the Chinese government to use devolution and consolidation as the major approaches to transform its telecommunications market (MII, 1999, See also footnote 6 on Minister Wu’s speech). In contrast, foreign telecommunications reforms have largely been carried out through liberalization to private capital as in the USA (Snow, 1995), the UK (Thatcher, 1999), the EU countries (Noam & Kramer, 1994; European Commission, 1997), and a wide range of developing nations (Petrazzini, 1995).

The successful experience of other countries acted as a catalyst for promoting China’s telecommunications transformation. It encouraged the potential competitors and society to appeal for a change, and pushed the state to review the monopoly policy in the interests of the nation. Yet, in general China did not follow the experience of any other country. It decided its strategy according to the special situation of the economic and political reforms and market development, and the requirement from technology advance.

## Implications

Our research results have theoretical implications for academia. The Chinese experience argues that the national strategy for telecommunications reform is a socio-technological construction. This finding can be drawn upon to understand other national strategies for technology diffusion, information infrastructure construction, and market development. This implies that researchers should consider the roles of not only policy makers but also the public and technology providers in strategy formulation. Moreover, they should interpret the influence of different partners on strategy selection by linking it with the social and technological contexts.

Researchers can use actor-network theory, in particular our model (Figure 1), to analyse the formulation mechanism of a strategy.

Generally, in using actor-network theory, researchers should take translation and inscription as key words. Furthermore, they should be able to not only describe the process of interest translation and inscription, but also expose the factors that determine this process. A contextual analysis is important as it enables researchers to capture the strength and flexibility of actors’ interests and their power to influence an inscription, i.e. the willingness of interest translation and the capability to resist a translation (Walsham, 1997).

Our case study has practical implications for the practitioners of technology diffusion and market development. For the government, our analysis suggests that the formulation of national strategy for promoting these processes should be based on a comprehensive consideration of the interests of different stakeholders. As a point of departure, the selected strategy should allow the important incumbents to survive, the competent new entrants to grow, the public to benefit, and the country to enjoy the fast market development and technology diffusion. Such a national strategy cannot be borrowed blindly from international models. To formulate an effective strategy, the government must consider technological requirements as well as the specific economic and political environment, and the situation of the market and infrastructure development.

Following these principles, China’s telecommunications transformation strategy has guided its market to develop at one of the highest rate in the world (MII, 1999). In contrast, without respecting these principles, the strategy of a country might lead to failure. Several examples serve as lessons. In the early 1990s, to obtain a strong financial basis for the fast telecommunications development, Latin American countries, such as Argentine, Jamaica and Mexico, initiated the telecommunications reforms by directly encouraging foreign carriers to participate in their telecommunications markets. The investors had an overriding power in bargaining for investment in these markets owing to the poor national economy and weak political status of these countries. The investors insisted on purchasing national operators, while a monopoly franchise was sustained. Hence, in these countries, private monopoly substituted state monopoly, and the plans of liberalizing the telecommunications markets failed. Other examples are Malaysia and the Philippines, where the markets were fully liberalized to international capital in the mid-1990s. They expected that competition might help to establish cross-country networks. Yet, on the contrary, the new entrants focused their investment on the lucrative urban areas, where PTTs already met the market demand. As a result, in these countries the interest translation was dominated by foreign operators instead of the governments (Petrazzini, 1995).

The Chinese experience tells the public and technology providers that they can have an effect on the national strategy concerning a market change in certain degrees. But the strategy formulation involves political negotiation and the trials of power. The public can strongly influence the market change when its voice catches the attention of the media and politicians. It is wise for technology providers to participate actively in the strategy formulation process but meanwhile get ready for market changes that will happen in the ways promoted by competitors and the public. All market participants should consider the contextual dynamics when trying to influence the strategy formulation.

Based on the past experience, we speculate on future telecommunications market changes in China. In the present telecommunication market, Unicom is the only comprehensive operator as its network covers the whole country, and it has licenses to provide both mobile and fixed telecommunications services. China should continue to deregulate the telecommunications market and allow current operators to provide services without geographic restrictions. China needs to relax the restrictions on domestic private capital. While convergence between telecommunications network and other communications and media networks is the trend of market development, advance in China will largely depend on the progress of the national economic and political reforms, e.g. whether the state is willing to translate its interest of tightly controlling the media.

## Limitations and future research

This study has considered the process of China’s telecommunications reform. Actor-network theory has been used largely to describe the results of interest inscription and translation rather than to disclose how and why these results have been achieved. A complex process covering two decades is impossible to study comprehensively resulting from the limitations of data sources. Questions that remain unanswered include: how did China Telecom negotiate with the government in the last round of the reform? Why did China Railcom remain untouched in the restructuring initiatives, but China Netcom was involved?

More case studies would be helpful to justify our theoretical model (Figure 1) and to extend our findings beyond China’s telecommunications industry. Our model may be used to examine and compare telecommunications reform in different countries, or to analyse particular policies of countries, for example, the mobile telecommunications market transformation from the second generation to the third generation, which includes issuing operating licenses and selecting standards for the third generation market, etc.

## CONCLUSIONS

Drawing upon actor-network theory, we have analysed the formulation of China’s national strategy for telecommunications transformation. We observe that it is a process whereby the public and society, the operators, and the state align their interests with the requirements of the telecommunications market. These actors’ interests are influenced by the telecommunications development, the macro reform progress and national policies concerning political and economic systems, the international trend in telecommunications reforms, and the technology advance. Owing to the dynamics of these social and technological contexts and the actors’ efforts involved in interest inscription and translation including the negotiations and trials of strength, China’s telecommunications transformation was implemented in four stages, where each stage had specific foci (Table 2).

Our case study makes primary contributions to actor-network research. At present, scholars are explicitly using the actor-network theory to analyse the process of technology implementation and design (Walsham, 1997). This article demonstrates that the application of actornetwork theory can be extended to investigate the mechanism of strategy formulation. Furthermore, our research results make it evident that an actor-network study should include contextual analysis. Specifically, in our research design the social and technological contexts are dynamically connected with strategy formulation (Figure 1). This framework provides comparatively high explanative power, and can be drawn upon by other actor-network studies. It incorporates a description of the strategy that China has adopted, a contextual analysis to identify the social and technological elements that determine the interest alignment, and a normal actor-network analysis study to address how the actors have aligned their interests into the strategy.
