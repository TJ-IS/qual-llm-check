# A SEM–neural network approach for understanding determinants of interorganizational system standard adoption and performances

Felix T.S. Chan ⁎, Alain Y.L. Chong

Department of Industrial and Systems Engineering, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hung Hom, Hong Kong

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 5 July 2011 Received in revised form 9 May 2012 Accepted 11 August 2012 Available online 25 August 2012

Keywords: E-business standard Technology adoption Neural network SEM Survey

## a b s t r a c t

This research examines the adoption of an interorganizational system standard and its bene<sup>fi</sup>ts by using RosettaNet as a case study. A comprehensive research framework derived from institutional theory and a Technology–Organization–Environment model was developed for this research. Data were collected from a sample of 212 Malaysian manufacturing <sup>fi</sup>rms. A multi-state analytic approach was proposed whereby the research model was tested using structural equation modeling (SEM), and the results from SEM were used as inputs for a neural network model for predicting RosettaNet adoption. The results showed that factors related to the environment, the Interorganizational Relationship (IOR) and an information sharing culture have a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the adoption of RosettaNet. In terms of organizational factors, top management support was found to have a positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship with RosettaNet adoption. The results also showed that RosettaNet adoption has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive relationship with organizational performance. The research <sup>fi</sup>ndings can also assist managerial decision making for those organizations planning to adopt RosettaNet. This research reduces the previous research gap by advancing understanding on the relationship of adoption factors and RosettaNet adoption, and extends previous approaches on RosettaNet adoption by investigating the relationships between RosettaNet adoption and organizational performance. Improved existing technology adoption methodology was achieved by integrating both SEM and neural network for examining the adoptions of RosettaNet.

## 1. Introduction

In business today, organizations are constantly undergoing transformations to face the challenges posed by globalization, global sourcing, and the emphasis on time and quality based competition. One way in which organizations have attempted to address these challenges is to reduce cost and improve ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency through the implementation of Supply Chain Management (SCM). The implementation of SCM to improve business performance has been studied by past researchers such as Koh et al. [17], and Li et al. [21]. However, a major challenge faced by organizations is how to integrate the supply chain effectively to form a collaborative supply chain [15]. Supply chain integration is one of the key objectives of SCM and it involves the sharing of information within and among organizations [3,4,26]. Kauremaa et al. [15] de<sup>fi</sup>ned supply chain integration as the “relational and operational coupling of autonomous businesses along a supply chain”. A collaborative and integrated supply chain will provide bene<sup>fi</sup>ts such as faster customer response, more <sup>fl</sup>exibility in changing market conditions, reduction in inventory level, improvement in customer service, and achieving better customer loyalty [5].

Information Technologies (IT) have an important role in helping the supply chain to achieve integration [5]. Organizations were implementing IT to exchange data with their supply chain partners as early as the 1960s [26]. Material Requirement Planning (MRP) was implemented by organizations to integrate internal functions during the 1960s and 1970s before moving to EDI and the e-business technologies that we have today [3]. Although it is logical to think that IT would enable the integration of business processes and information exchanges among supply chain trading partners, most business organizations are still <sup>fi</sup>nding this a major challenge. One main challenge is the need to consolidate data from various sources and integrate them with existing legacy systems used by organizations [2]. The process of integrating these IT applications and components is costly and time consuming, caused by the heterogeneity of the computing environment involved [2]. There are also various business processes involved in a complex supply chain environment, and organizations need to understand each other's business processes before integration can take place. These problems will be reduced if organizations use the same IT systems, and adopt similar meanings for business process terms and operations, which is the main aim of e-business standards. Recent literature from Chong and Ooi [3], Kauremaa et al. [15], and Malhotra et al. [23] have all called for the implementation of e-business standards to achieve supply chain integration between trading partners. EDI and RosettaNet are examples of e-business standards that aim to facilitate supply chain integration via system-to-system exchange of messages [15]. The RosettaNet is based on Extensible Markup Language (XML) and de<sup>fi</sup>nes message guidelines, interfaces for business processes, and implementation frameworks for supply chain interactions between <sup>fi</sup>rms. RosettaNet has many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts compared with EDI [3]. It offers high interoperability for various business segments and provides information exchange for cross business processes [25]. RosettaNet is also based on XML and data are transmitted in real time via the Internet. EDI on the other hand, suffers from high cost, dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in implementation, and its development and evolution have been very slow [25]. Furthermore, past EDIs have used value added networks (VAN) for communications rather than the Internet on which RosettaNet based systems are employed [14].

Despite the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of RosettaNet, its adoption has remained low [3]. Many organizations are still <sup>fi</sup>nding it a major challenge to migrate from existing EDI standards to RosettaNet [3]. Many large organizations also <sup>fi</sup>nd it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to convince their trading partners to adopt RosettaNet. RosettaNet faces competition from EDI as it has been used in the United States for more than 25 years, as well as being used by the majority of Japanese organizations [3].

Although there are many studies on the factors affecting the adoption of RosettaNet [2,3,26], empirical studies on the utilization of RosettaNet remain sparse [15]. There are also few studies which attempt to investigate the performance of organizations after implementing RosettaNet. There is also a lack of information aimed at providing organizations with practical assistance in the adoption of RosettaNet. This research aims to build a comprehensive adoption model by incorporating an Interorganizational Relationship (IOR), government factors, and information sharing culture to the traditional Technology–Organization–Environment (TOE) and Diffusion of Innovation models. This paper aims to answer the following research questions (i) What are the factors that affect RosettaNet implementation?, (ii) What is the relationship between RosettaNet implementation and operational performances of Malaysian organizations?, and (iii) What are the factors that can predict the adoption of RosettaNet? Malaysian companies are chosen for this study because e-business standards are still growing in Malaysia. Furthermore, Malaysia's primary economic sector still lies in manufacturing and many of these manufacturing <sup>fi</sup>rms are trying to improve their ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies and reduce costs through supply chain integration. The Malaysian Electrical and Electronics (E&E) manufacturing sector for example, is said to be losing \$3.9 billion per year due to supply chain inadequacies [3]. RosettaNet has been supported by the Malaysian government and they have been actively promoting RosettaNet to the manufacturing industries. In order to encourage <sup>fi</sup>rms to adopt RosettaNet, the Malaysian Government has even allocated a grant of RM5 million for <sup>fi</sup>rms to implement RosettaNet. However, out of the RM5 million allocated, only RM2 million has been used and interest from <sup>fi</sup>rms has reportedly been low [3].

In order to examine the research questions, two analytic methods were implemented. Structural Equation Modeling's (SEM) strength in path analysis was employed to examine the <sup>fi</sup>rst two research questions, while neural network's strength in prediction was used to answer the last question. This research therefore adopts the sequential multi-method research design recommended by Scott and Walczak [29], whereby such approach is deemed to be able to provide a richer understanding of the topic studied. SEM will therefore verify whether the causal relationships in the research model are valid by examining the goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t of the model. The supported relationships in the SEM are then used as the neural network structure to predict RosettaNet's adoption. The paper proceeds with the literature review that helped to develop the research model and hypotheses. Next, the research methodology is presented, followed by results and discussion.

Lastly, the paper discusses the implications, limitations, and suggested future studies in this research area.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Overview of RosettaNet

The RosettaNet consortium is a non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t e-business standard consortium and has one of the largest memberships among e-business standards consortia [3]. The e-business standard developed by the RosettaNet consortium is known as RosettaNet. Supply chain partners that implement RosettaNet will be able to automate their business process and conduct it over the Internet. RosettaNet consists of a robust open business process standard, which includes data dictionaries, implementation framework, and XML based message schemas and process speci<sup>fi</sup>cations [15]. The three core areas of RosettaNet are Partner interface processes (PIP), the RosettaNet implementation framework (RNIF) and RosettaNet business and technical dictionaries. PIP is the key component of RosettaNet and is an XML based dialog that speci<sup>fi</sup>es the message content and message exchange sequence [15]. Each PIP speci<sup>fi</sup>cation contains a business document template and a diagram of the business process [3]. The RNIF provides the framework that enables the transmission of PIP. Exchange protocols such as routing information and transport are provided by RNIF. The RosettaNet business and technical dictionaries provide consistent de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and terms used for conducting e-business transactions. The business dictionary contains de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of business activities between the trading partners while the technical dictionary provides the properties for de<sup>fi</sup>ning products and services [3].

## 2.2. Factors affecting adoption of RosettaNet

The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of RosettaNet when compared to EDI are supported by past studies such as Chong and Ooi [3] and Kauremaa et al. [15]. However, many organizations are still using EDI despite the well known limitations. One of the main adoption barriers of RosettaNet is the unwillingness of organizations to share information. Trust still remains as one of the critical issues in integrating the supply chain among trading partners.

Few empirical studies have attempted to analyze the factors that can affect the adoption of RosettaNet. Thatcher et al. [34] looked at B2B e-commerce which included RosettaNet and found that organizational, industrial, governmental, and cultural factors are some of the driving forces for organizations in Taiwan. Chong and Ooi [3] empirically examined the adoption of RosettaNet within the Malaysian electrical and electronics industry and found that partners' power, trust, and product characteristics have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on RosettaNet adoption. Nurmilaakso [26] examined the migration of EDI to XML-based e-business framework in 329 European companies and found that companies with higher skills, and those with a higher number of business transactions with their partner through computermediated networks are more likely to migrate from EDI to XML-based e-business frameworks.

Roger's Diffusion of Innovation and Tornatzky and Fleischer's [35] TOE models are two of the most commonly applied models in the study of technology adoption [4]. These models attempted to look at technology adoptions from the perspectives of technology characteristics (e.g. advantages offered, compatibility, ease of use), organization (e.g. top management support, organization size, organization structure) and environment (e.g. industry pressure, competition) issues. However, studies based on TOE and Diffusion of Innovation models might not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to explain an organization's RosettaNet adoption decisions. This is because an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet involves the needs to share information among trading partners and the co-adoption of the standards by organizations in the industry. Past management information system researchers have aimed at understanding technology adoption beyond the TOE and Diffusion of Innovation models. However, the adoption of RosettaNet may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the institutional environments in which the <sup>fi</sup>rms operate. In a supply chain, this environment consists of suppliers, trading partners, competitors, customers and government. A central tenet of the institutional perspective is that <sup>fi</sup>rms that share the same environment will employ similar practices and thus become “isomorphic” with each other [18]. IOR plays an important role in determining the adoption of c-commerce in <sup>fi</sup>rms in the institutional environment. These researchers have looked at speci<sup>fi</sup>c adoption issues such as culture [34], government in<sup>fl</sup>uence [3] and interorganizational relationship (IOR) factors [4]. However, by focusing on speci<sup>fi</sup>c adoption factors, these study models are not comprehensive enough to address all the adoption issues. As Huang et al. [11] stated in their study of internet EDI, a holistic technology adoption model is needed in technology adoption studies instead of myopic or fragmented ones to ensure accuracy and less bias. Besides issues related to the adoption of RosettaNet, studies on the outcomes of adopting RosettaNet remained limited.

Based on the existing literature, a comprehensive adoption model is proposed where the adoption factors are examined from the perspective of organization, technology/innovation, environment, IOR, government, and information sharing culture. The relationships between RosettaNet adoption and the operational performances of organizations were also examined. The next section provides the research model and hypotheses for this research.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

The research model for this study is shown in Fig. 1. The model proposes that organizations, innovation, environment, IOR, government and information sharing culture factors will in<sup>fl</sup>uence the adoption of RosettaNet. Furthermore, organizations that adopt RosettaNet will improve their operational and SCM related organizational performances. The next section discusses the hypotheses formulated based on the research model presented.

## 3.1. Organization

An organization's success in adopting a new technology is heavily dependent on the organizations readiness [11]. Jeyaraj et al. [12] conducted a meta analysis of 99 technology adoption studies published between 1992 and 2003 and found that the organization factors such as top management support and the organization's size are both significant determinants of whether organizations could implement a technology successfully.

Investing in technologies such as EDI or RosettaNet is a long term commitment which may require a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of resources from the organization. For example, organizations must purchase the necessary software and hardware, as well as training their employees. Such purchase might not be necessarily one off. As with many technologies, the organization might need to constantly invest in updates and upgrades of the equipment or send their employees for training to upgrade new skills. Thatcher et al. [34] stated that organizational readiness remains very low in many countries as the business cultures do not support innovation and the adoption of new technology. Besides support from top management, many studies have also considered whether organization size plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the adoption of technologies, and more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally SCM systems adoption [2,32,42]. Larger organizations are often thought to be adopters of RosettaNet as they have better technical and <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources [26]. Nurmilaakso [26] also found that larger organizations are more likely to migrate from EDI to XML-based e-business standards such as RosettaNet. Thus small organizations, especially those in developing countries, are thought to be inhibitors of the adoption of technology [34]. However, there have been contradictory results presented by studies such as Gibbs and Kramer [8], Huang et al. [11], and Zhu et al. [43]. They explained that it is possible for smaller organizations to have less legacy systems to overcome when adopting e-business. They are also more agile and <sup>fl</sup>exible when it comes to making decisions, and necessary changes, to adopt IT systems as reported by Gibbs and Kramer [8]. Huang et al. [11] also mentioned that technologies nowadays are more affordable and the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts will far outweigh the cost for small organizations. Given that there are mixed results when it comes to the role of organization attributes and technology adoption, we hypothesize that:

H1a. Top management support has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

H1b. Organization size has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

## 3.2. Innovation

Roger's [28] Diffusion of Innovation model proposed that the attributes of an innovation may determine the success of an organization's technology adoption. The innovation attributes proposed by Roger's [28] Diffusion of Innovation model are compatibility, trialability, complexity, relative advantage and observability. Compatibility is whether the technology is perceived as being compatible with existing systems. Trialability is the degree to which the technology may be experimented with. Complexity is the perceived dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of using of the technology. Relative advantage is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as better than the idea it supersedes. Observability is the degree in which the results of implementing the technology are visible to others.

Innovation attributes have been studied in various technologies as they are not dependent on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of technologies. It has been studied in e-business standards, e-commerce, mobile commerce and radio frequency identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (RFID) [2,36,39]. RosettaNet involves the migration from existing standards such as EDI, therefore it would be interesting to see if the characteristics of RosettaNet will in<sup>fl</sup>uence adoption. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we would like to know if RosettaNet is perceived to offer more advantages when compared to existing standards such as EDI. Perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of adopting technology are important factors in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing top management's investment decisions [28]. Organizations might also consider whether RosettaNet is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to use or if it is easy to integrate RosettaNet with their existing systems. Lastly, organizations might want to consider if the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of RosettaNet can be observed before investing in it. This is consistent with <sup>fi</sup>ndings from Jiménez-Martínez and Polo-Redondo [13] whereby the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts were found to have played a major role in the decision to adopt EDI. Based on the existing studies and the Diffusion of Innovation model, it leads us to our next hypotheses:

H2a. Compatibility has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

H2b. Complexity has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, negative impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

H2c. Trialability has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

H2d. Relative advantage has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

H2e. Observability has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

## 3.3. Environment

The business environment in which organizations are engaged may in<sup>fl</sup>uence their decisions to adopt RosettaNet. Pressure from business partners and competitors may sometimes force organizations to adopt a technology [19]. However, RosettaNet is different from many technologies, such as e-commerce, whereby the success of RosettaNet is dependent on the industry as a whole. For example, Chong et al.

[5] in their study of collaborative commerce adoption found that the trends and pressure from the electronic industry force many organizations to adopt collaborative commerce. Most of the organizations that adopt RosettaNet are from the manufacturing industry. The manufacturing industry is faced with the supply chain challenge of trying to reduce cost and operate ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently [5]. Therefore organizations might choose to adopt RosettaNet in order to reduce cost and prevent investors from moving their manufacturing sites to countries with lower cost. This trend can be observed in the electrical and electronics industry in Malaysia whereby many investors are shifting their investments to lower cost countries such as China [3]. Therefore, unlike pressure exerted by the trading partners, the pressure in adopting RosettaNet can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the entire industry.

Given that most organizations are still using EDI, it is no guarantee that RosettaNet will eventually replace EDI as the de-facto e-business standard. Furthermore, internet technologies are moving at such a fast pace that it is possible that other e-business standards might replace RosettaNet in the future. Organizations might choose not to adopt RosettaNet if they do not believe that the market trend for e-business standard is RosettaNet. This leads us to next our hypothesis:

H3a. Competitive Pressure has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

H3b. Expectations of market trend have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

## 3.4. IOR

RosettaNet is adopted between trading partners and IOR is formed when two or more organizations agree to adopt RosettaNet. RosettaNet also has far more implementation issues that need to be considered as they provide a wide range of functionality ranging from developing online business processes to facilitating cooperation with customers and business partners [22]. These attributes of RosettaNet require a good establishment of IOR between the organization planning to adopt RosettaNet and their supply chain partners. Huang et al. [11] in their study of Internet based EDI stated that building new electronic relationships is a more challenging task compared to technical issues.

Our study investigates IOR from the perspectives of partner power, relationship commitment, trust in trading partners, and transaction frequency. In the current business environment, many western countries have manufacturing plants in Asian countries such as China, Malaysia, and Indonesia. Organizations in many of these countries run their business based on relationships [4]. The relationships are based on organization trust, and given that organizations need to commit to <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources and share information in adopting the RosettaNet standard, they need to ensure that a long-term relationship can be developed. They also need to ensure that the trading partners will not act in an opportunistic manner even if there are short-term incentives to do so [31].

Besides trust, it is possible that an organization may exert pressure on their trading partners to adopt RosettaNet, based on partner power. Partner power is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the capability of a <sup>fi</sup>rm to exert in-<sup>fl</sup>uence on another <sup>fi</sup>rm to act in a prescribed manner” [27]. Therefore, it is possible that in an IOR, organizations with larger partner power are able to use either compulsory or convincing power over their business partners in the adoption of RosettaNet [16]. Lastly, we have also looked at the transaction frequency of the trading partners in measuring IOR. Kurokawa et al. [20] stated that a high transaction frequency re<sup>fl</sup>ects the importance of a seller's product for a buyer's operational process. Therefore products with high transaction frequencies will provide higher incentives for the trading partners to improve their coordination.

The literature on the impact of IOR on technology adoption includes Huang et al. [11], Kurokawa et al. [20] and Shang et al. [31].

However, these studies have only focused on EDI and e-business technologies. RosettaNet is a more recent XML based standard compared to EDI, and the role of IOR in the presence of other factors (e.g. competition, <sup>fi</sup>nancial status of organizations) needs further investigation. In this connection, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4a. Partner power has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

H4b. Relationship commitment has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

H4c. Trust in trading partner has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

H4d. Transaction frequency has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

## 3.5. Government

Governments have often played a key role in driving the adoption of IT technologies. The IT support that a government can offer includes IT infrastructure, quality of labor, national policy in training, and ensuring an adequate IT workforce [34].

The Malaysian Government for example, started the Multimedia Super Corridor (MSC) initiative with the aim of emulating the success of Silicon Valley. The government offers incentives such as tax breaks and facilities, including high speed Internet, to companies in the MSC area. The government also encouraged and helped set up a research based university in the MSC area to offer research and development in engineering and IT. The Malaysian government even offered tax reductions to small and medium enterprises (SME) that adopt RosettaNet. Singapore also had a successful result in the adoption of EDI due to the government's control of IT developments, as well as providing educational programmes and promotion on EDI [32].

Thatcher et al. [34] studied the relationships between government and the adoption of RosettaNet in Taiwan. They found that RosettaNet adoption in Taiwan's electronic industry was positive due to governmental RosettaNet implementation plans and <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives. However, in the textile industries, which have less government involvement in RosettaNet adoption, the adoption was hindered even though tax incentives were available. Chong and Ooi [3] in their study of interorganizational standards adoption, found that the Malaysian government's efforts were not able to help push the adoption of RosettaNet. However, the data was collected in 2007 and the government initiatives and awareness of RosettaNet in Malaysia have been changing since 2007. We therefore hypothesize that:

H5a. Government promotion has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

H5b. Infrastructure has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

## 3.6. Information sharing culture

An organization's willingness to share information with its trading partners plays a key role in the success of RosettaNet adoption. Thatcher et al. [34] used the Hofstede's model on national culture to study the adoption of technologies such as RosettaNet and IT. They found that an organization's decision to adopt a new technology may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by culture. Some of these cultural issues include the unwillingness to take risks in trying a new technology.

It is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to generalize the adoption of RosettaNet based on the national culture in Malaysia. This is because Malaysia is made up of three major ethnic groups, Malays, Chinese and Indians. Each of these groups has some elements of “Malaysian culture” as well as culture in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their traditional roots, e.g. Confucianism in Malaysian Chinese, Islamic lifestyle in Malays to name a few. It would also be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to ensure that the decision makers in the organizations are Malaysians. Studies on national culture would also make the <sup>fi</sup>ndings dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to generalize for other countries. However, one major element of culture which draws the attention of researchers such as Thatcher et al. [34] and Burn [1] is the information sharing characteristics. Therefore instead of looking at the overall national culture, this study focuses more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally on the organization's culture, as to whether there is an information sharing culture in place. Information sharing culture has been studied by current researchers on the adoption of EDI and e-business systems. Burn [1] found that one of the inhibitors of EDI adoption in Hong Kong was the unwillingness to share information by organizations. This is consistent with the information sharing culture conducted by Chong et al.'s [5] study on collaborative commerce. Therefore we hypothesize that:

H6a. Willingness to share information has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

H6b. Information distribution has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, positive impact on an organization's decision to adopt RosettaNet.

## 3.7. RosettaNet implementation and performance

SCM systems implementation such as EDI and the positive impacts on organizational performance have been well documented by past researchers. Ranganathan et al. [27], for example, found that web based SCM system adoption can improve customer service, reduce cost, inventory management, reduce cycle-time, improve supplierrelationship management, and generate competitive advantage. Frohlich [6] also found that there is a positive link between e-integration in the supply chain and business performance. Wu et al. [41] similarly concluded that e-business implementation is able to improve ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, sales performance, customer satisfaction and relationship development of organizations.

While these results have presented the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of SCM and e-business systems, there has been limited empirical examination on the outcome of XML. based e-business standards such as RosettaNet This may be due to RosettaNet being a more recent technology and its actual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are only just beginning to be realized by organizations. Nevertheless, there is a need to examine if RosettaNet implementation can bring positive outcomes before organizations are willing to invest in it or migrate from the existing EDI standards. Furthermore, it would be useful to investigate the outcomes from operational and SCM related organizational performances. Koh et al. [17] measured the operational performance of organizations based on the measures of <sup>fl</sup>exibility, reduced lead time in production, forecasting, resource planning, cost saving, and reduced inventory level. They further stated that although the <sup>fi</sup>nancial criterion is important for measuring business performance, it is also important to include other non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial, SCM related organizational performances when measuring operational performance. For SCM related organizational performance, they proposed measurements based on the increase in sales, accuracy in costing, coordination between departments, coordination with suppliers and coordination with customers.

We have adopted the measurement of performance from them as it is comprehensive in covering both SCM as well as the operational related performance of organizations (for further details, please see Koh et al. [17]). We also hypothesize that:

H7. Organizations that have a higher level of RosettaNet adoption will have a higher level of operational performance.

H8. Organizations that have a higher level of RosettaNet adoption will have a higher level of SCM related organizational performance.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Sample and data collection

A survey instrument was developed to test the research model. Although the majority of the items in the survey questionnaire were adopted from the existing literature, we have pre-tested the survey before it was distributed in Malaysian organizations. The survey was pre-tested with eight service and two manufacturing organizations to ensure that the wording and format of the questions were appropriate for Malaysian organizations. We requested the surveys to be completed by either the IT or logistics managers in the organizations. The survey questionnaire was emailed to 2000 organizations listed in the Federation of Malaysian Manufacturers (FMM) directory. For organizations that did not list down their email contacts, hardcopies of the surveys were sent to these organizations. Out of these 2000 survey questionnaires sent out, 212 were collected. The overall response rate was 10.6% (212/2000). In terms of demographic pro-<sup>fi</sup>les, 110 organizations were local <sup>fi</sup>rms, 69 multinationals and 33 joint ventures. Furthermore, 162 organizations have also operated in Malaysia for more than 10 years, while 50 have less than 10 years experience of operating in Malaysia. As the survey asked for self-reported data, there is a risk that a common method bias might occur. Harman's single-factor test was applied to examine this potential bias, and the results suggested that there was no significant common method bias in the dataset (e.g. single factors did not emerge from un-rotated factor solutions).

## 4.2. Variable measurement

## 4.2.1. RosettaNet adoption factors

There are 19 technology adoption variables used in this study. 65 questions were used to measure the 19 variables. Responses for all the items except organization size were measured based on a <sup>fi</sup>ve point Likert Scale, ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Organization size was measured based on a <sup>fi</sup>ve point scale measuring annual turnover from less than 200,000 to more than 10 million Malaysian Ringgits. The variables were adapted from Chong and Ooi [3], Chong et al. [4], and Chong et al. [5].

## 4.2.2. RosettaNet adoption

The measure for RosettaNet adoption follows Nelson and Shaw [25]. A <sup>fi</sup>ve scale measurement of whether the organization is 1— no interest, 2—fence sitter, 3—commitment, 4—light users, 5—heavy users was used to measure the adoption level.

## 4.2.3. Operational performance and SCM related organizational performance

The measurement of performance is adopted from Koh et al. [17], who mentioned that it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to measure a <sup>fi</sup>rm's performance based on single quantitative measurement. They also stated that the subjective approach has been used extensively by past empirical studies, using measurement such as executives' perceptions of performance. Based on their approach, six operational factors (e.g. <sup>fl</sup>exibility, reduced lead time in production, cost saving, forecasting, resource planning and reduced inventory level) and <sup>fi</sup>ve SCM related organizational performances (e.g. more accurate costing, increase in coordination between departments, increase in coordination with suppliers, increase in coordination with customers, and increase in sales) were selected. Organizations were asked to indicate their responses on a <sup>fi</sup>ve-point scale as to whether their organizations are de<sup>fi</sup>nitely better to de<sup>fi</sup>nitely worst in terms of business performance relative to their competitors over the past 3 years. All measurement items can be accessed at http://www.ijnmt.com/SurveyQuestionnaireRosettaNet. pdf.

## 5. Methodology

A multiple-analytic approach using SEM and neural network was employed in this research. SEM examined the hypotheses, reliability and validity of the measures, while the neural network veri<sup>fi</sup>ed the antecedents as predictors of RosettaNet adoption [29]. As stated by Hsu et al. [10], SEM is often applied in social and behavioral sciences to verify hypothesized relationships, but is rarely combined with other arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence algorithms. Linear models such as SEM will often oversimplify the complexities when making technology adoptior decisions [37]. To overcome this problem, the neural network approach will be incorporated to identify the non-linear relationships in the research model. The advantage of using the neural network is that it is able to learn complex linear and non-linear associations between the technology adoption factors and adoption decisions. By examining non-linear relationships, neural network is able to examine organizations' non-compensatory technology adoption decisions. Past linear, compensatory model has tended to assume that the shortfall in one of the adoption decision such as perceived usefulness can be compensated by improving another adoption factor such as perceived ease of use. However, an organization may decide not to adopt RosettaNet due to cost issues, and this may not be compensated by its ease of use. Further more, the neural network is able to offer more accurate predictions when compared to traditional regression [24]. Neural network's accuracy can also be improved as it has the ability to “learn” based on the data input, and therefore it is able to generalize to situations not taught to the network previously (for more information on neural network, see Scott and Walczak [29]). Although it serves as a good tool for prediction, a limitation of the neural network is its “blackbox” approach, making it unsuitable for hypotheses testing and examining causal relationships. It can also be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to understand how the neural nets arrive at their results [7]. As such, SEM is used to test the overall research model and hypotheses in this research and the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant hypothesized variables will be used as input to develop the neural network for predicting RosettaNet adoption. This is will address one of the weaknesses of the neural network which is its tendency to over<sup>fi</sup>t a model, and at the same time improve the predictions of RosettaNet model when com pared to traditional regression techniques. This is one of the few studies which integrate SEM with the neural network [10]. Similar technology adoption studies have often employed either SEM (e.g. [43]) or neural network (e.g. [24]) without integrating the two approaches. To summarize, the steps taken to generate the <sup>fi</sup>ndings are: 1) Factor analysis Cronbach's Alpha and Average Variance Extraction (AVE) are <sup>fi</sup>rstly ap plied to examine the reliability and validity of the research instruments, 2) SEM is conducted to examine the causal relationships in the research model, 3) variables found signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the SEM model will be used as input in the neural network model for predicting RosettaNet adoptions. Each step is discussed in the following sections.

## 5.3. Neural Network analysis for predicting RosettaNet adoption

As discussed in an earlier section, the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant independent variables from the SEM analysis were used to develop the neural network analysis. This research applied the sigmoid function as the hidden layer and output layer activation function [33]. As stated by Sexton et al. [30], there is no heuristic method for determining the hidden nodes in the neural network. As such, this research adopted the approach by Sexton et al. [30] and Wang and Elhag [38], whereby the preliminary network was examined by including two, four, six, eight, ten, 12, 14, 16, 18, and 20 hidden nodes. A network with ten hidden nodes was found to be complex enough to map the datasets without incurring additional errors to the neural network model. The input layer therefore consisted of the nine independent signi<sup>fi</sup>cant variables from the SEM (e.g. top management support, competitive pressure, expectation of market trends, partner power, relationship commitment, trust in trading partner, transaction frequency, willingness to share information, and information distribution), while the output layer consisted of one output variable (e.g. RosettaNet adoption).

A ten-fold cross validation was performed whereby 90% of the data was used to train the neural network, while the remaining 10% was used to measure the prediction accuracy of the trained network. Table 4 shows the relative error percentage of the validations.

From Table 4, the average cross-validated relative error percentage for the training model was 4.28% while the testing model was 5.61%. Hence, the network model is quite reliable in capturing the numerical relations between the predictors and outputs [40].

Table 5 shows the sensitivity analysis performance which was calculated by the average importance of the input variables in predicting the output for the ten networks. The importance of an independent variable is a measure of how much the network's modelpredicted value changes for different values of the independent variable. The normalized importance values were calculated by using the importance values divided by the largest importance values and expressed as percentages [33].

As shown in Table 5, all the independent variables were found to be relevant in all of the ten networks. The table shows that in general, variables related to IOR and information sharing culture have the greatest effect on how the network predicts RosettaNet adoptions when compared to the environmental variables. A limitation of the neural network is that it would not be able to show the “direction” of the relationships of the predictors and the outcomes. However, this is addressed by the SEM which showed the positive relationships between the predictors and RosettaNet adoptions.

## 6. Discussion

H1b and H1c are not supported in this research. Past technology adoption studies have provided mixed results on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the organization factor. Much of the existing literature has suggested that bigger organizations with more <sup>fi</sup>nancial and technical resources are usually more likely to adopt new technology [26]. In particular, past adoption of technologies such as EDI often needs commitment from top management as it is a long term commitment and also involves changes in business processes. However, given that RosettaNet is much more affordable for small organizations whereby the organization can choose to implement it in selected business processes (e.g. implementing certain PIPs), and gradually increase the number of PIPs when necessary, so resources might not be a major hindrance. RosettaNet is also based on XML, thus the technical skills required for implementing RosettaNet should no longer be a major adoption barrier, given today's IT development. RosettaNet is also a much more affordable technology as it is based on the Internet when compared to EDI. Therefore the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts could outweigh the costs for smaller organizations. However, similar to many past technology adoption studies [3], top management support is important in determining the adoption of RosettaNet. This shows that regardless of an organization's size or resources, a supportive management will play a more important role in RosettaNet adoption.

The results from H2a to H2e showed that the innovation factor also does not play a major role in the adoption of RosettaNet. One reason could be that e-business technologies such as RosettaNet are not totally new to organizations anymore. Most organizations are aware of the advantages compared to existing systems such as EDI. As RosettaNet is web based and uses XML, issues such as compatibility, which is often found in existing legacy systems will not occur. Other attributes, such as its perceived complexity, also have less in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the adoption decision given that web based technologies are much easier to implement compared to existing EDI systems.

H3a and H3b are both found to have no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship with RosettaNet adoption. Thatcher et al. [34] in their study of B2B adoption, which included RosettaNet, found that the government plays a major role in driving B2B adoption in industry. Our study is based on organizations in Malaysia where the government has been supportive of RosettaNet, and has provided incentives such as tax exemptions to drive RosettaNet adoption. The government has also provided the necessary technical infrastructure by setting up the Multimedia Development Corporation which foresees the development of the MSC initiatives. However, our result (H5a and H5b) shows that these government factors do not play a role in driving the adoption of RosettaNet. There are a few possible explanations for this <sup>fi</sup>nding. Thatcher et al.'s [34] study focused on Taiwan and China, in which the government plays a more major role in controlling the countries' industries. This is particularly true in China, where many Chinese <sup>fi</sup>rms are owned by the government. Kuan and Chau [19] stated that the pressure exerted by the government caused organizations to adopt EDI in Hong Kong. However, in Malaysia, many organizations are not controlled by the government, and the government has not exerted pressure on organizations to adopt RosettaNet. Secondly, the incentives offered by the Malaysian government might not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to drive the adoption of RosettaNet. Thirdly, the Malaysian government does not have a speci<sup>fi</sup>c plan on RosettaNet implementation and how they could support the various industries. This is similar to the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from Thatcher et al. [34] where the textile industry in Taiwan has lower RosettaNet adoption compared with the electronic industry, due to lack of government involvement in the textile industry. Government needs to develop long term RosettaNet implementation plans and works closely with the relevant industries to drive RosettaNet adoption.

Consistent with <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the past, H3a and H3b are supported in our study. Organizations in Malaysia are faced with competitive pressure not only locally, but globally as well. Manufacturing organizations in China, Vietnam, and Thailand are currently directly competing with Malaysia for foreign direct investments. This competitive pressure drives organizations to reduce cost while increasing productivity. Past studies on EDI have found that competitive pressure can in<sup>fl</sup>uence organizations to adopt EDI [11]. However, EDI has traditionally relied on the Value Added Network (VAN) which is more costly compared to the Internet based RosettaNet. In order to reduce cost through automation and integrating the supply chain, triggered by the competitive pressure, organizations will adopt RosettaNet as the result indicates. Organizations will also invest and commit to RosettaNet if they expect it to be the market trend. The main reason is that RosettaNet is not implemented by just one organization or a few organizations. Instead, the success of an e-business standard such as RosettaNet requires the co-adoption of the majority of trading partners. In this connection, organizations are more likely to adopt RosettaNet if they are assured that RosettaNet will become the de-facto e-business standard in the long run.

Both H4a–H4d and H6a–H6b are factors that are not included in traditional TOE adoption studies and both are found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. IOR has been studied in the past, such as by Kurokawa et al. [20], Shang et al. [31], Huang et al. [11], and Chong et al. [4]. Consistent with these <sup>fi</sup>ndings which have focused on EDI and e-commerce, RosettaNet adoption is heavily dependent on IOR, such as partner power, relationship commitment, trust in trading partner, and transaction frequency. Although organization size is found to be non signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant (H1a–H1c), organizations with higher power can persuade or even force their partners with less bargaining power to adopt RosettaNet. Studies have shown that organizations, such as Wal-Mart, are able to make it compulsory for their trading partners to adopt RFID or bear the risk of losing business. The implementation of RosettaNet requires integrating the supply chain processes. Therefore organizations need to ensure that they can trust their trading partners before sharing important supply chain information with them (e.g. forecast data). The IOR is built on the belief that there is a long term commitment between the trading partners. Our results suggest that if there is higher frequency in trading, there is better IOR which will increase the chances of RosettaNet adoption. As RosettaNet can be implemented in an incremental manner, organizations can choose to implement PIPs on products with high transaction frequency instead of products with low transaction frequency.

Instead of investigating national culture, as mentioned by Thatcher et al. [34], this study focused on the information sharing culture of organizations. The results show that organizations that are more open in sharing information are more likely to adopt RosettaNet. The willingness to share information can be cultivated internally, whereby departments and functions within the same organizations should be encouraged to share important information that are useful to each other. Organizations that have existing technologies such as knowledge management systems and Intranet will also have a better information sharing culture, thus ensuring that the organizations will be more successful in adopting RosettaNet.

Lastly, both H7 and H8 empirically validated that organizations that have implemented RosettaNet will have better operational and SCM related organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Existing studies on RosettaNet have often neglected linking the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of RosettaNet implementation with the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts gained. The results suggest that organizations will be able to gain operational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, such as improvement in <sup>fl</sup>exibility, lead time in production, cost saving, forecasting, resource planning, and inventory level, as well as SCM related organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts such as improvement in costing accuracy and increase in coordination between departments, suppliers and customers, and an increase in sales.

In terms of predictors of RosettaNet, this neural network model found that the nine signi<sup>fi</sup>cant variables from the SEM are all relevant in the prediction of RosettaNet adoption. The neural network model has high prediction accuracy, as noted in the earlier discussion. The neural network model also shows that IOR and Information sharing culture variables in general played larger roles in predicting the adoption of RosettaNet, thus further con<sup>fi</sup>rming the importance of extending the traditional TOE and Diffusion of Innovation models in studying modern interorganizational systems standards, such as RosettaNet.

## 7. Conclusion and implications

This research presents the relationship between the adoption factors of organization, innovation, environment, IOR, government and information sharing culture with the adoption of RosettaNet within Malaysian organizations, and also examines the relationship between RosettaNet adoptions with operation bene<sup>fi</sup>ts gained by organizations. The result suggests that the perceived attributes of RosettaNet, organizational size, support from management and feasibility, and Malaysian's current policy of providing <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives and promotion on RosettaNet will not in<sup>fl</sup>uence the adoption of RosettaNet. Instead, the IOR environment and the information sharing culture play a more important role in determining the adoption of RosettaNet.

This study has several important implications. Firstly, it has provided a comprehensive empirical examination on RosettaNet adoption, and has extended the traditional theories by including traditional TOE factors, together with government, information sharing culture and IOR factors. Past studies tended to focus on only certain aspects of these factors.

Secondly, we have moved away from traditional studies on EDI and focused on RosettaNet, a more current e-business XML standard that is being promoted and adopted by large organizations, such as Nokia, In<sup>fi</sup>neon, and Intel. This <sup>fi</sup>nding would be useful for <sup>fi</sup>rms in the electrical and electronics industry, given that RosettaNet is one of the main e-business standards that are being actively promoted by industry. Many <sup>fi</sup>rms in the electrical and electronics industry have traditionally implemented EDI systems, and they are planning various strategies to successfully migrate their existing EDI systems to RosettaNet [3]. This research provides recommendations on how to migrate from existing EDI to RosettaNet. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that unlike existing studies which found that overcoming technological barriers such as compatibility and complexity issues is important for the successful implementation of EDI, the success of RosettaNet implementation is dependent on the willingness to share information and in establishing IOR.

Thirdly, organizations that would like to adopt RosettaNet and encourage their trading partners to adopt RosettaNet can formulate strategies based on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this research. As stated earlier, organizations can focus on building IOR with their trading partners or improving their information sharing culture. One way of achieving this is to focus on the right type of products. Organizations are allowed to implement RosettaNet in a gradual, incremental process (e.g. increasing the number of PIPs in the long run), or by selecting only a few products in their supply chain to implement RosettaNet with their trading partners. The results show that products with higher transaction frequency are more likely to lead to successful adoption of RosettaNet, and are consistent with the results from Chong et al. [5]. When <sup>fi</sup>rms transact with each other more frequently for high volume products, in the long run, the IOR will be improved, resulting in the supply chain members being more willing to share information. Some organizations may choose to enforce partner power and make it compulsory for their trading partners to adopt RosettaNet.

Fourthly, for the Malaysian government, the results show that their current efforts of providing incentives and infrastructure are not suf<sup>fi</sup>- cient to drive RosettaNet adoption. The results showed that the <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives given by the government are not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to attract <sup>fi</sup>rms to implement RosettaNet. Instead, the government could consider what the Taiwanese government is doing in the electronics industry by having a long term RosettaNet implementation plan and in working closely with organizations in the industry. A more active role played by the Malaysian Government will give con<sup>fi</sup>dence to the electrical and electronics industry that RosettaNet will be the market trend for e-business standard in the industry, and will lead to organizations being more willing to take risks and invest in RosettaNet.

Fifthly, this study also shows that organizations that have adopted RosettaNet are able to achieve bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and this should encourage more organizations to adopt RosettaNet. One of the main reasons organizations are not willing to adopt RosettaNet is due to the lack or research which supports the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of RosettaNet implementation. However, from this research, organizations which have implemented RosettaNet have reported improvement in their operational performances, and this will encourage more organizations to adopt RosettaNet. Organizations can also use the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this research to encourage their trading partners to adopt RosettaNet.

Lastly, this research contributes to existing technology adoption study methodology by integrating both the SEM and neural network approaches. Although SEM and the neural network were applied individually in many technology adoption studies, few of these studies have combined these two approaches [10]. Although SEM is able to examine the causal relationships presented in the research, its predictive power lacks the ability of the neural network. However, using only neural network will not provide the causal analysis, and its dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of showing how the neural nets generate the results is one of the obstacles of using neural network in technology adoption studies [7,10]. Furthermore, it is often dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to construct a neural network model when learning from data. Using results from the SEM provides a way to develop the neural network model with a good prediction performance. The multi-analytical approach adopted is able to serve as a good reference for future research in technology adoption studies.

## 8. Limitations and suggestion of future work

This study has four potential limitations. Firstly, our data was only obtained from Malaysian organizations. This limits the generalization of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other emerging countries. Future work can focus on conducting a multi-country comparison study on RosettaNet adoption.

Secondly, we have only collected data from one respondent in each organization, which has the risk of response bias. A future study can consider collecting data from organizations across the supply chain as suggested by Koh et al. [17], or from middle managers, engineers, and technical personnel. Thirdly, although we have provided a comprehensive adoption model by including various adoption factors, future studies can include variables not included in this study, such as industry type and supply chain length. Lastly, future studies can consider measuring the adoption of RosettaNet based on the number of PIP processes. This will give a clearer indication of the actual RosettaNet diffusion.
