# Unlocking the Power of User Tie Strength: A Multistudy on Cross-Platform Content Sharing Behaviors

Jie Su,<sup>1</sup> Xin (Robert) Luo,<sup>2</sup> Han Li,<sup>3</sup> Haowen Xiao<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Business, Yunnan University of Finance and Economics, China, sujie1214@whu.edu.cn <sup>2</sup>Anderson School of Management, University of New Mexico, USA, xinluo@unm.edu <sup>3</sup>Anderson School of Management, University of New Mexico, USA, hanli@unm.edu <sup>4</sup>International Business School, Hainan University, China, howe.xiao@hainanu.edu.cn

## Abstract

Social media platforms such as Facebook and WeChat are fiercely competing for a limited pool of social media ad revenue. One pivotal competing battlefield revolves around populating their platforms with compelling and engaging content to increase user stickiness. Until now, extant literature has offered scant insights into potential differences in content-sharing preferences in a multiplatform context and underlying motivations and intricacies. To advance this line of research, this paper contextualizes and extends the self-presentation theory to unveil how the sharing of vice versus virtue content systematically varies with the strength of relationships (tie strength). This intricate phenomenon is meticulously examined through a field study across two distinct social media platforms and three carefully designed laboratory experiments. Notably, we found that social media users lean toward sharing vice content with their strong ties out of the desire to be liked, while they opt for virtue content with their weak ties to fulfill their desire for respect. Ushering in a supplementary layer of complexity, our research unveils online social exclusion as a pervasive yet often overlooked moderating force that challenges the fundamental assumption of self-presentation theory and defines its boundary condition. The findings of this study also carry important implications for social media companies to design effective measures to nurture engaging user content and for general businesses to make better digital marketing decisions.

Keywords: Tie Strength, Vice and Virtue Content, Self-Presentation, Social Exclusion, Social Media

## 1 Introduction

With the rising prevalence of social media platforms, consumers often have multiple accounts across various platforms and may selectively share substantial amounts of content daily on different social media platforms (Ham et al., 2019). This content spans the colossal volume of content shared daily, including news articles, videos, and personal narratives, disseminated across a diverse range of social media platforms. This nascent trend has gained increasing momentum and greatly expanded the landscape and opportunities for social media marketing (Yadav et al., 2013). In their pursuit of user engagement, online platforms continuously refine their functionalities and marketing strategies, exemplified by firms such as TikTok, specializing in video content, and WeChat, emphasizing text and image sharing (Oh et al., 2023). Lately, in the wake of global economic upheaval post-pandemic, social media giants have been grappling with decelerated growth in social media ad spending (Shalabi, 2024). Faced with this predicament, social media firms are being pressured to attract diverse and engaging content to increase the effectiveness of their digital marketing endeavors.

Recognizing the cruciality of content for user engagement and monetization on social media platforms, a substantial portion of extant social media research has predominantly concentrated on user-shared content within singular platforms such as Facebook or Instagram (Kim & Johnson, 2016; Iglesias-Sánchez et al., 2020). However, scant attention has been paid to the disparities in how contentsharing behaviors manifest across platforms of varying social and technological characteristics. Additionally, while a burgeoning body of research has explored the general usage patterns and the psychological impacts of social media (Karahanna et al., 2018; O’Day & Heimberg ., 2021), relatively few studies have delved into the systematic differences in sharing specific content with recipients of different tie strengths. Notably, as different levels of tie strength (such as strong ties and weak ties) reflect varying user needs and expectations, the alignment between specific content types and platform tie strength could be crucial for promoting user engagement in content sharing, thereby curating social networks and enhancing overall user stickiness. The nuanced effect of such alignment on social media users’ content-sharing behaviors, however, has not yet been examined. Given this important knowledge paucity, it is imperative to further probe and understand social media users’ preferences in sharing different types of content within diverse contexts of technologies and relationships (e.g., strong vs. weak ties). For instance, the semi-closed nature of the personal page (i.e., Moments) on WeChat, which is only visible to friends, diverges starkly from the public visibility of the personal page on Weibo, accessible to anyone on the internet. This dichotomy warrants an exploration into whether these platform-specific design approaches lead to varied perceptions of tie strength among users (Liang et al., 2011), and how this, in turn, exerts divergent influences on social media users’ behavior in sharing various types of content. In this context, user-shared content (USC) involves various forms of content posted by regular social media users on digital platforms, including text, images, videos, and audio (Santos, 2022).

Investigating these significant gaps not only holds important practical relevance, as illustrated above, but also builds the foundation for augmenting the selfpresentation theory to fit the context of social media users’ content sharing across multiple platforms. Prior literature suggests that social media users’ behaviors are inherently the outcomes of a self-presentation process (Sanaria, 2016). Self-presentation theory (Goffman, 1959) submits that individuals strive to present a positive self to others to gain positive impressions and build interpersonal relationships. This theory, however, is abstract and may appear as a “black box” unless the situation of social interactions is explicitly defined. This theory particularly underscores the importance of defining situations based on various contextual parameters such as time, space, and audience, among others (Goffman, 1959). Self-presentation behaviors may vary significantly depending on the situations of social interactions, such as interacting with strangers as opposed to friends. Consequently, the theory needs to be further specified to evaluate the nuanced effect of contextual parameters on different forms of selfpresentation behaviors.

Thus motivated, we follow the suggestions by Gopal et al. (2024) to perform theory specification to augment the depth of the self-presentation theory. Specifically, we identify content types and platform tie strength as two pivotal contextual factors that shape self-representation for content-sharing across multiple platforms. Exploring how these two factors collectively influence user content-sharing behaviors could shed new light on the underlying motivations and intricacies of contentsharing behaviors across social media platforms. In addition, we draw inspiration from Zor et al.’s recent investigation of the varying impacts of vice content for instant gratification and virtue content for long-term knowledge gains throughout the day (Zor et al., 2022). Our study seeks to scrutinize the diverging patterns of users’ sharing of vice vs. virtue content on social media platforms characterized by distinct user tie strengths. Grounded in the self-presentation theory, we posit that individuals leverage social media to satisfy diverse social needs for self-presentation. The nuanced image that individuals project on social media platforms becomes a critical consideration, as the attitudes of fellow users towards posted content significantly influence information-sharing decisions (Brough et al., 2020; Chakraborty et al., 2013). Although social media platforms provide a conducive environment for content sharing, disparities in technology and user relationships contribute to divergent user attitudes, ultimately resulting in distinct content-sharing patterns across platforms. Notably, our research conjectures that individuals strategically curate the content they share to present themselves positively to satisfy their intrinsic desires for respect from their weak ties and forge emotional connections with their strong ties.

In addition, following the theory problematization approach suggested by Alvesson and Sandberg (2011), we scrutinize and challenge an implicit assumption of extant literature on self-presentation behavior. Selfpresentation theory implicitly assumes that selfpresentation behaviors are inherently positive and supportive of interpersonal relationships. Yet, this perspective fails to account for conflicting individual behaviors in the existence of social exclusion. For example, social exclusion correlates positively with behaviors for elevating social interaction and repairing social relationships (e.g., higher frequency of uploading photos) (Lee et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024), but it may also lead to stagnation in positive self-presentation or even result in relationship-damaging behaviors (Debono et al., 2020). This demonstrates that social exclusion ushers in a contextual challenge to self-presentation theory. To address this limitation, it is important to adopt a more granular view of self-presentation behaviors and identify specific contextual conditions accounting for inconsistent self-representation behaviors. Therefore, we further introduce social exclusion as a moderator that influences the self-presentation process. We assert that social exclusion modifies the combined effects of platform tie strength and content type on contentsharing behaviors. Specifically, social media users may be less inclined to share vice content on strong-tie platforms when facing social exclusion. By incorporating social exclusion into the self-presentation framework, we aim to elucidate the potential boundaries of self-presentation theory and offer new insights into the contradictory self-presentation behaviors identified in the existing literature. As such, we propose three key research questions: (1) How do preferences of sharing vice versus virtue content vary across social media platforms with diverging tie strengths? (2) What are the underlying motives propelling the sharing of vice versus virtue content? (3) How does audience response in the form of social exclusion adjust social media users’ content-sharing preferences?

To rigorously address these research questions, our study unfolds through a comprehensive approach incorporating a field study across two distinct social media platforms and three prudently designed laboratory experiments. The empirical findings derived from the field study of WeChat and Weibo data illuminate evident content-sharing disparities (Study 1). To ensure the robustness of our findings and mitigate potential confounding factors such as platform technology and marketing methods, we conducted three comprehensive and meticulously designed laboratory experiments to further validate the findings from Study 1. In essence, Study 2 rigorously examined the impact of user tie strengths and content types on users’ likelihood of content sharing on social media platforms. Subsequently, Study 3 employed diverse approaches to validate the effects of tie strengths and content types on users’ sharing likelihood, while also unraveling mediation mechanisms. Finally, in Study 4, we delved into the moderating effects of social exclusion. This multifaceted approach allowed us to offer a detailed and nuanced understanding of the dynamics of content sharing on social media platforms.

This study makes significant contributions to selfpresentation theory and the literature pertaining to content sharing across social media platforms. Our study resonates with the recent call for additional research empirically validating and updating self-presentation theory in the context of social media (Hollenbaugh, 2021). It represents an early effort to contextualize and further extend self-presentation theory to gain a nuanced understanding of systematic disparities in content-sharing patterns across different social media platforms characterized by diverging tie strengths. Additionally, the rise of social media has led to a significant increase in online activities among individuals, which has, in turn, intensified the impact of online social exclusion (Greitemeyer et al., 2014). The design of social media platforms, particularly the prominence of the “like” feature, appears to play a crucial role in facilitating online social exclusion (Reich et al., 2018). When individuals experience social exclusion on a particular platform, they may switch to a different platform, reconstruct their social networks, and redefine their self-image. They may also refrain from self-presentation or adjust their selfpresentation by removing negative comments, sharing more positive content, or updating their profile pictures and bios to improve others’ perceptions of them. Online social exclusion offers a new perspective for studying the dynamic adjustments of self-presentation, revealing unique response mechanisms of individuals under virtual social pressure and deepening our understanding of the self-presentation process. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to explore complex self-presentation processes under the joint influence of tie strengths, content types, and audience response manifested in the form of social exclusion.

This study sheds new light on seemingly conflicting findings on content-sharing preferences with strong ties. Prior research suggests that individuals tend to share negative product experiences with their strong ties to strengthen their emotional bonds (Chen, 2017). However, our study on sharing vice and virtue digital media content reveals that people are more inclined to share vice or immediately gratifying content to emotionally connect with their strong ties (Hofmann et al., 2015). The relationship between the desire for emotional connection and the valence of user-shared content is complex and contingent on the context of content sharing (product experience vs. general media). Our study also has important practical implications for social media platforms, marketers, and individual users alike in terms of designing effective measures to nurture engaging user content.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 Self-Presentation Theory

Self-presentation theory (SPT), proposed by Goffman (1959), elucidates the mechanisms behind individuals shaping their expressions to project a desired image of themselves to others (Kim et al., 2012). This phenomenon, also referred to as impression management, consists of behaviors designed to deliver the desired image to others (Leary & Kowalski, 1990). It is goal-directed and performed for different motives, such as the desire to be seen as confident, competent, and likable (Berscheid & Hatfield., 1969). Recognized as an essential element of social interaction, self-presentation is instrumental in the establishment and sustenance of interpersonal connections (Ellison et al., 2007; Hudson et al., 2016) and the cultivation of social support (Krasnova et al., 2010).

Social media platforms are inherently driven by the fundamental objectives of self-presentation and impression formation, compelling users to engage with the aim of showcasing facets of their identity (Chua & Chang, 2016). Consequently, the curation of content on social media becomes a deliberate process wherein individuals sift through their posts to align with the objective of presenting themselves in a positive light (Sanaria, 2016). The array of tools employed for selfpresentation on social media is diverse, encompassing both direct textual communication and indirect expressions such as retweeting web links, sharing images, and utilizing animations (De Angelis et al., 2012). While considerable research has explored the ways in which social media is utilized and its psychological impact on users (Karahanna et al., 2018), there exists a dearth of studies investigating the sharing of specific types of content across social media platforms with varying tie strengths.

In essence, SPT underscores the role of contextual factors in driving one’s self-representation behaviors such that people tend to align their projection of self with the contextual definition of a given situation (Goffman, 1959). An individual first projects a definition of the situation before consciously and/or unconsciously presenting self to be a person of their desire in that situation (Leary & Allen, 2011). For technology-mediated social interaction, the interplay of social and technological characteristics is crucial for defining the situation of self-presentation and influencing the subsequent self-presentation processes (Hollenbaugh, 2021). In this study, we argue that the varying tie strengths enabled by different social media platforms/channels stand out as a salient social and technological parameter delineating the situations for self-presentation, which further leads to diverging motives and content-sharing behaviors.

Furthermore, SPT also notes the importance of audience response in the process of self-presentation, positing that individuals inherently expect the audience to voluntarily accord value and treatment congruent with the desired impression they seek to project (Goffman, 1959). The alignment of others’ responses with one’s expectations serves as a pragmatic indicator that the desired image has been effectively conveyed (Hepper et al., 2011). Such an alignment, to a certain extent, gauges the performance or success of one’s self-presentation, propelling individuals to actively seek to shape or monitor others’ perceptions by meticulously controlling and regulating information in their social interactions (Jones & Pittman, 1982). In this study, we incorporated social exclusion in the form of few or zero likes on a post into our research model to capture the influence of audience or receiver response on one’s selfrepresentation behaviors.

To better understand how and why individuals strategically adjust the types of content they share across social media platforms varying in tie strengths (e.g., weak ties versus strong ties), we extend and contextualize SPT by following leading guidelines for contextualizing theory (Hong et al., 2014; Johns, 2006, 2017). Specifically, we augment and contribute to SPT by introducing context-specific antecedents. Our study also advances SPT by incorporating social exclusion as a moderator that adjusts the core of SPT. Social media users are likely to alter their self-presentation processes in response to social exclusion that deviates from their expected audience responses. In the realm of online social exclusion, individuals might experience feelings of ostracism or isolation, leading them to tailor their online behavior to mitigate these adverse effects (Rawat et al., 2022). This could involve strategies such as refraining from posting certain content, seeking validation through alternative means, or withdrawing from online interactions altogether. Thus, understanding the dynamics of online social exclusion is crucial to comprehensively unveil the complexities of selfpresentation and social interaction in digital environments. Our extension of SPT provides novel insights into the boundary conditions for SPT to take effect, revealing how social exclusion may lead individuals to alter their self-presentation strategies and content-sharing behaviors. ## 2.2 User Tie Strengths Across Social Media Platforms

According to Granovetter’s (1973) definition, tie strengths represent the amalgamation of time invested, emotional intensity, and closeness, reflecting the depth of an individual’s involvement in social relationships, typically categorized into strong and weak ties. Strong ties encapsulate relationships between individuals who know each other well, trust each other, have close emotional bonds, and regularly interact, while weak ties signify connections between less closely affiliated individuals, such as casual acquaintances or sporadic interactions with strangers (McPherson et al., 2001; Ryu & Feick, 2007). Strong and weak ties carry important but distinct communication benefits such that strong ties are more effective in providing social support and promoting emotional well-being (Thoits, 2011), while weak ties prevail in providing useful and novel information through bridging different social circles (Lee & Kronrod, 2020). Research indicates that tie strengths between the message recipient and the communicator play a critical role in shaping users sharing behaviors on social media platforms (Spiliotopoulos & Oakley, 2021).

Besides tie strength, early research has also considered the direction of social ties and attempts to categorize social media platforms into unidirectional and bidirectional platforms (Shi et al., 2014). Unidirectional platforms (e.g., YouTube and Instagram) allow users to post content but generally lack direct user interaction. In contrast, bidirectional platforms (e.g., Facebook and X) support richer interaction modes for enhancing platform stickiness and commercial value. Currently, many unidirectional platforms are gradually transitioning to bidirectional platforms to facilitate interaction among individuals and gain increased commercial value. As a result, our research focuses on bidirectional platforms. We further extend the literature on bidirectional platforms to a multiplatform context, as it has become prevalent for a single user to own multiple social media accounts and share content on multiple social media platforms. Considering the significant impact of usershared content (USC) on user engagement, brand awareness, and digital marketing strategies (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Aral et al., 2013), it is crucial to investigate user content-sharing behaviors across different platforms.

Different social media platforms have distinct design features, user demographics, and types of relationships (Karahanna et al., 2018; O’Day & Heimberg, 2021). For example, the design of Facebook and WeChat centers on personal communication with friends and family members. The messages are largely directed to specific individuals or shared within personal circles. Facebook and WeChat also impose minimum restrictions on media length and media formats, including text, photos, and videos. These design features enable deeper engagement with the audience. In contrast, platforms like X, Weibo, TikTok, and YouTube are designed to connect message senders to a wide audience beyond those closely related ones by promoting high content visibility. These four platforms further differ in the targeted audience, media format, and length. For example, X allows short posts consisting of mini-videos and text of up to 280 characters, thereby largely enabling superficial engagement with weak ties. The main appeal of X is to disseminate realtime information/news, and it is often used as a news channel by young people to get quick updates about local and national issues (Brown et al., 2014).

Although both strong-tie and weak-tie relationships exist on all social media platforms, design differences could result in divergent content visibility and varying concentrations of weak-tie and strong-tie relationships across different platforms. For instance, the design of WeChat’s semi-private personal page (Moments) has low content visibility, which is accessible only to friends. WeChat’s design emphasizes private, small-scale interactions, which helps foster intimacy and exclusivity and gives rise to the main appeal among strong ties. Conversely, Weibo’s pages are publicly visible to everyone. Weibo’s design emphasizes openness, which promotes broader dissemination and information transparency and leads to a higher concentration of weak ties. As such, this study classifies social media platforms into strong-tie and weak-tie categories based on the level of content visibility enabled by the technological design of different platforms. Strong-tie platforms (e.g., Facebook and WeChat) usually restrict content visibility to friends, encouraging the sharing of private and personalized content such as personal experiences, emotions, and deep insights. Conversely, weak-tie platforms (e.g., X and Weibo) have few content visibility restrictions, promoting widespread dissemination and encouraging interaction with a broader audience by sharing news, opinions, and public information. This platform categorization formed the foundation for us to investigate the nuanced differences in user-sharing behavior, shedding new light on how tie strength dynamics across social media platforms contribute to divergent content-sharing patterns.

Until now, the existing literature has mainly examined the effect of tie strength on content sharing within a single platform (Zhao et al., 2012). There remains a lack of research on how users share content across platforms with different tie strengths. This study aims to fill this gap, focusing on how the overall tie strength at the platform level and content type influence user-sharing behaviors in a multiplatform context. Following the tenets of self-presentation theory (SPT), we posit that varying tie strengths across social media platforms are crucial in defining self-presentation contexts and subsequently influencing self-presentation behaviors in the form of content sharing.

## 2.3 Vice and Virtue Content Types

With the maturation of social media functionalities and the widespread embrace of crowdsourcing, individuals are increasingly inclined to share diverse aspects of their lives, work, and emotions online (Daugherty et al., 2008). Recent research has explored content shared on social media with different emphases, including brand-related content (Roma & Aloini, 2019), electronic word-ofmouth (Hennig-Thurau et al., 2004), content valence (Keller et al., 2017), and content form (Fardouly & Holland, 2018). Further, Stieglitz and Dang-Xuan (2013) found that highly cognitive (e.g., insightful, reasoning) information garners elevated engagement due to increased cognitive involvement, while Berger and Milkman (2012) proposed that emotionally arousing online content leads to more sharing. Akpinar and Berger (2017) discovered that emotionally charged content tends to spread more easily. These studies utilized methods of data scraping from social media platforms and identified content using scale scores provided by the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) program with the LIWC2015 dictionary. However, due to the use of archival data, these studies may be subject to potential confounding factors, making it challenging for social media companies to directly apply these findings, thereby limiting their practical applicability.

Previous research on the specific types of user-shared content on social media platforms is relatively limited. Building on earlier studies that correlated content features with user engagement and virality (Stieglitz & Dang-Xuan, 2013; Berger & Milkman, 2012), Zor et al. (2022) coined a distinctive categorization—vice and virtue—to systematically investigate social media content. This approach was validated through Twitter data and laboratory experiments, culminating in the proposition of a scale for content characterization. Notably, the vice and virtue dichotomy, often explored in the context of food choices (Chernev, 2011; Mishra & Mishra, 2011), is typically defined in relative terms on a vice-virtue continuum since, in reality, content shared by individuals tends to be complex and diverse. A vice can provide both long-term utility and short-term pleasure, and the same can be true for a virtue. However, vice and virtue differ in the extent of pleasure versus utilities offered across time frames. On a vice-virtue continuum, a relative vice offers more immediate pleasure in the short term or at the moment of consumption, whereas a relative virtue provides benefits in the long term that are mostly future-oriented (Zor et al., 2022).

Thus far, extant research on vice and virtue is mostly about food products (Chernev, 2011). The topic of vice vs. virtue has received sparse attention in other areas, such as media content consumption (Zor et al., 2022). For example, Oster and Scott Morton (2005) classified magazines like Forbes and The Nation as “investment magazines,” or virtue content offering future benefits. In contrast, they categorized Entertainment Weekly and People as “leisure magazines,” or vice content providing immediate enjoyment (Oster & Scott Morton, 2005). Zor et al. (2022) confirmed that social media content in the form of tweets could be categorized as vice vs. virtue, showing that the consumption of these two types of content could vary with the time of the day. Given its close relation to user engagement and its potential to enhance the effectiveness of digital marketing, this study seeks to embrace the vice and virtue dichotomy proposed by Zor et al. (2022) to further investigate the sharing of social media content in a multiplatform context.

Analogous to the immediate pleasure offered by a delicious dessert versus the long-term benefits of a bland salad, social media content could be classified as vice that provides immediate gratification or virtue content that offers future benefits (Khan et al., 2005). To unveil the underlying mechanisms for vice vs. virtue contents to exert their influence, we further draw upon selfpresentation theory and argue that individuals strategically adjust their behaviors based on the context to achieve their goals of self-presentation (Jensen Schau & Gilly, 2003). Particularly, the types of user-shared content (vice vs. virtue) together with platform tie strength are important contextual factors that influence social-media users’ self-presentation behaviors. Individuals can exhibit various self-presentation behaviors on social media (Hollenbaugh, 2021), such as sharing content with peers, following an account, clicking “like,” and commenting on existing posts. In this study, we posit that sharing vice vs. virtue content on social media platforms is one type of self-presentation behavior. We aim to investigate potential systematic differences in sharing vice vs. virtue content under the influence of platform tie strengths and social exclusion. This inquiry holds substantial significance as it delves into the intricate interplay between content types and the dynamics of social ties in the digital paradigm.

## 3 Hypothesis Development and Research Model

## 3.1 Interaction of Content and Social Media User Tie Strengths

Social acceptance, a fundamental human need for successful relationships (Baumeister & Leary, 1995), undergoes distinct phases in interpersonal relationships, primarily marked by formation and maintenance (Clark & Beck, 2011). During the formation stage, when social interaction occurs among unfamiliar weak ties, potential partners assess each other’s worthiness (Clark & Beck, 2011). Subsequently, as relationships progress into the maintenance stage, individuals navigate the complexities of sustaining social connections with familiar ties (Collins & Miller, 1994). Based on the depth of relationships, the strong-tie and weak-tie social media platforms discussed above correspond to the formation and maintenance phases of social acceptance, respectively.

According to SPT, self-presentation behaviors are performed to achieve different motives, which tend to align with the definitions of specific situations (Goffman, 1959). The evolving phases of social acceptance from formation to maintenance, along with related tie strength characteristics, are important parameters defining selfpresentation situations such that individuals could have distinct self-presentation motives and behaviors under the influence of the varying tie strengths of the two phases (Clark & Beck, 2011). In the paradigm of social media, content sharing serves as a primary means of selfpresentation for building and maintaining relationships, as individuals infer their self-concept from the choices they make (Dunning, 2007). We posit that variances in content-sharing motives and behaviors across different social media platforms are intricately tied to the strength of connections between users on social media platforms.

Weak-tie platforms are characterized by high content visibility; user-shared content is public or accessible to all. Users can rapidly disseminate information to a wider, unfamiliar, or weak-tie audience. To build relationships with weak ties, individuals tend to share virtue content that helps showcase their abilities or values (Wojnicki & Godes, 2017). Virtue content encompasses future valuable knowledge (Khan & Dhar, 2007) and demands cognitive resources for deep learning (Rocklage et al., 2021), which aligns closely with the sharer’s competence. Therefore, on social media platforms dominated by weak ties, people are more inclined to share competence-related virtue content that fosters relationship building rather than vice content geared towards relationship maintenance. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: On weak-tie social media platforms, individual users are more likely to share virtue content than vice content.

Strong-tie platforms have low content visibility that is characterized by more intimate and personalized communication through text, voice, images, and videos. These platforms prioritize interaction and socialization among acquaintances and friends. On strong-tie platforms, individuals primarily focus on maintaining relationships to foster social acceptance with close friends and family members. During the relationship maintenance phase, individuals become less self-promotional in front of friends who are already acquainted with their strengths (Khan et al., 2005). Instead, the focus of self-presentation shifts towards nurturing emotional connection rather than pursuing ability-related goals (Sezer et al., 2018). It is important to recognize that, while uncommon, authority figures such as leaders and teachers may also become friends or acquaintances with an individual when explicitly invited to join an individual’s social network on strong-tie platforms. The finding of Khan et al. (2005) regarding the diminished tendency for self-promoting in front of friends is likely to extend to these authority figures as well, once a friendship is established, as they become familiar with the individual’s strength. Therefore, this study does not treat these authority figures as a separate contextual parameter that affects individual contentsharing behavior on strong-tie social media platforms.

The emphasis on fostering emotional connections on strong-tie platforms may manifest in the content-sharing preferences of social media users. Prior research suggests that vice content is associated with short-term pleasure (Golman & Loewenstein, 2015) and heightened warmth levels (Peter & Ponzi, 2018), making it align well with the self-presentation motive to elicit emotional connections (Chen, 2017). Warm content often increases the attraction or likeability of content sharers (Wojciszke et al., 2009). Consequently, people tend to share vice content that provides immediate gratification with acquaintances, rather than virtue content associated with competence. Therefore, we propose the second hypothesis:

H2: On strong-tie social media platforms, individual users are more likely to share vice content than virtue content.

## 3.2 Mediation Mechanisms

The self-presentation desire for positive social perceptions significantly influences human behavior in social interactions (Leary & Kowalski, 1990), as social and material rewards, such as social approval, friendships, and career advancement, hinge on the impressions made on others (Baumeister & Leary, 1995). Self-presentation, integral to social interactions, stems from two primary motives: the desire to be liked and gain favorability (Baumeister & Leary, 1995), and the desire to convey competence and garner respect (Bergsieker et al., 2010). In the tapestry of everyday life, these motives coexist (Godfrey et al., 1986), resembling a seesaw where likes and respect tend to be high at one end or the other but seldom both high simultaneously (Fiske et al., 2002).

In the pursuit of respect, individuals may seek to employ strategies to demonstrate their competence (Godfrey et al., 1986), often engaging in self-promotion by highlighting achievements, successes, and unique attributes to draw attention to their superior qualities (Fiske et al., 2002). This self-enhancing motivation is particularly prominent when communicating with strangers, serving as a signal of competence to others (Baumeister & Jones, 1978). Virtue content, requiring deep learning and strong cognitive and informational exposition abilities, is perceived as a manifestation of competence (Kervyn et al., 2012). Thus, the sharing of virtue content on weak-tie social platforms is propelled by the desire to be respected. So, we propose our third hypothesis:

## H3: The desire to be respected mediates the likelihood of users sharing virtue content on social media platforms with weak ties.

Conversely, to fulfill the desire to be liked, individuals often employ strategies centered around others, such as offering help or expressing agreement with others, to garner favor (Wayne & Liden, 1995). In the pursuit of likability, individuals may deploy “other-focused” strategies, categorized as ingratiation, including offering praise, help, obedience to opinions, and indirect selfdescriptions of attribution to achievements to others (Jones & Pittman, 1982). People tend to be more “othercentered” in interactions with close friends (Hays, 1989), aligning with the motivation to be liked. Therefore, individuals are less likely to boast and self-promote to friends who are already aware of their strengths, as emphasizing positive traits can lead observers to perceive them as competent but less likable (Carlston & Shovar, 1983). Vice content, providing short-term pleasure and fostering discussion among consumers, conveys a level of intimacy that generates positive feelings. Thus, to be liked by others, individuals are more inclined to share vice content on social media platforms with strong ties. Thus, we propose our fourth hypothesis:

H4: The desire to be liked mediates the likelihood of the user sharing vice content on strong-tie social media platforms.

## 3.3 The Moderating Role of Social Exclusion

Social exclusion is defined as experiencing rejection or neglect in interpersonal relationships, posing a serious threat to fundamental and universal social interaction needs in humans (Lee & Shrum, 2012). Humans are social beings, and social connections are crucial for our psychological well-being and overall happiness (Seppala et al., 2013). When these basic social needs are unmet, it can lead to a range of negative consequences (Sheldon & Gunz, 2009). Research indicates that unmet social interaction needs can result in emotional distress, feelings of loneliness, increased stress, and various health issues (Verhagen et al., 2018). Online exclusion has similar psychological and emotional impacts as face-to-face exclusion, despite occurring in different environments, yet the outcomes are equally severe (Schneider et al., 2017). Online exclusion has been extensively studied and has been found to significantly jeopardize individuals’ sense of belonging and selfesteem (Schneider et al., 2017). Reich et al. (2018) surveyed social media likes and found that receiving few or no likes leads to a significant decrease in both belongingness and self-esteem. This negative impact is particularly pronounced for those who rely on social media for self-expression and social support.

In the self-presentation process, people expect the audience to respond in ways that align with the desired self-projection (Goffman, 1959). To capture the effect of the audience response, we include social exclusion in the form of few or zero likes on a post since social exclusion signals a violation of the user’s expectation for an appropriate response to their self-presentation (Reich et al., 2018). Social media users interpret such expectation violations in relation to the type of relationship since people have different expectations from their strong and weak ties. Individuals anticipate more positive and meaningful feedback from strong ties (Brown & Reingen, 1987). For instance, on social media, they expect likes and positive comments from close friends or family, as these responses contribute significantly to their self-worth and sense of belonging. In contrast, due to the typically superficial nature of weak-tie interactions, expectations for positive feedback tend to be lower from weak ties. Therefore, social media users may view social exclusion more negatively when they have close ties with the violators (Hayes et al., 2018). Research by Bevan et al. (2012) suggests that being unfriended on Facebook can evoke stronger negative feelings when it involves a close relationship (Bevan et al., 2012). Consequently, social exclusion by a strong-tie audience is posited to have a greater impact on social media users’ information-sharing preferences than that by a weak-tie audience. We argue that, in response to social exclusion, individuals are inclined to reduce the likelihood of sharing vice content on strongtie social media platforms, while the sharing behavior on weak-tie platforms remains unaffected. Therefore, we propose our fifth hypothesis:

H5: After experiencing social exclusion, the likelihood of sharing vice content is diminished on strong-tie platforms, while there is no effect on the likelihood of sharing on weak-tie platforms.

The theoretical model in Figure 1 delineates the interplay of tie strength, content type, and social exclusion on users’ likelihood to share content on social media platforms. To scientifically capture and gauge these intricate relationships, we propose employing four studies, comprising one analysis of streaming WeChat and Weibo data and three experimental investigations, to systematically test and validate the hypotheses. Study 1 evaluates the number of shares of vice and virtue

magazines on WeChat and Weibo platforms, serving as an initial test for H1 and H2. Study 2, an experimental investigation, delves into the impact of platform tie strength (WeChat vs. Weibo) and content on users’ likelihood to share, providing further verification for H1 and H2. Study 3 employs a virtual platform and varied materials to investigate the mediating effects of the desire to be liked and the desire to be respected, bolstering the robustness of the findings from Studies 1 and 2 and offering strong support for H3 and H4. Finally, Study 4 examines the moderation effect of social exclusion (H5). Through these studies, our research seeks to contribute nuanced insights into the dynamics of content sharing on social media platforms, further advancing our understanding of the complex interplay between tie strength, content type, and social exclusion.

## 4.3 Key Findings from Study 1

While the volume of content varied between the two platforms (with more content on WeChat than on Weibo), such discrepancies could stem from technological disparities or diverse marketing strategies. Nevertheless, our findings reveal a distinct pattern: vice magazines are more frequently shared on strong-tie platforms, whereas on weak-tie platforms, virtue magazines accumulate more shares than their vice counterparts. However, it is plausible that one might question whether our results were influenced by other factors. First, the distinct technology of each platform might lead to variations in the form and content shared by users. Second, divergent marketing strategies could result in varying degrees of content referrals by magazine companies. Emphasizing the importance of the “Share” button, which can be utilized either to share content with someone directly or to one’s personal homepage, our study specifically concentrates on the likelihood of users sharing content to their personal homepage. To fortify the robustness of our conclusions, conducted a follow-up investigation in Study 2, utilizing real social media platforms (i.e., WeChat/Weibo) in a controlled laboratory setting.

## 6.3 Key Findings from Study 3

The results of Study 3 reveal that participants demonstrated a higher likelihood of sharing content in strong-tie conditions when the content was categorized as vice. Conversely, for virtue content, participants displayed a higher likelihood of sharing virtue content in weak-tie conditions. These outcomes align with the expectations outlined in H1 and H2.

To delve deeper into the underlying mechanisms, we further investigated the mediating roles of the desire to be respected and the desire to be liked. The findings indicate that for the sharing of virtue content in weaktie scenarios, the desire to be respected mediated the impact of tie strength on the likelihood of content sharing, supporting H3. Similarly, for the sharing of vice content in strong-tie scenarios, the desire to be liked mediated the effect of tie strength on the likelihood of content sharing, thus supporting H4.

Notably, Study 3 enhanced external validity by employing different manipulations and measures for the independent and dependent variables, bolstering the robustness and generalizability of the study’s findings.

## 7 Study 4: The Moderating Role of Social Exclusion on Content Sharing

Expanding on the insights gained from the first three studies, the objective of Study 4 was to delve into the ramifications of social exclusion across different tie strengths concerning individuals’ decisions to share content. Building upon the foundations laid by Study 2, Study 4 employed identical materials.

Drawing on prior research by Vorderer and Schneider (2016), which suggested that social exclusion can be manifested through social media interactions, we note that individuals who receive comments on their Facebook updates tend to experience a heightened sense of belonging compared to those who do not receive comments (Tobin et al., 2015). In the same vein, Reich et al. (2018) demonstrated that “likes” from close friends have a more substantial impact on feelings of belonging and self-esteem than “likes” from acquaintances. Based on these insights, we hypothesized that the likelihood of sharing vice content would decrease when encountering social exclusion in strong-tie scenarios, while no such effect would be observed in weak-tie situations. To scrutinize this prediction, we conducted an examination of social media users’ “likes.”

## 7.1 Method

Utilizing the same materials as in Study 2, we employed videos featuring robot dogs, guided by the methodologies presented by Reich et al. (2018) and Wolf et al. (2015). Following Reich et al. (2018), we operationalized the social exclusion construct by varying the number of likes with 2 likes and 30 likes corresponding to social exclusion and social inclusion conditions, respectively. A total of 432 participants were recruited by Sojump. After excluding 70 participants who used only one platform, 362 participants successfully completed the experiment $( M _ { \mathrm { a g e } } = 3 1 . 7 7 , S D = 6 . 6 4 4 ,$ 59.7% female). Study 4 implemented a 3 (social exclusion: exclusion vs. inclusion vs. control) × 2 (platform user tie strength: strong vs. weak) × 2 (content type: vice vs. virtue) two-factor between-subjects design, where social exclusion and platform tie strength were the between-subjects factors for predicting subjects’ content-sharing choices between vice and virtue content types. Therefore, the design had a total of six between-subjects treatment groups, i.e., 3 (social exclusion: exclusion vs. inclusion vs. control) $\times \_ 2$ (platform user tie strength: strong vs. weak). Participants were randomly assigned to one of six groups. Then, within each of the six treatment groups, participants were required to watch two videos (i.e., vice and virtue) sequentially. Additionally, to mitigate order effects and ensure that the results were not influenced by the sequence of material presentation, each participant watched the two videos in a randomized order such that about 50% percent watched the vice video before the virtue video, while the other half watched the virtue video before the vice video. The randomized video presentation method was used not only to control for the potential confounding effect due to video viewing order but also to enhance the validity and reliability of the study findings.

In the first section of the study, we asked participants how frequently they updated their status on social media platforms, how much time they spent on the platforms, and how many friends they had on the platforms. We then presented participants with introductory materials (refer to Appendix C) concerning WeChat and Weibo. After reviewing the definition of tie strength used in the other studies, participants were asked to respond to the manipulation question concerning tie strength.

The second section of the study asked participants to envision the following scenarios: “You published new content on a WeChat friend circle/Weibo personal page today and you received two likes two hours later” (social exclusion); “You published new content on a WeChat friend circle/Weibo personal page today and you received thirty likes two hours later” (social inclusion). In the control condition, participants proceeded directly to the third section of the study. After encountering the social exclusion manipulation (see Appendix C), the participants were asked to complete the Needs Satisfaction Scale. To assess the soundness of our manipulation, participants were asked to rate the degree of interest that others had in their posts (7-point scale: 1 = very uninterested and 7 = very interested).

In the third section of the study, participants responded to demographic questions and viewed the vice video and virtue video. They were then asked which video they would share on which platform (WeChat/Weibo). Finally, they answered questions regarding their desire to be liked and their desire to be respected.

## 7.3 Key Findings from Study 4

The results of Study 4 support H5, addressing the moderating effect of social exclusion, and confirm that social exclusion significantly moderated the differences in the sharing of different types of content across platforms with varying relationship strengths. Specifically, when experiencing social exclusion, the facilitating effect on the likelihood of sharing vice content with strong-tie relationships was diminished. However, there was no impact on the likelihood of sharing virtue content with weak-tie relationships.

## 8 Discussion and Conclusion

This study delved into the dynamics of consumer behavior in sharing content, specifically exploring the distinction between sharing “vice” content that provides immediate gratification and “virtue” content associated with long-term knowledge gains. Our investigation unfolded across two social media platforms characterized by different user tie strengths. Through a field study and three lab experiments, we unveiled a discernible pattern: Users tend to share vice content on platforms with strong ties and virtue content on platforms with weak ties.

This behavioral pattern is intricately tied to the psychological motivations of users. On strong-tie platforms, the desire to be liked motivates users to share vice content, providing instant gratification. Conversely, on weak-tie platforms, the desire to be respected motivates users to share virtue content, aligning with their pursuit of long-term knowledge gains.

The validity of our findings was substantiated through a comprehensive approach, encompassing a field study and three laboratory experiments. We strategically manipulated both social media tie strength and content type to ensure the robustness and reliability of our results. This multifaceted methodology enhances the depth and credibility of our research hypotheses, shedding new light on the nuanced dynamics of content sharing across diverse social media environments.

## 8.1 Implications for Theory

Our study, focusing on content sharing and its management in the realm of social relationships, provides several key theoretical contributions. First, resonating with the research call for context-specific approaches (Hong et al., 2014; Karjalainen et al., 2019), our study extends and enriches the self-presentation theory (SPT) in the context of multiplatform content sharing. In particular, our study identifies platform tie strength and vice/virtue content types as two context-specific antecedents to individuals’ self-presentation behaviors. Our study also empirically validates content sharing as an important form of context-specific self-representation behavior for individuals to achieve social acceptance. More importantly, our study extends SPT by incorporating online social exclusion as a moderator that adjusts the self-presentation processes. Our findings on social exclusion not only unveil the complexity in individuals’ self-presentation processes but also provide novel insights into the boundary conditions for SPT to take effect. As social exclusion indicates unexpected neglect or ostracism from other social media users or unmet needs for social interaction, it could disrupt individuals’ attempts to align their self-projection with the context— for example, through reduced sharing of vice content on strong-tie platforms, as found in our study. Our extended SPT research model lays a foundation for future social media studies to investigate intricate self-presentation processes and potential unintended user behaviors such as reduced content sharing or platform switching.

Second, we enhance the understanding of selfpresentation processes through content sharing on social media platforms. Existing research on self-presentation strategies has primarily explored aspects such as likability or respectability (Rosenfeld et al., 1995), with scant exploration of their impact on the likelihood of sharing content. Our study establishes that individuals information-sharing tendencies are fundamentally influenced by the recipient’s self-presentation motives, specifically the desire to be liked or respected. This nuanced exploration is substantiated through data from real-world social media platforms and supplementary laboratory evidence, offering insights into sharing disparities across platforms of varying tie strength.

Third, our study provides a comprehensive and expansive framework that elucidates the motivations behind consumer sharing and how relationships are perceived across a spectrum. While previous studies have initiated investigations into the motivations behind content sharing, they often focus on a singular social media platform. Our framework transcends these limitations by encompassing both strong-tie and weak-tie platforms, generating unique predictions that extend beyond existing models. Contrary to the prevailing assumption that users are more willing to share with those in strong-tie relationships (Frenzen & Nakamoto , 1993), our findings highlight the complexity of sharing dynamics, emphasizing the impact of userperceived relationship strength on social media platforms and content types. Study 3, utilizing virtual platforms, reinforces the robustness of our results, guiding future research on user relationship manipulation or measurement on social media platforms.

Finally, we address the challenges posed by the sheer volume of content by exploring the sharing possibilities of specific content categories—vice vs. virtue content. This complements existing research on viral content in social media, which has predominantly focused on higharousal or highly cognitive content (Toubia & Stephen, 2013). Our study pioneers the evaluation of specific content types (vice vs. virtue), opening avenues for future investigations into the dynamics of content virality.

## 8.2 Implications for Practice

Beyond the theoretical contributions, our work provides actionable insights for social media companies to design effective measures to nurture user-shared content and for businesses to make better digital marketing decisions.

First, the findings of our study reveal that the alignment between strong-tie (weak-tie) platforms and vice (virtue) content is associated with elevated user sharing behaviors across social media platforms, regardless of whether it is WeChat vs. Weibo or other platforms like Facebook and X, because tie strength is a property inherent to all social media platforms. It is critical for social media companies to consider such alignment when developing features aimed at enhancing user engagement in content sharing. For example, strong-tie platforms such as Facebook or WeChat could implement machine-learning algorithms to identify vice posts and encourage the corresponding post creators to share them. Additionally, strong-tie platforms could also introduce a “boost” button for vice posts, further augmenting the users’ willingness to share content. Weak-tie platform companies, on the other hand, could adopt a similar approach to identify and facilitate users’ engagement in sharing virtue content.

Second, the alignment between platform tie strength and content type also carries important implications for businesses seeking to improve the design of their advertisements and increase the effectiveness of their digital marketing efforts. Marketers could consider matching the design of their advertisements with platform tie strength. For instance, content types such as entertainment, gossip, and humor are particularly suitable for strong-tie platforms. Advertisements tailored to such strong-tie platforms should aim to provide immediate enjoyment for the audience through engaging content, thereby increasing their likelihood of being shared and reaching a broader audience. Conversely, weak-tie platforms (e.g., X) are better suited for virtue content related to technology or scientific knowledge, which needs to be reflected in the design of advertisements on weak-tie platforms. Understanding these nuances can empower marketers to tailor content strategies more effectively.

Third, marketers should pay close attention to user feedback, such as likes and comments. Our study unveils the impact of online social exclusion (Study 4). To mitigate this impact on user content sharing, user enhancement services, such as providing increased exposure or the option to hide likes, could be incorporated into strong-tie social media platforms.

Finally, brands are encouraged to design diverse advertising and promotional programs tailored to social media platforms with different user tie strengths. Virtue content, such as corporate social responsibility initiatives and product patent knowledge, aligns well with weak-tie platforms. Conversely, vice content, like enticing flash discounts or charming brand spokesperson activities, is more suited for strong-tie platforms.

## 8.3 Limitations and Future Research

While our study combined a field study and three laboratory experiments, future research could extend our framework by examining user-generated content shared by other consumers. Exploring how brand-related usergenerated content varies across different social platforms or investigating the sharing dynamics of high-arousal and low-arousal content across platforms could further validate our theoretical framework.

Additionally, this study primarily focused on the tie strength among users across different platforms and content types in relation to the likelihood of sharing content. Future investigations could explore other potential influences on content sharing, such as variations in posting frequency driven by individual personality traits. Cultural backgrounds may also play a significant role, with Western cultures emphasizing positive self-expression and individualism and Eastern cultures prioritizing modesty (Lim, 2016).

Moreover, personality traits could be significant influencers of content-sharing behaviors. For instance, individuals with extroverted traits may tend to share personal experiences and opinions on social media more frequently due to their higher need for social interaction and feedback from others. In contrast, introverted individuals may be more cautious, selectively posting content to avoid potential negative evaluations. Future studies could also examine the impact of other demographic variables on sharing behaviors, such as gender, age, education level, and geographical location.

Furthermore, this study probed the moderating effects of contextual factors, particularly the impact of online social exclusion. Our research only investigated one aspect of online social exclusion in the form of the number of likes. Future research could delve deeper into the influence of other types of social exclusion resulting from explicit actions (e.g., negative comments) on content sharing. Understanding how negative comments, whether from strangers or acquaintances, affect sharing tendencies post-exclusion could provide valuable insights. Moreover, our study only examined reduced posting frequency as an outcome of social exclusion. It would also be interesting for future research to explore other potential outcomes, such as ceasing to post or platform switching.

Lastly, our study only examined two specific types of user-shared content (vice vs. virtue). Future studies could further inspect whether and how other forms of user-shared content may explain the differences in content-sharing behavior in a multiplatform context. For example, prior research has noted that verbally shared content tends to be more immediate and emotional, whereas written or textual content is delayed but more rational (Berger & Iyengar, 2013). It would be interesting for future research to explore the effect of voice vs. textual content on cross-platform sharing behaviors.

In essence, our current research reveals the nuanced ways in which individuals share different content on various social media platforms with diverging degrees of tie-strength to meet their self-presentation needs. As the landscape of online user-shared content continues to evolve, organizations must comprehend the dynamic nature of effective value co-creation in social media marketing.

## Appendix D: Alternative Content-Sharing Motivations

Beyond the motivations of being liked and respected, individuals may also share content for other purposes. For example, some people may share vice content to fortify their emotional connection with their friends and family members. Additionally, prior research has attempted to interpret self-presentation as a process of self-enhancement to present a better self (Scott, 2011). The self-enhancement motive may be particularly relevant for social interactions among weak ties where potential partners assess each other’s worthiness (Clark & Beck, 2011). Thus, the motive for self-enhancement may be an alternative reason underlying social media users’ inclination to share virtue content on weak-tie platforms. Therefore, in this appendix, we empirically tested two alternative motivations for sharing content across social media platforms: (1) sharing vice content on strong tie platforms for emotional connection and (2) sharing virtue content on weak tie platforms for self-improvement. We controlled for demographic variables that could confound our results, including age, gender, income, and educational background.

## Method

A total of 100 participants were recruited by Sojump, with 98 participants successfully completing the experiment $( M _ { \mathrm { a g e } } = 3 2 . 7 6$ , SD = 6.844, 54.1% female). The procedure is consistent with Study 3. Items measuring selfenhancement and emotional connection were adapted from Chen (2017). Self-enhancement was measured using three items (a = 0.81) using 7-point scales: “How do you think that talking about your movie watching experience will change people’s impression of you?” (1 = negatively, 7 = positively); “Do you think people will view you more or less favorably because you shared your thoughts about the movie watching experience?” (1 = less favorably, 7 = more favorably); and “Do you think people will like or dislike you because you shared your thoughts about the movie watching experience?” (1 = dislike, 7 = like). We measured emotional connection using one item: “How connected would you feel to the audience as a result of sharing your movie-watching experience?” (1 = not at all, 7 = very much).

## Data Analysis Results

Likelihood to share: We conducted a 2 (tie strength) × 2 (content type) ANCOVA analysis with likelihood to share as the dependent variable, controlling for consumer age, gender, income, and educational background. The results showed no main effects or interactions (p > 0.05) for these variables.

Moderated mediation: A moderated mediation analysis (Hayes, 2017, Model 8) with 5000 bootstrapped samples reveals that neither emotional connection nor self-enhancement served as moderators in the mediation process. Specifically, emotional connection did not mediate the interaction effect of tie strength and content type on the likelihood to share (indirect effect = -0.03, Boot SE = 0.07, CI = [-0.22, 0.19]). Similarly, self-enhancement also failed to mediate this interaction effect (indirect effect = 0.06, Boot SE = 0.012, CI = [-0.12, 0.32]).

Therefore, our results suggest that emotional connections and self-enhancement have insignificant mediating effects on consumers’ likelihood to share vice vs. virtue content on platforms of varying tie strength. This finding, to a certain extent, validates the robustness of our research model.
