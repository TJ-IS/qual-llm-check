# MODELING MULTICHANNEL ADVERTISING ATTRIBUTION ACROSS COMPETITORS<sup>1</sup>

Yiyi Li Alfred Lerner College of Business and Economics, University of Delaware, Newark, DE 19716 U.S.A. {yiyil@udel.edu}

Ying Xie and Zhiqiang (Eric) Zheng Naveen Jindal School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, TX 75080 U.S.A. {ying.xie@utdallas.edu} {ericz@utdallas.edu}

The bursts and multiplicity of Internet advertising have made multichannel attribution an immediate challenge for marketing practitioners. Existing attribution models predominantly focus on analyzing consumers’ conversion paths with respect to one focal firm while largely overlooking the impact of their interactions with competing firms, leading to biased estimates of advertising effectiveness. We address this problem by developing an integrated individual-level choice model that considers consumers’ online visit and purchase decisions across all competitors within one industry. We specifically analyze the effects of multichannel advertising on (1) consumer choice of entry site, (2) consumer search decisions concerning the remaining competing websites, and (3) subsequent purchase at one of the searched websites. We quantify the impact of different digital advertising channels on consumers’ decisions at different purchase funnel stages based on individuallevel click stream data from the online air ticket booking industry. We find that information stock of all online channels considered—search, display/referral, email, direct—contributes significantly to consumers’ visit and purchase decisions, among which search is the most effective advertising channel in driving all three decisions. We map the estimates to the conversion attribution of different channels. The result reveals that the relative contribution of the display/referral channel was underestimated under the popular single-firm attribution models by a factor of two on average. In terms of predictive performance, our model consistently outperforms the single-firm model in predicting the occurrences of future purchases.

Keywords: Multichannel attribution, competition, purchase funnel

## Introduction

The advancement of digital technology has enabled firms to reach consumers through a variety of advertising channels such as search engines, email, referral sites, and display ads. The United States digital advertising market reached U.S. \$83 billion in 2017, surpassing the same year TV ad spending by \$10 billion.<sup>2</sup> Consumers have also become more shopping savvy, often visiting multiple stores to seek the best deal before making a purchase (Park and Fader 2004). During this process, consumers may be exposed to multiple ads posted by different advertisers through multiple digital channels at different points in their purchase journeys. It is therefore vital for firms to have a sound understanding of ad attribution analytics regarding the effects of various types of online advertisement across multichannels and multi-touch-points (MCMT hereafter) in a competitive market.

Recently, determining the value of digital advertising channels has attracted a growing interest among researchers in Information Systems and Marketing. The effectiveness of cross-channel advertising was initially assessed with simple heuristic attribution rules such as the “last-touch” model, the linear model, the time decay model, and the position-based model (Tornquist and Tradewell 2012). However, these models often do not fully consider consumers’ entire purchase funnel and are not able to incorporate paths of touches that do not result in conversions (Li and Kannan 2014; Li et al. 2016). To address this issue, researchers have proposed a number of algorithmic attribution models in recent years as richer multichannel data becomes available (e.g., Li and Kannan 2014; Xu et al. 2014). Industry leaders such as Visual IQ, Convertro, and Google also provide their clients with advanced algorithmic-based models for understanding the relative contribution of different channels (Moffett 2014). These data-driven models attempt to go beyond measuring the direct effect of an ad on conversion by also considering a consumer’s non-purchasing paths. However, these models predominantly focus on consumer interactions with a single focal firm while leaving out the impact of competitive actions implemented by competitors, which we view as a critical determinant of consumer conversion.

Our goal is to develop a new multichannel attribution model that incorporates consumers’ search and purchase behaviors across multiple competing online stores within a competitive online shopping environment. The most important distinction of our model is that we consider consumers’ online store choice decisions across multiple sellers, thus expanding the literature’s single-seller scope to a multi-seller scope. Our model construction is driven by realism, not modeling convenience. Consumers are likely to be exposed to different types of digital ads from various competing sellers, and failing to account for consumers’ interactions with these sellers may yield biased estimates of ad effectiveness. Advertising can impact a consumer’s purchase decision in two ways: converting the consumer from not buying into buying, and influencing where the consumer makes the purchase. With a single-seller model, we are only able to capture the first effect. This may lead to misattribution of advertising effectiveness from different channels. Moreover, the problem of not accounting for competitive advertising is exacerbated in a purchase funnel model because competitive advertising not only influences the final purchase decision, but also the earlier product information search and alternative evaluation decisions.

Toward this goal, we propose an integrated two-stage choice model in which a consumer considers all relevant websites and then decides which ones to visit and from which one to make the purchase. We follow previous literature (e.g., Abhishek et al. 2014; Li and Kannan 2014) and adopt a funnel view of consumer online store choice decisions for two reasons. First, doing so allows us to capture the heterogeneity in consumers’ consideration set, which directly impacts their final purchase decisions (Goeree 2008). Second, this model structure differentiates the effects of various advertising channels between different purchase funnel stages.

We estimate this model using a unique panel dataset that records individual consumer’s interactions in the purchase funnel with all competing websites within the online air booking industry through various channels including search engines, email, display ads, referral engines (e.g., Kayak.com) and direct channel (e.g., customer directly typing in the URL of a website). Our results show that the effects of information stock from all channels (search, display/referral, email, direct) are significant in both the visit stage and purchase stage. However, the size of these effects varies across channels and stages. Specifically, we find that information stock from direct channels is the most effective one in driving both visit and purchase decisions, followed by search, email, and display/referral. With our competitive model, we are able to capture not only the marginal impacts of a focal firm’s advertising channel but also the cross-marginal impacts of various channels across competitors. The disparate effectiveness of the same advertising channel across competitors highlights the importance of ad content and message strategies in a competitive environment. A firm needs to determine its standing among the competitive pack and revise its strategy accordingly by benchmarking its own ad effectiveness against the competitors.

We further conducted an attribution analysis by breaking down the purchase likelihood among the four channels. We find that the relative contribution of display/referral is underestimated, by a factor of two on average, under the conventional single-firm model where each firm only knows consumers’ interactions within its own website. This could lead to severe misallocation of ad budgets. Finally, we demonstrate the necessity of considering competition by comparing our model against the single-firm model in terms of advertising effectiveness, relative contribution of ad channels to conversions, and predictive performance. Our model consistently outperforms the benchmarking single-firm model, especially in predicting purchase occurrences of a holdout sample.

To the best of our knowledge, this paper represents the first attempt in modeling multichannel attribution across competitors. Our primary contribution is that we develop an integrated model capturing individual consumers’ interactions with all competitors at different stages along their online purchase journey. Our competition-centric analysis represents a significant enrichment over extant ad attribution models that typically take a single firm perspective, and neglect competitive interactions.

## Literature Review

Information Systems and Marketing researchers have long been interested in measuring the effectiveness of different online advertising formats such as search advertising (Agarwal et al. 2011, 2015; Ghose and Yang 2009) and display ads (e.g., Goldfarb and Tucker 2011; Lewis and Reiley 2014; Rutz and Bucklin 2012). Recently as rich multichannel data becomes available, multichannel ad attribution has attracted special attention. Table 1 provides an overview of the relevant research, where we compare the studies along dimensions including the level of data aggregation, product category, ad channels investigated, the consideration of prepurchase decisions, the modeling approach, and the consideration of competition. Ghose and Todri (2016) study how exposure to various types of display ads (e.g., retargeting, affiliate targeting advertising, etc.) and the duration of exposures affect consumers’ active and passive search behaviors and conversion probability. Li and Kannan (2014) model a consumer’s consideration of advertising channels, visit through a specific channel and the subsequent purchase decision jointly. They find that information stock gained through firm-initiated channels significantly increases a consumer’s conversion probability, but the incremental impact of paid search channels is smaller than previously estimated. Abhishek et al. (2014) apply a hidden Markov model to uncover the impact of ads at different latent purchase stages. They show that display ads exert a positive impact on early stages but do not increase the conversion probability directly, while search ads affects all stages in the purchase funnel positively. Xu et al. (2014) report similar findings using a mutually exciting point process model in which ad clicks and purchases are modeled as different types of random points, where earlier points can affect the types of later points. Zantedeschi et al. (2017) conduct randomized field experiments to estimate the short- and long-term effects of multiple online and offline advertising formats on consumer purchases from a single firm.

Most of the above studies emphasize the importance of modeling multiple stages in a consumer’s purchase funnel. We accordingly adopt the same funnel view and model a consumer’s website visit decisions in addition to her final purchase choice. However, the key departure of our model from these studies lies in our explicit consideration of crosscompetitor interactions. Table 1 shows that all extant studies rely on data from a single focal firm. However, consumers search and purchase decisions are clearly influenced by their interactions with competing firms. Moreover, when confronting their rivals, firms take into consideration the response by competitors and react actively to their competitors’ price and advertising strategies. For example, Chellappa et al. (2011) show that competitors change their pricing strategy depending on who the competing firms are. Zheng et al. (2012) highlighted the importance of gauging competitive intelligence (CI) to characterize a competitive market. Previous literature also suggests that overlooking competition and focusing on a single-product view will lead to biased estimates of factors that affect consumer choice (e.g., Jabr and Zheng 2014). It stands to reason that neglecting competing ads in the online air travel industry is likely to also suffer from the same problem. Therefore, we take a multi-firm view in this paper to study the multichannel advertising effectiveness by explicitly considering cross-competitor interactions.

## Empirical Setting

We obtained a click-stream data set from a leading U.S. media measurement and analytics company in 2010. This company manages a large-scale consumer panel that is representative of the U.S. population. The click-stream data records visit sessions defined as consecutive visits to a specific website without a break of more than 30 minutes. It tracks a panelist’s ID, domain name, date and time of visit, referral domain name (from which advertising channel can be inferred), browsing activity measured by the number of pages viewed and duration in minutes, as well as the purchase activity including product category, product description, quantity, and price paid if a transaction occurs.

## The Online Air Travel Industry

The analysis is confined to data pertaining to online flight booking. We choose this category among more than 60 product categories for the following reasons. First, travel is the largest eCommerce category, comprising of 36% of all business-to-consumer eCommerce sales in 2012,<sup>3</sup> so we will have a sufficiently large sample to ensure reliable estimation. Second, air tickets tend to be non-differentiated search goods, which reduces the possible confounding effect resulting from product-level heterogeneity. The price differentiation across online agents for a given trip at a given time point provided by the same airline is also minimal. Price is therefore unlikely to play a major role in a consumer’s decision regarding from which website to purchase. Third, air tickets are relatively expensive, averaging U.S. \$450 per ticket (Li et al. 2014). Since pricing of air tickets is extremely dynamic, savvy consumers act strategically with extensive searches and patience to wait for discounted tickets (Li et al. 2014). Advertising plays an important role in informing and reminding consumers in such an environment, making it an appealing test-bed for our model.

## Competing Websites

We identify the set of relevant websites from the panelists’ purchase records. A website is included in the analysis if at least one purchase in the air travel category occurred on the website during our entire observational period.<sup>4</sup> These websites can be grouped into two types: (1) major airlines’ own websites, including American Airlines, AirTran, Alaska Airlines, Continental, Delta, Jet Blue, Northwest Airlines, Southwest, Spirit Airlines, United, and U.S. Airways, and (2) major online travel agencies (OTAs hereafter), including Expedia, Priceline, Orbitz, Travelocity, Cheaptickets, Hotwire, American Express Travel, and SabreSonic Web. We exclude Southwest from our analysis because their tickets are sold only via the company’s own website. Consumers who purchase from Southwest may have a completely different consideration set than others. Due to computational considerations (to alleviate the curse of dimensionality), we opted to consolidate small airlines and OTAs when constructing the full choice set. Among airlines’ own websites, we focus on Delta Airlines (Delta), American Airlines (AA), and United Airlines (United), the top three airlines in 2010 based on the number of customer purchases shown in Table 2.<sup>5</sup> We then combine the remaining airline websites into one “other airlines” (O/A) option.<sup>6</sup> By the same token, we focus on the top five online travel agencies in the air travel category— Expedia, Priceline, Orbitz, Travelocity and Cheaptickets— and group Hotwire, American Express Travel, and SabreSonic Web into one “other OTAs” (O/O) option.

## Defining Purchase Sessions

Toward a purchase, a consumer may need to search several websites and visit each website in her choice set repeatedly before a purchase decision is made. Therefore, one important task when processing the data is to aggregate related visit incidences pertaining to a purchase task into purchase sessions. This is not an easy task because (1) there is no universal rule on session length, and (2) the information searched within each visit incidence is obscure and consumers may be viewing pages irrelevant to air travel booking. To address the first challenge, we use a seven-day window policy which is also adopted by De los Santos et al. (2012). This is because one week is considered sufficient to capture all relevant visits for a purchase on Internet.<sup>7</sup> Moreover, a seven-day window yields the largest difference between intersession visit lapse and intra-session visit lapse on average among all seven window lengths we have tested.<sup>8</sup>

For the second challenge, we take several steps to ensure as many visits relevant to air travel booking as possible are included while irrelevant visits are excluded from the purchase sessions. First, we identify all purchases in the category and aggregate all visit incidences within seven days before each purchase into a session. If the lapse between two purchases is less than seven days, we only include visits after the first purchase in the second session. Second, if no purchase is made, we eliminate irrelevant visit incidences and purchase sessions as follows: (1) if the visit occurs within seven days after a flight ticket is booked, then it is deleted because most likely these visits are for after-purchase reassurance and confirmation, rather than looking for information about a new purchase; (2) if less than ten pages are browsed in total across all websites combined during the entire seven-day window, we assume it is too short for any serious product-related search to take place and thus such sessions are dropped; (3) if during the seven-day window, any hotel or rental car company’s direct website is visited but no airline direct website is visited, we deem such a session to be irrelevant because the consumer is more likely to be searching for other products, not flight tickets; (4) if the visit session is within seven days after a rental car or hotel purchase, and no airline direct website is visited, we drop the session for a reason similar to the first situation. For each session, we identify three dependent variables: (1) the entry site (i.e., the first website visited in the purchase session); (2) the set of other websites visited; (3) the conversion website (including the outside option of no purchase).

## Independent Variables

We construct three sets of variables: (1) individual information stock gathered through online channels, (2) individual website-specific experience, and (3) website’s aggregate ad spending on online and offline ad channels. All variables were dynamically constructed and therefore calculated up to the current purchase session.

## Individual Information Stock Gathered Through Online Channels

We identify four types of channels: (1) search where the consumer enters a keyword into a search engine and redirects to the website by clicking a search result;<sup>10</sup> (2) display or referral where a third-party website runs banner (or video) ads or provide referral links (for example, referral engines such as TripAdvisor.com and Kayak.com);<sup>11</sup> (3) email where the consumer clicks an embedded link in an email and be redirected to the website; (4) direct when a consumer types the URL directly into the browser or locates the site through bookmarks. The top five referral domains in the first three firm-initiated advertising channels are presented in Table 3. We observe website visits coming from each channel.

Table 4 presents summary statistics regarding the number of pages viewed through each channel in a purchase session. Most consumers visit the websites directly. Among the advertising channels, search is the most popular one, followed by display/referral. We also notice significant differences across websites in terms of different ad channel usage, suggesting the presence of large variations in ad budgets and allocations across competitors in this industry. We construct lagged individual information stock variables for every user at every purchase session by weighing ad clicks by the number of website pages viewed from the beginning of 2010 until the last purchase session. We follow Nerlove and Arrow (1962) to allow the individual information stock variables from different channels to depreciate over time. ## Individual Website-Specific Experience

For individual website-specific experience, we use both shortterm and long-term browsing and purchase history. For shortterm history, we use two dummy variables to indicate whether the consumer visited and purchased from the website in the last purchase session. These two state dependence variables are associated with the consumer’s intrinsic preference for a website and thus increase the search and conversion probability in the current purchase session. For long-term history, we use the number of pages browsed and the amount of dollars spent on the website from the beginning of the year 2010 until the last purchase session and weight them by time difference to construct two latent stock variables for an individual’s website-specific experience. Likewise, we allow the stock variables for past browsing and purchasing behavior on the website to depreciate over time. We argue that the more a consumer accesses the website, the lower the cost of information search and navigating through the website becomes, which in turn leads to reduced time in finding relevant information and a faster check-out process (Shugan 1980). Therefore, we expect the two stock variables to be positively associated with the website’s attractiveness, resulting in a higher utility of search and purchase.

## Websites’ Online and Offline Ad Spending

We augment our main click-stream dataset with an external data source from the Kantar Media Database (KMD). This database tracks all of the companies in our analysis on their monthly spending on offline advertising channels including TV, newspaper, and radio, as well as two major online advertising channels, search advertising and display ads at the national and local DMA (designated market area) level. The ad spending reflects potential unobserved demand shocks to a consumer’s purchase decision. Therefore, including ad spending as explanatory variables would help directly control for the increase in ad impression due to unobserved demand shocks.

We construct five ad spending stock variables using websites’ ad spending on different channels at both the local and national levels from the beginning of the year 2010 until the beginning of the current purchase session dynamically and weight them by time difference in a similar way as we construct the individual information stock variables. At the local level, we match each consumer’s zip code to a DMA and construct two ad stock variables: local display and local offline (including TV, newspaper, and radio). At the national level, we construct three ad stock variables: national search, national display, and national offline.

## The Model

In this paper, we specifically model a two-stage choice process in which consumers first select a subset of relevant websites to search for product information and then choose a single website from the subset to make the purchase or choose the outside option of no purchase. We allow the effect of individual information stock accumulated through different channels to vary across different funnel stages. Next, we describe the modeling details of each stage.

## Stage 1: The Website Visit Decision

The objective of the Stage 1 model is to capture and represent the consumer search process in terms of (1) at which website to start the purchase session and (2) what other websites (if any) to visit after the initial search. The decision regarding which website to visit first is critical. The search theory asserts that a consumer begins her search process with the highest-utility alternative (Weitzman 1979). Consumer $i ^ { \circ } \mathrm { s }$ utility of choosing website $j$ as the entry site at purchase session t can be expressed as

$$
U _ {i j t} = \alpha_ {1 i j} + \beta_ {1} \mathbf {\Gamma} _ {i j t} + \gamma_ {2} \mathbf {E} _ {i j t} + \varsigma_ {1} \mathbf {\Lambda} _ {i j t} + \varepsilon_ {i j t}\tag{1}
$$

where $\boldsymbol { a } _ { 1 i j }$ captures consumer $i ^ { \circ } \mathrm { s }$ intrinsic net preference for visiting website j and can vary across people but stay constant over choice situations for each person, and $\varepsilon _ { i j t }$ follows a generalized extreme value distribution. Γ represents a vector of channel-specific lagged individual information stock variables until the end of the last purchase session as described earlier. Ε represents a vector of individual website-specific experience variables including lag visit, lag purchase, and cumulative experience with the website due to past purchases as described earlier. Note that Ε does not contain the experience stock variable for past browsing at the website because it has already been captured by Γ. Λ represents a vector of website ad spending stock variables including local display, local offline, national search, national display, and national offline, described earlier. We take the logarithm of all continuous independent variables when estimating the model because many of these variables are highly skewed. $\beta _ { I } , \gamma _ { I } ,$ and $\zeta _ { I }$ are the coefficients to be estimated corresponding to Γ, $\mathbf { E } ,$ and Λ, respectively. The conditional probability of observing website $F _ { i t }$ being chosen as the entry site by consumer i at time t given the individual-specific preference is therefore expressed as

$$
\begin{array}{l} P \left(F _ {i t} \mid \alpha_ {1 i j}\right) = \prod_ {j = 1} ^ {J} \left[ \frac {e ^ {\alpha_ {1 i j} + \beta_ {1} \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j t} + \gamma_ {1} \mathbf {E} _ {i j t} + \varsigma_ {1} \boldsymbol {\Lambda} _ {i j t}}}{\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {J} e ^ {\alpha_ {1 i j} + \beta_ {1} \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j t} + \gamma_ {1} \mathbf {E} _ {i j t} + \varsigma_ {1} \boldsymbol {\Lambda} _ {i j t}}} \right] ^ {f _ {i j t}} \\ \left[ \frac {1}{\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {J} e ^ {\alpha_ {1 i j} + \beta_ {1} \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j t} + \gamma_ {1} \mathbf {E} _ {i j t} + \varsigma_ {1} \boldsymbol {\Lambda} _ {i j t}}} \right] ^ {1 - f _ {i j t}} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

where $f _ { i j t } = 1$ if website j is chosen as the entry site and $f _ { i j t } = 0$ otherwise.

Ideally, we would like to model all subsequent website visits made by the consumer one by one sequentially. However, the decision of which website to visit subsequently may depend on the consumer’s interactions with the prior website(s). Such dependency can be complicated and arbitrary and can render the model intractable. Moreover Figure 2 shows that the number of observations for later visits (third, fourth, fifth, etc.) is relatively small in our estimation sample, prohibiting reliable estimates even if one would like to factor in such dependencies. In compromise, we simplify the search decision for the remaining websites within all available websites as J – 1 independent binary choices. Formally, consumer $i ^ { \circ } \mathrm { s }$ utility of visiting website $j \left( j = 1 , . . . , J ; j \neq F _ { i t } \right)$ at purchase session t can be expressed as

$$
U _ {i j t} = \alpha_ {2 i j} + \beta_ {2} \Gamma_ {i j t} + \gamma_ {2} \mathrm{E} _ {i j t} + \varsigma_ {2} \Lambda_ {i j t} + \xi_ {i j t}\tag{3}
$$

where Γ, Ε, and Λ are defined in the same way as in Equation $1 , \beta _ { 2 } , \gamma _ { 2 } ,$ and $\zeta _ { 2 }$ are the coefficients to be estimated corresponding to Γ, $\mathbf { E } ,$ and Λ, respectively, and $\zeta _ { i j t }$ follows a generalized extreme value distribution. A consumer will only visit a website if the perceived value of that visit is positive. Given the entry site $F _ { i t } ,$ and her individual net preferences, the conditional probability of observing a consumer’s choice set $V _ { i t } ,$ a combination of any number of websites out of J websites, is given as

$$
\begin{array}{l} P \left(V _ {i t} \mid F _ {i t}, \alpha_ {2 i j}\right) = \prod_ {\substack {j = 1, \\ j \neq F _ {i t}}} ^ {J} \left[ \frac {e ^ {\alpha_ {2 i j} + \beta_ {2} \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j t} + \gamma_ {2} \mathbf {E} _ {i j t} + \varsigma_ {2} \boldsymbol {\Lambda} _ {i j t}}}{1 + e ^ {\alpha_ {2 i j} + \beta_ {2} \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j t} + \gamma_ {2} \mathbf {E} _ {i j t} + \varsigma_ {2} \boldsymbol {\Lambda} _ {i j t}}} \right] ^ {\nu_ {i j t}} \\ \left[ \frac {1}{1 + e ^ {\alpha_ {2 i j} + \beta_ {2} \boldsymbol {\Gamma} _ {i j t} + \gamma_ {2} \mathbf {E} _ {i j t} + \varsigma_ {2} \boldsymbol {\Lambda} _ {i j t}}} \right] ^ {1 - \nu_ {i j t}} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

where $\nu _ { i j t } = 1$ if website $j$ is visited in the current purchase session and $\nu _ { i j t } = 0$ otherwise.

## Stage 2: The Purchase Decision

The purchase stage can be conceptualized as an option to purchase from the set of websites a consumer has already visited, $V _ { i t } .$ Since we directly observe the entire search process made by each and every consumer, we are able to capture their respective choice set without any uncertainty. The consumer can also exercise the option of not buying if none of her searched alternatives yields a utility exceeding her purchasing threshold (which is normalized to zero). The utility of purchasing from website $j \in V _ { i t }$ can be written as

$$
W _ {i j t} = \alpha_ {3 i j} + \beta_ {3} \mathrm{K} _ {i j t} + \gamma_ {3} \mathrm{E} _ {i j t} + \varsigma_ {3} \Lambda_ {i j t} + \kappa_ {3} \mathbf {Z} _ {i j t} + \varsigma_ {i j t}\tag{5}
$$

where $a _ { 3 i j }$ captures consumer $i ^ { \circ } \mathrm { s }$ intrinsic net preference for purchasing from website $j ,$ and $\zeta _ { i j t }$ follows a generalized extreme value distribution. Κ represents a vector of current channel-specific individual information stock variables constructed using the logarithm of the number of webpages viewed by a consumer through each channel in the current purchase session. These current channel-specific information stock terms are likely to be correlated with the error term $\zeta _ { i j t }$ because the unobserved factors that drive purchase decision may also lead to more page views. We will discuss how we handle the resulting endogeneity in the next subsection. Ε here represents the vector of site-specific experience variables including the two short-term state dependence variables and the two long-term stock variables as described earlier, and Λ denotes the same vector of website ad spending stock variables as in our website visit model. We also take the logarithm of all continuous variables in Ε and Λ for model estimation. Ζ represents a vector of two dummy variables indexing the first and last website visited in the current purchase session.<sup>13</sup> If only one website is visited, then it will be counted as the first website rather than the last website or both. Parameters $\beta _ { 3 } , \gamma _ { 3 } , \zeta _ { 3 } ,$ , and $\kappa _ { 3 }$ are the coefficients to be estimated corresponding to Κ, Ε, Λ, and $\mathbf { Z } ,$ respectively.

The consumer also has the option of not buying and the utility of this outside option is normalized to 0. Taken together, the probability of observing website $B _ { i t }$ being chosen as the conversion site by consumer i at time t conditional on entry site, choice set, and individual-specific preferences is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} P \left(\beta_ {i t} \mid F _ {i t}, V _ {i t}, \alpha_ {3 i j}\right) = \prod_ {j \in V _ {i t}} ^ {J} \left[ \frac {e ^ {\alpha_ {3 i j} + \beta_ {3} K _ {i j t} + \gamma_ {3} E _ {i j t} + \varsigma_ {3} A _ {i j t} + \kappa_ {3} Z _ {i j t}}}{1 + \sum_ {k \in V _ {i t}} e ^ {\alpha_ {3 i j} + \beta_ {3} K _ {i j t} + \gamma_ {3} E _ {i j t} + \varsigma_ {3} A _ {i j t} + \kappa_ {3} Z _ {i j t}}} \right] ^ {b _ {i j t}} \\ \left[ \frac {1}{1 + \sum_ {k \in V _ {i t}} e ^ {\alpha_ {3 i j} + \beta_ {3} K _ {i j t} + \gamma_ {3} E _ {i j t} + \varsigma_ {3} A _ {i j t} + \kappa_ {3} Z _ {i j t}}} \right] ^ {1 - b _ {i j t}} \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

where $b _ { i j t } = 1$ if the consumer converts on website j and $b _ { i j t } =$ 0 otherwise.

## Endogeneity and the Instruments

Endogeneity in the context of measuring advertising effectiveness is a well-known concern for studies using observational data (Rossi 2014). In our case, the current channel-specific individual information stock variables are likely to be correlated with the error term in the purchase stage and thus pose challenge to identification. Field experiments are the ultimate arbitrator for causal inferences (Johnson et al. 2015). However, in our situation where many competing firms are involved, conducting a large-scale experiment is close to impossible because of the difficulty in controlling all competing firms’ advertising actions. Alternatively, we adopt the control function approach in discrete choice models popularized by Petrin and Train (2010). A premise of an effective control function approach is that the instruments constructed need to be valid and strong (Rossi 2014).<sup>14</sup> We elaborate below how our proposed instruments help mitigate the endogeneity concerns coming from multiple potential sources.

The first potential source of endogeneity is customer selection, which happens when these consumers who are exposed to and interact with advertising are systematically different than those who are not (Manchanda et al. 2004). This can occur for any marketing channels in our context, even for the direct channel. To alleviate this source of endogeneity, we construct a set of instrumental variables using the number of webpages viewed through each channel during the past 30 days in 18 non-travel categories including apparel and shoes, jewelry and watches, sports and fitness, electronics, event tickets, and flowers. We take the logarithms of these channelspecific information stock variables and include them when constructing the control function. These instruments are relevant and exogenous for the following two reasons. First, the more a consumer has interacted with the channel through the non-travel category in the past, the more familiar she becomes with the channel. This familiarity will lead to increased use of this channel to access travel-related websites as well. Therefore, these non-travel channel-specific information stocks ought to be correlated with those of the air travel category since they both are initiated by the same panelist. Second, despite the relevance, since these past interactions happened in the other categories, they are not co-determined by the consumer’s purchase utility in the travel industry. Therefore, they are exogeneous to the channel-specific information stock in the online air ticket booking industry.

The second potential endogeneity source comes from the temporal simultaneity between advertising and unobserved demand shocks (Zantedeschi et al. 2017). It can happen at both the market level and the individual level. At the market level, firms may increase advertising spending when there is an external demand shock unobservable to researchers. As a result, consumers are exposed to more ads, leading to simultaneous increases both in terms of ad clicks and purchases. We directly control this potential source using a website’s ad spending data acquired from KMD and also include time fixed effects to capture any remaining seasonal fluctuation in demand. The individual-level demand shock can occur due to factors such as a sudden change in income, a large bonus, etc. We construct an instrumental variable using the consumer’s discretionary consumption excluding the travel category: the total dollar amount a customer spent in nontravel categories. We argue that a random individual-level demand shock may simultaneously boost the consumer’s spending on other discretionary categories and the number of ad clicks in the air travel category. But the consumer’s discretionary consumption (excluding travel-related categories) should not be directly caused by the purchase utility for air tickets.

The third source arises from the firm’s activity of using targeted ads. To improve advertising effectiveness, firms may target the most receptive customers based on their geographic, demographic, psychographic, behavioral, or other characteristics. As information technology advances, behavioral targeting, especially retargeting ads that follow users who have looked at or purchased a particular item, becomes increasingly popular among online advertisers. We directly control this potential source using consumers’ browsing and purchasing history.

Nevertheless, despite the set of independent variables and controls we carefully crafted to account for the sources of endogeneity we identified, there might still exist confounders unaddressed, which we acknowledge as limitations. With regard to the endogeneity arising from the firm’s targeted ads, our identification approach works when firms in our dataset use a similar targeting strategy, that is, all the behavioral targeting algorithms (of different firms) would identify the same user as a valuable customer based on the customer’s browsing and spending history. However, different firms may develop different types of sophisticated behavioral targeting algorithms and so two firms observing the same behaviors of a user may arrive at a different conclusion on the value of a customer. Our current controls are not able to account for such subtlety. As it is almost impossible for us to know what algorithms each firm implements, this is one limitation we acknowledge. However, our 2010 data partially alleviates this concern because behavioral targeting was not as popular or sophisticated as it is today.

## Likelihood Function

Overall, the joint likelihood function in Equation 7 takes into account the selection of entry site, the formation of the choice set, and the purchase decision, conditional on the individualspecific preferences for various websites in the two stages. We assume the individual website-specific intercepts $\begin{array} { r } { \boldsymbol { a } _ { 1 i j } , \boldsymbol { a } _ { 2 i j } , } \end{array}$ and $a _ { 3 i j }$ follow a normal distribution. We further assume the correlation between intercepts of different stages remains the same across websites, but allow the variance of each intercept to vary by website and stage to reduce the number of parameters to be estimated.<sup>15</sup> We estimated the model using the simulated maximum likelihood approach. The details for the estimation procedure are provided in Appendix C.

$$
\begin{array}{l} L (B \mid \alpha) = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \prod_ {t = 1} ^ {T _ {i}} P \left(F _ {i t} = j \mid \alpha_ {1 i j}\right) \times P \left(V _ {i t} \mid F _ {i t}, \alpha_ {2 i j}\right) \\ \times P \left(B _ {i t} \mid F _ {i t}, V _ {i t}, \alpha_ {3 i j}\right) \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

## Stage 1: The Website Visit Decision

Table 5 presents parameter estimates for the first stage in the purchase funnel: website visit decision. This stage is composed of two subdecisions: (1) choosing an entry site from the full choice set of all relevant websites, and (2) deciding whether or not to visit other remaining websites. The estimates for the choice of entry site are presented in the first column of Table 5. We find that consumers are more likely to start their purchase sessions with O/A. This can be explained by the fact that O/A has the lowest average (paid) ticket price compared to major airlines and OTAs as shown in Figure 3. We also find that there is a significant variation in preferences for a website across consumers except for United, Orbitz, and Cheaptickets.

Regarding the effect of lagged channel-specific information stock, our results indicate that information stock accumulated through direct type-in (0.61) is the most effective in increasing search utility, followed by search engines (0.34).<sup>16</sup> Although display/referral is the least popular channel according to Table 5, information stock accumulated via this channel is still quite effective in making a website the entry site. The coefficients for lag visit, lag purchase, and past spending are all positive and significant. These results are not surprising as they suggest that the more experience a consumer accumulates with a website, the more likely she will choose it to be the entry site. The coefficient for offline ad spending by a website at the local level is positive and significant, indicating that local offline advertising is still important in generating awareness and goodwill for online consumers. However, the other four ad spending variables do not affect consumers’ choice of entry site significantly because most of the variations in these aggregate-level ad spending measures can be explained by the individual-level measures (individual information stock through different channels and past experience with the website).

Next, we discuss the estimation results for the visit decision beyond the entry site. In general, consumers have a low probability of visiting other websites beyond the entry site, and this is particularly true for United and Cheaptickets. Direct channel (0.39) is the most effective channel in enticing consumers to visit beyond the entry site, followed by search (0.25) and email (0.24). The coefficients for lag visit, lag purchase, and cumulative purchase on the website are all positive, but the effect of lag purchase is not statistically significant.

In terms of ad spending, we find that spending on display ads at both the national and local levels and spending on offline channels at the local level significantly increase the site visit probability beyond the entry site. Interestingly, we find that spending on search ads decreases the site’s visit probability if it is not the entry site. This result suggests spending on search advertising has little impact on a consumer’s search decision beyond the first click, consistent with Jerath et al. (2014).

## Marginal Impact of Individual Channel-Specific Information Stock

While parameter estimates show the relative significance of different channels, it is often more informative to know to what extent visit and purchase probabilities change in response to a change in information stock. We quantify the impact of information stock of different channels on visit and purchase probability using a marginal impact analysis. The marginal impact is measured as the change in visit and purchase probabilities for each website given a change of the same magnitude in information stock collected through a specific channel for a specific website. For example, a marginal impact analysis can inform us how probabilities of being chosen as the entry site change for each website if a consumer has collected more information about Expedia by browsing more webpages under the influence of search advertising in the past. This analysis therefore enables us to compare the effects of information stock for the same website across different advertising channels and for the same channel across different websites.

We need to simulate the change in choice probabilities because the net preferences for different websites are heterogeneous across consumers and are drawn from a normal distribution. The simulation follows six steps, which are elaborated in Appendix D. The resultant average changes across consumers for the choice of entry site, visit probability of remaining websites, and purchase probability of visited websites are reported in Tables 8, 9, and 10.

Table 8 tabulates the own- and cross-impacts on the entry site choice as a result of 10 additional pages of a focal site browsed in the last month through additional ad clicks resulting from the four different channels. The own marginal impacts show that, in general, information stock collected through direct channel is most effective in raising the probability of being chosen as the entry website, followed closely by search and email. Display/referral also increases the choice probability but the effect is small compared to the other three channels. Every website sees a boost in choice probability due to additional information stock, but this effect size varies significantly across websites. For example, increased information stock through search due to 10 more pages browsed can increase Expedia’s probability of being chosen as the entry site by 4.93%, but the same amount of change in information stock only increases United’s choice probability by 2.27%.

The off-diagonal entries of Table 8 represent the cross-impact of change in lagged information stock for the website in the row on choice probability for the website in the column. This effect size varies by channels. For example, if the consumer reaches Expedia through search, Priceline’s probability of being chosen as the entry site will decrease by 0.58%. However, the same probability will only decrease by 0.12% under the influence of display/referral.

Table 9 presents the changes in visit probability given an increase in lagged information stock through different channels by websites. Again, information stock collected through direct channel is most valuable in driving up the visit probability. However, information stock gathered through email and search are also very effective. For example, for AA, if a consumer browsed 10 more webpages in the last month through email, the probability that she will visit the website in this purchase session increases by 7.07%, more than double the change in visit probability due to the same increase in pages viewed through display/referral. Across websites, Expedia is most sensitive to the change in information stock through a channel, followed by O/A, Priceline, and Orbitz.

Table 10 tabulates the changes in purchase probabilities for every website as a result of 10 more pages browsed through additional ad clicks resulting from different channels on a focal website during the current purchase session. The diagonal entries record the own-marginal impacts for each website. In general, search has a greater impact on conversion probability than any other channels. For example, the change in information stock gathered through search increases conversion probability by 7.65% for Expedia. Email also has a strong effect on conversion. When a consumer browses 10 more pages on Expedia through email, her conversion probability will increase by 6.16%. While the high effectiveness of search or direct is expected because they are consumerinitiated, the large impact of email might be attributed to the higher level of consumer loyalty. Consumers who subscribed to a website’s email list are often more loyal than those who are not, so they may also become more responsive to email campaigns. Moreover, if a consumer happens to receive an email ad when she has a purchase need, the coincidence will also increase her purchase probability on the website.

However, even though every website enjoys a boost in conversion probability due to additional ad clicks in the current purchase session, this effect size is not uniform across websites. The marginal increase of information stock has the largest effect on Expedia and Orbitz, but has barely any material impact on O/O. The disparate effectiveness of the same advertising channel across competitors suggests the importance of ad content and message strategies. Firms can use these figures as the starting point toward a diagnostic process to improve their own ad messaging and copy strategies.

## Attribution Analysis and Economic Value of Channels

We follow Li and Kannan’s (2014) Shapley value-based attribution analysis.<sup>18</sup> We first evaluate the impact of a specific channel on a customer’s conversion rate at a given website by calculating the difference in the purchase probability between two scenarios: (1) when the channel was excluded from the model, and (2) when the channel was enabled in the model (i.e., the status quo). In our case, there are eight (2<sup>3</sup>) possible combinations of the three outside channels (i.e., search, display/referral, and email). So, we compute the incremental value of one channel as the average difference between the predicted purchase probabilities with versus without this channel given a specific combination of the other three channels. The total incremental values of the four channels add up to 100%.

The left panel of Table 11 presents the results of the attribution analysis. Direct channels contribute the most to conversion, followed by search. The contribution of display/ referral varies widely across websites. For example, it contributes to more than 5% of conversions for Priceline, Orbitz, and O/O. However, it has very little effect on Cheaptickets. Based on the percentage-wise attribution analysis, we are also able to attach a dollar value to a channel in terms of its contribution in converting a customer using the average conversion rate and information about the average basket value for each website. The results are presented in the right panel of Table 11.

## The Value of Modeling Competition

In order to demonstrate the importance and necessity of modeling competition between firms, we compare our competitive analytics approach with a baseline model. The baseline model mimics the scenario where the company only knows its customers’ interactions with itself, but not with their competitors. In this case, a consumer’s purchase decision is modeled as a binary choice between buying and not buying on a visited website (in contrast to a multinomial choice from all the visited websites in the proposed model). Our comparison is carried out on the purchase stage with a focus on their predictive performances in terms of predicting purchases in the out-of-sample.

We first present the parameter estimates and in-sample model fit statistics of the baseline model in the third and fourth columns of Table 7. It is clear that our proposed model outperforms the baseline model in terms of model fitness as measured by the log-likelihood and the AIC criterion. We then compare the predictive performance of these two models on a holdout sample, composed of 3,502 purchase sessions made by the same group of consumers between October 2010 and December 2010. Each consumer on average visited 2.41 websites in a purchase session, and 940 out of these 3,502 sessions ended up with a purchase, consistent with our estimation sample. The predicted purchase probability is simulated using the same method described in Appendix D.

We first compare the two models in terms of the inferred marginal impacts of the channel-specific information stock variables in Figure 4. Figure 4 shows that the differences between the two approaches are relatively minor for search and direct type-in; while the difference for display/referral is astounding—by a factor of two on average and it can be as high as a factor of four! This is because in a binary choice model, the underlying assumption is that when the latent utility is $U _ { j } > 0 ,$ , the consumer should make a purchase. However, this condition is not sufficient in reality because a second condition, $U _ { j } > U _ { j { ' } }$ , should also be satisfied when the consumer searches more than one alternative before making a purchase decision. This explains why the marginal effect of display/referral ads is underestimated in the baseline model: the more the consumer engages with a competitor’s ads, the more advertising is required to counter the competitive effect, which is overlooked in the single firm model. This result sends an important warning to the industry: the single-firm centric approach tends to severely underestimate the effect of display and referral ads.

Cumulative gains and lift charts are often used to visualize model performance. ## Conclusions, Limitations, and Future Research

Practitioners have long been calling for better methods to solve the multichannel attribution problem. Although recent research has made strides when estimating the effects of prior advertising touches, the current ad attribution models still suffer from one fundamental drawback. That is, they focus on analyzing the converting path with respect to one focal firm only while largely overlooking the impact of competitors’ advertising, leading to biased ad effectiveness estimates. The problem exacerbates in a purchase funnel model because competing advertising not only influences the final conversion stage, but also the earlier product information search and alternative evaluation stage.

We overcome these deficiencies and improvise the current multichannel attribution models by accounting for competition in a consumer’s purchase funnel, estimating both the direct and indirect effect of ad-clicks prior to conversion.

Specifically, we develop an integrated multistage choice model in order to measure the advertising effectiveness on a consumer’s online search and purchase decisions in a multichannel, multi-touch-point, multi-competitor environment using individual-level advertising response data. Our model considers (1) consumer choice of entry site, (2) consumer search decisions concerning the remaining competing websites, and (3) subsequent purchases at one of the websites searched. We estimate this model on a unique panel data set covering both search and purchase decisions across multiple websites in the online air ticket booking industry. We then use these parameter estimates to compute the direct and indirect marginal impacts of online advertising channels and predict a consumer’s future purchasing behavior based on observed variables. We further compare our proposed competition-centric model against a baseline model using data from a single-firm only. Our model outperforms the baseline model in terms of in-sample model fit as well as out-ofsample predictive performance, necessitating modeling competition in multichannel ad attribution.

Our findings offer managers new insights for evaluating advertising effectiveness. First, we find that in the online air travel industry, search is the most effective ad channel in increasing the visit probability, consistent with the industry belief that search engine ads are the king of online advertising. Moreover, our result regarding the significant direct effect of email and display/referral echoes findings in recent literature (e.g., Hoban and Bucklin, 2015) that challenges the conventional belief that email and banner ads are less useful in sealing the deal. This difference demonstrates the strength of our approach because we consider the entire purchase journey and reward all ad clicks prior to conversion, whereas most rule-based models adopted by practitioners only assign weights to touches that directly lead to a purchase; such approaches under-estimate the effect of assisting touches prior to purchase.

Second, we are able to quantify the channel- and stagespecific marginal ad impacts based on our model estimates. Some websites may find that advertising has small effect on improving its conversion probability directly, but this may not be the case for its competitors. These websites should accordingly benchmark against their competitors and focus more on ad designs. For example, firms may need to choose better keywords and improve their search ad rankings; they may also make display ads more attractive by matching the webpage content or targeting more interested online users. We also find that ad clicks through a specific channel can have either a direct impact on the conversion probability, an indirect impact through its influence on consumers’ search decisions during the next purchase session, or both. Additional information stock accrued through a specific channel may still be beneficial because they increase the likelihood of these websites being included in the consumer’s consideration set during the next purchase session and indirectly increase the future conversion probability, even if they have a small effect in leading to a higher direct conversion. Firms must consider both the direct and indirect effects of advertising when allocating their budgets.

Multichannel attribution is a rapidly growing new research area and many aspects await future investigation. Our model spawns at least five interesting directions for further investigation. First, one of the main limitations of our research is that we do not observe the content of webpages visited by the consumers. If page content data is available, future research can take advantage of such data to further investigate how product attributes such as prices and product features affect online advertising effectiveness across advertisers. Second, since we do not observe consumers’ ad exposure in our data we cannot model their click-through decisions subject to firms’ strategic targeting policies. It will be a very interesting extension to further model consumers’ ad click decisions together with their purchase funnel decisions. Third, another limitation of our estimation sample is that the data was collected in 2010 and several popular ad channels firms in use today were not as developed at the time. This model can therefore be readily extended to incorporate the effects of more recent ad channels such as social media, mobile ads, etc. Fourth, marketing practitioners are particularly interested in determining how online and offline advertising interact, as well as the spillover effect of online advertising on offline sales and vice versa. Future research may answer this question by incorporating offline advertising and purchase decisions into the model. Finally, our model can be applied to other product categories in order to test these findings regarding the direct and indirect effects of advertising. Different industries have their own characteristics and may differ widely in channels they employ to reach out to customers.

To conclude, as companies are attaching more importance to deriving competitive intelligence in ad attribution, there is an increasing availability in cross-competitor ad clicks and browsing data provided by many marketing analytics companies using two viable solutions. The first solution is to acquire competition data directly from the user side, as is done by VisualIQ’s ad-attribution platform IQ-Insight.<sup>20</sup> IQ-Insight tracks the consumer journey across all touchpoints (including those initiated by competitors) using its proprietary technology via a combination of cookies and third-party data integration. Such data are also available from third-party data providers such as ACNielsen and ComScore.<sup>21</sup>

The second approach is typified by Convertro’s ad-attribution product called Unified Marketing Activation Platform,<sup>22</sup> which integrates licensed data across 75 platforms including Verivzon, AOL, and Twitter. Any customer touchpoint (be it from the focal firm or competitors) coming from these 75 platforms is captured. Further, it is not uncommon for companies to form strategic alliances and share customer data with each other. Amazon and Salesforce made a strategic data sharing agreement in 2016.<sup>23</sup>

In addition, the advancement of the new digital technologies has also made it possible for companies to infer competitive intelligence through social media and online surveys.<sup>24</sup> In light of the increasing availability of data that tracks consumers’ MCMT interactions with all competing firms, our study represents a timely precursor to what is to come in the imminent competitive analytics field. Our model will become more and more feasible as the information technology advances. The inferences from our model will provide companies critical competitive intelligence that helps guide their advertising allocation decisions in an online shopping environment that is characterized by intense competition.

## Simulated Maximum Likelihood Estimation

We assume that the overall attractiveness of visiting or purchasing from website j (website-specific intercepts in Equations 1, 3, and 5) can vary over people but be constant over choice situations for each person (Erdem and Keane 1996). The individual website-specific intercepts $\alpha _ { 1 i j } ,$ $\alpha _ { 2 i j } ,$ and $\alpha _ { 3 i j }$ are specified to follow normal distribution:

$$
\left( \begin{array}{c} \alpha_ {1 i j} \\ \alpha_ {2 i j} \\ \alpha_ {3 i j} \end{array} \right) \sim N \left(\left[ \begin{array}{c} \overline {{\alpha}} _ {1 j} \\ \overline {{\alpha}} _ {2 j} \\ \overline {{\alpha}} _ {3 j} \end{array} \right], \boldsymbol {\Omega} _ {j}\right)
$$

where $j = 1 , 2 , . . . , J .$

The probability conditional on $a _ { i }$ is

$$
L _ {t} (\alpha) = \prod_ {t = 1} ^ {T _ {i}} L _ {i t} (\alpha) = \prod_ {t = 1} ^ {T _ {i}} P (F _ {i t} = j \mid \alpha_ {1 i}) \times P (V _ {i t} = C _ {i t} \mid F _ {i t}, \alpha_ {2 i}) \times P (B _ {i t} = k \mid F _ {i t}, V _ {i t}, \alpha_ {3 i})
$$

The unconditional choice probability is therefore the integral of $L _ { i } ( \theta )$ over all possible values of $\theta _ { i } .$

$$
P _ {i t} = \int L _ {i t} (\alpha) f (\alpha) d \alpha
$$

1. Draw $a _ { i r }$ from its distribution.

A set of random variables $\eta _ { i } ^ { r } = \big ( \eta _ { j 1 } ^ { r } , \eta _ { j 2 } ^ { r } , \eta _ { j 3 } ^ { r } \big ) , j = 1 , 2 , . . . , J$ are drawn from i.i.d. standard normal distribution. Then we compute the website-specific intercepts for each person as follows:

$$
\alpha_ {1 i j} ^ {r} = \overline {{\alpha}} _ {1 j} + \sigma_ {\alpha_ {1 j}} \eta_ {j 1} ^ {r}
$$

$$
\alpha_ {2 i j} ^ {r} = \overline {{\alpha}} _ {2 j} + \sigma_ {\alpha_ {2 j}} \left(c _ {2 1 _ {j}} \eta_ {j 1} ^ {r} + c _ {2 2 _ {j}} \eta_ {j 2} ^ {r}\right)
$$

$$
\alpha_ {3 i j} ^ {r} = \overline {{\alpha}} _ {3 j} + \sigma_ {\alpha_ {3 j}} \left(c _ {3 1 _ {j}} \eta_ {j 1} ^ {r} + c _ {3 2 _ {j}} \eta_ {j 2} ^ {r} + c _ {3 3 _ {j}} \eta_ {j 3} ^ {r}\right)
$$

The benefit of this specification is that the correlation between intercepts of different stages remains the same across websites, thus significantly reducing the number of parameters to be estimated. With this specification, the variance and covariance matrix can be written as follows:

$$
\Omega_ {j} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} \sigma_ {\alpha_ {1 j}} ^ {2} & \sigma_ {\alpha_ {1 j}} \sigma_ {\alpha_ {2 j}} c _ {2 1} & \sigma_ {\alpha_ {1 j}} \sigma_ {\alpha_ {3 j}} c _ {3 1} \\ \sigma_ {\alpha_ {1 j}} \sigma_ {\alpha_ {2 j}} c _ {2 1} & \sigma_ {\alpha_ {2 j}} ^ {2} \left(c _ {2 1} ^ {2} + c _ {2 2} ^ {2}\right) & \sigma_ {\alpha_ {2 j}} \sigma_ {\alpha_ {3 j}} \left(c _ {2 1} c _ {3 1} + c _ {2 2} c _ {3 2}\right) \\ \sigma_ {\alpha_ {1 j}} \sigma_ {\alpha_ {3 j}} c _ {3 1} & \sigma_ {\alpha_ {2 j}} \sigma_ {\alpha_ {3 j}} \left(c _ {2 1} c _ {3 1} + c _ {2 2} c _ {3 2}\right) & \sigma_ {\alpha_ {3 j}} ^ {2} \left(c _ {3 1} ^ {2} + c _ {3 2} ^ {2} + c _ {3 3} ^ {2}\right) \end{array} \right]
$$

2. $L _ { i t } ( \boldsymbol { a } )$ is calculated for each period, and the product of these $L _ { i t } ( \theta ) ^ { \prime } { \bf s }$ is taken:

$$
L _ {i} ^ {r} (\alpha) = \prod_ {t = 1} ^ {T _ {i}} L _ {i t} ^ {r} (\alpha) = \prod_ {t = 1} ^ {T _ {i}} P \left(F _ {i t} = j \mid \alpha_ {1 i} ^ {r}\right) \times P \left(V _ {i t} = C _ {i t} \mid F _ {i t}, \alpha_ {2 i} ^ {r}\right) \times P \left(B _ {i t} = k \mid F _ {i t}, V _ {i t}, \alpha_ {3 i} ^ {r}\right)
$$

3. Repeat 1 and 2 for many time, and the results are averaged: $\hat { P } _ { i } { - } \frac { 1 } { R } \sum _ { r = 1 } ^ { R } L _ { i } ^ { r } \left( \alpha \right)$

4. Calculate the simulated log-likelihood: $S L L = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { N } \hat { P } _ { i }$

## Appendix C

## Simulated Maximum Likelihood Estimation

We assume that the overall attractiveness of visiting or purchasing from website j (website-specific intercepts in Equations 1, 3, and 5) can vary over people but be constant over choice situations for each person (Erdem and Keane 1996). The individual website-specific intercepts $\alpha _ { 1 i j } ,$ $\alpha _ { 2 i j } ,$ and $\alpha _ { 3 i j }$ are specified to follow normal distribution:

$$
\left( \begin{array}{c} \alpha_ {1 i j} \\ \alpha_ {2 i j} \\ \alpha_ {3 i j} \end{array} \right) \sim N \left(\left[ \begin{array}{c} \overline {{\alpha}} _ {1 j} \\ \overline {{\alpha}} _ {2 j} \\ \overline {{\alpha}} _ {3 j} \end{array} \right], \boldsymbol {\Omega} _ {j}\right)
$$

where $j = 1 , 2 , . . . , J .$

The probability conditional on $a _ { i }$ is

$$
L _ {t} (\alpha) = \prod_ {t = 1} ^ {T _ {i}} L _ {i t} (\alpha) = \prod_ {t = 1} ^ {T _ {i}} P (F _ {i t} = j \mid \alpha_ {1 i}) \times P (V _ {i t} = C _ {i t} \mid F _ {i t}, \alpha_ {2 i}) \times P (B _ {i t} = k \mid F _ {i t}, V _ {i t}, \alpha_ {3 i})
$$

The unconditional choice probability is therefore the integral of $L _ { i } ( \theta )$ over all possible values of $\theta _ { i } .$

$$
P _ {i t} = \int L _ {i t} (\alpha) f (\alpha) d \alpha
$$

1. Draw $a _ { i r }$ from its distribution.

A set of random variables $\eta _ { i } ^ { r } = \big ( \eta _ { j 1 } ^ { r } , \eta _ { j 2 } ^ { r } , \eta _ { j 3 } ^ { r } \big ) , j = 1 , 2 , . . . , J$ are drawn from i.i.d. standard normal distribution. Then we compute the website-specific intercepts for each person as follows:

$$
\alpha_ {1 i j} ^ {r} = \overline {{\alpha}} _ {1 j} + \sigma_ {\alpha_ {1 j}} \eta_ {j 1} ^ {r}
$$

$$
\alpha_ {2 i j} ^ {r} = \overline {{\alpha}} _ {2 j} + \sigma_ {\alpha_ {2 j}} \left(c _ {2 1 _ {j}} \eta_ {j 1} ^ {r} + c _ {2 2 _ {j}} \eta_ {j 2} ^ {r}\right)
$$

$$
\alpha_ {3 i j} ^ {r} = \overline {{\alpha}} _ {3 j} + \sigma_ {\alpha_ {3 j}} \left(c _ {3 1 _ {j}} \eta_ {j 1} ^ {r} + c _ {3 2 _ {j}} \eta_ {j 2} ^ {r} + c _ {3 3 _ {j}} \eta_ {j 3} ^ {r}\right)
$$

The benefit of this specification is that the correlation between intercepts of different stages remains the same across websites, thus significantly reducing the number of parameters to be estimated. With this specification, the variance and covariance matrix can be written as follows:

$$
\Omega_ {j} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} \sigma_ {\alpha_ {1 j}} ^ {2} & \sigma_ {\alpha_ {1 j}} \sigma_ {\alpha_ {2 j}} c _ {2 1} & \sigma_ {\alpha_ {1 j}} \sigma_ {\alpha_ {3 j}} c _ {3 1} \\ \sigma_ {\alpha_ {1 j}} \sigma_ {\alpha_ {2 j}} c _ {2 1} & \sigma_ {\alpha_ {2 j}} ^ {2} \left(c _ {2 1} ^ {2} + c _ {2 2} ^ {2}\right) & \sigma_ {\alpha_ {2 j}} \sigma_ {\alpha_ {3 j}} \left(c _ {2 1} c _ {3 1} + c _ {2 2} c _ {3 2}\right) \\ \sigma_ {\alpha_ {1 j}} \sigma_ {\alpha_ {3 j}} c _ {3 1} & \sigma_ {\alpha_ {2 j}} \sigma_ {\alpha_ {3 j}} \left(c _ {2 1} c _ {3 1} + c _ {2 2} c _ {3 2}\right) & \sigma_ {\alpha_ {3 j}} ^ {2} \left(c _ {3 1} ^ {2} + c _ {3 2} ^ {2} + c _ {3 3} ^ {2}\right) \end{array} \right]
$$

2. $L _ { i t } ( \boldsymbol { a } )$ is calculated for each period, and the product of these $L _ { i t } ( \theta ) ^ { \prime } { \bf s }$ is taken:

$$
L _ {i} ^ {r} (\alpha) = \prod_ {t = 1} ^ {T _ {i}} L _ {i t} ^ {r} (\alpha) = \prod_ {t = 1} ^ {T _ {i}} P \left(F _ {i t} = j \mid \alpha_ {1 i} ^ {r}\right) \times P \left(V _ {i t} = C _ {i t} \mid F _ {i t}, \alpha_ {2 i} ^ {r}\right) \times P \left(B _ {i t} = k \mid F _ {i t}, V _ {i t}, \alpha_ {3 i} ^ {r}\right)
$$

3. Repeat 1 and 2 for many time, and the results are averaged: $\hat { P } _ { i } { - } \frac { 1 } { R } \sum _ { r = 1 } ^ { R } L _ { i } ^ { r } \left( \alpha \right)$

4. Calculate the simulated log-likelihood: $S L L = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { N } \hat { P } _ { i }$

## Appendix D

## Simulated Choice Probabilities

We use the change in the probability to be chosen as the entry site due to a change in information stock through the search engine for Expedia as an illustration. In step 1, we update the corresponding variable in the dataset, for example by adding 10 pages browsed a month ago through search advertising for Expedia to each individual and then recomputing the lagged individual information stock through search advertising. In step 2, we make a draw of net preferences for each website as described in step 1 in Appendix C. In step 3, we calculate the probability of being chosen as the entry website for each website using the updated data and the net preferences drawn in step 2. We repeat steps 2 and 3 as many times as necessary and, in step 4, we calculate a weighted average of these probabilities. The weight is given as $w _ { i t } ^ { r } = \frac { P \big ( F _ { i t } | \alpha ^ { r } \big ) } { \displaystyle \sum _ { r } \big ( F _ { i t } | \alpha ^ { r } \big ) }$ where $F _ { i t }$ is the actual chosen entry-site for individual i in purchase session t. This is the probability of Expedia to be

chosen as the entry site due to a change in information stock through search engine. In step 5, we follow steps 2 through 4 to calculate the choice probability of Expedia to be chosen as the entry site without the change in information stock. In the last step, we calculate the differences in choice probabilities for Expedia.
