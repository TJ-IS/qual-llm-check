# To share or not to share: the effects of extrinsic and intrinsic motivations on knowledge-sharing in enterprise social media platforms

Henning Rode

Faculty for Business Administration and Economics, Heinrich Heine University Duesseldorf, Duesseldorf, Germany

Correspondence:

H Rode, Faculty for Business Administration and Economics, Heinrich Heine University Duesseldorf, Universitaetsstrasse 1, Duesseldorf, D-40225, Germany.

E-mail: henning.rode@hhu.de

## Abstract

In order to foster knowledge-sharing among employees, an increasing number of companies has recently begun implementing social network-based enterprise social media platforms (ESMPs). Although employees are expected to adopt these platforms quickly and use them as actively as they use public and free social media, patterns of private and corporate usage differ. While some research on the determinants of using public and free social media like Facebook has been conducted, empirical studies on the factors determining active participation in ESMPs is scarce. Based on research derived from a survey of 492 respondents carried out in a large knowledge-intensive <sup>fi</sup>rm, this paper addresses this gap. The results of our empirical quantitative study reveal that extrinsic motivation in particular – that is, anticipated gains in reputation and reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts – drives employees to share knowledge in ESMPs. Knowledge-sharing self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy also facilitates participation, while the enjoyment in helping others does not. The study contributes to information systems and knowledge management research, as it reveals motivational factors that drive the individual adoption in terms of active use of ESMPs. Managerial implications are derived from these results.

## Introduction

n light of the increasing importance of knowledge as a resource (Van den Hooff and Van Weenen, 2004) and knowledge as a source of sustainable competitive advantage (Barney, 1991; Davenport and Prusak, 1998), knowledge-Iintensive enterprises strive to make better use of their internal knowledge-resources. To reduce prevailing technological, organizational, and individual barriers of knowledge-sharing, and in order to mitigate knowledge workers’ reluctance to share their knowledge (Bibbo et al., 2012), an increasing number of knowledge-intensive <sup>fi</sup>rms has recently begun implementing enterprise social media platforms (ESMPs). ESMPs are comparable to social networking services like

Facebook and integrate the social media functions of social networking, searching for, linking, tagging, and authoring information (McAfee, 2006) into a single corporate platform (Leonardi et al., 2013). In light of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that social media yield as a means of corporate communication with external parties (e.g., consumers, job candidates), ESMPs promise to improve the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, capture, and exploitation of knowledge existing in the company that is not yet fully exploited (Fulk and Yuan, 2013; Noe et al., 2014), at least in part because employees often avoid sharing their knowledge if it requires using dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and time-consuming information and communication technologies (ICTs) (Kaiser et al., 2009).

Based on the principles of web 2.0, which is characterized by social interaction, user participation in the creation of content, openness, and network effects (Musser and O’Reilly, 2007), ESMPs follow a self-organization and bottom-up approach (McAfee, 2009), that is, interaction is coordinated by the ‘collective intelligence’ of the employees (Erol et al., 2010) without imposing ‘predetermined work<sup>fl</sup>ows, roles and responsibilities, or interdependencies among people, but instead allow them to emerge’ (McAfee, 2011: 1). By this means, ESMPs provide a number of affordances that make them distinct from ICTs commonly used for internal communication and collaboration in organizations (Treem and Leonardi, 2013; McFarland and Ployhart, 2015): First, they provide knowledge-workers with insights into the conversational interactions of other organizational members. Second, the traces of these interactions are persistently available. Third, as interactions are visible and persist over time, knowledge-workers can learn from an organization-wide array of people, networks, and content, which opens up new opportunities for social learning in organizations (Leonardi et al., 2013).

In order to allow the realization of such bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, the passive consumption of content is important, but rather insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient. More importantly, employees must actively contribute their knowledge to ESMPs, as the provision of user-generated content is a basic requirement for the retrieval of content by others and as it forms the foundation for conversational interactions. Although some studies do not distinguish or specify the mode of usage they investigate into (e.g., Kügler et al., 2013; Antonius et al., 2015), we argue that the passive as well as active use of ESMPs are two different facets of individual ESMP-adoption and use (e.g., Günther et al., 2009).

Given the widespread use of public and free social media as a private means of communication, employees are usually expected to adopt ESMPs quickly and to use them actively. However, ESMPs often suffer from a lack of active participation, as many organizational members resist behavioral changes like switching from commonly used collaborative tools or communication channels to ESMPs (McAfee, 2009). Hence, ESMPs are seen as not ‘living up to their promises of better communication, collaboration and productivity’ (Healey, 2011: 5). Lack of participation, which is a general problem associated with the implementation of new ICTs in organizations (e.g., Davenport, 1998; Brown, 2001; Loonam et al., 2014), may not only result in cost-intensive and disruptive failure, but may also inhibit the organizational learning essential for realizing sustainable competitive advan tages (Huang et al., 2003). Against this background, an important question concerns which factors drive employees to actively participate in ESMPs. Insights into these factors may function as points of reference in increasing employees active participation in ESMPs. However, present research offers only limited guidance to overcome low active participation in ESMPs, as social media use in organizations is currently grounded in theory only insuf<sup>fi</sup>ciently and there are only few empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings that provide insights into the antecedents of active participation (Treem and Leonardi, 2013; McFarland and Ployhart, 2015).

While research has provided some <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding why people use public and free social media like Facebook (e.g., Gangadharbatla, 2008; Ross et al., 2009; Lin and Lu, 2011; Nadkarni and Hofmann, 2012), as well as the organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts likely to result from implementing enterprise social media (e.g., Andriole, 2010; Chui et al., 2012), little is known about the factors that drive employees to actively participate in social media platforms in work environments (Löcker et al., 2014). To date, knowledge management (KM) research has focused on single social technologies (Jarrahi and Sawyer, 2013), for example, on wikis (e.g., Stocker et al., 2012), blogs (e.g., Kaiser et al., 2009), microblogs (e.g., Müeller and Stocker, 2011), and enterprise social networks (e.g., Stein<sup>fi</sup>eld et al., 2009), but has largely neglected the growing importance of social network-based ESMPs that integrate these technologies (e.g., Koplowitz, 2014; Kügler et al., 2015). Moreover, those studies that investigate the adoption of ESMPs in IS research existing to date do not specify the mode of usage under investigation, that is, knowledge-sharing or knowledgeseeking. Due to this, initial <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors that drive employees to share knowledge in ESMPs are blurred (e.g., Kügler et al., 2013; Antonius et al., 2015).

Being aware of the general value of parsimonious models such as Davis’ Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh and Davis, 2000; Venkatesh and Bala, 2008), the Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Theory of Acceptance and Usage of Technology (Venkatesh et al., 2003) or Delone and McLean’s Model of Information Systems Success (DeLone and McLean, 1992) in explaining individuals’ adoption of new ICTs, we have reason to believe that explaining knowledge-sharing in ESMPs by transferring <sup>fi</sup>ndings or models from related research is subject to limitations: First, ESMPs are distinct from centralized, intermittent, and repository-based information systems (IS) that primarily cater to information storage and retrieval rather than to conversational knowledge-sharing. Second, the conditions, practices, and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of active participation in ESMPs are likely to differ from those in public and free social media (e.g., Kügler et al., 2013; Treem and Leonardi, 2013). Third, models such as TAM do not properly capture the transparent, participatory, conversational, and collaborative nature that characterizes ESMPs (Günther et al., 2009), that is, ‘[s]ome of the features unique to social media may challenge the ability of existing theories and frameworks to explain [employees’] cognition, affect, and behavior’ (McFarland and Ployhart, 2015: 1653) regarding ESMP-usage. As these features are likely to shape an individual’s willingness to share knowledge, which is widely considered as an essential precondition for successful knowledge-sharing in organizations (Bock et al., 2005), and an individual’s motivation to participate is likely to determine the quality and quantity of knowledge contributions in ESMPs (Janzik, 2010), scholars have called for dedicated research on ESMPs (Richter et al., 2011) and on the factors that motivate employees to engage in knowledge-sharing in such platforms (Wattal et al., 2010).

In an attempt to answer this call, our study analyzes the effects of extrinsic and intrinsic motivations on knowledgesharing in ESMPs. To this end, a questionnaire survey is <sup>fi</sup>elded to employees of a multinational knowledge-intensive German <sup>fi</sup>rm that introduced a social network-based ESMP 10 months prior to our study. The study contributes to research by shedding light on the adoption of ESMPs in the workplace in a general sense (e.g., Treem and Leonardi, 2013), and on the effects of extrinsic and intrinsic motivations on knowledgesharing in social network-based ESMPs in particular (e.g., Löcker et al., 2014). In so doing, the paper identi<sup>fi</sup>es motivational factors that determine ESMP-adoption and reduces a relevant gap in IS and KM research (e.g., Kaiser et al., 2009; Paroutis and Al Saleh, 2009; Papadopoulos et al., 2013). From these results, managerial implications are derived. The paper is structured as follows: First, we outline the conceptual background regarding ESMPs as well as knowledge-sharing and its motivational determinants. Next, the research model and hypotheses are developed and the research methodology is outlined. After presenting the results, discussing them in light of the extant literature, and deriving managerial implications from them, we identify limitations to our research and point at opportunities for further research.

## Conceptual background

## Enterprise social media platforms

Companies use social media in two primary ways: for external communication with parties like vendors, customers, and job candidates (e.g., Kaplan and Haenlein, 2010) and for internal communication and collaboration. Regarding the latter, an increasing number of companies implements social network based applications like IBM connections, Jive, Atlassian Con-<sup>fl</sup>uence, or proprietary solutions that bundle social media technologies such as wikis, blogs, and social networking services, into a single platform for internal use (Koplowitz, 2014; Kügler et al., 2015). Based on the principles of web 2.0, these platforms are mostly characterized by voluntary, unpaid participation in the creation and modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of content (Chui et al., 2012). They often emulate private social network ing services like Facebook in look, feel, and functions (Leonardi et al., 2013), and allow employees to share various types of media (e.g., text messages, photos, activity streams) (Kim et al., 2010). As ESMPs’ design, features, and surround ing conditions are likely to vary among companies, we subsequently outline their basic characteristics. Drawing on Leonardi et al.’s work, we characterize ESMPs as ‘[w]eb-based platforms that allow workers to (1) communicate messages with speci<sup>fi</sup>c coworkers or broadcast messages to everyone in the organization; (2) explicitly indicate or implicitly reveal particular coworkers as communication partners; (3) post, edit, and sort text and <sup>fi</sup>les linked to themselves or others; and (4) view the messages, connections, text, and <sup>fi</sup>les communicated, posted, edited and sorted by anyone else in the organization at any time of their choosing’ (Leonardi et al., 2013: 2).

Although many conventional ICTs cater to one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst three aspects of Leonardi et al.’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition, ESMPs differ substantially from conventional ICTs as they integrate these functions into one platform. In contrast to ‘capsuled’ communication channels like e-mail and telephone (McAfee, 2006), ESMPs provide a means for communication among employees that is at once visible throughout the organization, persistent over time, and editable. Moreover, they reveal the associations between communication partners and between users and content (Treem and Leonardi, 2013; Vaast and Kaganer, 2013), facilitating the ability to trace requests in and contributions to the ESMP back to speci<sup>fi</sup>c users and to form social networks. This transparent, interactive, and participatory nature of ESMPs contrasts the access-control of applications that organizations commonly use for communication and collaboration, that are often dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to use and administer, are time-consuming, in<sup>fl</sup>exible, and, most problematically, lack the opportunity for social interaction (Paroutis and Al Saleh, 2009).

The virtual environment for conversational interactions among employees provided by ESMPs facilitates the construction of shared cognitive patterns and conceptual frames among employees (Hendriks, 1999). These patterns and frames enable the sharing of implicit ‘know how’ and ‘know why,’ which is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to capture and store in knowledgebased repositories (Yoshioka et al., 2001). Hence, ESMPs are not only effective in overcoming temporal and spatial barriers to knowledge-sharing, but also in reducing social barriers, which are considered to be the most dif<sup>fi</sup>cult problem associated with knowledge-sharing in geographically dispersed settings (Hendriks, 1999). Against this background, ESMPs facilitate a shift ‘from centralized to decentralized, from intermittent to continuous, and from repository-based to emergent knowledge sharing’ (Majchrzak et al., 2013: 40). However, in order to exploit ESMPs’ full potential, knowledge workers must not only passively retrieve content but, more importantly, must actively contribute valuable content, that is, sharing knowledge that is useful for others to solve workrelated problems or to develop new ideas.

## Knowledge-sharing and its motivational determinants

While some researchers have distinguished knowledge from information (e.g., Huber, 1991), others have used the terms interchangeably, arguing that there is limited practical bene<sup>fi</sup>t in making such distinctions (Alavi and Leidner, 2001; Bartol and Srivastava, 2002). In line with this argument, we consider the term knowledge to encompass information, experience, ideas, and expertise, all of which are useful in performing tasks for organizations (Bartol and Srivastava, 2002; Wang and Noe, 2010). While explicit knowledge is codi<sup>fi</sup>ed and easily transmittable, tacit knowledge is bound to individuals and ‘has a personal quality, which makes it hard to formalize and communicate’ (Nonaka, 1994: 16). Thus, the creation, capture, and transmission of tacit knowledge require social interactions between employees (Ratcheva, 2008; Boateng et al., 2010), to which social media cater in particular.

Accordingly, knowledge-sharing occurs when an individual provides his or her acquired knowledge to other organizational members (Bock et al., 2005; Hsu et al., 2007). Scholars have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a wide range of factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge-sharing behavior (e.g., Bock et al., 2005; Cabrera et al., 2006; Wang and Noe, 2010). With regard to the use of social media, they are comprised of technological, organizational, and individual factors (Paroutis and Al Saleh, 2009; for a comprehensive review of knowledge-sharing determinants, see Wang and Noe, 2010). In particular, research has pointed at the prominent role of individual motivation for knowledgesharing, which is based on the fact that knowledge resides primarily in individuals (Bock et al., 2005), and physical and mental effort has to be exerted on the part of the knowledgeholder to externalize and share knowledge. Regarding knowledge-sharing in online communities such as ESMPs, individual motivation is likely to determine ‘the quality and quantity as well as the selection and modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of contributions’ (Janzik, 2010: 251). Likewise, it is argued that ‘[o]nly when employees are willing to share knowledge with colleagues can organizations begin to manage knowledge resources effectively’ (Lin, 2007: 136). The prominent role of individual willingness in the processes of knowledge-sharing should be taken into account when analyzing determinants of knowledge-sharing in ESMPs. Against this background, a motivational perspective seems promising (Kietzmann et al., 2012), which has previously been applied to the use of information technology (e.g., Lee et al., 2005; Kim et al., 2007; Lin and Lu, 2011), to knowledge-sharing (e.g., Osterloh and Frey, 2000) and more recently, to the active participation in business-related online communities (Löcker et al., 2014).

Motivation is widely considered the most important driver for individual behavior, as it determines its strength, intensity, and persistence – and its direction or purpose (Lee et al., 2005). As one of the two broad classes of motivation, extrinsic and intrinsic (Ryan and Deci, 2000b), extrinsic motivation drives individuals to perform an action ‘because it is perceived to be instrumental in achieving valued outcomes that are distinct from the activity itself, such as improved job performance, pay, or promotions’ (Davis et al., 1992: 1112). From an economic (or, more precisely, opportunistic) perspective, one could argue that employees are more inclined to perform a speci<sup>fi</sup>c action – in our case, to share knowledge in an ESMP – the more positive the perceived individual outcomes of this action (Osterloh and Frey, 2000). Extrinsic motivational factors are comprised of factors such as <sup>fi</sup>nancial bene<sup>fi</sup>t, peer recognition, reputation, and reciprocity (Janzik, 2010).

By contrast, intrinsic motivation ‘refers to doing an activity for the inherent satisfaction of the activity itself’ (Ryan and Deci, 2000b: 71). In this case, the process of performing a behavior is motivation in itself (Ryan and Deci, 2000a) because the behavior ‘is interesting, involving, exciting, satisfying, or personally challenging’ (Amabile, 1997: 39). Intrinsic motivational factors comprise, for example, enjoyment, internal satisfaction, altruism, or the feeling of competence and autonomy (Janzik, 2010).

In order to foster active participation of employees in ESMPs, a sound understanding of the effects of extrinsic and intrinsic motivations on knowledge-sharing in ESMPs is needed. To this end, we perform a quantitative empirical study. In the following section, we describe our study’s research model and the hypotheses development in detail.

## Research model and hypotheses development: the effects of extrinsic and intrinsic motivations on knowledge-sharing in ESMPs

In this section we apply the notions of extrinsic and intrinsic motivation, which is a common approach in IS (e.g., Davis et al., 1992; Kim et al., 2007) and KM research (e.g., Osterloh and Frey, 2000; Kaiser et al., 2009) to analyze and explain individual IT-usage and knowledge-sharing behavior. As motivation to participate is considered a critical success factor for ESMPs (Janzik, 2010), applying these notions seems promising for an analysis of knowledge-sharing in ESMPs. We focus on non-monetary extrinsic motivational factors such as reputation and reciprocity and intrinsic motivational factors, for two primary reasons: First, prior research suggests these factors are likely to be the main determinants of knowl edge-sharing in online communities such as ESMPs (Lakhani and von Hippel, 2003; Wang and Fesenmaier, 2003). Second, no <sup>fi</sup>nancial rewards are provided for active participation in the ESMP we investigate (which is likely to be the case for most ESMPs, as this would contradict the principles of social media (McAfee, 2009; McAfee, 2011) and may have negative effects on intrinsic motivation (e.g. Frey, 1997)).

According to the Social Exchange Theory (Blau, 1964; Emerson, 1976), which has successfully been applied to knowledge-sharing in prior studies (e.g., Liang et al., 2008), individuals base their interactions with others on a selfinterested evaluation of costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. While costs refer to the time and effort expended, bene<sup>fi</sup>ts refer to economic (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>nancial rewards) and socio-emotional resources (e.g., status, approval, respect) (Lin, 2007), also referred to as social rewards (Wasko and Faraj, 2005). Provided that these resources are valuable to the individual, they are likely to motivate him or her to perform a particular behavior, that is, to share knowledge in an ESMP. Adopting an extrinsic motivational perspective and presuming that no economic rewards are provided for knowledge-sharing (as is the case in our study), we assume that expected gains in reputation as well as reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts determine employees’ knowledge-sharing in ESMPs.

Reputation is considered to be a valuable resource for people both in of<sup>fl</sup>ine and online communities (Kügler et al., 2015). In line with Baker and Bulkley, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne reputation as ‘a person’s history of actions toward others – speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, how helpful the person has been to others in the same social system’ (Baker and Bulkley, 2014: 1496). We argue that a good reputation in a work context is desirable, as it increases one’s social recognition among peers and one’s status within the peer group (e.g., Lerner and Tirole, 2002), which may result in individual economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (e.g., promotions, salary increases). Based on the Social Exchange Theory, we argue that, by sharing their knowledge in ESMPs, employees can signal their personal competence and expertise to organizational members who may rely on others’ knowledge to solve work-related problems (Kankanhalli et al., 2005; Kaiser et al., 2007). Thus, employees can earn respect from co-workers and/or superiors and ‘establish themselves as experts’ (Ardichvili et al., 2003: 69). Moreover, as ESMPs provide a way to share knowledge that is visible throughout the organization, persistent over time, and traceable to an individual person (Treem and Leonardi, 2013; Vaast and Kaganer, 2013), employees can increase their professional reputations in the organization on a larger scale than they could through capsuled communication channels like e-mail or telephone (Constant et al., 1996). Research on electronic networks supports the assumption that the opportunity to build one’s professional reputation motivates employees to share knowledge in virtual communities (e.g., Wasko and Faraj, 2005; He and Wei, 2009). Gains in reputation can motivate employees to share knowledge even if the knowledge recipient is personally unknown to the knowledge contributor (Constant et al., 1996). Against this background, we argue that employees who believe that knowledge-sharing in an ESMP positively affects their professional reputations are more likely to use an ESMP than are those who do not hold such beliefs. Hence, our <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis is as follows:

Hypothesis 1: The greater the expected positive effects of knowledge-sharing in an ESMP on professional reputation, the more likely an employee is to share knowledge in an ESMP.

The concept of reciprocity strongly relates to social exchange and is considered a relevant determinant of active participation in online communities (Janzik, 2010). Reciprocity occurs directly or generalized. Whereas direct reciprocity is based on the principle of ‘I help you, and you help me, generalized reciprocity functions according to the principle of ‘I help you, and you help someone else’ (Baker and Bulkley, 2014: 1493) in return. While the former requires only the two parties directly involved in the exchange process to achieve reciprocity, the latter requires that the act of helping is visible to a wider community of people so that someone else can provide reciprocity. ESMPs make the act of helping visible throughout an organization, thus making it possible for employees who share knowledge in ESMPs to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from direct as well as generalized reciprocity. Reciprocity may either be provided by the knowledge recipient directly involved in the resource exchange who feels a moral obligation to give back for receiving (Coleman, 1988; Baker and Bulkley, 2014), or by strangers that have bene<sup>fi</sup>ted from the knowledge provided to the platform. Moreover, other community members who feel a moral obligation to give back to the community for receiving may provide reciprocity, too (Wasko and Faraj, 2000). Reciprocity may result in a condition of mutual obligation, where knowledge contributors repeatedly provide their knowledge and expect help and useful information from others in return (Kollock, 1999; Lin, 2007). Previous research has indicated that reciprocity is a strong motivator for knowl edge-sharing in virtual communities (e.g., Wasko and Faraj, 2005), which applies even when knowledge-sharing occurs through weak ties (Granovetter, 1983), that is, between employees who have similar professional backgrounds but who may never have met or have met only once or twice. It is this kind of knowledge-sharing that ESMPs facilitate particularly. Therefore, we argue that, if employees believe that reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts will result from knowledge-sharing in an ESMP, they will be more inclined to share their knowledge than if they do not believe so. In line with this argument, our second hypothesis is as follows:

Hypothesis 2: The greater the expected reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts related to knowledge-sharing in an ESMP, the more likely an employee is to share knowledge in an ESMP.

However, <sup>fi</sup>ndings from prior research on online communities indicate that extrinsic motivation may not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to explain employees’ voluntary, active participation in ESMPs (e.g., Lakhani and von Hippel, 2003; Wang and Fesenmaier, 2003). In light of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we assume that intrinsic motivation resulting from intrinsic motivational factors such as enjoyment in helping others or a feeling of competence when performing a particular behavior, positively affects knowledge-sharing in ESMPs, too.

Enjoyment in helping others derives from the concept of altruism (Lin, 2007). While enjoyment generally refers to ‘the extent to which [an] activity (…) is perceived to be enjoyable in its own right, apart from any performance consequences that may be anticipated’ (Davis et al., 1992: 1113), enjoyment in helping others results from the act of helping, such as when one helps coworkers solve work-related problems (Organ et al., 2006). Current research supports the assumption that employees are not only extrinsically motivated but also intrinsically motivated to share knowledge because they enjoy helping others (e.g., Wasko and Faraj, 2000; Lin, 2007).

This view also applies to knowledge-sharing in corporate KM systems (He and Wei, 2009). With regard to knowledgesharing in ESMPs, studies on the creation of digital public goods have underpinned the (assumed) positive effect of enjoyment in helping others (e.g., Wang and Fesenmaier, 2003; Wasko et al., 2009). For example, Wang and Fesenmaier (2003) found that enjoyment in helping others was a predictor of knowledge-sharing in a virtual travel community. Accordingly, we argue that enjoyment in helping others motivates employees to share knowledge in ESMPs. Hence, our third hypothesis is as follows:

Hypothesis 3: The greater an employee’s enjoyment in helping others, the more likely he or she is to share knowledge in an ESMP.

Furthermore, we assume that the perception of self-efficacy in knowledge-sharing is a relevant intrinsic factor determining knowledge-sharing in ESMPs. Self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is a form of selfevaluation that determines what behaviors to perform and how much effort and persistence to put into the behaviors in question (Hsu et al., 2007). In line with Deci and Ryans Cognitive Evaluation Theory, we argue that a feeling of selfef<sup>fi</sup>cacy enhances a person’s intrinsic motivation to perform a particular behavior, as it leads to the satisfaction of a person’s psychological need for competence (Deci et al., 1999). Against this background, we believe that the perception of self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy can serve as a self-motivational force of knowledge holders to contribute knowledge to an ESMP (Kankanhalli et al., 2005) Conversely. if emplovees feel they lack knowledge that is valuable to other organizational members, employees are less likely to contribute knowledge to an ESMP. As Bandura put it, ‘[p]eople avoid activities that they believe exceed their coping capabilities, but they undertake and perform assuredly those that they judge themselves capable of managing’ (Bandura, 1982: 123). Based on the notion of behavioral control (Ajzen, 1991), whether a person feels capable of performing a speci<sup>fi</sup>c action or not depends on internal and external factors. While external factors include resources like time and managemen support (the latter of which Paroutis and Al Saleh (2009) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a determinant of web 2.0-based knowledgesharing in the workplace), internal factors include a feeling of self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy (Bock et al., 2006). The concept of self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy has been successfully applied to a variety of contexts and objects o research, including the use of computers (Compeau and Higgins, 1995; Johnson and Marakas, 2000), knowledgeseeking (Bock et al., 2006), and knowledge-sharing (Hsu et al., 2007). Previous research has indicated that self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in knowledge-sharing motivates employees to share knowledge in electronic knowledge repositories (Kankanhalli et al., 2005), communities of practice (Ardichvili et al , 2003), and employee weblogs (Papadopoulos et al., 2013). As knowledgesharing in ESMPs is highly visible throughout the organiza tion, we argue that self-con<sup>fi</sup>dence about one’s ability to contribute valuable knowledge to the platform is crucial for knowledge-sharing to occur. Based on the concept of selfef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, we argue that employees with a keen sense of selfef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in knowledge-sharing are more likely to share knowledge in ESMPs than are those with no such sense. We believe that integrating self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy into our analysis substantially adds to our understanding of the motivational factors deter mining knowledge-sharing in ESMPs, as ‘in any given instance, behavior would be best predicted by considering both self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and outcome beliefs’ (Bandura, 1982: 140), in our case employees’ perceptions of the effects of knowledgesharing in an ESMP on professional reputation or reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Therefore, our fourth hypothesis is as follows:

## Method

## Sample and procedure

In order to analyze the effects of extrinsic and intrinsic motivations on knowledge-sharing in ESMPs, we collected data by means of a web-based survey of (potential) users of an ESMP in June 2014. The survey was designed for employees of a large German, multinational high-tech <sup>fi</sup>rm that offers knowledge-intensive products and services, primarily to business clients. While the companies’ headquarter is located in Germany, there are subsidiaries in more than 190 countries, so knowledge-workers are geographically dispersed. The portfolio of products and services comprises, but is not limited to, automation, drive, and healthcare technologies. Prior to our survey, we consulted one of the organizations’ internal communication managers in charge of the intranet of one company division, who supported the survey’s <sup>fi</sup>elding. We conducted a preliminary interview, which con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that work was highly knowledge-intensive, collaborative, and required employees to share knowledge with other organizational members frequently. Here, knowledge-work is com prised of engineering, manufacturing, and maintaining high tech products and performing commercial tasks (e.g. tender preparation, order processing).

In light of prevailing problems regarding internal knowledge-sharing among the geographically dispersed workforce, the company introduced a social network-based ESMP. After a successful pilot phase, the platform was made available to almost 330,000 employees worldwide about 10 months prior to our survey. Intranet access and a company email address are suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for employees to log in to the platform, whose user interface is comparable to the social networking service Facebook. By integrating web 2.0 functions like social networking, searching, tagging, commenting, and authoring information, the platform facilitates sharing business-related knowledge, that is, information, expertise, experience, and ideas on a global basis. To this end, employees can (1) set up and maintain an individual user-pro<sup>fi</sup>le, comprising information such as job title, job description, organizational unit, skills, education or contacts, can (2) seek knowledge by following co-workers, joining interest groups, customizing and watching their personalized information stream, searching for colleagues, keywords or topics, and can (3) share knowledge by posting status updates to keep the community informed about individual activities, commenting on others posts, answering questions and providing various kinds of content to the community (e.g., articles, documents, pictures, or videos). To achieve a high level of transparency of conversational interactions among employees, knowledgerequests and contributions as well as the names of the respective employees requesting or contributing knowledge are visible to all users of the ESMP. Using the platform is voluntary and neither seeking nor sharing knowledge is rewarded monetarily.

To avoid the complexities of multi-language surveys and the potential confounding effects of national culture on knowledge-sharing behavior, we decided to limit the study to participants in Germany. For practical reasons, we conducted the survey in one of the company’s business divisions. The division provides healthcare technologies and related services like magnetic resonance imaging, laboratory diagnostics, and IT-solutions to business clients. Prior to the survey, we pilottested a draft questionnaire with fellow researchers and employees of the communications department of the business division supporting the survey’s <sup>fi</sup>elding. Based on their remarks, we adapted some of the questionnaire’s content and wording. The <sup>fi</sup>nal version of the questionnaire was approved by the business divisions’ works council.

Participation in the survey was voluntary and was not rewarded monetarily. We distributed the web link to the survey to 7946 of the division’s employees, using an internal mailing list. To increase the response rate, the division’s communication department encouraged the employees to participate in the survey (Baruch and Holtom, 2008). A total of 492 employees (response-rate = 6.23%) completed the questionnaire. According to the division’s communication department, which frequently runs internal surveys and thus could properly evaluate the response-rate of our survey, this was a common response rate for internal surveys. Among the participants, 78.7% were male, a percentage that is consistent with the gender distribution in the business division, as 75.4% of the workforce is male. To ensure anonymity (which was a major requirement of the works’ council), we measured age using a categorical variable (1 ≤ 29; 2 = 30–49; 3 ≥ 50). Forty-<sup>fi</sup>ve respondents were ≤ 29 years old, 287 respondents were 30–49 years old, and 160 respondents were ≥ 50 years old. The average respondent had been employed by the company for almost 18 years. Sixty-one of the participants (12.4%) indicated that they had actively participated in the ESMP. As no log-<sup>fi</sup>les or similar measures existed to calculate the actual rate of active participation in the ESMP, we had to rely on the predictions put forward by the division’s communication department to verify our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. According to the communication department, presumably 10% of all registered users actively participated in the system at the time of our data collection, which supports our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## Measures

The dependent variable in this study is ‘sharing knowledge in an ESMP.’ The respondents were instructed to think of knowledge-sharing as making their knowledge, that is, information, expertise, experience, and ideas helpful in performing work-related tasks, available to co-workers. Respondents were asked to indicate whether they used the ESMP to share knowledge by writing new comments, commenting on existing posts, and answering questions, through a binary yes/no scale.

Independent variables were measured through 7-point Likert scales, anchored by ‘strongly disagree’ and ‘strongly agree’ and by ‘does not apply at all’ and ‘applies fully.’ All of the study’s constructs were measured as respondents perceptions. To increase the measurement instrument’s validity, most constructs were measured through validated scales that were translated into German. Some scale items were slightly reworded in the questionnaire to refer speci-<sup>fi</sup>cally to the name of the company’s ESMP, although the term ‘the ESMP’ functions as a placeholder in the subsequent scale description. (For a complete list of items, see Table 1.)

## Reputation

The reputation construct focused on the respondents’ perception that reputational effects would result from knowledgesharing in the ESMP. The construct was measured using a four-item scale (Kankanhalli et al., 2005). Respondents were asked to indicate the extent to which they agreed with statements like ‘sharing my knowledge through [the ESMP] improves my image within the organization’ and ‘people in the organization who share their knowledge through [the ESMP] have more prestige than those who do not.’ The scale showed very good reliability (α = 0.91).

## Reciprocal benefits

The reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts construct assessed the respondents’ belief that knowledge-sharing would result in their future requests for knowledge being reciprocated. The construct was measured using a four-item scale (Lin, 2007). Respondents were asked to indicate to what extent they agreed with statements like ‘when I share my knowledge with colleagues through [the ESMP], … I strengthen ties between existing members of the organization and myself’ and ‘… I expand the scope of my association with other organization members.’ The scale showed good reliability (α = 0.88).

## Enjoyment in helping others

This construct assessed the degree to which respondents derived pleasure from knowledge-sharing in the workplace. Enjoyment in helping others was measured using a four-item scale (Lin, 2007). Respondents were asked to indicate the extent to which they agreed with statements like ‘I enjoy sharing my knowledge with colleagues’ and ‘sharing my knowledge with colleagues is pleasurable.’ The scale showed very good reliability (α = 0.96).

## Self-efficacy in knowledge-sharing

This construct assessed the respondents’ perception of his or her ability to provide knowledge to the ESMP that is valuable to the organization. The construct was measured using a fouritem scale (Kankanhalli et al., 2005). Respondents were asked to indicate the extent to which statements like ‘I have con<sup>fi</sup>dence in my ability to provide knowledge [to the ESMP] that others in my organization consider valuable’ and ‘I have the expertise needed to provide valuable knowledge [to the ESMP] for my organization’ applied to them. Because of initially inadequate scale reliability, two inverted items were dropped from the scale, which improved the scale’s reliability signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly $( \alpha = 0 . 6 8 )$

To rule out alternative explanations for knowledge-sharing in the ESMP, we included several control variables in the analysis. As motivation theories and prior KM research have suggested that individual work experience and job function may affect knowledge-sharing behavior (e.g., Lin, 2007), we included job tenure and job function as control variables. We also included task interdependence as a control variable (measured with a two-item scale adapted from Jarvenpaa and Staples (2000) $( \alpha = 0 . 8 0 ) )$ , which prior research has indicated as a structural factor likely to determine knowledge-sharing (e. g., Jarvenpaa and Staples, 2000; Staples and Webster, 2008). Moreover, we included private active social media use as a control variable, as research on technology acceptance has suggested that experiences with information technology in<sup>fl</sup>uences its use because the user is already familiar with the technology (e.g., Venkatesh and Bala, 2008). Finally, we collected demographic data, including gender and age (using a categorical variable $( 1 \leq 2 9 ; 2 = 3 0 { \bar { - 4 } } 9 ; 3 \geq 5 0 ) \rangle$ ).

Model 1 shows that the control variables gender, age, job tenure, and job function do not have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on knowledge-sharing in the ESMP, whereas task interdependence has a weak positive effect and private active social media use has a strong positive effect. Model 1 explains 14.0% of the variance (pseudo R<sup>2</sup>), primarily because of the strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence of private active social media use.

Model 2, which includes the predictor variables, allows us to test Hypotheses 1, 2, 3, and 4. The results of the logistic regression analysis show that expected gains in reputation signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase the likeliness that an employee engages in knowledge-sharing in an ESMP, as the odds ratio is higher than 1, the effect is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and the statistical power exceeds 0.80 at a signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level of α = 0.05 (OR = 1.496; P = 0.002; statistical power = 0.972). Therefore, Hypothesis 1 <sup>fi</sup>nds support. The results also indicate that anticipated reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase the likeliness that an employee shares knowledge (OR = 1.353; P = 0.018; statistical power = 0.847), so Hypothesis 2 also <sup>fi</sup>nds support. The results do not indicate a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact of enjoyment in helping others on the likeliness that an employee engages in knowledge-sharing (OR = 0.870; P = 0.359; statistical power = 0.339), so Hypothesis 3 receives no support. However, the regression analysis indicates that self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in knowledge-sharing signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increases the likeliness that an employee engages in knowledge-sharing (OR = 1.590; P = 0.000; statistical power = 0.993), so Hypothesis 4 is supported.

According to Bosco et al., our results indicate moderate effect sizes for the impact of reputation (Cohen’s d = 0.22), reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Cohen’s d = 0.17), and self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in knowledgesharing (Cohen’s d = 0.26) on knowledge-sharing in ESMPs, and no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect for the enjoyment in helping others (Cohen’s d = −0.08), as the benchmarks for moderate effect sizes in applied psychology and social science regarding behavioral intention and behavior of employees range from 0.11 to 0.27 (Cohen’s d) (Bosco et al., 2015). Moreover, the statistical power of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects is adequate, as it ranges from 0.847 to 0.993 at a signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level of α = 0.05.

First, we sought to determine whether the extrinsic motivational factors interacted with each other and, second, whether the intrinsic motivational factors interacted with each other. Third, we sought to determine whether extrinsic and intrinsic motivational factors interacted with one another. None of these potential interactions were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. ## Discussion and implications

A primary goal of employing social network-based ESMPs is the support and improvement of communication and collaboration among employees. This goal cannot be achieved without employees’ active participation in such platforms. In light of reports that ESMPs attract too little active participation among knowledge workers (Healey, 2011), the primary objective of our study was to investigate into the factors that motivate employees to share knowledge in ESMPs. Gaining insights into these factors reduces a relevant research gap on the determinants of knowledge-sharing in ESMPs and enables practitioners to promote active participation.

Three of the four hypotheses made to this end found support. The regression analysis revealed that extrinsic and intrinsic motivational factors both drive employees to use ESMPs. Expected gains in reputation and anticipated reciprocal benefits were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as extrinsic motivational factors determining knowledge-sharing in ESMPs. Our analysis showed that the more <sup>fi</sup>rmly an employee believes that knowledge-sharing results in social rewards, that is, improved reputation and reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, the more likely it is that he or she shares knowledge in the ESMP. These results are in line with other studies’ <sup>fi</sup>ndings that point out the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of extrinsic motivation on information technology use in general and ICT-based knowledge-sharing in the workplace in particular (e.g., Kankanhalli et al., 2005; Papadopoulos et al., 2013). The results of our study allow a transfer of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings to ESMPs. Plausible explanations for the impact of both kinds of extrinsic motivational factors analyzed in this study are that ESMPs are geared to the purpose of encouraging communication and collaboration and that the study was conducted in a work context. ESMPs are designed to make employees’ knowledge visible and accessible throughout the organization. This opportunity to provide ones’ knowledge to a larger number of colleagues than possible via email, telephone, or face-to-face communication, raises the chance of realizing bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from social rewards such as professional reputation and reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, as knowledge-sharing is visible throughout the organization and thus surpasses the visibility of knowledgesharing activities using conventional ICTs. These social rewards may result in individual economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (e.g., promotions) at a later stage. Therefore, it should come as no surprise that respondents expecting social rewards are more likely to actively participate in ESMPs.

Contrary to the common notion that the intrinsic motivational factor enjoyment in helping others determines knowledge-sharing in work environments (e.g., Lin, 2007) and virtual communities (e.g., Wasko and Faraj, 2000), we found no evidence for this relationship for the social network-based ESMP we investigated. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is remarkable, as enjoyment is widely considered to be one of the core reasons people use public and free social media (e.g., Lin and Lu, 2011). A reason for this contrary <sup>fi</sup>nding may be that, as the primary goal of ESMPs is improving communication between and collaboration of knowledge workers, they would rather use ESMPs to gain extrinsic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts than for intrinsic enjoyment.

Moreover, the results of the study indicate that employees who have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their ability to add valuable knowledge to an ESMP are more likely to participate actively than are those who have no such con<sup>fi</sup>dence. A plausible explanation for this <sup>fi</sup>nding is that knowledge-sharing in ESMPs is highly transparent, so employees with low perceptions of their self-efficacy in knowledge-sharing may fear contributing knowledge that turns out to be of low value to their co-workers. Therefore, the results of our study support the assumption held by KM researchers that subjective perceptions of self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in knowledge-sharing function as a selfmotivational force that determines individuals’ knowledgesharing behavior (e.g., Hsu et al., 2007; Papadopoulos et al., 2013). Overall, our study provides valuable empirical insights into the effects of extrinsic and intrinsic motivations on knowledge-sharing in ESMPs and broadens our understanding of critical success factors for individual ESMP adoption.

Against this background, our study contributes to existing literature in several respects: First, it provides insights into the adoption of social media platforms in enterprises, which adds to research on enterprise social media in a general sense (e.g., McAfee, 2006; Leonardi et al., 2013). Second, it sheds light on the motivational factors that determine employees’ active participation in ESMPs (e.g., Kaiser et al., 2009; Paroutis and Al Saleh, 2009; Kügler et al., 2013). As IS’ success, and consequently ESMPs’ success, largely depend on employees’ adoption and use (DeLone and McLean, 1992; Lee et al., 2005), insights on the underlying factors of ESMP adoption are valuable to both IS and KM researchers and to practitioners, who may wish to promote employees’ active participation in ESMPs.

By empirically investigating the effects of extrinsic and intrinsic motivations on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c mode of use, knowledgesharing, our study identi<sup>fi</sup>es the individual determinants of active participation in ESMPs. It shows that immaterial extrinsic motivational factors (gains in reputation and reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts) play a decisive role in employees’ decisions to share knowledge in ESMPs. This <sup>fi</sup>nding corroborates research on determinants of virtual knowledge-sharing in the workplace (e.g., Kankanhalli et al., 2005; Wasko and Faraj, 2005; He and Wei, 2009; Kaiser et al., 2009), and allows a transfer of previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings on conventional KM systems to ESMPs, which provide visibility of the communicative interactions of others to knowledge workers and make these communicative interactions persistently accessible over time. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that employees engage in knowledge-sharing in ESMPs in order to gain reputation in the community of ESMP members and to strengthen ties with other organization members, both being primary affordances of ESMPs (Treem and Leonardi, 2013).

Our results also help to clarify how intrinsic motivation affects social media-based knowledge-sharing in the workplace. The results show that self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in knowledge-sharing positively affects knowledge-sharing in ESMPs, which corroborates research on the determinants of knowledge-sharing in work-related virtual communities (e.g., Hsu et al., 2007), electronic knowledge repositories (e.g., Kankanhalli et al., 2005), and weblogs (e.g., Papadopoulos et al., 2013). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also show that employees with low levels of selfef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in knowledge-sharing are less likely than others to actively use ESMPs. In accordance with the concept of psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999), a plausible explanation for this <sup>fi</sup>nding is that people with low levels of selfef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in knowledge-sharing do not feel comfortable expressing themselves in a highly transparent ESMP. Therefore, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also contribute to research on organizational error culture (Edmondson, 1999; Van Dyck et al., 2005; Zhao and Olivera, 2006). Besides identifying motivational factors that determine knowledge-sharing in ESMPs, our study contributes to theory by revealing that intrinsic enjoyment in helping others does not affect knowledge-sharing in ESMPs. Thus, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings corroborate Amabile’s proposition that, ‘although combinations of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation are common, one is likely to be primary for a given person doing a given task’ (Amabile, 1997: 44), which is also in line with Deci et al., who argue that extrinsic motivation is likely to ‘crowd out’ intrinsic motivation (Deci et al., 1999). Our results suggest that, in contrast to private active social media use and the creation of public goods, which are largely driven by enjoyment (Lin and Lu, 2011), extrinsic motivational factors predominate in a corporate context (Richter et al., 2011).

Moreover, our study indicates that a relatively low number of employees has adopted the ESMP investigated. A plausible explanation for this is that, as ‘people’s perception of the utility of a technology is formed differently when that technology is used in the workplace rather than outside of it’ (Treem and Leonardi, 2013: 147), only few employees recognize how they can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from active participation in the ESMP. As suggested by Orlikowski’s research on the implementation of new ICTs, employees need appropriate cognitive mental models that help them to properly assess a technologies’ potential. If this is not the case, employees ‘may resist using it, or may not integrate it appropriately into their work practices’ (Orlikowski, 1992: 3), which might also be the case for knowledge-sharing in ESMPs, as they substantially differ from conventional ICTs and yield speci<sup>fi</sup>c bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (e.g., gaining reputation, reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts), which may not be obvious at a <sup>fi</sup>rst glance (Leonardi et al., 2013).

Besides these contributions to research, our results have managerial implications, as our study provides insights on motivational success factors that may function as points of reference for increasing employees’ active participation in ESMPs. First, as employees believing that reputational gains will result from knowledge-sharing in ESMPs are more likely to participate than are those who have no such belief, managers should ensure that mechanisms are in place that facilitate and highlight the positive effects of knowledgesharing in ESMPs on professional reputation. To this end, ESMPs should ‘keep track of past actions’ (Wang and Fesenmaier, 2003: 42) and provide a visualization of individuals’ knowledge contributions. While the quantity of knowl edge contributions can be counted and displayed on the platform, additional functions should be in place that enable knowledge-seekers to evaluate the quality of others’ knowl edge contributions. Highlighting helpful knowledge contributions may not only improve the dissemination of valuable knowledge within the organization, which is a core aim of ESMPs, but may also increase the knowledge contributor’s reputation within the community of users. To this end, managers could assign certain statuses to users in order to improve knowledge contributors’ visibility in the ESMP. In addition, managers should openly acknowledge and highlight knowledge-sharing in ESMPs in of<sup>fl</sup>ine contexts to increase non-users’ awareness that supervisors value active participation in the platform, and that it positively affects professional reputation. Second, management should ensure that knowledge-sharing results in individual reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. To this end, superiors should establish a strong collaborative norm within the organization that ensures that knowledge-seekers reciprocate knowledge-sharers. Third, management should work to improve employees’ self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in knowledge-sharing. A set of basic rules could help to reduce employees’ anxiety associated with uncertainties about what kind of knowledge-sharing is acceptable and what violates corporate security rules (Ardichvili et al., 2003). Highlighting that any kind of contribution is welcome, regardless of its value to others, could help to provide a sense of psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999) encouraging employees to contribute their knowledge to the ESMP. To this end, (top) managers should function as role models by using the platform, thus becoming advocates of technological change and enabling greater ESMP-success (Loonam et al., 2014). Finally, speci<sup>fi</sup>c trainings can help knowledge-workers to appreciate the potential that active participation in ESMPs’ bear for their everyday work as well as their organizations’ effectiveness.

## Limitations and future research

As is the case with most studies, our study has some inherent limitations:

First, data were collected in only one company in order to rule out the variance that would have been generated if data were collected in multiple companies with various forms of ESMPs. Although this approach is common in IS and KM research (e.g., He and Wei, 2009), it limits external validity. Therefore, in order to be able to generalize the results, further research is needed that replicates the study in additional companies and organizational settings.

Second, the study was based on a broad understanding of the term ‘knowledge’; that is, we made no distinction between explicit and implicit knowledge in the questionnaire. We took this approach, as distinguishing between forms of knowledge could have posed dif<sup>fi</sup>culties to the respondents in answering the questionnaire and would have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increased its length and complexity. However, we acknowledge that the explanatory power of the results is limited by this broad de<sup>fi</sup>nition of knowledge.

Third, the study is based on cross-sectional data. As the ESMP under investigation had been in place for only 10 months when data were collected, it overlooks the possibility that employees’ beliefs regarding the predictor variables of this study and their usage behavior can develop over time as the ESMP becomes a state-of-the-art communication and collaboration tool in the long run. To detect and analyze such developments, longitudinal data are necessary.

Fourth, we have to acknowledge that nowadays, knowledgeworkers have access to a large variety of ICTs that allow them to share knowledge with their colleagues. As these tools have been available prior to the implementation of social network based ESMPs, employees are more experienced in using them and do not need to learn how to handle them anymore. Hence, using ESMPs for knowledge-sharing is likely to be impacted by the availability of these alternative technologies. Conceptualizing the effects of intrinsic and extrinsic motivations on knowledge-sharing in ESMPs thus comes with certain limitations. However, we believe that the increasing dissemination of ESMPs (Kügler et al., 2013; Koplowitz, 2014), the relatively low number of active participants (Healey, 2011), and the fact that social media use in organizations is currently grounded in theory only insuf<sup>fi</sup>ciently (Treem and Leonardi, 2013; McFarland and Ployhart, 2015) underscores the importance to understand the reasons as to why employees use social network-based ESMPs to openly share their knowledge. Our investigation into the motivational factors that drive knowledge-sharing in ESMPs may function as a starting point for further research in this regard.

Fifth, the triangulation with qualitative data using in-depth interviews with potential users would help in the investigation of why employees are hesitant to share knowledge in ESMPs. This perspective should be included in future research to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the antecedents of active participation in ESMPs.
