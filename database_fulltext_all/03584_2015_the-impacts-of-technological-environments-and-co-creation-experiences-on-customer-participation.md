# The Impacts of Technological Environments and Co-creation Experiences on Customer Participation

Abstract: Social media offer unprecedented opportunities for companies to involve their customers in value co-creation through the use of social media sites. However, why customers participate in co-creation activities on these firm-initiated social media sites has not received enough attention in research. Based on the stimulus-organism-response paradigm, this study develops a model to theorize how site characteristics improve customers’ co-creation experiences, and subsequently affect their intention of future participation in co-creation. The results indicate that future participation intention is determined by customer learning value, social integrative value, and hedonic value experiences, which are influenced by perceived task-relevant and affection-relevant cues.

Keywords: Social media, co-creation experience, S-O-R framework, task-relevant cues, affection-relevant cues, future participation intention

## 1. Introduction

Driven by the infusion of social media in customer-firm and customer-customer interactions, the digital media is moving into a consumer-centric co-creation era (Zwass 2010). Social media that combine user-generated contents with social networking features are used by users to create, initiate, and circulate online information among online social networks (Tang et al. 2012). Examples include social networking sites (SNSs), blogs, micro-blogs, Wikipedia, social shopping sites, and other content sharing sites such as YouTube and Flickr (Liang and Turban 2011). There are two main reasons that social media are seen as an enabling technology for co-creation. First, social media use the latest technological advances that allow for real-time, media-rich, and highly personalized interactions and social collaboration between firms and customers (Johannessen and Olsen 2010), thus enabling firms to involve their customers in product plan, product development, and product marketing activities (Rishika et al. 2013). The increased interactions between customers and businesses provide customers with a sense of “empowerment” that further drives their desire to engage in co-creation activities (Hoyer et al. 2010). Second, social media facilitate firms and customers to create contents on social media, termed as marketer-generated content (MGC) and user-generated content (UGC) respectively, as well as allowing customers to efficiently share MGC and UGC in social networks (Goh et al. 2013, Rishika et al. 2013). These two kinds of contents about a brand and its products in online social communities are engines for co-creation (Zwass 2010). More than 1.5 million businesses have launched brand communities on social media platforms to leverage the potential of social sharing (a kind of co-creation activities) to co-create marketing value (Goh

#

et al. 2013). For instance, Dell has created a corporate Twitter profile to co-opt customer competencies in disseminating information (e.g., new product information and promotion information) for marketing purposes. Meanwhile, marketers, on behalf of Dell, generate contents on Dell-initiated Twitter site.

Without a doubt, the success of these businesses that aim at tapping into the economic value of co-creation activities depends on the large number of customers participating in value creation activities (Nambisan and Baron 2009). Thus, failure to create appropriate incentives to motivate customers to engage in participation would jeopardize the viability of a firm-initiated social media site (FüLler et al. 2009). However, little work has been done to examine customers’ motivation behind participation in value co-creation in the phase of product marketing (Berger and Milkman 2012, Kohler et al. 2011). This research aims to fill in this gap. We draw upon the stimulus-organism-response (S-O-R) model to theorize how characteristics of firm-initiated social media sites improve customers’ co-creation experiences, and how experiences subsequently affect their intention of future participation in co-creation.

This study brings forth a few important contributions to the extant literature. First, although prior research on co-creation has examined the antecedents of customer participation, the focus is on the new product development phase (FüLler et al. 2009, Hoyer et al. 2010) and the product support phase (Nambisan and Baron 2007, Nambisan and Baron 2009) of value creation. For instance, FüLler et al. (2009) investigated the determinants of consumer intention to participate in future co-creation activities in the new product development phase from the consumer empowerment perspective. Hoyer et al. (2010) presented a conceptual framework to theorize the antecedents and consequences of

#

co-creation at each stage of the new product development processes from the perspective of the degree of consumer co-creation. Nambisan and Baron (2007) examined the antecedents of customer participation in product support from an integrative perspective of interaction-based customer benefits and affective evaluation. Our study differs from the extant literature in significant ways: we focus on the product marketing phase and explain co-creation behavior from a co-creation experience perspective. Further, in our setting, co-creation is valuable primarily as a mechanism of information dissemination rather than a way of capitalizing on customers’ innovative knowledge that will improve the innovative productivity of the organizations (Kohler et al. 2011). Second, we employ the S-O-R model to link the characteristics of social media to customers’ participation behavior. Although the extant studies have enriched our knowledge about how the human-computer interface influences consumer behavior (Jiang et al. 2010, Parboteeah et al. 2009), little attention has been paid to the emerging social media. Based on a generic typology of the online environmental cues proposed by Eroglu et al. (2001), we focus on two kinds of characteristics: task-relevant cues and affection-relevant cues. These two characteristics capture key features of firm-initiated social media sites and thus shed light on this new phenomenon. Third, based on prior studies (Kohler et al. 2011, Nambisan and Baron 2007, Nambisan and Baron 2009), we develop a framework of co-creation experiences including customer learning value, social integrative value, and hedonic value for the social media environment. We believe this framework would be useful for examining the internal state of customers in the context of social media.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The next section presents the theoretical background of the paper. The third section proposes a research model based on the S-O-R framework. The research methodology is discussed in the fourth section, followed by the analysis of results in the fifth section. The final section concludes with a discussion of the results, the theoretical and practical implications, as well as limitations and future research directions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. The S-O-R framework

This study builds on the S-O-R paradigm in environmental psychology (Mehrabian and Russell 1974), which depicts how the various aspects of the environment as stimuli (S) affect people’s internal organisms (O), and in turn behavioral responses (R). In the context of online retailing, stimuli refer to the design features of the online environment which consumers interact with (Eroglu et al. 2003). The organisms pertain to internal processes and structures intervening between the stimuli and the final responses emitted, which consist of perceptions, experiences, and evaluations (Jiang et al. 2010). The responses represent consumer behaviors in terms of approach and avoidance behaviors (Eroglu et al. 2003).

The use of the S-O-R model as the overarching theory is appropriate in this study for two reasons. First, the S-O-R model has been applied extensively in previous studies on online consumer behavior (e.g., Eroglu et al. 2003, Parboteeah et al. 2009). Their findings have supported the applicability of this model in explaining customers’ internal reactions and behavioral responses to environmental cues. Second, given the critical roles of site characteristics and customers’ experiences in influencing their behaviors in social media, the S-O-R model provides a parsimonious and structured manner to examine the effects of site features of social media (stimuli) on customers’ evaluations of their co-creation experiences (internal organisms) and in turn their intention to participate in future co-creation activities (external responses).

## 2.2. Site characteristics as environmental stimuli (S)

In social media, the interaction between customers and the company is mediated by the technological characteristics of the media (Animesh et al. 2011). Thus, it is intuitive that customers’ experiences of interaction/co-creation in a firm-hosted social media site would be influenced by the characteristics of the co-creation environment (i.e., the social media site). The technological features of social media sites not only reflect their objective properties independent of customers but also their subjective properties as perceived by customers (Jiang et al. 2010).

Information Systems (IS) Researchers have asserted the salience of task-relevant (TR) behavior (Eroglu et al. 2001, Parboteeah et al. 2009, Ping 2013). Given that customer interactions in social media vary in the nature of the customer task and affection (Wang and Zhang 2012), our study focuses on these two kinds of site characteristics, namely TR cues and AR cues. TR cues and AR cues as site characteristics can be understood in two ways. First, they can be seen as structural properties of a social media site. TR cues refer to all descriptors on the site which facilitate and enable the customer’s goal attainment, including security, ease of navigation, and information fit-to-task (Eroglu et al. 2001), while AR cues are affective elements that can induce a customer’s core affect (Ping 2013). Second, they can be understood as dynamic qualities as experienced by customers. Since our study focuses on customers’ perception, experience and behavior, the TR and AR cues here refer to the subjective properties of social media sites as experienced by the customers. Thus, we examine the quality of such cues as perceived by the customers.

TR cues are regarded as utilitarian in nature as a result of the orientation toward the efficient and effective execution of customer tasks, which will determine how a customer reacts to an online medium (Parboteeah et al. 2009). Given the importance of TR cues in the online retailing environment, Eroglu et al. (2001) posited that online environmental cues consist of two general categories (high and low TR cues), and these cues will influence shoppers’ organismic states, which then affect their shopping behaviors. In a similar vein, Parboteeah et al. (2009) asserted that various environmental cues on a site can be classified into two groups, namely task-relevant cues manifested as information fit-to-task and mood-relevant cues manifested as visual appeal. Their study found that high-quality task-relevant and mood-relevant cues increase the likelihood and magnitude of online impulse buying. Adapting from Parboteeah et al. (2009), this study conceptualizes perceived TR cues as perceived information fit-to-task and defines it as a customer’s perception of the extent to which information presented on a site is accurate and appropriate for directly supporting the fulfillment of the customer task.

AR cues are specific features of a site that can manifest the affective quality of the site (Ping 2013). In the online channel, music (Wu et al. 2008), images (Chowdhury et al. 2008) and visual presentations (Park et al. 2008) are found to be distinctive affective cues. These cues are crucial in creating an atmosphere to make the customer experience more pleasant (Eroglu et al. 2001). AR cues have been studied as environmental cues containing affective information that can influence emotions and cognitive processing strategies (Ping 2013).

#

Thus, AR cues on a site induce a customer’s cognitive and emotional reactions to the site, which then influence his or her interaction behavior. Visual appeal (Loiacono et al. 2007) relates to the visual elements such as image and video, which act to enhance the overall look of a site and lead to positive affective reactions (Parboteeah et al. 2009). Thus, perceived visual appeal is operationalized as a proxy for perceived AR cues in our study and is defined as a customer’s perception of the extent to which visual elements presented on a site induce the customer’s positive affection (Ping 2013).

TR and AR cues are not mutually exclusive. Rather, they can be complementary or mutually dependent and unified. AR cues are low-task-relevant cues that can indirectly support the fulfillment of customer tasks by making the customer experience more pleasant. Hence, these two types of cues are complementary to each other in the orientation toward task completion. On the other hand, AR cues often act as the presentation formats of TR cues to improve information vividness. For example, informational graphs and videos are typical AR cues that can enhance the visual appeal of a site, while these cues include useful task-relevant information. In this case, TR and AR cues are mutually dependent and unified in enhancing site quality.

## 2.3. Co-creation experiences as organisms (O)

Co-creation, as a fundamental area of e-commerce research (Zwass 2010), sheds light on the value creation process from a new perspective. It was originally defined in the late 1990s by Kambil et al. (1999) as co-production or joint production. Bendapudi and Leone (2003) reviewed the relevant literature on this topic and found two important themes. The first theme comprises early studies on co-creation which focus largely on the benefits that a firm obtains

#

from customer participation in co-production, including enhancing productivity gains (Lovelock and Young 1979, Mills and Morris 1986), optimizing customer segmentation (Song and Adams 1993), and increasing customer satisfaction (Czepiel 1990). The second theme focuses on identifying the motivations of customer participating in co-production, such as technology readiness (Dabholkar 1996) and provision of adequate training (Goodwin 1988). Armed with new connectivity tools, interconnectivity among customers has been increased (Johannessen and Olsen 2010), thus heralding the evolution of co-creation, that is, the shift of sponsored co-creation to autonomous co-creation (Zwass 2010). Prahalad and Ramaswamy (2000) predict the evolution and transformation of customers from passive recipients to active co-creators in the value creation process. In a similar vein, Matthing et al. (2004) argue for a newly customer-centric view of value according to which value is defined and co-created with the customer and determined by the customer on the basis of value-in-use. Co-creation is thus defined as the process in which customers take an active role and co-create value in the entire value chain (Prahalad and Ramaswamy 2004a).

Co-creation experience refers to the mental state of customers that results from their participation in the value co-creation process (Kohler et al. 2011). Given that customers’ co-creation experiences as the source and basis of value significantly influence their actual continued participation in value co-creation (Nambisan and Nambisan 2008), our study focuses on the co-creation experience. Previous studies in this stream focus on developing conceptual frameworks of the co-creation experience from various perspectives. Prahalad and Ramaswamy (2004b) pointed out the emergence of a new logic for value creation where value is embedded in personalized customer experience and the interaction between

#

customers and the firm. They first proposed a conceptual framework to build blocks of interactions between the firm and customers that facilitate personalized co-creation experience, namely dialog, access, risk-benefits, and transparency (DART) (Prahalad and Ramaswamy 2004b). Consistent with this theme, Nambisan and Baron (2007) examined customers’ actual co-creation experiences (interaction-based customer benefits) in online product forums and provided empirical support to Prahalad and Ramaswamy’s (2004b) assertion that customers’ experience in value co-creation is an important source of value. Further, Nambisan and Nambisan (2008) developed an analytical framework of virtual co-creation system consisting of four experience dimensions (pragmatic, sociability, usability and hedonic). In virtual customer environments, Nambisan and Baron (2009) developed a conceptual model to examine the motivation of customers participating voluntarily in value co-creation activities. Their model incorporates an integrated set of four benefits (cognitive benefit, social integrative benefit, personal integrative benefit, and hedonic benefit) that customers derive from their interactions in virtual customer environments. Based on the basic framework proposed by Nambisan and Nambisan (2008), Kohler et al. (2011) conducted action research to explore co-creation experiences in virtual worlds. Their work confirms the significant role of three experience components, namely pragmatic, sociality, and hedonic experience. These studies thus contribute to the theoretical base of co-creation experience frameworks.

Given the usability design principle of social media, we argue that the three experience components, namely, the pragmatic, sociability, and hedonic components, serve as the components of the underlying framework for the co-creation experience in social media. The

#

pragmatic dimension refers to the customer’s experience in realizing cognitive benefits that relate to the information acquisition process (Kohler et al. 2011). In the co-creation process of brand values, customer learning values, namely, knowledge about the brand (its underlying cultures, value propositions, strategies, technologies, and product sales) derived from brand-related learning, capture the nature of cognitive benefits. Previous studies on co-creation have asserted that the rationale of value co-creation is that customers engage in a learning process based on their interactions with the company (Nambisan and Baron 2009, Payne et al. 2008). Thus, this study conceptualizes the pragmatic dimension as customer learning value.

The sociability component refers to the underlying social and relational aspects of interactions between the company and its customers (Kohler et al. 2011). Given that contents and social relationships are the underlying characteristics of social media, the sociability component reflects the customer experience derived from social relationship building and strengthening among participating entities (i.e. customers and the company). Thus, the sociability component in our study is consistent with social integrative benefits which include the enhancement of social identity, social network, and a sense of belongingness (Nambisan and Baron 2009). Therefore, the sociability component is conceptualized as social integrative value.

Finally, the hedonic dimension of the co-creation experience refers to experiences which are mentally stimulating, entertaining, pleasant, and enjoyable (Kohler et al. 2011). Customers’ interactions in the social media could be a source of highly interesting and pleasurable as well as mentally stimulating experiences as a result of the playful nature of social media. Schau et al. (2009) observed value co-creation practices within brand communities and posit that participants derive social and hedonic values from the practices. Adapted from Nambisan and Baron (2009), we conceptualize the hedonic dimension as hedonic value.

## 3.1 Effects of TR cues

In our study, information fit-to-task represents the quality of TR cues and refers to the accuracy and appropriateness (the diversity and depth) of brand-related knowledge for

#

facilitating the efficient and effective execution of customer tasks (Parboteeah et al. 2009). It is natural to believe that better-quality TR cues on a site will induce higher customer evaluations and better customer experiences. Parboteeah et al. (2009) found that TR cues manifested as information fit-to-task significantly influence customers’ perceived usefulness of a site. On a firm’s social media site, the higher the extent of information fit-to-task in the interactions with a site, the greater would be the opportunities to acquire useful information and to learn about how to accomplish customer tasks efficiently and effectively. In other words, perceived information fit-to-task will determine a customer’s experience in obtaining learning values. Hence, we hypothesize:

H1a: Perceived TR cues manifested as perceived information fit-to-task are positively related to the customer learning value dimension of a customer’s experience.

Information fit-to-task represents the fit between site information and customer task, namely, the degree to which a site is congruent with the task’s characteristics (Parboteeah et al. 2009). On a firm-hosted social media site, it reflects the quality of TR cues including brand-related cognitive information which will facilitate more social interactions among customers (Nambisan and Baron 2009). Research on Twitter found that complete strangers may join a list purely because they like the contents that are being tweeted (Virk 2011). With more social interactions, customers will build and strengthen their social relationships and develop a stronger sense of social identification with peer customers. Previous studies on brand communities show that product-related contents link community members together and are critical for members to derive the associated social integrative benefits (Muniz Jr and O’guinn 2001, Nambisan and Baron 2009). Therefore, we argue that the higher the perceived information fit-to-task, the greater the potential to derive social integrative benefits.

H1b: Perceived TR cues manifested as perceived information fit-to-task are positively related to the social integrative value dimension of a customer’s experience.

Perceived information fit-to-task is also likely to increase the hedonic value derived from social interactions on a firm’s social media site, which is linked to pleasure, arousal, and flow experience (Wang et al. 2007). First, the S-O-R model suggests that environmental cues will influence people’s affective state such as pleasure, arousal, and dominance (Mehrabian and Russell 1974). Parboteeah et al. (2009) adapted the S-O-R model for e-commerce and found that, as environmental cues, TR cues have a positive effect on consumers’ perceived environmental cues will increase pleasure. Second, diversified TR information may induce significant enjoyments in customers by satisfying their brand-related curiosity (Muniz Jr and O’guinn 2001). The higher perceived information fit-to-task, the greater the extent of the improvement on the task-fulfilling competence, hence, the greater the mental stimulation that the customers may experience (Nambisan and Baron 2009). Thus, we may conclude that perceived information fit-to-task leads to the hedonic value. Third, customers’ social interactions facilitated by higher-quality TR information could be a source of hedonic experiences (Animesh et al. 2011, Schau et al. 2009). Social interactions among customers help them solve product-related problems, influence others or generate ideas, leading them to realize a sense of self-worth (Tsai and Pai 2012). When customers feel that engagement on the site reflects their personal values, they will find the co-creation activities to be more enjoyable and involving (Pagani and Mirabello 2011). Thus, customers’ perceived information fit-to-task would induce a hedonic experience. This argument has been supported by empirical studies on value co-creation, which show that the amount of product-related content enhances the experience of hedonic value (Nambisan and Baron 2009). Hence, we hypothesize:

## 3.2 Effects of AR cues

The affect appraisal theory posits that AR cues trigger people to react affectively in the form of learned affective evaluations (Ping 2013). In other words, in an interaction with a medium, AR cues would drive the corresponding affective and cognitive perceptions and evaluations. Thus, it is also likely that the quality of AR cues will influence customers’ perceived learning value, social integrative value, and hedonic value.

Perceived visual appeal represents a customer’s perception of the design esthetics of a firm’s social media site (Liu et al. 2013). In this study, we argue that perceived visual appeal positively correlates with a customer’s perceived learning value. First, since the visual design of a site is its dominant characteristic, it influences the customer’s perceptions and experiences (Liu and Goodhue 2012). For example, visual characteristics of a site are found to significantly influence immediate impressions of the site (Lindgaard et al. 2006). Second, from an information processing perspective, visual appeal enables a larger extent of customer attention as the result of its saliency in spontaneous responses. Therefore, a social media site with high perceived visual appeal may evoke a more elaborate memory trace, which facilitates learning and instills and enforces a sense of learning values. Hence, we

hypothesize:

H2a: Perceived AR cues manifested as perceived visual appeal are positively related to the customer learning value dimension of a customer’s experience.

Affective elements on a firm’s social media site arouse customers’ sense of emotional support, which facilitates understanding and results in a better relationship with the site (Schau et al. 2009). In other words, AR cues manifested as visual appeal elicit an affective experience of emotional concerns such as caring, understanding, and empathy (Liang et al. 2011). Therefore, visual appeal of a site fulfills customers’ needs and acts as a significant switching cost for customers who become to depend on it (Schau et al. 2009). This will instill and strengthen their feelings of belongingness and social identification with this site (Ren et al. 2012). Given the importance of visual appeal in influencing thoughts and behaviors (Liu et al. 2013), it may also motivate more social interactions among customers. These social interactions bring customers together and facilitate them to build stronger interpersonal bonds, that is, creating, enhancing, and sustaining ties among them (Kreijns et al. 2007, Ren et al. 2012). Hence, we hypothesize:

H2b: Perceived AR cues manifested as perceived visual appeal are positively related to the social integrative value dimension of a customer’s experience.

Researchers assert that affect provides value to a customer’s experience (Babin et al. 1994, Wang et al. 2007). The exposure to affect-loaded visual elements of a social medium will automatically activate the affective attitude toward the medium including perceived enjoyment and pleasure, which are positively correlated with the hedonic value (Eroglu et al. 2001, Ping 2013). Prior studies have found that various features representing AR cues such as visual appeal influence perceived enjoyment (Parboteeah et al. 2009, Van der Heijden et al. 2003). Therefore, it is reasonable to argue that perceived visual appeal of a firm-hosted social media site drives the hedonic value aspect of a customer’s experience.

H2c: Perceived AR cues manifested as perceived visual appeal are positively related to the hedonic value dimension of a customer’s experience.

## 3.3 Interaction effects of TR and AR cues

There can be an interaction effect between perceived TR cues and perceived AR cues on a customer’s co-creation experience. First, the affect as information theory suggests that affect can serve as information that influences how people process cognitive contents (Taute et al. 2011). Since visual appeal that relates to the exhibition of visual elements acts to induce customers’ positive affection, we argue that it will facilitate customers to process brand-related cognitive information in a more elaborate and comfortable way, thus creating a better experience. For example, if a site is visually attractive and pleasing, better experience will be elicited when a visitor interacts with brand-related cognitive information (Liu et al. 2013). On the other hand, if visual appeal of the site is low, customers may fairly quickly reject the cognitive information on the site as not worth dealing with (Liu and Goodhue 2012). Second, high visual appeal may also elicit positive affective reactions such as satisfaction, trust, and perceived quality to the site, which may lead customers to be more responsive to information diagnosticity, namely information fit-to-task (Liu and Goodhue 2012, Wang et al. 2010, Wells et al. 2011). For example, on a firm’s social media site with high visual appeal, customers may grant a greater importance to brand-related information. This will further strengthen the effect of information fit-to-task on the interaction experience with the firm.

#

The literature on cognitive science also suggests that even relatively mild shifts in affective reaction can exert on both cognition and behavior powerful effects, such as perceptions of the nature of information, creativity, and decision-making strategies (e.g., Forgas and George 2001; Lyuobmirsky et al. 2005). Further, IS research has asserted that increased visual appeal not only can affect evaluations of objective prosperities (e.g., usability) but also is a positive atmospheric benefit in a virtual context where a customer interacts with a site. Campbell et al. (2013) found that visual appeal positively affects perceived relationship rewards, namely, perceptions of overall possible benefits from interactions with a Web-based organization in an online business-to-consumer (B2C) relationship. Therefore, we posit that the impact of perceived information fit-to-task on a customer’s co-creation experience would be stronger when a customer perceives a high degree of visual appeal:

H3a: There is an interaction effect between perceived TR cues manifested as perceived information fit-to-task and .perceived AR cues manifested as perceived visual appeal on the learning value dimension of a customer’s experience, that is, the positive effect of perceived information fit-to-task on learning value experience will be stronger when a customer perceives a higher visual appeal.

H3b: There is an interaction effect between perceived TR cues manifested as perceived information fit-to-task and .perceived AR cues manifested as perceived visual appeal on the social integrative value dimension of a customer’s experience, that is, the positive effect of perceived information fit-to-task on social integrative value experience will be stronger when a customer perceives a higher visual appeal.

H3c: There is an interaction effect between perceived TR cues manifested as perceived information fit-to-task and .perceived AR cues manifested as perceived visual appeal on the hedonic value dimension of a customer’s experience, that is, the positive effect of perceived information fit-to-task on hedonic value experience will be stronger when a customer perceives a higher visual appeal.

## 3.4 Effects of co-creation experiences

Previous studies on co-creation have shown that learning, social integrative, and hedonic value motivate customers to participate in product support activities in virtual customer environments (Nambisan and Baron 2007, Nambisan and Baron 2009). Thus, extending these findings to social media, we propose that customers’ co-creation experiences regarding experience (hedonic value) will influence their future participation in brand value co-creation on the firm-initiated social media site:

H4: The customer learning value dimension of a customer’s experience is positively related to his or her intention to participate in brand value co-creation on the firm-initiated social media site.

H5: The social integrative value dimension of a customer’s experience is positively related to his or her intention to participate in brand value co-creation on the firm-initiated social media site.

H6: The hedonic value dimension of a customer’s experience is positively related to his or her intention to participate in brand value co-creation on the firm-initiated social media site.

## 3.5 Control variables

To test the research model, we also included several control variables known in the literature to affect future participation intention. We expected that young people and women feel more comfortable with social sharing (Nosko et al. 2010). Therefore, the present study controls for such effects by including gender and age in the model.

Prior literature on value co-creation has indicated that customer differences in tenure and product involvement affect co-creation behavior (FüLler et al. 2009, Nambisan and Baron 2009). Tenure is the membership duration of a customer in a virtual community (Nambisan and Baron 2009). It is defined here as the number of years that a customer has spent on a firm-initiated social media site. This study controls for customers’ tenure since customers who have participated in the firm-initiated social media site for a long time may feel obliged to maintain their participation. Product involvement refers to the importance of a certain product category for the customer (FüLler et al. 2009). It has been found to be an important construct in studying customers’ online behavior (Novak et al. 2000). In the current context, it reflects customers’ intrinsic motivation to participate in the interactions with a firm-initiated social media site (FüLler et al. 2009). High product involvement suggests that customers assign more importance to the product of the firm, which will drive them to engage in interactions with the firm-initiated social media site. Thus, highly product-involved customers will have a stronger desire to participate in brand value co-creation. Thus, product involvement is also included in the model.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Instrument

To ensure content validity, the items used to measure the constructs were adapted from the extant literature and modified to fit the study context. Perceived information fit-to-task was measured using three items adapted from Loiacono et al. (2007). Perceived visual appeal was measured using items adapted from Loiacono et al. (2007) and Watson et al. (1988). Measurement items for customer learning value, social integrative value, and hedonic value were adapted from Nambisan and Baron (2009). Measurement items for product involvement were adapted from Nambisan and Baron (2009). Intention of future participation was measured by adapting items used by FüLler et al. (2009) and Wang et al. (2007). Finally, measurement items for perceived usefulness were adapted from Davis (1989).

As the original items were in English, we conducted a back translation to ensure translation validity. First, a researcher whose native language is Chinese translated the source items from English into Chinese. Next, another researcher independently translated these items back into English. Subsequently, the two researchers compared the two English versions and revised the first Chinese version of the items together. We then invited a panel of experts in the e-commerce field to examine the face validity of the survey instrument. Based on their feedback, minor modifications were made to improve the comprehensiveness and user-friendliness of the measurement items. A pre-test of the survey instrument was conducted to conceptually validate the instrument. The final survey questionnaire is presented in Appendix A. All items were measured on a 7-point Likert scale, which ranged from 1 (not agree at all) to 7 (absolutely agree).

## 4.2. Data collection

Subjects for the study were Dell Inc.’s customers in China who were members or fans of the firm’s Sina Weibo site (http://weibo.com/dell). Dell’s Sina Weibo was chosen for the study for three reasons: (1) Micro-blog is a service recently receiving the most attention as a corporate news, technology discussions, product information, and coupons and deal information with customers (Wagner and Majchrzak 2007); (2) Dell’s Sina Weibo has become an invaluable tool for Dell China to listen to, engage, and converse with customers; and (3) Dell’ Sina Weibo is a very active and successful site in engaging customers in marketing activities and achieving marketing purposes on the Sina Weibo platform. Before taking part in marketing activities on a firm’s Sina Weibo site, individuals have to register for it online, thus the list of participants is available on the site.

The data collection was conducted in summer 2013. First, we identified customers who participated in Dell’s Sina Weibo. Dell China had been tracking the participants on Dell’s Sina Weibo since the inception of the site. Dell China matched its customer base in 2012 with the lists of participants. For this purpose, full names were used. Whenever there were multiple matches, the relevant individuals were removed from the study so only unique names with exact matches were retained. To ensure customer confidentiality, the matching process was conducted by Dell China, and we were provided with the data set containing only the customers’ email addresses. A total of 1000 customers were identified. Then, each customer was sent an e-mail requesting his or her participation in the study with a survey hyperlink. To encourage participation, we used a reciprocity argument in the survey.

## 5. Data analyses and results

## 5.1. Reliability and validity

We performed both principal components factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess the reliability and validity of the scales. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) statistic was 0.922, indicating that the data was amenable for factor analysis (Kaiser 1974). As shown in Appendix B, all indicators loaded on the expected factors. The factor loadings were higher than 0.7, while the cross loadings were lower than 0.4, suggesting good convergent and discriminant validity (Chin 1998).

Construct reliability and validity were further examined by CFA. As shown in Table 2, the Cronbach’s α and composite reliability (CR) value of each construct ranged from 0.897 to 0.967, both of which were above the suggested threshold of 0.7 (Straub et al. 2004) and exhibited a satisfactory level of reliability. For construct validity, both convergent validity and discriminant validity were examined. Convergent validity was confirmed by examining both the average variance extracted (AVE) and indicator loadings. As shown in Table 2, all AVE values were higher than the recommended level of 0.5 (Fornell and Larcker 1981). The standard loadings of all items were above the desired threshold of 0.7 and significant at 0.001. This showed a good convergent validity (Chin et al. 1997).

The discriminant validity was evaluated using two approaches. First, discriminant validity was assessed by comparing the square root of AVE for each construct with the correlations between that construct and other constructs (Fornell and Larcker 1981). Table 3 shows that the square roots of the AVEs (diagonal elements) were larger than the inter-construct correlations depicted in the off-diagonal entries, suggesting adequate discriminant validity. Second, for each pair of constructs, we compared the $\chi ^ { 2 } { \mathrm { - v a l u e } }$ for the baseline model (without constraining their correlation) with the constrained model (constraining their correlation as one). The $\chi ^ { 2 } { \mathrm { - d i f f e r e n c e } }$ test was performed for each pair of constructs. As reported in Appendix C, all $\chi ^ { 2 } { \mathrm { - d i f f e r e n c e } }$ tests were found to be significant (p $< 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , suggesting that the unconstrained model is superior to the constrained model in each case. That is, each pair of constructs could not be united as one construct. Thus, discriminant validity was adequate.

## 5.2. Competing model testing

The technology acceptance model (TAM) suggests that perceived usefulness is a crucial determinant of behavioral intention in technology adoption, such as online shopping (Benlian et al. 2012, Parboteeah et al. 2009), social commerce (Shin 2012), and e-service (Jingjun et al. 2013). Perceived usefulness represents the instrumental belief (Benlian et al. 2012) and captures a customer’s overall behavioral belief of the utilitarian value obtained from interactions with firm-initiated social media sites (Benlian et al. 2012). Therefore, we compared two research models to verify whether perceived usefulness played a significant role in explaining consumer behavior on social media sites. We added perceived usefulness to our hypothesized research model to develop an integrated model, which specifies all the paths in the hypothesized model as well as three additional paths related to perceived usefulness. First, we used AMOS 17.0 to analyze the hypothesized model and the integrated model. The results are presented in Fig. 2 and Fig. 3, which showed that the path coefficient values between two models were consistent except for those related to the newly included perceived usefulness. Customers’ co-creation experiences, namely, customer learning value, social integrative value, and hedonic value had stronger effects on intention of future participation compared to perceived usefulness. The explained variances $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } )$ for intention of future participation were 55.9% in the hypothesized model and 56.5% in the integrated model, 27

respectively. This demonstrated the comparative descriptive powers of the two models.

To compare the two models statistically, we tested the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value and fit indices. Since the two models have a nested relationship, they can be compared directly using a $\chi ^ { 2 }$ difference test (Bentler and Bonett 1980). As shown in Table 5, the fit values for the hypothesized model were better than those in the integrated model, showing that the integrated model had a relatively poorer fit than the hypothesized model. The aforementioned model comparisons indicated that the hypothesized model is superior to the integrated model. Thus, we did not include perceived usefulness in our research model.

## 6. Discussions and implications

## 6.1. Discussion of findings

This study generates some interesting findings. As the results indicate, characteristics of firm-initiated social media sites significantly influence customers’ co-creation experiences and subsequently their intention to participate in brand value co-creation in the future. First, regarding site characteristics, perceived TR cues and AR cues are found to have significant impacts on customers’ perceptions of learning value, social integrative value, and hedonic value. Moreover, perceived TR cues have a stronger effect on customer learning value $( \beta =$ 0.486) than perceived AR cues $( \beta = 0 . 3 1 9 )$ , and perceived AR cues have stronger effects on social integrative value $( \beta = 0 . 3 7 1 )$ ) and hedonic value $( \beta = 0 . 3 8 5 )$ than perceived TR cues (β $= \ 0 . 2 7 4$ and $\beta \ = \ 0 . 2 5 8$ , respectively). Our findings extend those of previous studies (Parboteeah et al. 2009, Wang et al. 2007), which suggest that TR cues have a greater effect on perceived utilitarian value (i.e. perceived usefulness) and AR cues including mood-relevant cues and social cues are more important for stimulating hedonic value. TR cues are productivity-oriented that aim to provide instrumental value to customers, and thus are better in motivating the pragmatic experience (Wu and Lu 2013). In contrast, AR cues aim to arouse positive affective experiences in customers, and are employed for pleasure and relaxation (Ping 2013). Therefore, perceived AR cues are more salient in predicting the sociability and hedonic experience.

Second, regarding the effects of co-creation experiences on intention of future participation, our results indicate that customer learning value, social integrative value, and hedonic value can predict intention of future participation, and hedonic value $( \beta = 0 . 3 0 0 )$

plays a greater role in determining participation intention than customer learning value (β = 0.185) and social integrative value (β = 0.159). This suggests that firm-initiated social media sites that can induce hedonic value in their customers may create a valuable competitive advantage. The findings are consistent with those from previous studies that hedonic value is the most prominent driver for using hedonic systems (Hsu and Lu 2004, Van der Heijden 2004, Wu and Lu 2013). Dell’s Sina Weibo mainly serves as a conduit of information sharing for customers to interact with Dell China and their peer customers. It is not commonly used for work-related tasks and is regarded as having a substantial hedonic orientation (Wu and Lu 2013). Furthermore, studies on social media have documented the considerable value customers place on hedonic experiences (Chung and Austria 2010, Shin 2012). Thus, customers participate in Dell’s Sina Weibo mainly to attain a positive psychological state of enjoyment, namely, a hedonic experience.

Finally, our results show that perceived TR cues have weaker effects on customer learning value and social integrative value when customers perceive higher-quality AR cues. A brief summary of our findings and comparison with the previous literature are presented in Table 7.

Previous studies assert that AR cues should have a stronger influence on customers who have low motivation to engage in detailed cognitive processing and limited access to processing resources (e.g., Ludwig et al. 2003, Cohen et al. 2008). #

Weibo customers show that customers often unconsciously visit site and participate in site activities during fragments of time, which suggests that customers have limited time and cognitive resource to process site cues. Thus, customers are less likely to cognitively elaborate on TR cues under strong AR cues as a result of resource constraints. In other words, strong AR cues induce customers to perform a low level of TR cues elaboration. According to the dual process theory, AR cues arouse simple heuristic information processing rather than systematic cognitive information processing under low elaboration and thus TR cues become relatively less important (Darke et al. 2006). Therefore, the positive effects of perceived TR cues on pragmatic and sociality experiences will be weaker when customers perceive higher-quality AR cues.

Also, contrary to our hypothesis, the moderating effect of perceived AR cues on the relationship between perceived TR cues and hedonic value is found to be insignificant. One plausible explanation is that diversified TR cues may induce significant enjoyment in customers and be a source of hedonic experience and thus their impact on hedonic experience remains stable under strong AR cues (Muniz Jr and O’guinn 2001).

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

First, few studies have empirically examined customer co-creation behavior in product marketing. Given that co-creation often occurs during the innovation process, the extant research on co-creation has focused mainly on the new product development phase of value creation (FüLler et al. 2009, Hoyer et al. 2010, Kohler et al. 2011). This study contributes to the co-creation literature by explaining co-creation behavior in the product marketing process from a co-creation experience value perspective.

#

Second, no study to our knowledge has established the link between the characteristics of a firm’s social media site and customer participation from a customer perspective (Berger and Milkman 2012, Rishika et al. 2013). Our study identifies two technological components of a firm-hosted social media site (TR cues and AR cues) as environmental stimuli from a customer perspective, and links these two technological features to the future participation intention based on the S-O-R model. The findings indicate that both TR cues and AR cues are indirect predictors of future participation intention.

Third, the current study contributes to the literature on social commerce by identifying the key dimensions of co-creation experiences. Given that most TAM-based research suggests that perceived usefulness plays a key role in customer behavior (Wu and Lu 2013), we perform an analysis to compare the utility of customer co-creation experiences with that of perceived usefulness. Customer learning value, social integrative value, and hedonic value are shown to have stronger impacts on customer participation than perceived usefulness. Thus, we confirm a systematic multi-dimensional framework of customer co-creation experiences that considers pragmatic, sociability, and hedonic components. The new knowledge obtained in this study augments the prior literature by providing an improved understanding of why one will choose to participate in co-creation in the era of social media.

Forth, this study uncovers the interaction effects between TR cues and AR cues in affecting customer experiences. Extant studies have ignored the potential interplays between these two kinds of cues, and our study is the first to fill this gap by capturing the intricate dynamics between these two technological characteristics in social commerce. Our study reveals negative moderating effects of AR cues on the relationships between TR cues and co-creation experiences. Especially, our study highlights that AR cues play an essential role in shaping the customer experience. Due to the ever-evolving nature of social commerce from content-centric to relationship-centric (Wang and Zhang 2012), AR cues have become imperative to the prediction of customer experience and behavior, which not only directly shape the customer experience but also strengthen the impacts of TR cues on customer experiences.

## 6.3. Practical implications

Some important practical implications can be derived from our research. First, this study examines the effects of TR and AR cues in shaping a customer’s experience. From the design perspective, distinguishing and understanding the roles of site characteristics allow designers to focus on areas that can be the most effective in inducing desired customer experiences. The findings suggest that pragmatic experience should be maximized by providing high-quality TR cues; simultaneously, the sociability and hedonic experiences should be boosted through the provision of high-quality AR cues.

Second, this study reveals the importance of both high-quality TR and AR cues in site design (Parboteeah et al. 2009). Visual appeal of a site can be improved by a proper use of fonts, color, background patterns, and images, as well as by embedding high-quality audios, animations, information graphics, and videos in the site (Liu et al. 2013). Ideally, virtual product experience should enable customers to experience products virtually, thus tapping their affective responses of enthusiasm and creativity. In order to enhance the quality of TR cues, such as information fit-to-task, sites need to focus on increasing the fit between site information and customer tasks rather than the breadth and depth of product-related content

#

in general. In this sense, practitioners may categorize customers by matching customer base and mapping click-streams to different categories of visiting tasks (i.e., specific product search, hedonic browsing, or product knowledge building), and provide different types of customers with personalized information based on their primary visiting goals (Tam and Ho 2006). In addition, site designers should also make such contents more accessible to customers. For example, when a customer has a specific product in mind, sites should provide easy to use and efficient search function. On the other hand, when a customer visits the site for knowledge building, it is better to present brand-related knowledge to facilitate the customer’s learning process.

Third, our study demonstrates that customer learning value is a key component of co-creation experiences that motivate customer participation. It is imperative for managers to understand how TR and AR cues should be constructed to support customer learning and enhance co-creation experiences. Firm-hosted social media sites can support customer learning manifested as remembering (a simple form of learning about customer attention) by providing attractive visual presentation such as images and videos (Payne et al. 2008). Another level of customer learning, namely internalization (a competence to interpret and assimilate knowledge), can be realized by constructing useful brand contents and appealing affective contents that take the customer’s task and emotional need into account (Payne et al. 2008). This will facilitate customers’ interpretation and assimilation of a firm’s brand knowledge and understanding of the brand’s value proposition, thus improving their learning value (Payne et al. 2008).

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

The limitations of this study should be taken into account before generalizing its findings. First, because the present study focused on customer involvement in product marketing, it was conducted in the context of a social medium and the data were collected using a technology-based firm’s social media site. Thus, generalizations of the findings to other product contexts should be performed with care. Furthermore, the context of interaction is likely to play an important role in co-creation experiences (Ping 2013). Future research should examine diverse product types and social media types so that the results obtained can be validated across different product categories or types of social media. Second, there exists high correlations among the research constructs, as evidenced in Table 3. This may be due to the research context, where each dimension of the virtual customer experience influences the others (Animesh et al. 2011, Wang et al. 2007). Future research can replicate our study to test if the high correlations remain. Third, given the scope of the study, there are other variables that were not included in the model. Future study can extend our model to include different customer psychological variables as causes or moderators, including customers’ goal and need, achievement motivation, and product involvement (Nambisan and Baron 2009). Finally, our study only considered two broad set of site characteristics as the independent variables. There is a need to identify and determine, at a finer granular level, variables manifested as TR cues and AR cues in social media. Future research can also replicate this study using experiments to control site characteristics.
