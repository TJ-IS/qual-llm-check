## Abstract

Crowdfunding founders increasingly rely on reward-based platforms to raise capital for their projects. However, the low success rate of reward-based project campaigns remains a concern for many founders and platform managers. Prior studies investigating participants’ motivations have focused on either the founder or backer perspective, with little research examining the joint effects of founder and backer motivations. Drawing on the theory of basic human values, we assert that founders and backers are strategic about their motivations for engaging in crowdfunding campaigns. We identify four basic human values—self-transcendence, self-enhancement, openness to change, and conservation—that may reveal their intrinsic motivations and investigate how the degree of congruence between founders and backers on these values can influence campaign success. Using data from Kickstarter, we first extract evidence of these four values from the founder’s project description and backers’ comments for each project and empirically test a moderated model of reward-based crowdfunding success. The findings indicate that congruences in the openness to change, self-transcendence, and conservation values between founders and backers positively relate to project campaign success, but these relationships weaken when the congruence becomes excessive. However, self-enhancement value congruence between founders and backers can consistently maintain a stable positive influence on project campaign success. Furthermore, linguistic conformity is positively related to campaign success but reduces the distinctiveness of the congruence effects related to self-transcendence and openness to change. The study’s findings contribute to the value congruence literature and provide insights on how to improve the chances of project success in reward-based crowdfunding.

Keywords: Reward-Based Crowdfunding, Participation, Self-Enhancement, Self-Transcendence, Openness to Change, Conservation, Value Congruence, Linguistic Conformity

## 1 Introduction

Reward-based crowdfunding, a fundraising approach in which project founders offer potential backers extrinsic benefits for their support, has grown in popularity in recent years, with a projected transactional value of US 1.2 billion by 2024 (Statista, 2024). Despite its growing popularity, reward-based crowdfunding faces significant challenges; for instance, statistics reveal that 60% of projects fail to meet their funding goals (Kickstarter, 2024), often due to founders’ struggles to attract backers. These high failure rates have spurred interest among academics and practitioners seeking to better understand the factors that drive success for reward-based crowdfunding campaigns (André et al., 2017; Jancenelle et al., 2018; Jiang et al., 2021).

While prior research has focused on backers’ extrinsic motivations, such as tangible rewards and financial returns (Jiang et al., 2021), there is an increasing recognition of the role of intrinsic motivations too— such as personal fulfillment, social impact, and reputational gain (Herrero et al., 2020; Ryu et al., 2020; Wald et al., 2019). Recent studies suggest that intrinsic motivations, including the desire for personal satisfaction or a sense of community, are crucial for understanding backer behavior in reward-based crowdfunding (Herrero et al., 2020; Nielsen & Binder, 2021; Ryu et al., 2020). Moreover, intrinsic motivations often represent shared interests between founders and backers, providing common ground that can enhance the alignment between these stakeholders (Gleasure & Feller, 2016).

The project narratives by founders and the comments by backers serve as rich indicators of diverse intrinsic motivations and highlight the personal connections that often drive crowdfunding campaign success (Martens et al., 2007; Nielsen & Binder, 2021; Zhou et al., 2018). However, backers’ intrinsic motivations are highly diverse, ranging from altruism and social benefits to personal recognition. This diversity complicates backers’ decision-making processes, making it challenging for founders to select which strategies will be most effective in driving the success of their crowdfunding campaigns (Bagheri et al., 2019; Gerber & Hui, 2013; Gleasure & Feller, 2016; Jiang et al., 2021; Nielsen & Binder, 2021). Consider the narrative description for Spark Makerspace, a crowdfunding project aimed at fostering a cooperative space for creative individuals.

Quote 1: We are forming as a nonprofit organization in which members cooperatively share tools, workspaces, office & meeting space, and retail storefront space to sell creations in downtown New London. Part of the Spark vision is to grow into a cooperative that starts other cooperative endeavors so that some of our shared projects become social enterprise businesses.

Quote 2: We want Spark to work like a community center in which people get to know their neighbors, so a majority of classes are led by Working Members sharing knowledge based on their expertise.

In Quote 1, the founder’s narrative is related to the shared motivation of societal benefits based on signaling self-transcendence, values that emphasize the welfare and interests of others. Quote 2 reflects conservation values, emphasizing the importance of preserving community stability through cooperative efforts, shared knowledge, and the continuity of community relationships.

On the other hand, consider the sample narrative comments from backers of the same project.

Quote 3: “I’ve really liked this kind of community workshop projects. They provide excellent outlets for people who like to have fun, create, experience, and learn” (Backer 1)

## Quote 4: “Do you need the help of a serious DIY-er. I am available most days and would love woodworking education” (Backer 2)

In Quote 3, the backer’s emphasis on fun and new experiences signals an openness to change, reflecting values that prioritize novelty, creativity, and independence. In contrast, Quote 4 strongly emphasizes self-enhancement, showcasing values related to personal success, achievement, and the desire to develop skills and competence through hands-on learning and participation.

These differing values highlight the complexity of stakeholders’ motivations and hence the need for innovative research approaches to better understand them (Cai et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2021; Shneor & Vik, 2020). To enhance the likelihood of reward-based crowdfunding success, founders need to strategically align their motivational values in such a way as to influence backers’ perceptions, interpretations, and motivation to back the project (Defazio et al., 2021; Moradi & Dass, 2019; Nielsen & Binder, 2021). One approach is for founders to tap into backers’ values through an appropriate framing of project narrative, which effectively serves as an interpretative framework that guides how backers perceive the project (Andrews et al., 2017; Borah, 2011; Falchetti et al., 2022). This framing aims to create congruence with backers and encourage them to support the project towards success.

Given that founders typically incorporate value frames in narratives to convey their motivation, while backers express their values in their comments on projects, we draw on Schwartz’s basic human values theory as the foundation for this study (Schwartz, 1997). Rooted in basic values (Schwartz, 1997), value frames are not arbitrary but are enduring principles that individuals hold (Brewer, 2002; Brewer & Gross, 2005; Gleasure & Feller, 2016; Nielsen & Binder, 2021). Since individuals maintain a stable set of values, Schwartz’s basic values provide an effective framework for understanding the similarities and differences in the motivations underlying individual behavior and decision-making (Schwartz, 1997, 2012). We argue that the impact of narrative framing can be enhanced when aligned with Schwartz’s values (Brewer, 2002; Brewer & Gross, 2005; Nielsen & Binder, 2021). However, given the differences in intrinsic motivations between founders and backers in crowdfunding, it is unclear how the values underlying these motivations could be utilized to help increase the likelihood of achieving a successful crowdfunding campaign. Therefore, we pose the following research question: How does value congruence between founders and backers affect crowdfunding success?

To address this research question, we analyzed data from crowdfunding campaigns on the Kickstarter platform, focusing on founder-backer interactions and project success. Through text analysis, we identified and extracted value frames from project descriptions and backers’ comments, measured the level of congruence between these two stakeholders, and assessed their curvilinear effect on the crowdfunding campaign’s success. Furthermore, we examined the moderating role of linguistic conformity—the extent to which founders and backers share a common language style—to explore the boundary conditions of value congruence. Linguistic conformity is crucial in reward-based crowdfunding because founders are expected to adapt their communication to the specific linguistic norms of different project types to enhance the project’s perceived legitimacy and relatability among backers.

The study findings demonstrate that congruences in openness to change, self-transcendence, and conservation values between founders and backers are positively related to project success in reward-based crowdfunding, but these relationships weaken when the congruence becomes excessive. In contrast, selfenhancement value congruence has a consistent positive relationship with project campaign success. Additionally, the study results reveal a trade-off. While linguistic conformity is positively associated with project success, it can reduce the distinctiveness of value congruence in self-transcendence and openness to change. Conversely, congruences in selfenhancement and conservation remain robust and unaffected by linguistic conformity, highlighting their consistent role in driving reward-based crowdfunding success.

This study makes several key contributions to value theory and crowdfunding literature. First, while prior research has often examined crowdfunding motivations from the perspectives of either project founders or backers separately (Gleasure & Feller, 2016; Jiang et al., 2020, 2021; Moysidou & Spaeth, 2016), we unveil the interplay between stakeholders’ value systems as a key intrinsic motivation for them to engage in a particular crowdfunding campaign. Second, from the human value theory perspective, the study focuses on leveraging human values as an intrinsic motivational strategy for achieving congruence across multiple dimensions, demonstrating that distinct value congruences directly influence crowdfunding success. Third, our findings reveal the boundary conditions that affect the role of value congruence in crowdfunding contexts, such as the tension between signaling distinct human values and conforming to linguistic norms. Methodologically, this study examines the impact of value congruence using observed crowdfunding data and offers insights into stakeholders’ (founders and backers) actual motivations for crowdfunding participation. Specifically, the findings provide insights into founders’ and backers’ motivations, which can be strategically leveraged by founders and platform managers to enhance the likelihood of crowdfunding success.

## 2 Background Literature

## 2.1 Motivations in Crowdfunding Participation

Crowdfunding is often categorized into four different models: donation, rewards, debt, and equity (Beaulieu et al., 2015). This study focuses on reward-based crowdfunding, which is known to encounter challenges with the complex and contradictory effects of extrinsic motivations (Bagheri et al., 2019; Davis et al., 2017; Gerber & Hui, 2013). Extrinsic motivations, such as exclusive rewards, can have a dual impact on backer behavior in reward-based crowdfunding (André et al., 2017; Jancenelle et al., 2018; Jiang et al., 2021). On the one hand, extrinsic motivations serve as incentives, encouraging more people to back projects for the expected tangible benefits (Bagheri et al., 2019; Gerber & Hui, 2013). On the other hand, extrinsic motivations can diminish backer’s internal drive (André et al., 2017). Such a reduction in intrinsic motivation can be problematic because, for backers, (1) the exclusive reward is well-known or publicized along with the project, and (2) they might feel less connected or committed to the project. Thus, given that extrinsic motivations are explicit on the platform, could the alignment of intrinsic motivations, which are not readily observable in rewards-based crowdfunding, between founders and backers explain the differences in projects gaining crowdfunding success? Because the underlying mechanism that explains individuals’ intrinsic motivations is based on their personal values, which are regarded as more reliable, stable, enduring, and consistent in guiding decision-making, we investigate the roles of diverse personal values in the potential success of crowdfunding campaigns (Boer & Fischer, 2013; Schwartz, 2012; van Dijk et al., 2019).

## 2.2 Values

Values represent a motivational construct that influences individuals as they perform expressive behaviors to achieve important goals (Schwartz, 2012). Values are considered stable predictors of human behavior because they play a dominant role in individuals’ cognitive structures (Brangule-Vlagsma et al., 2002). This study adopted personal basic human values to explain crowdfunding behavior (Gleasure & Feller, 2016; Nielsen & Binder, 2021; Schwartz, 2012). The rationale behind a value-based approach to understanding crowdfunding behavior is that, as explored in prior crowdfunding research (Moysidou & Spaeth, 2016; Wang et al., 2018a; Xiang et al., 2019), sentiments can change rapidly, often impulsively, and are heavily influenced by perception, while values are seen as foundational and generally stable (Schwartz, 2012).

While values could provide a robust framework for understanding crowdfunding behavior due to their stability, it is important to distinguish them from personality traits, which offer a broader, less specific perspective of individual motivational behavior. Values reflect what individuals consider important in their lives and directly guide their choices and actions (Schwartz, 2012). In contrast, personality traits, such as those in the Big Five framework (Costa & McCrae, 1999), which have also been examined in the crowdfunding literature (Neuhaus et al., 2022), are consistent patterns of behavior but do not prescribe specific actions (Blickle, 1996). Personality traits are broader in scope, reflecting general dispositions that influence a wide range of behaviors without directly dictating specific actions (Blickle, 1996; Costa & McCrae, 1999). Our focus on values as motivational constructs for studying crowdfunding behavior aligns with prior research (Gleasure & Feller, 2016; Moysidou & Spaeth, 2016; Nielsen & Binder, 2021; van Dijk et al., 2019).

## 2.3 Value Congruence

Value congruence occurs when values connect individuals and/or organizations together (Kristof, 1996). Value congruence provides an opportunity for passive information seeking by the backer to be reinforced by the founder, which causes the backer to feel that their contribution would be worthwhile. Generally, value congruence occurs when a person’s values are in harmony with their surroundings. By reinforcing a shared motivation to engage on a crowdfunding platform, i.e., what drives the backer is also what drives the founder, the founder is enabling the backer to justify why they should contribute and back the project. Studies on value congruence have been conducted in diverse areas, such as software development (Maruping et al., 2019), consumer service relations (Zhang & Bloemer, 2008), interorganizational relations (Schuh et al., 2018), and human resource management (Brown & Treviño, 2006). The organizational behavior literature highlights the role of value congruence in predicting employee and organizational attitudes and outcomes. Substantial research on organizational behavior has demonstrated that value congruence positively influences employee commitment, satisfaction, morale, and turnover intention (Edwards & Cable, 2009; Kristof, 1996; Kristof‐Brown et al., 2005). For example, previous research has shown that value congruence favorably affects trust, loyalty, and commitment between leaders and subordinates (Lau et al., 2007; Ostroff & Schulte, 2007) and influences consumers’ purchasing behaviors toward company brands (Sagiv & Schwartz, 2000). Similarly, the social psychology literature indicates that value congruence has a positive impact on relationship quality and outcomes (Arthur et al., 2006; Gaunt, 2006). These values are expressed in stakeholder communication and interactions. In this study, we focus on how values are framed in the interactions between the two sides (i.e., founders and backers) of reward-based crowdfunding campaigns.

As illustrated in the preceding discussion, although studies have investigated the motivations for participation in reward-based crowdfunding, the majority focusing on extrinsic motivations presented mixed results. In addition, most prior research on the role of values in online participation has not considered values as a strategic mechanism for attracting backers in crowdfunding. The growing popularity of rewardbased crowdfunding as a fundraising method for entrepreneurs presents an opportunity to understand the role of values as an intrinsic motivational strategy for both founders and backers. In crowdfunding, founders and backers exhibit a broad set of shared ideological values (Gleasure & Feller, 2016; Jancenelle et al., 2018, 2018). This study argues that understanding how these values align between founders’ and backers’ motivations is crucial for both parties to benefit from crowdfunding relationships (Burtch et al., 2015; Gleasure & Feller, 2016; Kuppuswamy & Bayus, 2017, 2018; Mollick, 2018; Moysidou & Spaeth, 2016). In donation-based crowdfunding, where there is no direct economic exchange, values such as benevolence and universalism are key drivers of donor engagement (Sneddon et al., 2020; van Dijk et al., 2019). This raises an important question: Can values also operate effectively in contexts characterized by economic exchange, such as reward-based crowdfunding? This study argues that even in reward-based crowdfunding, where economic exchange is central, founder-backer values and their congruence remain important, and understanding such congruence can provide deeper insights into backer behavior and enhance the effectiveness of crowdfunding efforts.

## 3 Theory and Model Development

## 3.1 Schwartz’s Value Theory

Schwartz’s theory of basic human values posits that values are core beliefs that guide individuals’ behavior in various life situations (Schwartz, 2012). This theory provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how such values influence decision-making, attitudes, and actions in diverse contexts, ranging from personal choices to social interactions. The theory identifies 10 basic human values, which are organized into four higherorder dimensions—self-transcendence, selfenhancement, openness to change, and conservation— based on their underlying motivations and compatibility. Schwartz’s theory of basic human values provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the complexities of human values and their implications across various contexts. Table 1 highlights how individuals prioritize values differently, based on their cultural and personal experiences, leading to variations in behavior and social engagement (Boer & Fischer, 2013; Sagiv & Schwartz, 2022; van Dijk et al., 2019).

The theory’s applicability spans multiple fields, including psychology, sociology, and organizational behavior, where it has been used to analyze personal values in the workplace. For instance, understanding the alignment between individual values and organizational goals has been shown to enhance employee satisfaction, motivation, and overall organizational effectiveness (Edwards & Cable, 2009; Erdogan et al., 2004; Maruping et al., 2019). Additionally, Schwartz’s theory has been used to examine value development across different life stages, particularly during adolescence, emphasizing how value structures evolve over time due to socialization and cultural influences (Cieciuch et al., 2024). In crowdfunding, past studies have used the theory to explore investor decision-making processes (Gleasure & Feller, 2016; Nielsen & Binder, 2021). Overall, Schwartz’s theory of basic human values provides a strong basis for studying human motivation and social interaction. In this study, we use the theory as the primary framework for understanding value congruence between founders and backers in rewardbased crowdfunding campaigns, as it offers a strong and distinct basis for examining individuals’ intrinsic motivations to participate in crowdfunding projects.

## 3.2 Contextualizing Value Congruence in Reward-Based Crowdfunding

In reward-based crowdfunding, campaigns often involve conceptual or early-stage products, where the exact specifications of the products, completion, and delivery times are uncertain, but there is a stated explicit reward for potential backers (Stanko & Henard, 2017). Backers face the decision dilemma of whether to support a conceptual product that will only be realized after the crowdfunding campaign has successfully ended. Since backers cannot experience and evaluate the proposed products, this study argues that founders differentiate their projects by communicating their core human values and that backers, in addition to inferring the viability of the proposed product (Rose et al., 2021), draw upon their internal motivation to decide whether to support a project based on the human values communicated by the founder. Therefore, we focus on the alignment between founders’ and backers’ human values, i.e., value congruence, in underexplored crowdfunding campaign situations where rewards are expected yet not guaranteed (as illustrated in Figure 1) because (1) product evaluation is impossible due to the early stage of the projects, (2) intrinsic motivations are equally relevant, and (3) founder intent can be inferred from the project description, while backers’ perceptions are expressed in the comments on the project proposal.

This study’s inquiry into value congruence is based on two concepts: first, value congruence, which allows for understanding the satisfaction of stakeholders in an activity (Erdogan et al., 2004), and, second, value congruence, as reflected in both verbal and nonverbal communication (Wiener et al., 1972). Communication in reward-based crowdfunding occurs through project descriptions provided by the founders and backers’ comments about their expectations, concerns, or other information relevant to the project. Founder-backer communication is highly important, as it can reduce misconceptions and uncertainties in the proposed projects. Communication can be successfully used to reduce uncertainty (Berger & Calabrese, 1974), and communication patterns often reflect the degree of uncertainty in an observed environment (Tushman, 1979). In operations support system (OSS) communities, developers communicate to minimize ambiguity and friction, enabling them to concentrate on their primary tasks, such as writing programs to solve technical problems (Maruping et al., 2019). Similarly, online communities, like social networks and crowdfunding platforms, rely heavily on text-based communication to reduce uncertainties and facilitate collaboration. Therefore, this study, building on prior studies that have explored stakeholder sentiments (as shown in Figure 2), examines whether the success of reward-based crowdfunding campaigns is influenced by how well the values framed in founders’ project descriptions resonate with the values expressed in backers’ comments.

## 3.3.1 Self-Transcendence Value Congruence and Project Success in Crowdfunding

Self-transcendence values, which include universalism and benevolence, emphasize the welfare of others and have been shown to increase cooperation and support for prosocial initiatives (Mei et al., 2024; Schwartz, 2012; Utz, 2004). In reward-based crowdfunding, campaigns frequently function as a form of presale, where backers’ pledges serve as prepayments for certain promised rewards (Jiang et al., 2021). Nevertheless, selftranscendence values can play a significant role in influencing reward-based crowdfunding campaign outcomes (Defazio et al., 2021; Nielsen & Binder, 2021). When founders in reward-based crowdfunding frame their projects around self-transcendence values, they tend to attract backers with an intrinsic motivation to engage in collective efforts that emphasize both prosocial goals and entrepreneurial ambitions. This results in self-transcendence value congruence that can enhance a campaign’s chances of success.

Since reward-based crowdfunding operates as a presale mechanism, projects are expected to have a value proposition with an underlying economic utility (Frydrych et al., 2014; Jiang et al., 2021). Thus, even though a project description that ranks high in selftranscendence may attract impact-first backers, the effect may lower the project’s chances of success, given that the crowdfunding platform operates based on the promise of rewards (Gerber et al., 2012). In rewardbased crowdfunding, while self-transcendence values may be a component of a campaign’s appeal, the effort to maintain increasing self-transcendence and attract backer support can become costly if the economic benefits are marginalized. As such, while selftranscendence may initially be helpful when campaigns lean heavily into self-transcendence alignment, their perceived value proposition may weaken and potentially reduce the campaign’s overall appeal. Since selftranscendence congruence indicates an alignment between the altruistic and prosocial motives of the project founder and backers, an overly strong emphasis on this congruence may require additional effort to attract backers who do not share these values. We thus hypothesize:

H1: Founder-backer self-transcendence congruence is curvilinearly (inverted U-shaped) related to reward-based crowdfunding success.

## 3.3.2 Self-Enhancement Value Congruence and Project Success in Crowdfunding

Self-enhancement values prioritize personal gain, emphasizing the pursuit of status, success, and competence (Schwartz, 2012). Reward-based crowdfunding serves as a platform where these values can not only be recognized but actively leveraged to motivate backers (Jiang et al., 2021; Nielsen & Binder, 2021). Crowdfunding campaigns that strategically emphasize exclusive rewards and early-backer prestige directly engage self-enhancement values by offering tangible benefits that align with backers’ desires for status, recognition, and achievement. Backers with selfenhancement values would be attracted to such projects because of the desire to be associated with exclusive products or to gain early access to innovations that could elevate their personal status (Gerber et al., 2012; Gerber & Hui, 2013; Nielsen & Binder, 2021). Selfenhancement congruence fosters a reciprocal relationship where founders and backers engage in a cycle of giving and receiving, enhancing the campaign’s financial appeal (André et al., 2017).

Self-enhancement values align more closely with the primary motivations of rewarding backers (Gerber et al., 2012; Jiang et al., 2021). However, maintaining a reciprocal relationship requires continuous stakeholder involvement. For instance, the effectiveness of stretch goals in sustaining backer engagement highlights the reinforcing impact of self-enhancement congruence, as backers who have already pledged will likely need ongoing motivation to continue contributing, which can ultimately influence the overall success of reward-based crowdfunding campaigns (Yasar et al., 2022). However, excessive self-enhancement congruence can also slow a project’s progress toward its funding goals by overemphasizing rewards catering to status-driven backers. Specifically, as the campaign prioritizes exclusive and prestige-oriented rewards, it may inadvertently signal that the project is more about individual gain than collective impact, making it less attractive to potential backers who are more motivated by collective benefits. This overemphasis on personal gains can thus deter the accumulation of support from the diverse range of backers needed to drive momentum and effectively achieve the campaign’s broader impact goals. As such, we argue:

H2: Founder-backer self-enhancement congruence is curvilinearly (inverted U-shaped) related to reward-based crowdfunding success.

## 3.3.3 Openness to Change Value Congruence and Project Success in Crowdfunding

Openness to change values encompasses innovation, novelty, and a willingness to embrace new experiences (Schwartz, 2012). Crowdfunding platforms democratize access to capital by providing a testing ground for innovative ideas, prototypes, and early-stage inventions. When project founders emphasize innovation, they position themselves as visionary entrepreneurs capable of bringing fresh, novel ideas to the market, which can significantly enhance their appeal to potential backers (Seigner et al., 2022). This portrayal is particularly attractive to reward-based crowdfunding backers who, despite being much like professional investors, have a general appetite for novelty and are drawn to projects that promise creativity and innovation (Pan et al., 2020; Seigner et al., 2022). In traditional fundraising contexts, highlighting the novelty of a business idea is often rewarded, and this preference for innovation extends to the reward-based crowdfunding space, where backers may perceive innovative projects as more aligned with their preferences for cutting-edge products and ideas (Calic & Shevchenko, 2020; Davis et al., 2017). Founderbacker congruence on openness to change can effectively drive success when both stakeholders share a similar willingness to engage with groundbreaking ideas and drive innovation (Stanko & Henard, 2017).

However, while a greater alignment on openness to change can attract backers who are early adopters, it may simultaneously raise concerns among more cautious backers about the feasibility and reliability of highly innovative projects (Seigner et al., 2022). Therefore, founders need to put in additional effort to keep early adopters engaged and confident in the project’s progress. Unlike professional investors, most reward-based crowdfunding backers do not have the experience or resources to conduct detailed due diligence. These concerns are particularly heightened in the reward-based crowdfunding environment, where many campaigns fail to meet deadlines or deliver on their promises, leading to increased backer skepticism (Allison et al., 2017; Mollick, 2014; Seigner et al., 2022). Innovation, by its very nature, involves deviating from established practices, which can draw attention to the risks and challenges associated with bringing innovative products to market (Chan & Parhankangas, 2017; Kang et al., 2016; Seigner et al., 2022). While early adopters may be eager to support new ideas, it can be challenging to appeal to the broader crowdfunding audience, who may prioritize reliability and feasibility and be fearful of the potential risks and challenges. This is especially true in rewardbased crowdfunding, where many projects fail to deliver the promised rewards (Appio et al., 2020; Schiavone, 2017; Tran et al., 2017). These concerns are particularly relevant given the presale nature of reward-based crowdfunding, where backers are not only interested in supporting innovation but also expect the reliable delivery of their rewards.

Thus, while openness to change value congruence can have an initial positive influence on participation by attracting early adopters who prioritize innovation, increased alignment with such values may also burden more cautious backers who may have concerns about the project’s practicality and feasibility. In reward-based crowdfunding, where backers expect to receive promised rewards, rising concerns about project feasibility can lead to a diminishing chance of campaign success as the perception of the risk level increases. As such we argue:

H3: Founder-backer openness to change congruence is curvilinearly (inverted U-shaped) related to reward-based crowdfunding success.

## 3.3.4 Conservation Value Congruence and Project Success in Crowdfunding

In reward-based crowdfunding, conservation values are those that are focused on preserving established norms, traditions, and heritage while minimizing risk. Founders who prioritize these values often design their projects to emphasize stability and reliability. This approach involves creating products that feel familiar and trustworthy, such as offering vintage items, reviving discontinued products, or continuing traditional practices with cultural or historical significance (Schwartz, 2012). Conservation-minded backers are drawn to products that provide a sense of security, offering reassurance that the product will perform as expected, with minimal risk of failure or unexpected changes. This preference for familiarity over novelty plays a crucial role in preserving cultural heritage and ensuring long-term environmental and societal stability (Jackson, 2005; Calic & Mosakowski, 2016). Conservation congruence occurs when projects with broader sustainability goals resonate with a specific group of backers who value the preservation of resources (Boer & Fischer, 2013).

However, the diverse motivations of those who support reward-based crowdfunding projects can create challenges. While conservation congruence can help create a niche audience of like-minded founders and backers, it could also limit a project’s ability to engage backers who are seeking innovation and change. In reward-based crowdfunding, many backers are driven by the desire to support projects that push boundaries and offer groundbreaking solutions (Mollick, 2014). These backers are often attracted to campaigns that promise disruption, offering products that challenge the status quo and can provide the potential for high rewards. As a result, projects with strong conservation congruence may appear less innovative and may struggle to capture the interest of these forward-thinking backers (Kuppuswamy & Bayus, 2017).

We argue that as founders and backers become more aligned with conservation values, the campaign may initially increasingly appeal to a specific group that prioritizes continuity, predictability, and the preservation of familiar standards. However, this alignment on conservation may come at the expense of engaging a broader audience, particularly those seeking innovation. Given these diverse motivations and tradeoffs, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4: Founder-backer conservation congruence is curvilinearly (inverted U-shaped) related to reward-based crowdfunding success.

## 3.3.5 Moderating Role of Linguistic Conformity

Linguistic conformity involves aligning the language used with the norms and expectations of a specific audience to ensure that messages will resonate with the target group (Beckner et al., 2015; Omidian et al., 2018). In reward-based crowdfunding, linguistic conformity occurs when founders tailor project narratives to match the established communication styles within relevant categories, such as technology or arts, to increase the perceived legitimacy of the project (Navis & Glynn, 2011; Soublière & Gehman, 2020; Vossen & Ihl, 2020). This alignment is expected to make the project more relatable to potential backers and improve audience evaluations by tapping into familiar cultural codes and meanings (Vergne & Wry, 2014). Prior research has shown that linguistic conformity positively impacts job performance by aligning language with shared group values, enhancing understanding and cooperation (Lu et al., 2024).

However, while linguistic conformity may enhance perceived legitimacy, it can introduce a trade-off between conformity and differentiation. The literature on optimal distinctiveness suggests that differentiation is key to competitive advantage, yet there is an inherent tension between standing out and fitting in (Vossen & Ihl, 2020; Zhao et al., 2017). In reward-based crowdfunding, where backers may be attracted to novelty, excessive conformity can diminish a project’s distinctiveness, leading to it simply blending into a possibly already-crowded marketplace (Taeuscher & Rothe, 2021; Vossen & Ihl, 2020). Overconforming to linguistic norms might stifle the unique narrative elements that set a project apart (Fisher et al., 2016; Vossen & Ihl, 2020).

This trade-off is particularly pertinent to value congruence in reward-based crowdfunding. Past research has demonstrated that narratives that excessively conform to category norms can obscure the unique values that would resonate with diverse backer segments, thus weakening the impact of the otherwise strong value congruence (Navis & Glynn, 2011; Soublière & Gehman, 2020). When linguistic conformity is high, the emphasis shifts toward standardization rather than differentiation, potentially reducing the effectiveness of value congruence in signaling the project’s unique appeal (Zuckerman, 2016). This suggests that the positive effects of value congruence could be moderated negatively by linguistic conformity, as overly standardized narratives may fail to highlight the distinctiveness needed to attract backers who are motivated by unique, value-driven elements. Thus, linguistic conformity may limit a project’s ability to leverage value congruence fully, as the narrative becomes too aligned with norms at the expense of its distinctiveness (Fisher et al., 2016, 2017; Navis & Glynn, 2011; Vossen & Ihl, 2020). Therefore, we hypothesize:

H5: Linguistic conformity negatively moderates the relationship between value congruence and reward-based crowdfunding success, such that higher levels of linguistic conformity will diminish the positive effect of value congruence on rewardbased crowdfunding success.

## 4 Methodology

## 4.1 Data Collection and Context

To evaluate our research model, we collected data from Kickstarter, a reward-based crowdfunding platform that offers an appropriate context for our hypotheses. On Kickstarter, project founders are allowed to raise funds, execute their proposed projects, and deliver the promised rewards to backers (Zvilichovsky et al., 2018). Given that our study focuses on values and their congruence, we specifically targeted project descriptions and backer comments as key metrics, which were subsequently analyzed using the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) tool. Before analysis, the data were carefully cleaned to remove duplicates, irrelevant entries, and any inconsistencies. This was done to ensure the accuracy and reliability of our results. The final dataset consisted of 3625 campaigns with pledges from 861,152 backers, generating a total of 293,149 comments.

## 4.1.1 Variable and Operationalization

Project success in crowdfunding has been measured in the crowdfunding literature using various indicators, such as whether a project met its funding goal or not (Colombo et al., 2015; Frimpong et al., 2024; Kuppuswamy & Bayus, 2018; Mollick, 2014). Therefore, following prior research, project success, the outcome variable for this study, was measured with a binary indicator to assess whether a project met its funding target (Kuppuswamy & Bayus, 2018; Van de Rijt et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2018a). Additionally, in the crowdfunding literature, the project description has been identified as a crucial factor that influences project success (Manning & Bejarano, 2017). In particular, the language used in project descriptions, such as word count, readability, and tone, has been found to affect project success (Zhou et al., 2018). Project descriptions are essential because they provide useful information to decrease information asymmetry and uncertainty and act as important quality signals for project success (Mollick & Kuppuswamy, 2014), similar to how online comments provide important signals to consumers making purchasing decisions (Jiang et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2018a).

The project description allows founders to signal their values to potential backers, which, in turn, may resonate with backers, who may reflect these values in their comments on the project proposal. The literature indicates that individuals—both founders and backers— are more likely to signal values they consider significant (Nielsen & Binder, 2021; Ponizovskiy et al., 2020; Tausczik & Pennebaker, 2010). Here, we define a reference to a value as any statement that embodies the essence of that value in describing an object, event, state, or evaluation (Gleasure & Feller, 2016; Nielsen & Binder, 2021; Schwartz, 2012). Our analysis focused on four specific values: self-transcendence, selfenhancement, openness to change, and conservation (Schwartz, 2012).

Table 2 presents the definitions of the dependent, independent, and control variables used in this study. The estimation models incorporated several variables to control for potential confounding effects. The first was video presence, which has been shown to significantly enhance project success, whereby projects with promotional videos generally outperform those without (Korzynski et al., 2021; Mollick, 2014). We also controlled for the project location, as geographic proximity has been shown to affect backer behavior and impact overall campaign success (Agrawal et al., 2015). Additionally, we included the project category as a control, given that certain categories, like technology or art, tend to attract more backers, thereby affecting success (Zheng et al., 2017). The campaign duration was also included as a control (Cumming et al., 2023). Further, we controlled for description sentiment, as sentiments have been shown to affect project funding (Moysidou & Spaeth, 2016; Xiang et al., 2019). The “Staff Pick” status on Kickstarter was also added as a control, as it has been shown to boost visibility and increase the likelihood of success (Deng et al., 2022). Lastly, we controlled for project goal, as projects with lower, more attainable goals are generally more successful (Deng et al., 2022; Mollick, 2014).

Building on prior research that identified values through textual analysis (Ponizovskiy et al., 2020), we extracted the four aforementioned values from the textual data of both backers (comments) and founders (project descriptions) to conduct our empirical analysis. We employed text analytic techniques consistent with the methodologies used in previous crowdfunding studies (Blaseg et al., 2020; Jancenelle et al., 2019) to derive insights from the project descriptions and backers’ comments. To operationalize the analysis, we loaded the comments and project descriptions into LIWC software and applied a value-based dictionary adapted from Ponizovskiy et al. (2020). The LIWC software generated feature vectors corresponding to the four values—selftranscendence, self-enhancement, openness to change, and conservation—across both sets of textual data (project descriptions and backers’ comments). These feature vectors provided quantitative representations of the presence and intensity of each value in the texts. We then used these vectors to measure the congruence between the values signaled by the project founders in their descriptions and those that seemed to resonate with backers based on their comments for each project.

## 4.2 Analysis and Results

## 4.2.1 Measuring Value Congruence and Linguistic Conformity

We measured value congruence by calculating the Euclidean distance between the feature vectors of a founder’s value scores extracted from the project description and the average value scores for the backers extracted from their comments on the same project (Fink, 2011; Majhi et al., 2022; Xu & Li, 2020). This allowed quantifying how closely aligned the values expressed by the project founder are with those of the project backers. The Euclidean distance is an effective metric for measuring individual value congruence because it captures the magnitude of differences across single dimensions and sensitively quantifies deviations between values, here related to founder-backer values. The use of Euclidean distance to measure congruence has been validated in various research contexts, such as the assessment of IT capabilities and strategic alignment (Fink, 2011; Majhi et al., 2022), as well as interestoccupation congruence (Xu & Li, 2020). Furthermore, we took an inverse of the Euclidean distance, such that higher values indicate stronger value congruence, making the results more intuitive and easier to compare across different projects.

Linguistic conformity in crowdfunding campaigns was assessed using the cosine similarity metric (Hwang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024). We computed cosine similarity, which quantifies the degree of similarity between the language used by project founders in their descriptions and the language reflected in backers’ comments. We used cosine similarity to assess conformity because unlike the Euclidean distance, which focuses on the magnitude of differences in individual dimensions, cosine similarity is particularly effective for measuring overall similarity across multidimensional spaces. To measure the degree of conformity between the project descriptions and backer comments, each document was represented as a vector in a multidimensional space, with each dimension corresponding to a specific word.

## 4.3 Robustness

First, to ensure the robustness of the results to the sensitivity of the dependent variable, we conducted two additional analyses using alternative measures of project success: project participation and percentage of funds raised. Project success has been measured in the crowdfunding literature using the percentage of the funding goal achieved and the level of backer participation in a project (Colombo et al., 2015; Frimpong et al., 2024; Kuppuswamy & Bayus, 2018;

Mollick, 2014). This measure helps us understand the level of financial backing a project has garnered, even if it did not fully meet its goal. We also measured project support as backer engagement, referring to the total number of backers who supported the project. This metric is crucial for understanding crowd engagement, as the literature has shown that a larger number of backers can trigger a form of “herding behavior,” leading to increased project success (Kuppuswamy & Bayus, 2018). For project participation, defined as the number of backers, we applied a negative binomial regression model since the dependent variable is a count. This approach is recommended for count data, particularly when the variance exceeds the mean, offering more reliable estimates compared to linear or Poisson regression (Chen et al., 2020; O’Hara & Kotze, 2010). Additionally, we employed linear regression to assess the impact of our independent variables on the percentage of funds raised. Both analyses showed consistent findings for our primary models, reinforcing the robustness and reliability of our results.

Second, to address potential endogeneity concerns, we adopted Lewbel’s approach by introducing a mathematically computed instrumental variable—the average pledge per backer—under the assumption that the values considered in the study are exogenous (Lewbel, 2012). This method accounted for variations in individual backer contributions, which is important in this context since not all backers leave comments. This enabled controlling for a key aspect of backer behavior that could impact project success. The analysis showed the consistent effects of value congruence on project success even after including the average pledge per backer as a control variable. By isolating the effects of value congruence from backer pledges, our results highlight the distinct and robust impact of value congruence between founders and backers in driving crowdfunding success (see Appendix C).

Additionally, following Haans et al. (2016), we employed a three-step approach for testing the robustness of our proposed nonlinear relationships (Lind & Mehlum, 2010). This procedure for testing U-shaped or inverted U-shaped relationships includes assessing the significance of the quadratic term, testing the slopes at the low and high ends of the data range, and ensuring that the turning point falls within the observed data range (Haans et al., 2016). Since the results in Table 5 show that the quadratic terms for self-transcendence, conservation, and openness to change congruences were significant and negative, this satisfies the first criterion, indicating that the observed relationships were nonlinear. The quadratic term for self-enhancement was not significant, indicating the absence of a nonlinear relationship. Given that project success is a binary variable, the model’s S-shaped response curve and bounded nature can compress effects near the boundaries, making it difficult to detect U-shaped or inverted U-shaped significance of the slopes at the lower and higher ends of the curve (Cameron, 2005; Haans et al., 2016; Hoetker, 2007). This compression reduces the visibility of quadratic effects, particularly at the high and low ends of the data range, where the slopes are most critical for confirming continuously curvilinear paths. Thus, we also found a curvilinear relationship between self-transcendence, conservation, and openness to change congruences and project success, but we could not confirm the continuous inverted U-shape that was proposed. To satisfy the third criterion, we calculated the turning points for the value congruences. These turning points represent the optimal levels of these congruences for maximizing project success in the quadratic model. The findings indicate that the turning points for conservation congruence (20.96), selftranscendence congruence (62.54), and openness to change congruence (29.76) fell within the observed data range, making them practically relevant and interpretable. These turning points are crucial as they identify the specific levels of value congruence where the alignment between founders and backers is most effective.

## 4.4 Post Hoc Analyses

Following prior literature (e.g., Wu et al., 2024), we gathered data from another crowdfunding platform as a robustness test. Consequently, we analyzed data from Republic.com, an equity crowdfunding platform where investors receive a share of ownership or future profits (see Appendix B). This platform differs from Kickstarter, which uses a reward-based model where backers receive nonmonetary rewards. Despite their differences, both platforms engage intrinsic and extrinsic motivations, allowing founders to align their values with their audience (Cholakova & Clarysse, 2015; Nielsen & Binder, 2021; Wald et al., 2019). Like Kickstarter, Republic.com provides detailed project descriptions and allows investors to comment, enabling us to examine whether the effects of value congruence and linguistic conformity observed on Kickstarter also apply to Republic.com. The results from our analysis on Republic.com mirrored those of our main analysis on Kickstarter, confirming that value congruence and linguistic conformity significantly influence project outcomes on Republic.com as well. These findings suggest that the effects of value congruence and linguistic conformity extend beyond specific platform types or crowdfunding models, highlighting the robustness of these mechanisms across both rewardbased and equity-based crowdfunding contexts.

Additionally, the robustness checks on Republic.com addressed concerns regarding potential selection bias and missing data that may have influenced our findings on Kickstarter. We believe that comments on Kickstarter often reflect the perspectives of highly enthusiastic backers, which could lead to biased results. By replicating the analysis on Republic.com, where investment decisions are primarily financially driven, we were able to test whether the observed effects of value congruence and linguistic conformity on Kickstarter would hold in a different context with financially motivated investors. The significant findings across both platforms suggest that the effects of value congruence and linguistic conformity were not confined to a specific type of backer or platform, thus mitigating concerns about selection bias and demonstrating robustness across various crowdfunding settings.

Furthermore, this replication reduces concerns related to missing data, as our conclusions are not solely dependent on the completeness of data from one platform. The significant effects identified on Republic.com confirm the robustness and generalizability of these relationships, highlighting the critical role of value congruence in influencing investment decisions across diverse crowdfunding environments (see Appendix B).

## 5 Discussion and Implications

The findings reveal a positive effect of openness to change congruence on project success, highlighting that a mutual preference for innovation between backers and founders enhances the likelihood of project success in reward-based crowdfunding campaigns. This suggests that founder-backer alignment on openness to change can effectively drive success when both stakeholders share a willingness to engage with novel ideas that lead to innovation (Stanko & Henard, 2017). However, the negative curvilinear relationship indicates that excessive alignment may lead to diminishing returns, as concerns about project feasibility may surface. In such cases, perceived risks could reduce participation and ultimately undermine project success.

Similarly, self-transcendence congruence demonstrated a positive effect on project success, underscoring the beneficial role of backers’ intrinsic motivation in supporting collective efforts that blend prosocial goals with entrepreneurial ambitions. Yet the observed curvilinear pattern suggests that while initial congruence on altruistic values fosters project success, excessive congruence may conflict with the extrinsic motivations of backers, particularly within the transactional design of reward-based platforms. For conservation congruence, we observed a positive relationship with project success. However, the downward-sloping curve at higher levels of congruence suggests that excessive alignment may result in negative outcomes, possibly due to perceptions that the project is overly conventional or lacking innovation- qualities that could limit broader audience engagement.

Regarding self-enhancement congruence, the hypothesis was not supported. However, the positive linear relationship indicates that congruence in values related to personal achievement may consistently bolster the likelihood of project success. Campaigns that strategically highlight exclusive rewards and earlybacker prestige engage self-enhancement values by offering tangible benefits that resonate with backers’ desires for status, recognition, and achievement. Unlike the other values, the absence of a curvilinear effect suggests a stable linear relationship, indicating that selfenhancement values can foster a reciprocal dynamic that continuously enhances a campaign’s financial appeal.

Finally, regarding the moderating role of linguistic conformity, the findings suggest that its effect on the relationship between value congruence and project success is pronounced when congruence follows a linear pattern but weakens when the relationship becomes nonlinear or curvilinear.

## 5.1 Theoretical Implications

This study makes significant contributions to the growing literature on crowdfunding and value theory. In particular, while previous research has primarily examined motivations from the perspective of either project founders or backers separately (Allison et al., 2017; Manning & Bejarano, 2017; Wang et al., 2018b; Xiang et al., 2019), this study uniquely investigated the congruence of motivations (i.e., values) between both parties. Our research explicitly reveals that despite the presence of external motivation as an observable reward, backers and founders are both strategic agents who are motivated to seek value congruence in the projects they support or create (Gleasure & Feller, 2016; Nielsen & Binder, 2021; Wang et al., 2018a). This study highlights the critical importance of a two-sided resonance in crowdfunding, where shared values as indicators of motivation effectively bridge the gap between founders and backers, aligning their goals and actions. The study enriches the reward-based crowdfunding literature by introducing value congruence as a strategic element. While value congruence is not only pertinent but also significantly impactful in economic contexts, the study advances the discourse on the interactive and complex nature of stakeholders’ motivations in crowdfunding.

Furthermore, our findings extend the literature by illustrating how individual values manifest as intrinsic motivations in economic contexts. The study provides a granular view of intrinsic motivations, demonstrating how distinct values can have varying impacts. By employing the theory of basic human values, we measured the distinct impact of each value, revealing various dimensions of the intrinsic motivations of founders and backers. Our findings highlight how stakeholders must strategically leverage their intrinsic motivations to achieve their goals. We demonstrate that self-transcendence, self-enhancement, openness to change, and conservation values can all influence a campaign’s success.

Additionally, this study reveals the conditional effects of the strategic factors that influence crowdfunding campaigns and demonstrates the need to study value congruence as having nonlinear effects. Specifically, while prior research has often assumed that values have unique linear effects (Nielsen & Binder, 2021; van Dijk et al., 2019), our findings demonstrate that as strategic factors, these values have curvilinear effects. Previous research has shown that value congruence typically enhances outcomes like employee commitment, satisfaction, and organizational effectiveness through the alignment of individual and organizational values (Cazier et al., 2007; Deeds Pamphile & Ruttan, 2023; Edwards & Cable, 2009; Zhang & Bloemer, 2008). On the other hand, our findings indicate that the impact of value congruence can vary widely depending on the context. Specifically, we discovered that excessive congruence in value dimensions such as selftranscendence, openness to change, and conservation can diminish the appeal of crowdfunding campaigns. In reward-based crowdfunding, excessive alignment may cause backers to perceive projects as overly idealistic or unrealistically ambitious, potentially overshadowing their economic practicality and feasibility. These findings highlight the context-dependent nature of value congruence, demonstrating that while alignment can be beneficial, it must be strategically balanced with practical considerations, particularly in diverse digital environments like crowdfunding.

Additionally, our study provides insights into the boundary conditions that amplify or weaken the impact of value congruence in crowdfunding contexts. Our findings enhance our understanding of value congruence and show that it is not a uniformly positive force in crowdfunding. We argue that the effectiveness of value congruence is contingent upon the level of founder narrative differentiation and that overly standardized narratives may fail to emphasize the distinctiveness that would appeal to backers motivated by unique, value-driven elements. Consequently, our findings suggest that linguistic conformity may act as a boundary condition that limits the potential of value congruence by aligning narratives too closely with established norms at the expense of distinctiveness (Fisher et al., 2017; Navis & Glynn, 2011; Vossen & Ihl, 2020). This insight advances the theoretical discourse on value congruence by highlighting the role of narrative strategies in enhancing or constraining value impact, thus providing a deeper understanding of the complex mechanisms that govern backer engagement on crowdfunding platforms.

Finally, regarding methodology, we complement prior research that has studied crowdfunding backers’ motivation through experiments and surveys (Rodriguez-Ricardo et al., 2018; Sneddon et al., 2020; van Dijk et al., 2019). We believe that our choice of rewards context allowed us to model the impact of individual value congruence on project success using actual crowdfunding behavior rather than contribution intentions (Rodriguez-Ricardo et al., 2018; Sneddon et al., 2020; van Dijk et al., 2019). Thus, our methodological approach provides insights for future studies to explore other personal value congruences, particularly on crowdfunding platforms.

## 5.2 Implications for Crowdfunding in Practice

This study provides valuable insights for both project founders and backers in reward-based crowdfunding by highlighting the strategic importance of value congruence as a key driver of backer engagement and project success in crowdfunding campaigns. Our findings indicate that founders should actively align their project’s values with those of potential backers, viewing backers as strategic partners rather than passive participants. By fostering this value congruence, founders can significantly enhance their project’s appeal, going beyond traditional engagement tactics to create a shared vision that resonates with their potential and actual backers’ values. We assert that strategic value congruence can attract support and strengthen backer commitment, thereby increasing the likelihood of project campaigns gaining their desired funding in competitive crowdfunding environments.

For platform managers, the study suggests implementing features that enhance the articulation and discovery of shared values between founders and backers. Platforms could benefit from integrating tools that facilitate a clearer expression of value propositions and offer personalized project recommendations aligned with backers’ preferences. For example, advanced search algorithms, value-based tagging systems, or filters for value congruence could help backers find projects that resonate with their personal beliefs and goals.

Finally, our findings have significant implications for marketing and communication strategies within crowdfunding campaigns. The study shows that while value congruence is essential, its effectiveness is highly context dependent and moderated by linguistic conformity. Founders should craft their narratives to highlight their project’s unique values without overconforming to category norms, as excessive standardization can dilute the distinctiveness that would attract value-driven backers. Instead, by balancing conformity with differentiation, founders can leverage value congruence as a powerful signaling mechanism that emphasizes the unique appeal of their project. We argue that understanding the boundary conditions that enhance or constrain the impact of value congruence impact can help founders and platform managers tailor their strategies more effectively.

## 6 Conclusion and Future Research Direction

Reward-based crowdfunding has emerged as a democratizing tool for entrepreneurship, providing broader access to funding for various ventures. This study examined the impact of value congruence on crowdfunding success, specifically focusing on how alignments in self-transcendence, self-enhancement, openness to change, and conservation values between founders and backers can influence crowdfunding campaign outcomes. Our findings reveal the distinct impact of these value congruences and highlight the role of linguistic conformity as a boundary condition for project success.

Despite these contributions, we acknowledge several limitations of our study that should be considered when interpreting our results. First, the study was conducted using a reward-based crowdfunding model, which has distinct features compared to other crowdfunding models, such as donation and debt models. Although the results were replicated on an equity platform, differences emerged in how value congruence and linguistic conformity influenced project success. This distinction is important for the generalization of the results, as backers’ participation behaviors differ in different types of crowdfunding. Therefore, our results are limited to crowdfunding contexts in which backers expect rewards. Future research should consider whether the values and congruence considered in this study apply to other contexts.

Second, because only cross-sectional data were available, the impact of value congruence between founders and backers could not be examined longitudinally. It would be interesting to investigate value congruence in founders’ descriptions and backers’ comments over the course of a project’s timeline.

Third, this study focused on Schwartz’s (1997) four values and their influence on crowdfunding participation. However, we recognize the possibility of additional values that could be extracted from founders’ descriptions and backers’ comments. Future research could explore the influence of additional values and their interactions with those examined in this study through other methods, such as focus group discussions with founders and backers.

Notwithstanding the limitations, the study findings emphasize the need to strategically align values and communication styles to enhance project success in reward-based crowdfunding.

## About the Authors

Bright Frimpong is an assistant professor at Washington and Lee University, where he specializes in data science and information systems. He received his PhD in information systems from the University of Texas Rio Grande Valley. His research interests include digital entrepreneurship, consumer behavior analytics, and cybersecurity. His work has appeared in the Information Systems Journal, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, Journal of Business Venturing Insights, International Journal of Entrepreneurial Venturing, and Journal of Manufacturing Technology Management. He has also presented at various conferences including Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences, Americas Conferences on Information Systems, Production and Operations Management Society, and American Marketing Association Summer Conference.

Emmanuel W. Ayaburi is an assistant professor of information systems at Baylor University. He received his PhD in information systems from the University of Texas at San Antonio and his MBA from the Colorado Heights University. His research interests include behavioral information systems security and privacy, economics of information systems, and knowledge sharing. His work has been published in European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Information Technology & People, Information Systems Frontiers, International Journal of Information Management, Computers in Human Behavior, and International Journal of Innovation and Technology Management; he has also published in several conference proceedings including International Conference on Information Systems, Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences, and Americas Conference on Information Systems.

Francis Kofi Andoh-Baidoo is a professor in the Department of Information Systems at the Robert C. Vackar College of Business and Entrepreneurship, University of Texas Rio Grande Valley. He has a PhD in information systems from Virginia Commonwealth University. His research interests are in business analytics, ICT for development, information security, and privacy. His research has appeared in journals such as European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Information and Management, International Journal of Information Management, Communications of the Association for Information Systems, Information Systems Frontiers, and The Data Base for Advances in Information Systems. He serves on the editorial board of Information Systems Frontiers and Information Technology for Development.
