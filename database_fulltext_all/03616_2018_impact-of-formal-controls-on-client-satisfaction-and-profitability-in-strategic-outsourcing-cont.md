# Impact of Formal Controls on Client Satisfaction and Profitability in Strategic Outsourcing Contracts

NISHTHA LANGER AND DEEPA MANI

ABSTRACT: The reach and impact of outsourcing has grown rapidly to include a variety of strategic objectives. Significant issues of cooperation and coordination in strategic outsourcing necessitate investments in formal controls that specify and monitor execution of the outsourced task to mitigate unforeseen contingencies and improve predictability in the attainment of desirable goals. In this study, we investigate the nature of formal controls in strategic outsourcing contracts and their impact on client satisfaction and financial performance. Specifically, using rich field data on 390 strategic outsourcing contracts, we examine the differential impact of output controls, activity controls, and capability controls on client satisfaction and vendor profitability. We find that activity and capability controls are positively associated with client satisfaction and profitability; in contrast, output controls differentially impact satisfaction and profitability, reflecting potential tradeoffs between the two outcomes. Our results, in addition to contributing to the research in control theory, provide actionable insights for technology vendors into the appropriate strategy and tactics required to compete efficiently and effectively in strategic services markets.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: outsourcing, strategic outsourcing, formal controls, client satisfaction, profitability, IT outsourcing, outsourcing controls.

## Introduction

Over the past decade and a half, information technology outsourcing (ITO) has evolved from being a cost-saving tool for transaction-intensive business processes to a powerful strategic lever that is fundamental to firm competitiveness [36, 58]. Strategic outsourcing engagements encompass a wide range of strategic purposes such as increased revenue, reduced time-to-market or transformation of operations and differ from more transactional outsourcing engagements in two important ways.<sup>1</sup> First, strategic outsourcing engagements involve creation of substantial relationship-specific investments that increase the potential for hold-up and opportunistic renegotiation of ex post surplus [61]. That is, incentive conflicts and threats to cooperative adaptation are higher in strategic outsourcing contracts. Second, the underlying task in transactional outsourcing contracts is more codifiable and measurable. In turn, the vendor often assumes complete ownership and control of the outsourced task with investments in bilateral instruments rendered redundant [60]. In contrast, task in strategic outsourcing contracts is often complex, ambiguous, and requires significant integration of effort with the client through a “cheek by jowl” implementation [43, 60, 78, 86]. That is, cognitive conflicts and the allied inability to respond in a coordinated manner to changes in exchange conditions are sig nificantly greater in strategic outsourcing contracts [60, 82].

Significant issues of cooperation (through alignment of incentives) and coordination (through alignment of actions) necessitate investments in formal controls that specify and monitor execution of the outsourced task to “induce desired performance while inhibiting dysfunctional behavior” [29]. Formal controls mitigate unforeseen contingencies and improve predictability in the attainment of desirable goals in inter-firm relationships [4, 25, 69]. As a result, they are integral to the uncertain, ambiguous, and complex task environments of strategic outsourcing relationships.

The nature of strategic outsourcing engagements, however, renders the impact of controls less straightforward than that in transactional outsourcing. Specifically, in transactional outsourcing relationships, the sources of client satisfaction and contract profitability are aligned - the vendor is profitable because of economies of scale and the client too is satisfied since the cost savings from scale economies are passed on to the client through reduced costs of ownership of the outsourced task.

The contract provides a framework for cooperation and coordination for these standardized, analyzable tasks and little investments in bilateral or client-specific governance are required [59, 60]. Therefore, as is with most business contexts (see [64] for a review of the literature), the relationship between client satisfaction and profitability is synergistic.

However, the context of strategic outsourcing raises the potential for tradeoffs between these important constructs. Strategic outsourcing contracts, because of the strategic and idiosyncratic nature of the outsourced task, require investments in control mechanisms that resolve information asymmetry and continually update common ground between the client and vendor to address incentive and cognitive conflicts. These investments, to the extent that they are non-contractible, clientspecific, and reflect customization more than standardization, may increase client satisfaction but not necessarily profitability of that contract in the short run. On the other hand, these investments in client satisfaction may also lower the marginal costs of task execution and transacting with the customer. Continued association with satisfied clients is associated with greater trust and learning that help reduce friction, measurement costs, and other transaction costs. Furthermore, client investments in coordination that complement those of the provider are required to meet task objectives [59], and satisfied clients are more likely to make these investments. In such case, controls in strategic outsourcing may render client satisfaction and contract profitability compatible objectives in the short run.

Our study uses rich field data on 390 strategic outsourcing contracts implemented by a leading global technology vendor to illuminate the above debate. Specifically, we examine how investments in formal controls—output controls, activity controls, and capability controls—in strategic outsourcing projects differentially impact contract profitability and client satisfaction.

Our study has important theoretical implications for scholars studying governance of inter-firm relationships. Prior research in IS [21, 35, 60] has largely examined control in terms of the contractual structure used to formalize the relationship [38, 70, 83]. These studies largely examine contractual control through allocation of risks and incentives in the relationship and how such contractual control influences the impact of the relational or information structure in the outsourcing engagement. However, performance outcomes are contingent on the implementation of the contract [81]. Yet, a minimal amount is known regarding process controls that actually effect the contract to directly influence task execution [17]. In not paying attention to these controls, we may underestimate the importance of implementation of the outsourcing contract and incompletely understand control mechanisms in outsourcing. Our study provides insights into specific outcome and process controls in strategic outsourcing.

Our results for the impact of outcome and process controls on client satisfaction and profitability also provide insights into the appropriate strategy and tactics for achieving each of these objectives. These findings are salient to a competitive environment for services, where cost structures are making price competition difficult, compelling vendors to reduce price points to retain its customers. The ensuing pursuit of satisfaction is especially pertinent to the delivery of strategic outsourcing services, whose reach and impact are growing in contemporary firms. The costs of this pursuit must be well understood so vendors may compete both efficiently and effectively in the global technology services market.

## Theory and Hypotheses Development

## Profitability and Client Satisfaction as Dual Objectives in Strategic Outsourcing

The fact that managers are interested in profitability is a truism. Strategic outsourcing contracts are typically longer-term contracts involving significant resource deployments on the vendor’s part. It is unsurprising that managers attempt to generate profits from these projects, and indeed project profitability is a key metric in the performance evaluation of most project managers heading such initiatives.

It is a common practice in the industry that project managers of strategic outsourcing projects are also evaluated on client satisfaction. Client satisfaction has been linked to long-term profitability and the success of outsourcing near-core, strategically relevant activities [31, 60, 85]. Indeed, satisfied clients are more likely to invest in governance mechanisms that may be less costly to implement; furthermore, reputation effects engendered by satisfied clients reduce overall customer acquisition costs, both from additional business from the current client, and recommendations to new clients [4]. In sum, managers responsible for delivering strategic services face a dual mandate – that of increasing client satisfaction with their services while ensuring project profitability.

However, there are contrasting views on whether vendors can simultaneously maximize client satisfaction and profitability. Prior research in marketing and strategy emphasizes that the pursuit of both customer satisfaction and profitability, in general, is compatible. For example, providing high levels of customer-satisfaction could be the basis of a differentiation strategy – such firms make large profits because they target customers who are willing to pay a premium for these services [74, 75]. Other research points out that in industries highly dependent on long-term relationships and the ability to win added business, customer satisfaction could lead to superior profitability based on economies of scope – either inter-temporal or by cross-subsidization. This argument dominates the rich literature on buyer-supplier relationships, especially those that focus on customized products/services (for example, see the expansive treatments by [22, 24]). Finally, studies in marketing and operations management argue that higher customer satisfaction reduces customer management costs [20, 47] and costs of future transactions [79], both of which increase profits.

However, industry and academic research in outsourcing argue that in the specific context of strategic outsourcing, project profitability of the vendor and client satisfaction may be incompatible. Several surveys of Chief Executive Officers (CXOs) suggest that clients have multiple objectives in mind when they pursue strategic outsourcing relationships, including reduction in cost, improvements in quality, and innovation (e.g. [16, 57]). Unless there is agreement among senior management on the most important outcomes expected from outsourcing, such mixed motives result in poor performance, especially in terms of client satisfaction [82]. This finding is consistent with Anderson et al. [5], who argue that it is difficult to optimize service-based businesses for both productivity and customer satisfaction when the latter depends more on customization than standardization. Their argument refines Porter’s initial insight that activity systems that support efficiency militate against differentiation [74, 76]. Yet, despite this seeming incompatibility in the two outcomes, in strategic outsourcing projects, managers are measured on both profitability and client satisfaction. Project valuation models often consider increased profit streams over the project lifecycle, and managers are pressed to find improvements in execution that could lead to incremental efficiency gains.

In other words, although managers of strategic outsourcing initiatives are required to simultaneously improve project profitability and client satisfaction, there is no consensus in the literature on whether managers can indeed implement a set of policies that improve both objectives or whether such policies lead to improvements in one of the desired outcomes at the expense of the other. As far as we are aware, there are no empirical studies of how managers optimize for multiple objectives in the delivery of strategic outsourcing services. This study aims for a preliminary investigation of this gap in the literature by examining how control systems in strategic outsourcing differentially impact project profitability and client satisfaction.

## Control Systems in Strategic Outsourcing Relationships

Control systems refer to the set of procedures that organizations use to monitor, direct, and evaluate organizational activities. Given that strategic outsourcing relationships are structured more as tight partnerships than arm’s length contracts, controls in these relationships are bilateral instruments that are jointly established through dialogue and negotiation to foster effective cooperation and coordination between the client and vendor. Prior research on inter-firm relationships in IS has studied the role of formal and informal controls in outsourcing, including relational controls [43, 48, 49, 78], behavioral controls [59, 86], and outcome controls [59, 86]. Our paper advances this rich body of work to examine the role of process controls—activity and capability controls—in addition to outcome controls on client satisfaction and profitability in strategic outsourcing contracts.

Formal controls are typically negotiated with the client, explicitly measured, and widely shared. Prior research (e.g., [4, 24, 63]) classifies formal controls as outcome-based output controls or behavior-based process controls. Output controls monitor the final outcome of an activity and are used to influence directly the ends achieved whereas process controls monitor the inputs and human behavior used to achieve the outcomes. Hence, they are used to influence the means used to achieve desirable ends [10, 44]. Challagalla and Shervani [14] further disaggregate process controls into activity controls that specify the activities that must be performed on a regular basis to achieve task objectives and capability controls that specify the resources and skills required for good performance. In the following section, we explore the impact of these different controls on profitability and client satisfaction in strategic outsourcing projects.

## Impact of Controls on Project Profitability and Client Satisfaction

Activity controls refer to the specification, monitoring and reporting of activities that the vendor is required to perform on a regular basis to execute the outsourced task. Examples include specification of standards or templates for process execution, checks for regulatory compliance, and safeguards for classified or proprietary information of the client and vendor, among others. The vendor may not be incentivized to invest in activity controls (as in the case of a shift from traditional task execution and management processes of the vendor to reduce “waste” or processes and output that do not add value to the customer) or may incur nontrivial expenses in effecting these controls. In addition to the costs of effecting the controls, service providers, including the firm that we worked with for the purpose of this study, additionally incur the costs of demonstrating to their clients the existence and efficacy of controls.

For example, consider a service provider’s contractual (and often, regulatory) commitment to providing the highest standards of data security for its clients. In such case, the provider must implement controls for change management, access control, monitoring, transaction security, virus protection, physical access control, data transmission, application and data backups, and data security, among others. Furthermore, the provider must facilitate third-party audits of these controls to document and demonstrate their presence and impact.

The significant cost of implementing activity controls emphasizes the need to understand whether they yield high returns. We posit that despite their high costs of implementation, activity controls are likely to positively impact client satisfaction and the contract’s financial performance. These impacts of activity controls follow primarily from their role in promoting information flow between the client and vendor that reduces appropriation concerns and task asymmetries. Indeed, controlled access to proprietary information, allocation of responsibility for regulatory compliance, and frequent monitoring, and periodic reporting on accounting of resources and assets act as safeguards in the execution of strategic tasks. These activity controls are likely to make clients more aware of the challenges faced by the vendor and the overall costs and effort expended in task execution. Such information is particularly valuable in strategic outsourcing contracts, where the complexity of the outsourced task leads the client to trade off relatively complete contracts marked by high-powered incentives with more incomplete contracts marked by lower costs of ex post renegotiation. Activity controls act as safeguards in these contracts, where the client bears the risk of cost overruns. Finally, activity controls necessitate frequent feedback from the client on the outsourced task, furthering the vendor’s knowledge of client expectations, and improving service quality and satisfaction.

Increased information flow facilitated by activity controls also improves the financial performance of the contract [23, 24]. First, an improved client understanding of the costs of task execution reduces ex post renegotiation costs for the vendor that potentially improves profits. Feedback from the client necessitated by activity controls also enhances the vendor’s understanding of the outsourced task to reduce task asymmetries and enable swifter problem recognition and faster problem resolution, thereby reducing rework and costs of coordination. Furthermore, activity controls that routinize and codify knowledge are likely to help the vendor and client achieve process improvements by facilitating learning by doing [88]. Finally, activity controls contribute to vendor performance by reducing the chance of noncompliance and incidence of performance misses and allied penalty payments. For all of these reasons, we posit:

Hypothesis 1: An increase in activity controls in strategic outsourcing engagements positively relates to an increase in client satisfaction and the financial performance of the engagement.

Capability controls refer to the specification of resources, assets, or capabilities that the vendor draws on to execute the outsourced task. As we noted earlier, strategic outsourcing services are necessarily complex in execution, heterogeneous in their delivery, and involve integration of effort between the client and vendor. In such case, capability controls may be conceptualized as the use of client-specific resources that are likely to have an in-depth understanding of the client’s business context, and in turn, more likely to be able to meet client needs on a timely basis. Conversely, the absence of capability controls may be conceptualized as the use of standardized resources that display breadth of understanding across multiple clients and scale to deliver cost-effective solutions.

Various longitudinal surveys of offshoring trends [57, 63] suggest that cost reduction and efficiency are less of a driver of client satisfaction in strategic outsourcing services. Rather, strategic outcomes such as innovation and business intelligence dominate these relationships. In turn, strategic outsourcing services require of the vendor the ability to partner with the client to continually and dynamically update common ground. Client-specific resources with whom there exists “contextual common ground” and lower psychic distance arising from differences in language, culture, time zones, status, and norms [45, 46, 56] are more likely to deliver on these partnership goals. For these reasons, client satisfaction in strategic outsourcing engagements is likely to be positively associated with the use of capability controls.

Client-specific resources of the vendor with depth of understanding of the client’s business context and the ability to continually and dynamically update common ground with the vendor contribute not only to client satisfaction but also to the contract’s financial performance. We argue that client-specific resources allow the vendor to engage as an “extended organization” of the client [7], marking a shift from a command-and-control structure to a sense-and-respond form of collaboration. In such a case, the loss of scale economies associated with client-specific resources is therefore, more than offset by the allied improvement in coordination costs of the vendor or costs of aligning actions and integrating effort with the client.

Mani et al. [62] emphasize these coordination costs in their discussion of distributed offshored work. They argue that feedback or mutual adjustment that is essentially ongoing communication is powerful in co-located teams. However, this important coordination mechanism is not available to distributed teams [27, 53]. In such cases, common ground is difficult to develop even with rich media such as videoconferencing. Effective information sharing is no longer effortless or free and requires careful investments in processes and technologies:

Whereas concerns about coordination are applicable for work that is distributed between say, New York–San Francisco or New York–Bangalore, we believe the question assumes greater importance in the latter situation. In this case, in addition to geographic distance, project teams need to coordinate across greater psychic distance arising from differences in language, culture, time zones, status, and norms [45, 46, 56]. With increasing psychic distance, communication alone may not improve shared understanding [15]. The information sharing strategy needs to be much more sophisticated and perhaps carry a much greater volume of information to generate common ground [62:847].

Coordination costs are especially significant when the underlying task is less routinized, less analyzable, and less familiar to the vendor as is the case in strategic outsourcing. We argue that in such case, when the vendor leverages standardized resources that have greater psychic distance from the client, significant coordination costs ensue as illustrated above, eating away from the expected gains from the project. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2: An increase in capability controls in strategic outsourcing engagements positively relates to an increase in client satisfaction and the financial performance of the engagement.

Output controls refer to the specification, measurement, and monitoring of outcomes of the outsourced process, often through detailed service level agreements and service level objectives in the contract. The greater the number of output controls, the more complete is the formal contract governing the outsourcing relationship. Firms self-select contractual completeness to minimize the trade-offs between the higher (lower) incentive properties of complete (incomplete) contracts and their higher (lower) ex ante contracting and ex post adaptation costs [8, 18]. Incomplete contracts allow the contracting parties to make modifications if contingencies arise ex post [8, 18]. However, they increase the likelihood to the client of moral hazard. The moral hazard risk in incomplete contracts arises primarily because of assumptions of opportunistic behavior and cost overruns that are difficult to monitor or measure for complex and dynamic tasks, thereby, resulting in asymmetry in understanding of the true costs of executing the outsourced task. In such cases, potential dissipation of gains from risks of moral hazard, costly bargaining, and privately favorable redistribution of ex post surplus may be significant. Complete contracts minimize the aforementioned moral hazard losses. However, if unanticipated contingencies require task changes and adaptations, relatively complete contracts require costly renegotiation. They also involve significant a priori design costs related to specification of potential contingencies and responses thereof [87].

The design of output controls therefore involves trade-offs between losses from vendor moral hazard of less complete contracts on the one hand and transaction costs of ex ante contract design and ex post adaptation of more complete contracts on the other [8, 50]. Transaction cost economics (TCE) theory [8] posits that given this tradeoff, more incomplete contracts are preferred when a project is more complex. Specifically, in the case of strategic outsourcing engagements, the uncertainty and complexity of the underlying task requires a contract that permits the client and vendor to deal with such uncertainty and complexity in an adaptive fashion. However, complete contracts “impair the ease with which adaptive, sequential adjustments to disturbances are accomplished [8:403].”

Similarly, Bernheim and Whinston [11] argue that in the case of complex tasks such as those that underlie strategic outsourcing contracts, contractual incompleteness is a strategic decision that provides the client and vendor much discretion. They argue that if some aspects of performance are non-contractible, then it may be optimal to leave some other verifiable aspects of performance unspecified. The underlying assumption is that the design of the explicit contract, in addition to affecting outcomes directly by specifying particular choices for verifiable actions, also affects outcomes indirectly by altering the set of self-enforcing implicit agreements that are determined by strategic play and impact performance. For these reasons of high renegotiation costs and strategic ambiguity, we argue that more complete contracts comprising greater output controls are negatively related to client satisfaction and project profitability. In brief, we expect:

Hypothesis 3: An increase in output controls in strategic outsourcing engagements negatively relates to client satisfaction and the financial performance of the engagement.

## Empirical Analyses

## Data and Operationalization of Variables

We analyze archival data on 390 strategic outsourcing contracts signed by 194 USand Canada-based firms during 2007–2009. The vendor for these outsourcing contracts is one of the largest strategic outsourcing services providers. The umbrella of services rendered to clients includes infrastructure management, business process management, and strategic IT initiatives, among others. We note that each client has exactly one contract with the vendor; we therefore use the term account/client/ contract interchangeably. We provide details of a sample contract that lists some of these services in the online Appendix. Data limitations reduce the effective sample size in most analyses to 390 observations.

Each year, the vendor performs a detailed survey of clients for its strategic outsourcing services to assess their satisfaction with various dimensions of their outsourcing engagement. To qualify for this survey, the contract must be active for at least six months. These surveys are administered to clients via an external research partner; direct contact between the vendor and client is not allowed. All clients who respond to the survey are assured that their responses will remain confidential and that results will be reported only in aggregate, thereby, addressing privacy concerns and minimizing potential bias in self-reported data. In interviews lasting an average of over 60 minutes, clients rank their satisfaction with various dimensions of the outsourced service such as quality of contracted services, quality of value added services, governance of the outsourcing relationship, the likelihood of continuing the relationship, and recommending the vendor to other clients. The survey also takes into account vendor’s responsiveness to the client’s strategic IT initiatives, the vendor’s awareness and appreciation of these initiatives, and the vendor’s capability in taking suitable action in the design and delivery of services to attain the aforementioned strategic goals.

The vendor complements this survey of client satisfaction with a detailed monthly internal assessment of the financial performance, efficiency, and effectiveness of services delivered, comprising rankings of business controls used, financial health of the outsourcing contract and quality of service. The client approves the vendor’s assessment of these service dimensions before they are recorded in the contracts database. These measures of client satisfaction and account health form the primary data inputs to our study. The structured process described above in garnering client satisfaction with the vendor alleviates potential bias that may otherwise reflect in the survey responses. Measures of these constructs and their reliability are detailed in the following section.<sup>2</sup>

## Satisfaction

Measures of client satisfaction in prior empirical research on outsourcing [55, 73] have largely emphasized satisfaction with service quality based on the SERVQUAL instrument [71]. However, for strategic outsourcing, the focus of the outsourcing contract shifts from mere attainment of quality to value delivered, vendor initiated innovations, and whether tangible business outcomes were achieved through these outsourcing services. In keeping with this notion, the client survey for each of the three years in our dataset measures satisfaction with contracted services and value created beyond contracted measures of quality. Specifically, these surveys include eleven questions on 1) overall satisfaction with the vendor, 2) likelihood of purchasing again from the vendor, 3) likelihood of recommending the vendor’s services, 4) satisfaction with the quality of partnership with the vendor, 5) satisfaction with the vendor’s responsiveness to the client’s changing business needs, 6) satisfaction with support provided by the vendor (in terms of both quantity and quality of resources and teamwork with the client teams), 7) satisfaction with the vendor in meeting their promises and commitments, 8) satisfaction with value provided by the vendor (in terms of products and services received for the money and effort expended by the client), 9) satisfaction with value-add provided by the vendor, 10) satisfaction with innovation levels of the vendor, and 11) satisfaction with the governance mechanisms established by the vendor.

The degree of satisfaction is measured by the client’s response to these eleven questions along a ten-point scale where “1” represents extremely dissatisfied and “10” represents extremely satisfied (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.958). We use an average of these eleven parameter scores as our measure of client satisfaction (CSAT). A principal component analysis was used to assess the convergent validity of the satisfaction measures, our analysis shows that only one factor emerges with Eigen value > 1 [40], indicating convergent validity.

## Financial Performance

The vendor tracks the financial performance (FIN\_PERF) of the contract in order to gauge its financial health each year. The financial performance is measured along an 8-point scale where “1” represents accounts whose financial performance is extremely poor, and “8” represents contracts with excellent financial health. The questions used to assess the financial performance are globally agreed upon, such that there is parity in their interpretation. Financial performance is estimated through a rule-based assessment of whether financial targets have been established for the contract after taking into account the time elapsed since the start of the contract, whether the contract has been missing its targets in the past and estimating whether the financial outlook for the account indicates that financial targets for the quarter and year will be met. The financial assessment takes into account not just whether the account has met the specified financial targets, but also whether these have been met within the desired or predicted timelines and within a certain threshold of the contracted or target values. For instance, when the contract’s financial target is at risk, whether it will risk meeting the target by more than 10% or \$500K, whichever is less, or whether the contract missed the targets for three consecutive months will result in a differential score relative to contracts that are within these thresholds. In these cases, the manager is required to provide details on any open issues that the contract is dealing with, and whose financial performance is declining. This homogenous rule-based assessment across contracts lends a degree of objectivity in estimation of financial performance; this measure of financial performance is one of our primary outcome variables.

## Activity Controls

We operationalize activity controls as the usage of lean methods in service delivery (LEAN), and business controls (BIZ\_CTRLS). The vendor has developed a set of lean practices (e.g., agile development, shorter iterations of customer feedback, and “work-out” techniques for empowerment, among others) in an effort to mitigate the risks and uncertainty inherent to delivering cognitively intense, strategic outsourcing projects from delivery centers located across the globe as well as reduce process redundancies and activities that do not contribute to customer value. The vendor uses the dummy variable LEAN as “1” to indicate if a contract leverages these practices in the pertinent year, and “0” otherwise.

In addition to the indicator variable LEAN that specifies whether lean practices were leveraged in the contract or not, we utilize a richer measure for activity controls. Every outsourcing contract in our sample involves the establishment of business controls for data privacy and security, effective financial and other regulatory compliance, reporting, disclosure, and operations. Each month, the delivery account manager assesses the effectiveness of business controls used in the contract. Comprehensive checks are performed to assess whether procedures are documented, corporate instructions are complied with, project risk is effectively identified and managed, and control issues identified in prior audits have been closed. If not, the vendor assesses whether any steps to resolve the issue have been identified. Based on all these input parameters, the vendor ranks the business controls used in the contract along an eight-point scale where “1” reflects extremely poor controls, “5” reflects average controls, and “8” reflects extremely good controls. Thus, BIZ\_CTRLS provides a rich and exhaustive measurement for the efficacy of the activity controls. The efficacy of controls is estimated through an examination of the extent of usage of diverse business controls including those mentioned in the submission. This internal review of controls by the vendor is reviewed by standards-based third-party audits of controls in service organizations (e.g., SAS70, AUP, etc.) that examine whether control objectives are accurately stated, processes are suitably designed to achieve the objectives, and controls are being implemented with sufficient effectiveness to achieve the objectives. They are also validated by the clients’ assertion that enough information on the outsourced processes is available to them to make an assertion about their internal controls. The vendor’s assessment of business controls is always revised to include any divergence from its original assessment. Because these are monthly scores, we average these ratings for each of the three years in our panel dataset. The online Appendix details the estimation results from an alternate model that uses geometric means instead of arithmetic means for BIZ\_CTRLS.

## Capability Controls

We operationalize capability controls as the use of developed market (DEVM\_CTR) service centers, those located in higher cost developed countries such as the USA/ EU, and of emerging market (EM\_CTR) service centers, those that are located in lower cost emerging economies. The former embodies on-shore or near shore vendor presence that can help with critical business needs, and the latter exemplifies the low cost solutions that the vendor can leverage to reduce the cost of ownership of the strategic task. With its global presence, the vendor can optimize service delivery from both cost and resource perspectives, and uses one or more global delivery centers to reduce the cost of ownership of the outsourced function. The vendor tracks the site or location from where the outsourced services are delivered. Using this information, we calculate the total number of developed market centers (DEVM\_CTR) and emerging market centers (EM\_CTR) involved in the delivery of the outsourced service each year.

## Output Controls

Output controls are conceptualized in terms of completeness of the contract. In our study, we measure the extent of completeness of a contract for a particular year as the number of service level objectives (SLOs) and service level agreements (SLAs) implemented during that year. The number of SLOs and SLAs is decided at the beginning of each contract year. Both of these measures have been widely used in the IT outsourcing literature to assess the contract completeness. Specifically, SLAs are defined as “a formal written contractual agreement between the service recipient (SR) and the service provider (SP) that specifies the various facets of the service to be provided at certain levels to meet business objectives [34:122].” SLAs are detailed enough to include not only the details of the service, but also define the service metric, performance evaluation criteria (e.g., how many SLAs missed may raise a red flag), whether this performance is measured at the contract or event level, the tools that may be used to measure the performance, the audit structure in place that allows the client to assess whether the contractual SLAs are being met, the time period of measurement (e.g., daily, weekly, monthly, yearly), the escalation mechanism, and the rewards and penalties associated with these agreements being met. In sum, SLAs are part of the vendor-client contractual agreement and a specific service commitment contracted.

SLOs, on the other hand, refer to specific performance objectives within SLAs for each service activity, function and process that must be achieved. They define specific, measurable metrics for the service, such as response time, resource availability, or quality, and are an important element of the service contract in strategic outsourcing and implement the common objectives, expectations, and philosophy between the vendor and the client to ensure performance [33]. SLOs provide specific metrics that must be achieved for the SLA to be met. As an example, an SLA may stipulate the rewards and penalties for IT infrastructure upkeep [59], while an SLO may specify the performance metric, for instance, the systems should be available 99.99% of the time. Together, both SLAs and SLOs are complementary measures of contractual completeness.

## Service Quality

Prior literature points to the significant impact of service quality on client satisfaction. Although we do not hypothesize about the performance impact of service quality, we include this important construct (SQUAL) in our analyses. The vendor systematically utilizes various rules to rank the quality of the project along an eightpoint scale where “1” reflects extremely poor quality, “5” reflects average quality, and “8” is indicative of extremely good quality. In particular, the survey takes into account SLA misses, SLO misses, allied penalties and major incidents, time taken to resolve such incidents, any critical pattern in quality target miss rate, and the previous period’s operational health of the contract in determining the score. The vendor also emphasized that this quality score imputed by the vendor is not recorded in the vendor’s database unless the client concurs with this score. By contract, vendor has to record the measure SQUAL each month; we use the average of these monthly values for a given contract year. In the online Appendix, we also report the results from an alternate model that uses geometric means instead of arithmetic means for SQUAL.

## Other Controls

In addition to the main constructs in our study, we control for other potential differences in the outsourcing initiatives that may impact client satisfaction and project profitability, such as the relationship length (TENURE) and total contract value (TCV). We also control for project complexity. Specifically, we control for the total competencies that the contract draws on (TOTAL\_ COMPTNCS) as well as for the total number of service lines that the contract caters to (TOTAL\_SVC\_LINES). Longer-term relationships are associated with greater relational embeddedness [60, 77] that complements the contract to positively impact satisfaction. Embeddedness also decreases transaction costs to increase profitability [73]. TCV, TOTAL\_COMPTNCS, and TOTAL\_SVC\_LINES are indicators of the complexity of the contract [84]. For instance, total competencies refer to the distinct groups of skill sets in the firm - methods, processes, tools, and infrastructure - that the contract leverages for service delivery and is a proxy for contract inputs. Similarly, total number of service lines represents the umbrella of output activities that the vendor does in the strategic outsourcing contracts examined in this study, such as network and infrastructure provision, customer relationship management, web hosting, etc. and is indicative of the complex nature of this contract. We also control for the client’s industry and country fixed effects (whether US or Canada).

## Methodology

Since we have multiple observations per client, our data form an unbalanced panel. To validate our hypotheses, we estimate the following model:

$$
\begin{array}{l} C S A T _ {i, t} = \alpha_ {0 i} + \alpha_ {1 i}. L E A N _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {2 i}. B I Z \_ C T R L _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {3 i}. D E V M \_ C T R _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {4 i}. E M \_ C T R _ {i, t} + \\ \alpha_ {5 i}. S L O S _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {6 i}. S L A S _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {7 i}. S Q U A L _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {8 i}. T E N U R E _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {9 i}. T C V _ {i} + \\ \alpha_ {1 0 i}. C O N T R O L S _ {i, t} + \varepsilon 1 i, t \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

and

$$
\begin{array}{l} F I N \_ P E R F _ {i, t} = \beta_ {0 i} + \beta_ {1 i}. L E A N _ {i, t} + \beta_ {2 i}. B I Z \_ C T R L _ {i, t} + \beta_ {3 i}. D E V M \_ C T R _ {i, t} + \beta_ {4 i}. E M \_ C T R _ {i, t} + \\ \beta_ {5 i}. S L O S _ {i, t} + \beta_ {6 i}. S L A S _ {i, t} + \beta_ {7 i}. S Q U A L _ {i, t} + \beta_ {8 i}. T E N U R E _ {i, t} + \beta_ {9 i}. T C V _ {i} + \\ \beta_ {1 0 i}. C O N T R O L S _ {i, t} + \varepsilon 2 i, t \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

In the aforementioned equation, $" i ^ { \prime }$ is each account and $" t ^ { \prime }$ is the year. We assume the error terms $( \varepsilon _ { 1 i }$ and $\varepsilon _ { 2 i } )$ to be normally distributed and uncorrelated across the two equations.<sup>4</sup> We use the simplified notation $a _ { i } ^ { \prime } = [ \alpha _ { 0 i } \alpha _ { 1 i } . . . \ : \alpha _ { 1 0 i } ]$ and $\beta _ { i } ^ { \prime } = [ \beta _ { 0 i } \beta _ { 1 i } . . . \beta _ { 1 0 i } ]$ , such that $a _ { i } ^ { \prime }$ and $\beta _ { i } ^ { \prime }$ denote all the coefficients in our model that affect an account’s client satisfaction and financial performance. For instance, a $\beta _ { I i }$ is positive shows that for account $\mathbf { \widetilde { \Gamma } } _ { i } \mathbf { \widetilde { \Gamma } } _ { j }$ , ceteris paribus, LEAN practices result in better financial health.

Strategic outsourcing services are idiosyncratic to a business context, and hence, distributed heterogeneously across the clients and their contracts in our sample. Specifically, clients are heterogeneous with respect to the total competencies being leveraged and the total service lines being catered to in the contract. These two constructs are not only proxies for contract complexity [84] but are also indicators of the idiosyncratic and specific investments that vendors need to make for strategic outsourcing contracts to be successful. Not accounting for such client heterogeneity would bias the coefficient estimates in Equations 1 and 2 [32] and may result in poor managerial decisions [3].

We evaluate a continuous client heterogeneity model in which we allow the coefficients to be client specific, that is, we assume that the individual level coefficients $a _ { i } ^ { \prime }$ and $\beta _ { i } ^ { \prime }$ follow a common prior distribution, as follows:

$$
\alpha_ {i} = \delta_ {1 0} + \delta_ {1 1}. T O T A L \_ C O M P T N C S _ {i} + \delta_ {1 2}. T O T A L \_ S V C \_ L I N E S _ {i} + v _ {1 i};\tag{3}
$$

$$
\beta_ {i} = \delta_ {2 0} + \delta_ {2 1}. T O T A L \_ C O M P T N C S _ {i} + \delta_ {2 2}. T O T A L \_ S V C \_ L I N E S _ {i} + v _ {2 i}\tag{4}
$$

$$
w h e r e v _ {1 i} \sim i i d N (0, V _ {\alpha}) a n d v _ {2 i} \sim i i d N (0, V _ {\beta})
$$

Thus, $a _ { i } ^ { \prime }$ and $\beta _ { i } ^ { \prime }$ become functions of the three observable client attributes and allow us to examine the effect of client heterogeneity on our model parameters. For example, the effect of TOTAL\_COMPTNCS on $\beta _ { 6 i }$ denotes the differential impact of number of $S L A s$ on the financial performance between contracts with fewer or more competencies. Thus, if $\beta _ { 6 i } < 0$ and $\delta _ { 2 1 } < 0$ , then greater number of SLAs are associated with poorer financial performance, but this effect is greater for clients with more number of competencies.

The appeal of estimating such a hierarchical model is high as it allows us to examine client specific coefficients. Given that the contracts vary significantly across clients, taking into account the specific complexity that a particular contract brings in enhances the ability to predict outcomes for that client, and thus has important managerial implications for the vendor [3, 51]. However, while the attraction of the hierarchical model in Equations 1–4 is manifest, our dataset may not allow us to use classical inference methods, which rely on the asymptotic properties of large samples for estimation and may not provide meaningful estimates at the individual parameter level (see [65] for a detailed discussion). That is, using classical statistical methods for the hierarchical model that we are interested in is computationally and econometrically infeasible, given the data constraints that we face. We, therefore, use Bayesian inference process to help with the computation intensity typical with such models [80].<sup>5</sup> In particular, we use standard techniques used in estimating Bayesian inference models: we use diffuse priors for the model parameters, and then apply Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods using a Gibbs sampler for data augmentation. We adapt the widely used and publicly available bayesm package coded in R for our estimation. We run the MCMC simulation for 50,000 draws, and only use the last 30,000, discarding the first 20,000 as burn-in.<sup>6</sup>

A limitation of our data is the possibility that some of the predictor variables (for instance, LEAN, DEVM\_CTR, EM\_CTR) are endogenous, resulting in biased and inconsistent estimates. Both the vendor and the client choose these contractual variables mutually and it is likely that such mutual selection rules out any strategic choice exercised by the vendor. However, even if these contractual variables are jointly selected, unobserved client and vendor characteristics correlated with these predictor variables may affect satisfaction and profits. The controls and client/vendor may be chosen simultaneously, thereby rendering the choice of controls endogenous. We follow Davidson and MacKinnon [19] to conduct an augmented regression test using the residuals from the suspected endogenous variables. The null hypothesis (H0) is that the regressors are exogenous. Our test results show that none of the variables previously outlined are endogenous; the p-values from the DWH test all exceed 0.1; indicating that endogeneity may not be of concern in our model. That said, any remaining endogeneity issues would potentially limit our analysis.

## Client Heterogeneity

We are also interested in examining how client/contract heterogeneity affects the impact of activity, output, and capability controls on client satisfaction and financial performance. Recall that Equations 3 and 4 allow us to examine the effect of two relevant heterogeneous attributes: a) total competencies, that is, the set of myriad skill sets required for delivering the range of services that the vendor performs as part of the strategic outsourcing contracts, and b) total service lines, which pertain to the range of these services.

## Robustness Checks

The hierarchical Bayesian model gives us insight into the nuanced interactions in our dataset. As robustness checks, we estimate a number of alternate models, details of which are provided in the Online Appendix. We first examine whether customer satisfaction mediates the pathway to financial performance using a structural equation model and find that our analysis does not support this premise, likely because of the limited time period for which data are available to test the hypothesized relationships. Investments in client satisfaction take longer than two years to translate into profits for the vendor.

We next estimate the Seemingly Unrelated Regression (SUR) model to demonstrate the structural validity of our model. Specifically, we estimate this model using the xtsur command in Stata, which fits a many-equation SUR model for panel data using random effects estimators [12]. These results largely echo the main findings presented in Tables 3–5.

We use secondary data provided by the vendor for analysis and thus, are necessarily dependent on the measures the vendor uses to gauge business controls and service quality. In addition to the SUR model, we perform additional robustness checks by estimating Equations 1–4 using alternate values of business controls (BIZ\_CTRLS) and service quality (SQUAL). Specifically, the additional analyses in the Online Appendix use a) recomputed values to a Likert like scale and b) geometric means instead of arithmetic means of BIZ\_CTRLS and SQUAL and are consistent with those shown in Tables 3–5.

## Discussion And Conclusion

Strategic outsourcing or the outsourcing of near-core functions is increasing, but its performance drivers are poorly understood. While prior research in IS [21, 60] has largely focused on the role of broad governance artifacts such as the contractual or information structure, to our knowledge, our study is the first to understand how specific process and output controls that instantiate these governance artifacts impact satisfaction and financial performance. Specifically, our study builds on existing literature in marketing and information systems on seemingly dichotomous goals of client satisfaction and profitability [5, 64, 66, 67, 82] to a) contend that these outcomes are synergistic rather than antagonistic for strategic outsourcing contracts, and b) to provide an understanding of different types of formal controls in strategic outsourcing by clearly explicating, measuring, and testing the impact of activity, capability, and output controls on these outcomes. Furthermore, our results support the hypothesized performance impacts of formal controls, thence advancing the literature in control theory by testing the performance impacts of formal controls in an inter-firm context.

## Activity Controls

We find that on average, activity controls contribute significantly and positively to client satisfaction and profitability. Lean methods ensure that the vendor’s processes are optimized for service delivery, thereby improving satisfaction and profitability. Business controls too are particularly important for the success of strategic outsourcing relationships, where the outsourced process is near core and involves transformational objectives. As a result, clients are often required to share strategic assets, knowledge, and resources with the vendor to develop a shared understanding of the process [60]. Business controls that ensure regulatory compliance, information security, or data confidentiality, amongst others, provide a secure environment to share such assets, resulting in improved client satisfaction. This information is also salient to efficiency of task execution and hence enhances project profitability.

## Capability Controls

We find support for our hypotheses on capability controls. We find that on average, the use of development centers located in emerging markets negatively affects client satisfaction and profitability. In contrast, the use of development centers located in emerging markets negatively affects client satisfaction and profitability. The results emphasize the importance of cultural or “psychic” distances in execution of strategic work, especially when distributed across organizational and geographic boundaries [26, 27, 52, 53, 56]. Our results suggest that emerging markets, while providing access to a vast pool of human capital at potentially lower costs of ownership, may also be associated with coordination costs required to manage the complexity of strategic outsourcing relationships [1, 53, 60, 82]. The ensuing maladaptation may adversely impact contract financials, possibly through rework, penalties, or lower cost efficiencies.

## Output Controls

Our results for output controls suggest that success of strategic outsourcing contracts are driven more by relational governance and mutual goals rather than contractual completeness [37]. In line with our theoretical arguments, we find that an increase in both SLAs and SLOs, which proxy contractual completeness, is detrimental to client satisfaction, and that an increase in the number of SLAs also detracts from financial performance. The results are consistent with the findings of prior research [11, 28] that incompleteness is an essential feature of well-designed contracts in the case of complex initiatives – when certain aspects of performance are unverifiable as in the case of complex strategic outsourcing initiatives, it is often optimal to leave other verifiable aspects of performance unspecified. The results also reveal the curse of too much measurement in complex initiatives. The more the metrics measured, the more likely the shortfall in at least one of the parameters. However, even a minor infraction is likely to have a psychological effect on client satisfaction—since the vendor is not meeting standards—and perhaps, also triggers penalty payments that hurt project profitability. Therefore, in complex strategic outsourcing contracts, relative to a more narrow “measurement” focus on output metrics, relational contracting that allows the relationship to be responsive to unanticipated contingencies in the task environment and provides trust and incentives for the vendor to invest in non-contractibles is likely to be associated with superior performance and satisfaction [28]. Contrary to our hypoth esis, we find that an increase in the number of SLOs enhances financial performance. SLOs are the measurable performance objectives [33] that translate to more codifiable processes and tasks, making it easier to inspect the vendor output [6]. In the absence of commitment to measurable SLOs for a contracted SLA, the vendor may be tempted to behave opportunistically and thereby, incur penalties. Conversely, for a given SLA, an increase in the number of SLOs, likely impedes opportunistic behavior and lowers the penalties (and associated costs such as litigation and arbitration) that the vendor could incur through opportunistic behavior or myopic optimization of near-term costs. Therefore, for a given SLA, an increase in the number of SLOs contributes positively to the contract’s financial performance [6]. Our results for the impact of SLAs and SLOs, taken together, suggest that in general, fewer service commitments are better for strategic outsourcing contracts; however, given specific commitments, it is beneficial for the vendor to articulate these commitments in terms of specific service objectives and measures. Thus, SLOs allow the vendor to amortize the costs of implementation of various contractual commitments through better specification, fewer errors, less rework, lower penalties, and higher quality output, thereby increasing profitability [6, 59]. For instance, if the client has committed to application upgrades as part of an end-to-end IT outsourcing contract, an SLO would articulate upgrade times for different categories of applications across client locations, codify the upgrade process so as to minimize errors, and get buy-in from the client, thereby, reducing subsequently renegotiation costs, resulting in higher profitability.

## Heterogeneity in Strategic Outsourcing Contracts

The tradeoffs inherent to the use of these controls are evident as the idiosyncratic complexity of the outsourced task increases. Our data allows us to examine complexity in a more nuanced manner, both in terms of the number of competencies required to execute the project as well as the scale of a particular contract. Consistent with prior research (e.g., [60]), we find that it is difficult for the vendor to simultaneously increase both the scale of the project and the range of competencies involved in its execution without denting financial performance of the contract. Our results that incorporate contract heterogeneity also point to the difficulty in designing and implementing contractual measures to improve both client satisfaction and profitability. For instance, an increase in the number of competencies increases the effect of SLAs on contract’s financial performance while decreasing their effect on satisfaction. This may be because the greater the range of competencies required for servicing a complex task, the greater the vendor’s costs. SLAs, in improving the contractibility of the task, place limits on the cost of these competencies for the vendor and hence improve profitability. Specifically, SLAs make the service requirements more explicit and contractible such that they offer a stopping point for the vendor’s efforts; vendor need not strive to invest excessively on competencies. Additionally, increasing competencies would also increase productivity, making it easier to adhere SLAs and reduce associated penalties. However, as noted earlier, they may detract vendor attention from the non-contractible tasks that improve client satisfaction [11]. Similarly, an increase in the number of service lines decreases the effect of SLAs on profits while increasing their effect on client satisfaction. While increasing competencies may increase productivity and make it easier to adhere to SLAs, the same cannot be said of increasing service lines. Each service line may require distinct resources, making it more difficult to scale and it is more likely that SLAs will be missed. The commensurate penalties of missing SLAs may explain why increasing service lines decreases the effect of SLAs on financial performance. That is, in the case of strategic outsourcing, holding competencies constant, increasing service lines through scaling operations may involve significant design and measurement costs that decrease the financial performance of a contract. However, they safeguard the client against appropriation concerns, thus improving satisfaction. We find similar tradeoffs in the case of SLOs that emphasize the difficulty in contracting for these complex tasks. Our nuanced findings are thus not only significant in contributing to the extant literature on outsourcing and contract design, but also help both clients and vendors make more informed decisions as they manage strategic outsourcing contracts.

## Limitations

As with all research, our study is subject to certain limitations. We rely on archival data on North American clients from a single vendor and may be limited by issues of endogeneity not fully addressed by the Bayesian estimation. We are also constrained by the lack of granular information on vendor compensation; our dataset only includes variable price contracts but does not distinguish between different compensation types within this class of contracts (such as incentive contracts or time and materials) that may impact client satisfaction and contract profitability. Our design also cannot track temporal economies of scope – in profitability or satisfaction. For example, the short time period of this study does not support a satisfaction mediated pathway to contractual profits; one explanation may be that investments in client satisfaction likely take longer than two years to translate into contractual profits for the vendor, or that they vary by industry sectors (e.g., [54]). Future research could explore these temporal relationships in greater detail.

## Conclusions

Strategic outsourcing contracts involve significant uncertainty, complexity, and interdependence between the client and vendor. They also typically exemplify the difficulty of optimizing for multiple performance objectives; in this case, for client satisfaction and project profitability. Our examination of formal control mechanisms that aid their implementation suggests that while activity and capability controls enhance both client satisfaction and financial performance objectives, output controls may detract from these objectives; both clients and vendors need to acknowledge the relational nature of strategic outsourcing contracts and incomplete contracts may be more desirable in this context. These insights provide a new academic perspective on strategic outsourcing, notably, that the implementation of formal controls to suit the idiosyncratic task environment of a client resolves information asymmetries and builds relational trust to not only improve client satisfaction but also enhance vendor profits. In turn, our study advances the literature on control theory and performance of outsourcing relationships.

We hope that our study will generate further interest in the antecedents and consequences of different process and output control mechanisms in outsourcing relationships. For instance, prior literature suggests a cost-mediated pathway to profit for vendors [39]; it is likewise possible that customer satisfaction mediates the pathway to profit for vendors in strategic outsourcing contracts. It would also be interesting to understand how different pricing regimes affect the incentives as well as the impact of these control mechanisms for strategic contracts. We therefore hope that a richer dataset with a longer timeframe and different pricing regimes allows future research to examine more nuanced, granular, and longterm relationships between satisfaction and profitability for strategic outsourcing contracts.
