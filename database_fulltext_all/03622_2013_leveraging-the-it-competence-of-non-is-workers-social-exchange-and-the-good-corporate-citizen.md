RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Leveraging the IT competence of non-IS workers: social exchange and the good corporate citizen

Joshua M. Davis

Department of Marketing and Supply Chain Management, School of Business, College of Charleston, Charleston, SC, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Joshua M. Davis, Department of Marketing and Supply Chain Management, School of Business, College of Charleston, Charleston, SC 29424 U.S.A. Tel: + 1 843 953 6653

## Abstract

Organizational IT competence is fundamental to achieving strategic value through technology investments. Importantly, as business users continually gain experience with enterprise systems and a new generation of tech-savvy workers enters the labour force, IT competence is increasingly distributed beyond the IS department. Despite its strategic potential however, IT competence residing within the functional areas of the firm is essentially untapped until it is volunteered by functional area knowledge workers. Addressing the changing landscape of IT competence across the enterprise, the current study examines factors that drive business professionals to volunteer their IT competences to the firm. This research introduces the concept of IT competence volunteering, which is conceptualized as a form of organizational citizenship behaviour that is not explicitly contracted by the firm and may not be directly rewarded. Guided by social exchange theory, a multi-foci model is introduced that positions IT competence as well as workplace exchange relationships as antecedents of IT competence volunteering intention. Overall, the study’s results empirically demonstrate the important impacts that user-IS department exchange and perceived organizational support have on the business professional’s intention to volunteer his/her IT competence to the firm. ## Introduction

Organizational information technology (IT) competence is fundamental to delivering value through IT investments (Sambamurthy et al, 2003; Tippins & Sohi, 2003). Importantly, this competence resides primarily within the heads of the collective members of the firm in the form of workrelated knowledge, skills, and abilities (Nordhaug & Gronhaug, 1994; Bassellier et al, 2001; Newell et al, 2004). The traditional view of IT human capital has typically looked to the IS department as the sole source of organizational IT competence (Peppard, 2007; Davis et al, 2009). However, over time many functional area members have grown technically competent through constant exposure to IT and frequent participation in IS implementation efforts (Davis et al, 2009). Meanwhile, a generation of tech-savvy workers who grew up with IT is entering the labour force. As a result, the landscape of how IT competence is distributed across the enterprise is changing, requiring fresh thinking about IT human capital and how to leverage this expanding resource.

Past research argues that IT success depends heavily on functional areas taking leadership positions in IT activities such as IT planning and IT implementation (Bassellier et al, 2003). In this context, IT competence in the user base can be valuable to the organization for multiple reasons. For one, with functional expertise and technical expertise, IT competent business professionals are better positioned to identify and evaluate IT-based opportunities (Bassellier et al, 2001). Furthermore, past research indicates that leveraging the IT competence of business users in IT implementation can increase user satisfaction with implemented systems by enhancing the IT competence of the implementation team overall (Davis et al, 2009). Finally, IT competent business professionals are central to effective post-implementation knowledge transfer (Baskerville et al, 2000). With the ability to understand ‘why’, ‘when’, ‘where’, and ‘how’ to use implemented systems; these individuals can be instrumental in knowledge diffusion from development and implementation teams, out to the user base of the organization (Baskerville et al, 2000).

While there is potential value in leveraging IT competence distributed beyond the IS department, organizations attempting to do so must overcome an important agency problem. Specifically, before a business professional’s IT competence can be leveraged it must be volunteered to the firm. Because individually held IT competence is largely tacit in nature (Bassellier et al, 2001), leveraging it often requires active engagement in the technical side of the organization (Davis et al, 2009). Thus, for the business professional, volunteering IT competence carries the open-ended consequences of increased workload and responsibilities which have not been explicitly contracted by the firm, and may not be directly rewarded. While voluntary extra-role behaviour such as this often serves as the glue holding an organization together (Yen et al, 2008), many companies lack a general sense of ‘communityship’ whereby organizational members contribute in ways beyond their stated job descriptions (Mintzberg, 2009). As a result, a firm’s ability to draw out and apply IT competence distributed beyond the IS department is recognized as a valuable capability for achieving value through IT (Sambamurthy et al, 2003; Bhatt & Grover, 2005; Peppard, 2007).

Addressing the changing landscape of distributed IT competence across the enterprise, this study examines workplace factors that motivate business professionals to go beyond their functional roles, and volunteer their IT competence to the organization. Extending the research on IT competence and IT human capital, a theory-based construct labelled IT competence volunteering intention is introduced, which captures the business professional’s intention to reveal his/her valuable IT competence to the firm and volunteer it to the technical side of the organization. Guided by past research, IT competence is positioned as an antecedent of IT competence volunteering intention (Bassellier et al, 2003). Extending this stream and guided by social exchange theory (SET), this study introduces relational antecedents of IT competence volunteering intention, targeting department-level and organization-level exchange partners (Lavelle et al, 2007; Karriker & Williams, 2009). Overall, this study focuses on answering the following research question:

RQ1: What workplace factors drive business professionals to go beyond their functional roles and volunteer their IT competence to the firm?

## Conceptual background

## Business professionals’ IT competence

At the individual level, competence has been generally conceptualized as the potential that leads to effective behaviour (Bassellier et al, 2001). Importantly, competence can also be defined specifically to fit a particular discipline, job, or functional area (Kollmann et al, 2009). As a result, a variety of competence sets can exist; each scoped to a relevant workplace context. In the domain of IT, individually held competence has been defined as IT-related knowledge and experience possessed by an individual that enables him or her to contribute to the technical side of the organization (Bassellier et al, 2001). As the definition implies, individually held IT competence is not necessarily directly linked to a specific task, but rather relates to the ability to transfer knowledge across tasks (Bassellier et al, 2001). Moreover, IT competence possesses a substantial tacit component that is difficult to isolate and measure (Bassellier et al, 2003; Tippins & Sohi, 2003). Thus, applying individually held IT competence requires active involvement in the technical side of the organization. The focus of this study is specifically on the IT competent business professional, although his/her primary area of expertise is an area other than IT. In this context, IT knowledge and experience can be generally classified into five domains – technology, applications, system development, management of IT, and access to IT knowledge (Bassellier et al, 2001).

Technology – Knowledge and experience regarding the technologies in the organization.

Software applications – Knowledge and experience regarding the software applications in the organization.

System development – Knowledge and experience regarding the system development methods and project management practices in the organization.

Management – Knowledge and experience regarding the vision, goal setting, resource allocation, and performance monitoring regarding IT in the organization.

Access to special information – Knowledge of how/where to acquire additional information about the information systems or IT practices employed in the organization.

## IT competence volunteering: a social exchange perspective

SET is one of the most influential conceptual paradigms for understanding workplace behaviour (Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005). The theory frames interactions between parties using a cost-benefit perspective. Much like economic exchange, social exchange generates an expectation of some future return for contributions (Gefen & Ridings, 2002); however, unlike economic exchange the exact nature of the rewards for beneficial acts provided are rarely explicitly negotiated or specified a priori (Konovsky & Pugh, 1994; Whitener et al, 1998). As such, the stability of any social exchange relationship depends largely on the norm of reciprocity (Gouldner, 1960). The norm of reciprocity is the social expectation that exchange parties will respond in kind to one another; when one party does another a favour, some future return can be expected (Wayne et al, 1997). This norm helps establish trust that individuals providing beneficial acts will be repaid, even though the timing and form of repayment are unclear.

A core tenet of SET is that relationships evolve over time into trusting, loyal and mutual commitments guided by reciprocation of beneficial acts (Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005). In the organizational context, SET posits that employees hold long-term views of their social exchange relationships, with the norm of reciprocity guiding evaluations of relationship quality (Blau, 1964; Wayne et al, 1997). Moreover, employees can maintain distinct perceptions about – and direct meaningfully different attitudes toward – multiple organizational foci concurrently (e.g., the organization as a whole, workgroups and/or departments, individual co-workers, etc.) (Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005; Lavelle et al, 2007; Karriker & Williams, 2009).

A key mechanism for maintaining these exchange relationships in the workplace is organizational citizenship behaviour (OCB) (Korsgaard et al, 2010), referring to discretionary employee behaviour that goes above and beyond the call of duty and is not explicitly rewarded in the context of an organization’s formal reward structure (Konovsky & Pugh, 1994; Organ et al, 2006). Framed in SET, OCB constitutes beneficial action that a worker provides either to reciprocate for prior benefits received (paying back) or for expected receipt of future benefits (paying forward) (Organ et al, 2006; Korsgaard et al, 2010). OCB inevitably provides relevant information regarding attributes of the individual that may or may not be easily accessible to others in the organization (Salamon & Deutsch, 2006). Thus, an essential component of committing to OCB is revealing information to others about possessing the capabilities which underpin the actual citizenship behaviour (Salamon & Deutsch, 2006). OCB can influence firm performance in several ways. For one, this behaviour frees up valuable organizational resources for more productive purposes. In addition, OCB reduces the need to devote scarce resources to purely maintenance functions. Furthermore, OCB facilitates the coordination of activities across workgroups. Finally, OCB enhances an organization’s ability to adapt to environmental change (Bolino et al, 2002).

In their theory-grounded conceptualization of OCB, Van Dyne and colleagues (1994) describe OCB along five interrelated dimensions:

Organizational obedience – respect for organizational rules and willingness to expend appropriate effort on the organization’s behalf.

Organizational loyalty – allegiance to the organization and promotion of its interests.

Social participation – participation in organizational affairs that involves interpersonal and social contact, such as engaging in positive communications with others and involvement in group activities.

Advocacy participation – participation in organizational affairs that may challenge other members of the organization and reflect a willingness to be controversial; demonstrating behaviours which are typical of an internal change agent.

Functional participation – participation in organizational affairs that contributes to organizational effectiveness but is personally focussed, reflecting commitment and selfdevelopment. These behaviours include performing additional work activities, self-development, and volunteering for special assignments.

Along these lines, a business professional going beyond his/her functional role and volunteering IT competence to the organization can be conceptualized as an explicit instance of OCB. First, this behaviour reflects willingness to expend appropriate effort on the organization’s behalf and promotion of its interests. Furthermore, by demonstrating willingness to contribute to the technical side of the firm, volunteering IT competence serves as an explicit form of participation with others and involvement in group activities. This type of behaviour also reflects willingness to participate in organizational affairs that may be controversial and promote change. Finally, volunteering IT competence involves willingness to perform additional work activities and volunteer for special assignments. Following the theory and research discussed above, this study uses the term IT competence volunteering to refer to an explicit form of OCB whereby the business professional goes beyond his/her formal job role by volunteering his/her IT competence to the technical side of the organization.

## Exchange relationships and IT competence volunteering: a multi-foci approach

The perceived quality of exchange relationships in the workplace is recognized as an important driving force of OCB (Farh et al, 1990). While the relationships between perceptions and attachment-driven behaviours can be reciprocal at times, SET scholars theorize that the primary direction of causality proceeds from perceptions to behaviour (Van Dyne et al, 1994; Cardona et al, 2004). Thus, remaining consistent with the literature and responding to the prescription that studies investigating exchange relationships in the workplace incorporate multiple organizational foci concurrently (Lavelle et al, 2007), this study investigates the business professional’s perceived exchange quality with department-level and organization-level parties as relational drivers of IT competence volunteering intention.

User-IS department exchange A worker’s overall perception of exchange with members of a particular work group varies in terms of quality, based on the content and process of exchange (Seers, 1989; Liden et al, 2000). Specifically, high-quality exchange relationships between the individual and a work group go beyond task-oriented exchanges required for completing jobs, to include work-related expertise and feedback, consideration for one another, and social rewards (Liden et al, 2000; Anand et al, 2010). Adapted specifically to the business professional’s perception of exchange with the IS department, a high-quality relationship is characterized by the IS department’s demonstrated willingness to give and receive suggestions for improving IT in the business, the presence of mutual consideration for the interdependence between one another’s work, and the IS department’s recognition of the user’s potential to contribute to the technical side of the organization. Following this rationale, the relationship between the business professional and the IS department in an organization is labelled user-IS department exchange, and is formally defined as a business professional’s perception of his or her exchange relationship the IS department as a whole (adapted from Seers, 1989, p. 119).

Perceived organizational support Organizational support theory (OST) (Eisenberger et al, 1986; Rhoades & Eisenberger, 2002) identifies perceived organizational support as a key element defining the nature of the relationship between the employee and the firm overall (Organ et al, 2006). OST is founded on the premise that the organization’s readiness to provide employees the necessary aid to perform effectively, recognize and reward work effort, and provide for their socio-emotional needs will determine workers’ beliefs about the extent to which the organization values their contributions and is concerned with their well-being (Organ et al, 2006). Rooted in OST, the construct perceived organizational support (POS) reflects the notion that ‘employees develop global beliefs concerning the extent to which the organization values their contributions and cares about their well-being (Eisenberger et al, 1986, p. 501).

While POS is not a direct measure of the overall quality of reciprocated employee-organization exchange, it is considered by SET scholars to be the primary surrogate of such a concept (Cole et al, 2002; Organ et al, 2006). Framed in terms of the norm of reciprocity, POS underlies an employee’s inferences about the organization’s commitment to him or her (Wayne et al, 1997); the quality of what is provided in exchange is implied (Cole et al, 2002). Remaining consistent with the large volume of research on social exchange between the employee and the organization as a whole, the current study uses POS to measure the business professional’s perception of his/her exchange relationship with the organization overall. Borrowing from past conceptualizations, POS is defined here as the business professional’s global beliefs concerning the extent to which the employing organization values his/her contributions and cares about his/her wellbeing (Eisenberger et al, 1986).

## Conceptual model

Addressing the changing landscape of IT competence across the firm, the objective of this study is to gain deeper understating of workplace factors that motivate business professionals’ IT competence volunteering. Past research has investigated a direct link between the IT competence of business professionals and proactive IT behaviours in the organization (Bassellier et al, 2001). Building on this stream and guided by SET, the conceptual model (Figure 1) extends the knowledge base in this area by taking a multi-foci approach to understanding the role of exchange relationships in motivating IT competence volunteering. In addition to the business professional’s level of IT competence, the conceptual model positions the perceived quality of exchange relationships with department-level and organization-level parties as direct antecedents of IT competence volunteering.

Given the cross-sectional nature of the current study, IT competence volunteering intention was used as a surrogate measure of future behaviour. This approach is supported by the theory of reasoned action (TRA) (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975), which is one of the most prominent social-psychological accounts of individual behaviour. According to TRA, the primary determinant of an individual’s future behaviour is his/her intention to commit that behaviour (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). Taken further, TRA identifies attitude and subjective norm as factors influencing behaviour intention (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). Attitude is the individual’s overall evaluation of what it would be like to perform a particular behaviour whereas subjective norm is the individual’s perception of social pressure to perform the behaviour. As it relates to the current study, IT competence volunteering intention implicitly captures positive attitudes about the act of volunteering and, as explained earlier, the influence of the norm of reciprocity. Thus, consistent with TRA and past cross-sectional research on the effects of individually held IT competences in the workplace (Bassellier et al, 2003), IT competence volunteering intention was measured as the dependent variable.

## IT competence and volunteering intention

IT competent business professionals are more likely to identify with the challenges faced by the IS department and understand the role of IT in the business, which should positively influence volunteering intention (Bellou et al, 2005). Furthermore, IT competent business professionals are more likely to anticipate and proactively seize opportunities to implement practices that add value and engage in IT behaviours (Bassellier et al, 2001). Through greater understanding of the value of IT in the business and the ability to identify with the challenges faced by the IS department, IT competent business professionals should be more driven to volunteer valuable IT knowledge and experience to the technical side of the organization.

## H1: A business professional’s IT competence will positively impact his/her IT competence volunteering intention.

## IT competence and user-IS department exchange

IT competence held by the business professional can create mutual knowledge and understanding with the IS department (Davis et al, 2009), which increases the likelihood that communication will be understood between the parties (Cramton, 2001). Enhanced communication and comprehension between exchange partners fosters trust in the viability of the exchange relationship. Taken further, mutual knowledge can lead to assurance for the exchanging parties that giving benefits will result in receiving comparable benefits. This trust and assurance provides the basis for quality social exchange, especially when perceived asymmetries exist between partners (Konovsky & Pugh, 1994). In the context of a business user’s relationship with the IS department, mutual knowledge that underpins the exchange relationship should encourage moving beyond simply accomplishing a job, toward mutual consideration for the interdependence between one another’s work, mutual recognition of the user’s potential to contribute to the technical side of the organization, and finding ways for improving IT in the business.

H2: A business professional’s IT competence will positively impact the perceived quality of his/her exchange relationship with the IS department.

## User-IS department exchange and IT competence volunteering intention

Business professionals are more likely to go above and bevond the requirements of their iob roles and contribute their technical competences when quality exchange exists with members of the IS Department (Love & Forret, 2008). Individuals in quality social exchange relationships tend to rely on the norm of reciprocity and commit to the party with which they are engaged (Rupp & Cropanzano, 2002). This commitment motivates behaviours that have favourable consequences for the exchange partner over time (Lavelle et al, 2007). In the case of user-IS department exchange, a high-quality exchange relationship should lead to more satisfaction, trust, and identification with the IS department. Ultimately, a satisfied, trusting user that identifies with the IS department is more motivated to commit to positive, supporting behaviours toward IT in the business (Carr, 2006).

H3: The perceived quality of a business professional’s exchange relationship with the IS department will positively impact his/her IT competence volunteering intention.

## Perceived organizational support and IT competence volunteering intention

In their meta-analytic review of research on OCB, Podsakoff and colleagues (Podsakoff et al, 2000) found support for a direct relationship between POS and OCB. Framed in terms of the norm of reciprocity, as an employee’s socio-emotional needs are met by the organization, a positive perception is formed about the organization’s feelings toward him or her, creating a perceived imbalance between his or her own contributions and the support provided by the organization (Cole et al, 2002). As a result, high levels of perceived organizational support create a sense of obligation to the organization, with the employee seeking to reciprocate with OCB (Eisenberger & Fasolo, 1990; Wayne et al, 1997). Given that IT competence volunteering is an explicit form of OCB, it is reasonable to expect POS will motivate IT competence volunteering intention.

H4: Perceived organizational support will positively impact IT competence volunteering intention.

## Control variables

In this study, two organizational variables were controlled for that may influence IT competence volunteering intention – organizational IT governance and IT outsourcing. By granting line functions formal responsibility and decision-making power over technology in the business, IT governance structures that empower the line functions should bear influence on business professionals’ IT-related behaviours (Tiwana, 2009). IT outsourcing is another organizational structure that may bear influence on IT competence volunteering intention. Past research identifies lack of user involvement and participation is a key risk associated with the decision to outsource IT (Iacovou & Nakatsu, 2008; Nakatsu & Iacovou, 2009). Thus, it is reasonable to expect that organizations characterized by a high degree of IT outsourcing will be characterized by lower volunteering intentions.

## Research method

## Scale development

A literature search was first conducted within the domain of SET and the constructs of interest to generate sample items. The items were further developed via a series of independent, in-depth telephone interviews with four functional area and three IS department professionals at senior executive and enterprise levels, representing a global aerospace firm and a global construction equipment firm with major operations in the Midwest region of the United States. These companies were deemed appropriate because of their heavy use of technology enterprise-wide as well as their diverse user bases.

Interview questions centred on understanding the nature of IT competence residing in the user base; understanding how this IT competence could be leveraged to deliver value to the company; and identifying specific relational drivers and barriers associated with volunteering this IT competence as a functional area professional. After completing telephone interviews, the content of the discussions was analysed to tailor the measurement items for each of the constructs in the model. In line with past research, the interview process suggested that business professionals’ IT competence comprises both explicit and tacit technical knowledge (Bassellier et al, 2001). The interview process further highlighted that the most important IT competence residing within the user base pertains to the current systems and methodologies used within the organization, as they could be immediately applied. Thus, the items measuring business professionals’ IT competence were tailored to explicitly focus on the systems and methods currently employed within their focal organization.

The interview process informed the development of measurement items for the IT competence volunteering intention construct as well. Specifically, a clear overarching theme across interviewees was that revealing one’s IT competence to the organization carried the open-ended risk of being asked to participate in current and future IT projects. In light of this result, each of the items measuring IT competence volunteering intention explicitly incorporated the associated open-ended risk of being asked to contribute to the technical side of the organization. Following the norm of reciprocity, explicitly incorporating associated risks into these measures implicitly taps into action motivated by the obligation to reciprocate from previously received benefits or the expectation of receiving benefits in the future. The same interview process was used to verify and/or tailor the items measuring user-IS department exchange and perceived organizational support.

IT governance was measured using a single item borrowed from Davis and colleagues (Davis et al, 2009). In addition, degree of outsourcing was measured using a single item, which asked the respondent to assess how much of the firm’s IT operations he/she believes are outsourced to other companies. Details of the measurement items used to capture the constructs of interest are presented in Appendix A.

## Survey procedure

The sampling frame for the study comprised of working professionals enrolled in a professional MBA programme at a large research university in the Eastern United States. In addition to being employed in professional roles, all of the targeted participants had completed an MBA-level course on management information systems (MIS). A total of 422 potential participants were initially targeted for the survey. An initial correspondence was sent to each targeted participant, explaining the nature of the study and clearly indicating that target respondents could not be employed in a formal technical role in their organization at the time of the study. Those holding a technical position and/or wishing to opt out of future correspondence for any other reason were asked to reply indicating so. A total of 57 individuals replied and were eliminated from future correspondence.

Because all constructs were measured via single informants, a two-phase survey approach incorporating a two-day time lag between phases was employed to mitigate any issues with method bias. The first phase of data collection captured measures of participant demographics, IT competence, and user-IS exchange. The second phase of data collection captured measures of perceived organizational support, IT competence volunteering intention, and the control variables IT governance and degree of outsourcing. One week after sending the initial introductory correspondence, a follow-up correspondence was sent to the remaining 365 targeted participants with a link to the first survey. Exactly two days after completing the first survey, each respondent received a final correspondence with a link to the second survey. During both phases of data collection, an introductory page was provided before granting access to the survey, which restated that respondents must be currently formally employed in a non-technical role. Of the 365 initially invited to participate, 286 completed both surveys, resulting in an overall response rate of 67.8%. In both surveys, participants were reminded to focus on their current organization and current functional job role. In order to match surveys, each participant was asked to provide a personal code that would not reveal his/her identity but could be remembered and used on both surveys. The goal of this approach was to avoid any halo effects, and further ensure the anonymity of the respondents. At the completion of both rounds, there were no issues of mismatched codes. Overall, the average age of the respondents was 29.7 years old, with an average of 4.7 years working for their current organizations. Gender breakdown of the participants was 58.7% male and 41.3% female.

## Data analysis

Following the decision criteria provided by Petter and colleagues (Petter et al, 2007), all of the latent constructs were modelled as reflective except for the IT competence construct, which was modelled as formative in nature. Analysis of the data was conducted using the partial least squares (PLS) estimation technique (Chin, 1998) as applied in the software package SmartPLS version 2.0.M3 (Ringle et al, 2005). PLS uses a component-based approach to analysing structural models with multi-item latent constructs (Chin, 1998) and is capable of analysing models with formative and reflective constructs (Gefen & Straub, 2005). It has been suggested that the PLS algorithm used to analyse the data in the present study requires at least 10 times as many data points as the maximum number of indicators or latent constructs leading to a given latent construct (Straub et al, 2004). The data set used to empirically test the conceptual model meets this requirement.

Means and standard deviations for each of the reflective constructs employed in the model are provided in Table 1. Because some of the scales differed across constructs, standardized values were utilized throughout the remaining analyses.

## Measurement model assessment

For the formative IT competence construct, the validation steps prescribed by Petter and colleagues (Petter et al, 2007) were followed to evaluate the construct’s psychometric properties. Construct validity was assessed by conducting principal component analysis to examine the item weights. The results of principal components analysis indicated that all of the formative items had significant weights except for one. The non-significant item pertained to knowledge and experience regarding the system development methods and project management practices used in the organization. While this path was non-significant, past conceptualizations of IT competence indicate that competence in system development methodologies and project management practices serves as an important dimension of IT competence overall (Bassellier et al, 2001, 2003; Davis et al, 2009). Thus, the decision was made to keep the item in order to preserve content validity (Petter et al, 2007). Reliability of the IT competence construct was assessed by conducting variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis for each of the measurement items (Petter et al, 2007). VIFs were calculated using SPSS v. 16 for Windows by regressing each item for IT competence on the other items in the model. The VIF analysis resulted in no values exceeding the cut-off of 3.3 (Petter et al, 2007), suggesting multicollinearity is not a problem in the data.

The psychometric properties of the reflective measures were assessed by examining internal consistency reliability as well as convergent and discriminant validities (Gefen & Straub, 2005). Internal consistency reliability was assessed by Cronbach’s alpha and composite relia bility scores. As illustrated in Table 2, all of the reflective constructs in the model exceeded the suggested cut-off of 0.70 for Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability (Straub et al, 2004).

The PLS approach to confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), as outlined by Gefen and Straub (Gefen & Straub, 2005), was employed to assess the convergent and discriminant validities of the reflective items. Convergent validity is shown when each of the measurement items loads significantly on its intended latent construct (Gefen & Straub, 2005). A bootstrapping procedure was run to determine the significance levels of the loadings of each item on its intended construct. The results of the procedure indicate that each reflective measurement item loads significantly on its intended latent construct at Po0.01, demonstrating good convergent validity.

Discriminant validity was assessed by comparing the loading of each measurement item on its intended construct, with that item’s loading on every other factor in the model (Gefen & Straub, 2005). The cross loadings matrix provided by SmartPLS was used to for this analysis, and all items loaded higher on their intended constructs by multiple magnitudes than on any other reflective factor in the model, demonstrating good discriminant validity (Gefen & Straub, 2005). Further testing of discriminant validity was accomplished via average variance extracted (AVE) analysis, which involved examining whether the square root of the AVE for each construct was larger than the inter-construct correlations (Fornell & Bookstein, 1982). As illustrated in Table 3, all constructs in the model shared more variance with their indicators than with any of the other constructs, further indicating good discriminant validity. Overall, the results of testing indicate that the psychometric properties of the measurement model are sufficiently strong for valid testing of the structural model.

When data for the independent and dependent variables are collected from the same informants, common method variance (CMV) may lead to inflated estimates of the relationships between variables (Podsakoff & Organ, 1986). Testing for CMV was conducted using the PLS approach as outlined by Liang and colleagues (2007) as well as Vance and colleagues (2008). Each reflective indicator was converted into a single-indicator construct, which was then linked to the original construct as well as a central CMV factor. Squared values of factor loadings are interpreted as percentage of indicator variance explained by that factor. If CMV factor loadings are insignificant and substantive factors account for substantially more variance explained than the CMV factor, it can be concluded that CMV is unlikely a serious concern (Liang et al, 2007). From this analysis, of the 20 paths from the CMV factor to single-indicator constructs only one was significant; meanwhile, all of the substantive factor loadings were significant at Po0.05. The average of the substantively explained variance of the indicators was 0.76 and the average method-driven variance was less than 0.01. As a result, it was determined that common method variance was not a serious concern.

## Structural model results

Assessment of the structural model was accomplished by examining standardized path coefficients and the variance explained in the dependent constructs, as derived from a bootstrapping procedure (Chin, 1998). The results are illustrated in Figure 2.

As Figure 2 shows, all of the hypotheses were confirmed. IT competence (b ¼ 0.257; Po0.01), User-IS department exchange (b ¼ 0.369; Po0.001) and perceived organizational support (b ¼ 0.212; Po0.01) significantly predicted IT competence volunteering intention. In addition, IT competence (b ¼ 0.459; Po0.001) significantly predicted user-IS department exchange.

## Discussion and implications

The landscape of IT competence across the enterprise is changing, becoming increasingly distributed beyond the IS department (Bassellier et al, 2003; Davis et al, 2009). Importantly however, effectively leveraging IT competence within the user base is a matter of motivating business professionals to volunteer that competence to the organization. Addressing this issue, the current study contributes to the knowledge base by identifying factors that motivate business professionals to go beyond their functional roles and volunteer their technical competence to the firm. In doing so, this study responds to calls for building an organizational perspective of the factors that impact successful IT investments (Peppard, 2007; Davis et al, 2009).

The findings of this study hold important implications for MIS research. For one, this study introduces a new theoretically grounded construct, labelled IT competence volunteering intention, which captures the business professional’s intention to reveal his/her IT competence and contribute to the technical side of the business. Past research suggests that involving IT competent users in enterprise-level IS initiatives can result in greater payoff from IT (Davis et al, 2009); however, before the technical know-how of functional area workers can be leveraged, it must be offered up. In line with past research that has found a direct link between business professionals’ IT competence and proactive IT behaviours in the workplace (e.g., Bassellier et al, 2003), the results of this study suggest that IT competence alone does bear unique influence on IT competence volunteering intention. However, extending the knowledge base this study also provides clear evidence that social exchange relationships in the workplace bear strong influence on IT competence volunteering intention. Rooted in SET, this study uncovers the important roles that user-IS department exchange and perceived organizational support play in business professionals’ IT competence volunteering intentions.

This study also extends previous research on SET. For one, this study introduces a new exchange relationship labelled user-IS department exchange, which measures the perceived quality of an employee’s exchange relationship with the IS department. While SET scholars advocate that workers have exchange relationships with distinct groups/units within the organization, most studies in this area focus within the functional domain of the focal employee. This research applies the concept of exchange quality to one of the most important yet challenging cross-functional relationships in the organization, the IT/ business relationship. The results suggest that the individual-department exchange relationship can bear important influence on OCB intentions. An additional theoretical contribution of this study is the application of the multi-foci approach to the MIS domain. Social exchange theorists advocate the multi-foci approach for understanding the complexity of the employee experience and predicting workplace behaviour. Consistent with SET, the results of this study indicate that business professionals can concurrently hold distinct exchange relationships with the IS department, as well as the organization as a whole. Overall, the results indicate that the multi-foci approach is appropriate for explaining behaviour in the MIS domain as well.

This study’s results also have important implications for practice. For one, the results suggest that the relationship between the business professional and the IS department carries substantial influence on IT competence volunteering intention. In line with past work that identifies quality user-IS department relations and collaboration as a necessary condition of achieving value through IT (e.g., Van Den Hooff & De Winter, 2011), this study suggests that a meaningful step toward leveraging IT competence distributed beyond the IS department is to carefully manage the relationship between the IS department and the rest of the business. Relationship marketing initiatives (Morgan & Shelby, 1994) focused on developing and maintaining effective business unit/IS department exchange may actually be an effective way of tapping into IT competence residing in the user base. Given the importance of user-IS department exchange in tapping IT competence distributed beyond the IS department, this study echoes Carr’s (Carr, 2006) prescription that IS managers detect, understand, measure and manage factors influencing user-IS relationship quality.

In addition to managing the exchange relationship between IS and the business, top management should recognize the exchange relationship that exists between a tech-savvy workforce and the organization as a whole. While the importance of top management support has been discussed in past MIS research on IS success, the exchange relationship between the knowledge worker and the organization as a whole has not received much attention. User-IS department relations alone may not be adequate for uncovering the hidden IT community within the firm, and low or no perceived organizational support can get in the way of management’s ability to tap this resource. Employee relationship management (ERM) strategies and technologies are emerging as a way to foster strong exchange relationships between individual workers and the organization as a whole (Yang et al, 2011). By tending to workers’ socio-emotional needs and increasing satisfaction, ERM and similar initiatives can potentially lead to uncovering new knowledge-based resources inside the user base that ultimately increase the value created through IT.

## Limitations and future research

Like most empirical studies, there are limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results of this research. For one, this study was cross-sectional in nature and examined established exchange relationships. While the model is rooted in theory, it is possible that reciprocal relationships exist between IT competence and user-IS department exchange, as well as between user-IS department exchange and IT competence volunteering intention. Future research in this area might take a longitudinal approach to examining these relationships. In addition, all of the variables were measured by single informants. While rigorous testing for common method variance indicated no serious problems with method variance exist, certain constructs in the model may be better captured using other informants. Future research could take an approach similar to those used by Davis et al (2009) and Van Den Hooff & De Winter (2011), which incorporate responses from both functional area and IS department respondents. Another limitation of this study involves the single-item measurement of the control variables in the model, making it impossible to assess the reliability or validity of these scales. Future studies incorporating IT governance could adopt a multiitem measure of the construct that better captures its complexity, such as that developed by Tiwana and Konsynski (Tiwana & Konsynski, 2010). Furthermore, degree of IT outsourcing could be measured along the lines of the specific IT activities outsourced, similar to the approach used by Gilley and Rasheed (2000) in the general business context. Also related to the measurement model is the fact that revealing IT competence and volunteering IT competence were combined in the conceptualization and measurement of IT competence volunteering. While this approach is justified by past research which indicates that OCB inevitably conveys information to others about the capabilities of the individual, they may be considered distinct processes, in which case combining them into a single construct may obscure their uniqueness. Thus, the results should be interpreted with this in mind. Future research might choose to separate revealing IT competence and volunteering IT competence into two separate constructs, and then examine the influence of exchange relationships on each variable in isolation. ## Conclusion

The current study extends the research on IT competence and IT human capital by addressing the roles of social exchange relationships on business professionals’ intentions to volunteer their IT competences to the technical side of the organization. For the business professional, IT competence volunteering is an act of organizational citizenship that carries the open-ended consequences of increased workload and responsibilities which have not been explicitly contracted by the firm, and may not be directly rewarded. Therefore, simply possessing IT competence does not guarantee it will be volunteered. This study’s results suggest that leveraging IT competence distributed beyond the IS department is largely a function of managing exchange relationships within the organization. This study provides clear evidence that the quality of a business professional’s social exchange in the workplace bears strong influence on his/her IT competence volunteering intention; particularly when considering the relationships with the IS department and the organization as a whole. Overall, this research serves as a first step toward understanding and developing an organizational capability with increasing importance – leveraging the IT competence of non-IS workers inside the firm.
