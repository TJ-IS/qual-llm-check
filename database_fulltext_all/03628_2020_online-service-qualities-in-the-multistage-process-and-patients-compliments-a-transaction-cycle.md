## Abstract

This study examines patients’ use of different compliments for healthcare service quality through a three-stage process in the transaction cycle: pre-, during-, and after-sale. Based on the transaction cycle framework, we explore how a patient’s choice for psychological and material compliments is affected by service quality received at different stages, and how the effects vary depending on the service price. Using data collected from an online health community (OHC) in China, we reveal several important findings. First, service quality at each stage is an important driver of patient compliments. Second, high service quality at the pre- and after-sale stages motivate patients to give material compliments rather than psychological compliments, whereas psychological compliment is more likely to be triggered for high service quality received at the during-sale stage. Third, service quality at one stage enhances the effect of service quality at its subsequent stages on compliments. Fourth, service price increases the influence of service quality on compliments, and patients tend to give material returns over psychological returns for doctors charging high service price. This paper extends prior research on service quality from a transaction cycle perspective and provides OHCs with implications for theory and practice.

Keywords: Service quality; compliment; online health communities; transaction cycle; psychological and material.

## 1. Introduction

Because of the uneven distribution of healthcare resources in China, there is a lack of high-quality healthcare service providers in smaller cities or rural areas [1]. As a result, online health communities (OHCs) have flourished by providing patients with a reliable channel to obtain information, support, and paid or unpaid services from healthcare professionals across the country. Such OHCs also allow patients to give feedback on their experience and provide other patients with valuable information to gauge a doctor’s quality and make their selections accordingly [2-5]. For doctors, OHCs present both benefits and risks: while the publicly available doctor- and service-related information can reduce patients’ perceived risk and enhance their awareness of doctors available nationwide, it requires doctors to pay careful attention to and effectively manage patients’ feedback, which is critical to their image and reputation.

In an online setting, positive valence (compliments), negative valence (complaints), and valence-free (suggestions or comments) feedback are the primary types of consumer feedback [6] and provide potent opportunities for sellers to improve their performance [7, 8]. However, complaints and compliments are two opposing behaviors and may not be motivated by the same attributes. Complaints play a central role in the studies of consumer satisfaction [9], while issues

pertaining to consumer compliments have received far less attention in the literature. The potential of compliments in shaping sellers’ behaviors may be greater than that of complaints as compliments represent a potent form of feedback on products or service [10] and the responses to compliments can produce a higher level of satisfaction than those to complaints [11]. Companies should not only address consumers’ complaints but also actively probe their satisfaction [12]. Compliments not only boost sales but also promote employees’ morale and service attitude [13]. Compared with other types of positive feedback such as leaving a good rating or “like,” which has been examined in previous studies [14, 15], compliments such as monetary gifts or thank you notes to doctors contain more information and emotion, and require more energy, time, knowledge, and money on the patients’ part. As a result, they may be valued more by other patients in their doctor selection process. Despite the popularity of patient compliments such as monetary gifts and thank you notes, no research has is gap in the literature.

Compliments deserve special attention in healthcare, especially in an online environment. Compliments in traditional hospitals such as silk banners to doctors are costly and cannot be easily seen by other patients. OHCs provide a mechani nts to compliment doctors in a controlled fashion using small monetary gifts, appreciation tokens, or thank you notes. For doctors, them in providing high-quality healthcare service. In addition, compliments as a form of visualized reputation contribute to improving patients’ trust and attracting more patients by disseminating the information about a doctor’s competence [3, 14]. Prior studies have shown that compliments from patients improve doctors’ subsequent service (e.g., [16]). Compliments also affect employees attitude, income, and behavior, and ultimately, a company’s performance and profitability [13]. In addition, public compliments, when very visible, can promote competition and imitation by others who strive for higher performance standards [17], thus leading to quality improvement on OHCs and enhanced doctor-patient trust and relations. Therefore, a better understanding of the determinants of compliments can inform doctors on their efforts to increase their returns, help OHC platforms improve their website design, pricing mechanism, and subsequent operational efficiency to train, motivate, and encourage doctors to actively and consistently provide high-quality services online.

The treatment process, medical environment, and service quality are regarded as key factors influencing patient behavior [15] and are highly valued and continuously improved in traditional hospitals to improve patient satisfaction [18]. Therefore, patient satisfaction in the hospitals can be affected not only by doctors’ service quality but also by hospitals’ environment and service quality from other staff. Different from the offline environment, online doctor-patient interactions are not embedded within a hospital environment. Hence, patient satisfaction is mainly decided by doctors behavior, namely, doctors’ service quality. In addition, the processes of service delivery in offline and online environments are quite different. For example, patients may pay more attention to doctors communication skills, manner etiquette, facial expressions, and intonations in the face-to-face channel, resulting in a complex measure of service quality in the offline environment. On OHCs, doctors provide services on the Internet through written communication. As a result, service quality measured by these written words can be more easily measured and compared across doctors. Studies have revealed the importance of service quality in improving patients’ intention to share their positive experiences (e.g., [2]). However, how service quality online influences a patient’s compliments is largely overlooked. Therefore, based on the characteristics of online service delivery process, our research helps doctors to better understand how service quality affects patients’ complimenting behavior to identify strategies to improve service quality and patient satisfaction.

There are multiple ways through which patients can publicly give compliments to doctors on OHCs. These compliments differ in their psychological and material attributes. Psychological compliments, such as praises, can enhance doctors’ self-efficacy and self-worth [19-21]. Material compliments such as virtual gifts can be converted to a cash equivalent and bring doctors economic benefits [20, 21]. Material compliments are similar to tips in other service fields, such as the catering industry. Both psychological and material compliments are extrinsic rewards. We distinguish the case in which the psychological dimension plays a dominant role in determining patients’ choice between the two compliments from the case in which the material dimension plays an important role. We call the former psychological compliment and the latter material compliment. The feedback mechanism on OHCs improves doctors’ expressive benefits and addresses their social needs such as esteem and being valuable to others.

Even though OHCs have been widely used around the world for over a decade, little is known about the incentive mechanisms that could foster patients’ willingness to give doctors compliments on OHCs. Among various drivers, service quality has been discussed widely in the literature as a key influence of consumer behavior (e.g., [2, 3, 22, 23]), especially for health services (e.g., [24]). Studies have indicated the importance of service quality for improving patients’ intention to share their positive experiences (e.g., [2]). With the recent economic development in China, Chinese consumers have become more aware of the quality of services received [25]. Moreover, compared with the traditional channel, the online channel supports uch easier product quality comparison, so service quality has become a key factor that influences consumer behavior [22].

Sterne [26] proposes an online consumers’ experience transaction cycle framework consisting of three stages: pre-, during-, and after-sale interactions based on whether the core service is offered. The framework is widely used in existing studies (e.g., [27-29]). Correspondingly, there are three indicators of service quality during these three stages: presale service quality, during-sale service quality, and after-sale service quality. Although prior studies have examined the role of service quality, most combined service quality at different stages without differentiating them and were unable to separate the impacts of service quality in the multistage process (e.g., [3]). Therefore, this research fills this knowledge gap by taking an in-depth look at the effects of service quality on patients’ use of compliments in later stages on OHCs. Moreover, because each patient has his or her assessment of the medical services as well as individual preference on different ways of giving compliments, we also examine the heterogeneity in the impacts of the antecedents (service qualities in different stages and service price) on patients’ use of compliments.

To further extend the extant literature, we investigate how compliments are differentially obtained by doctors charging different service prices. On the basis of the signaling theory, price is often regarded as a pivotal quality indicator for virtual products [30]. First, in medical services, patients lack the knowledge and expertise in both diagnostic skills and treatment options, whereas the medical service providers are the specialists who possess such relevant technical skills and knowledge. As a result, price is an important indicator for patients in judging doctors’ medical service quality, and patients often seek doctors charging higher prices in major hospitals [31]. Second, high-quality medical resources are greatly desired but scarce [32]. Doctors on OHCs can price their service, which helps distinguish doctors’ skills and encourages doctors to improve the quality of their service. This, in turn, leads to increased variability in prices and thus influences patient decisions. Given the asymmetry of medical knowledge between the two parties and characteristics of medical services online, this study aims to explore the role of service price in the domain of medical service.

Specifically, we address the following research questions:

RQ1: How does a doctor’s service qualities at pre-, during-, and after-sale stages affect patients’ use of compliments?

RQ2: How does the service quality affect patients’ use of compliments in later stages?

RQ3: How does service price moderate the relationships between service quality in the different stages and patients’ use of compliments?

RQ4: How do the impacts of the antecedents differ on patients’ choice of psychological versus material compliments?

To answer the above research questions, the current work applies a quantitative approach and examines the effect of service quality in the multistage process on patients’ compliments using data collected from an OHC in China. The empirical results suggest that there are significant effects of service quality in both current and later stages on compliments. Patients’ compliment behavior shows heterogeneity in the transaction cycle, high service quality at the pre- and after-sale stages motivate patients to take material compliments rather than psychological compliments, whereas psychological compliments are more likely to be triggered for high-quality service received at the during sale stage. In addition, service price positively moderates the relationships between service quality and compliments. Our study provides evidence on patients’ behavior from the whole transaction process perspective and enriches the knowledge of OHCs. Moreover, we provide several implications for practitioners, doctors, and OHC providers.

## 2 Theoretical foundations

## 2.1. The framework of the customer-processing operation and transaction cycle

Johnston [33] develops the customer-processing operation framework (Fig. 1), where a consumer goes through a series of stages during a complete service operation cycle. This framework identifies the significant points and activities for each stage. The critical points are the points of entry, impact, and departure. The significant activities with which the consumer is involved are selection, response time, delivery, and follow-up. After having selected a service provider, the consumer chooses the moment (the point of entry) to enter the service system. A consumer usually has to wait time is usually based on a queue such as an invisible queue of incoming telephone calls. When the service provider responds to the consumer in the service system, the process of service delivery begins. During the delivery stage, the services and associated products are provided. On completion of the service delivery, the consumer leaves the service system, then the consumer may choose to give feedback for the service in the follow-up stage. In this research, we examine a doctor’s service quality in the service system that includes response time and delivery stages.

![](/api/attachments/AENPHHDG/fulltext/images/418d65a46d37e55e6ec659af6744cd790ccee2a2856035b1fc7414f7a4b2ef4e.jpg)  
Fig. 1. The customer processing operation framework [33].

A consumer goes through various stages to complete a transaction cycle in the service system. The outstanding classification for a complete transaction cycle is based on whether the service is offered before, during, or after the sale [26]. In product fields, the presale features are those related to activities that consumers perform before placing an order. Examples are membership registration and information gathering. At the during-sale stage, activities associated with product selection and ordering (e.g., comparative shopping and order placement) are carried out. The after-sale features are those related to after-sale services such as problem solving and order tracking [34]. In the online environment, presale service refers to Internet-based consumer service regarding product or transaction issues before a purchase decision is made. During sale is the ordering and payment process. After-sale service refers to customer services such as delivery, returns, and other relationship maintenance activities [35].

## 2.2. Consumer compliments and motivations

Kraft et al. [10] define positive feedback as a compliment taking the form of an acknowledgment or an expression of gratitude. Compliments are consumer-inspired communications and opportunities for companies to gain consumer loyalty and repeat purchase [7, 8]. The counterpart to compliment is complaint, which has been widely examined by scholars. In contrast, research on compliments is lacking in psychology [36]. However, Kraft et al. [10] assert that compliments may be even more valuable than complaints because they provide ideal opportunities for sellers to get in touch with their customers. The responses to compliments produce a higher level of satisfaction than

responses to complaints [11].

With the recent growth in the field of positive psychology, research on compliments has emerged [37]. Positive psychology rediscovers gratitude as a positive emotion [38] that contributes to social and emotional well-being [39]. Gratitude has both short- and long-term effects on individuals [37] and has been found to enhance favorable life appraisal and optimism [40], foster social support, and protect against stress and depression [41], predict social integration, prosocial behavior, and life satisfaction [42], and provide an excellent opportunity for relationship building, and successful marketing efforts [8, 10]. As many services are intangible and highly customized in nature, it is difficult for companies to monitor and control the quality [43]. Therefore, customer compliments are helpful in monitoring employees’ service quality [44]. Customers express their compliments in many ways, such as word-of-mouth, tips and rewards, repeat purchase, etc. Among them, word of mouth has the most powerful impact as it can easily influence other prospective customers’ decisions [13]. How to motivate consumers to express their compliments is an important strategic issue. However, research on this topic is lacking.

Primary motivations that lead to consumer compliments online have been identified as worthiness within a virtual world [45]. Kraft et al. [10] develop eight specific motives that stimulate compliments, including delight or grea satisfaction, dissonance reduction, reciprocity or social norms, an improved relationship with a service person, high involvement with a product or service, voting behavior to continue special services or products, to buffer complaints and increase Among these motivations, satisfaction has been extensively investigated and is regarded as a particularly important one [46-48]. It has long been recognized that consumer (dis)satisfaction and profits are closely related to service quality [49] and influence their decisions [14, 50]. Studies have indicated the importance of service quality in improving the intentions of consumers to share their positive experience [2]. Managing consumer relationships entails attending to consumer (dis)satisfaction, improving service quality and consumer loyalty, and dealing with consumer feedback [51]. However, how service quality online influences patients’ compliments is largely overlooked.

## 2.3. Scarcity of medical resources

High-quality medical resources refer to the superior quality of resources in the entire health care system, such as highly skilled medical staff, advanced instruments and equipment, advanced technology, good educational system, and advanced medical information systems. In reality, the scarcity of high-quality medical resources has become a common problem around the world. In China, there are few ophthalmologists, especially those specialized in fundus [52]. There are around 114 million diabetics and 36 thousand ophthalmologists, and the patient to doctor ratio has reached an astonishing 3,166 to 1 [52]. For a long time, high-quality medical resources are concentrated in large hospitals in major cities and require medical experts to diagnose common diseases, which in turn exacerbate the depletion of limited high-quality medical resources. The development of the health sector is still hindered by structural problems with the supply of medical services. The resulting “difficulty and high costs in accessing medical services” has become one of the major challenges in the Chinese society [53].

The market-driven pricing mechanism on OHCs requires patients to pay a premium for the service received from a high-quality doctor. Because of the lack of medical knowledge and their health concerns, patients use price as an important indicator to determine doctors’ service quality. As a result, patients often seek doctors charging higher prices in major hospitals. Thus, on the one hand, charging a high price improves incomes and makes doctors feel valued, and encourages them to improve service quality. On the other hand, price variance changes the patients’ decisions on compliments. Therefore, we examine the effect of this price variance on patients’ compliment decisions.

## 3. Hypotheses development

Consumer compliments are largely a matter of conscious perception [10]. When patients express their appreciation, gratitude, and happiness to doctors, the affiliated hospitals may also benefit by receiving patient compliments as a result. However, there is a lack of research on positive consumer feedback in the service areas. Moreover, although service quality is regarded as the main factor affecting consumer behavior in existing studies [2], how service qualities in different subprocesses influence consumer behavior has not been examined. We fill this knowledge gap by taking an in-depth look at the effects of online service qualities in the multistage process (pre-, during-, and after-sale) on patients’ compliments in the form of psychological compliments and material compliments on an OHC. ## 3.1. Pre-, during-, and after-sale service quality

In health services, service quality is generally viewed as more closely aligned with behavioral intention (e.g., [3, 24]). Researchers divide service quality into several dimensions, including functional quality, technical quality, environmental quality, and administrative quality. However, the complexity of service quality evaluation is also evident in many failed attempts to replicate the dimensional structure of service quality perceptions. For example, Brown et al. [54] find service quality to be unidimensional. However, most researchers view service quality as multidimensional and have obtained mixed results (e.g., [3]). Because patients lack professional medical knowledge, it is difficult for them to express their perceived service quality based on different dimensions accurately. Therefore, in this research, we use the overall service quality perception at each stage.

On the basis of Johnston’s [33] customer processing operation framework, the evaluation of service quality occurs when consumers are in the service system in the response and delivery process stages. Combined with the transaction cycle framework, we evaluate presale service quality in the response process. Before service delivery, patients need to undergo the response process. Each patient may have expectations about what an acceptable waiting time for the service is. Time factors that may influence his or her perception of service quality include the desire or need for the service, the alternatives available, whether it is peak or off-peak time, and the waiting time relative to process.

On OHCs, at the presale stage, patients need to wait in an invisible queue for doctors to respond to them. At the during-sale stage, the core services (e.g., diagnosis and treatment) and associated services are provided to patients, and patients can contact doctors if they have follow-up questions at the after-sale stage.

## 3.2. Service quality and compliments

A number of scholars have examined the effects of service quality on consumer behavior, such as loyalty [55], switching behavior [25], recommendation, and repurchase [56]. Even though compliments are an important kind of consumer behavior, they have been overlooked in the extant literature (e.g., [9]).

Researchers who believe that service quality is an antecedent to satisfaction argue that a positive service quality perception can lead to favorable behavior (e.g., compliments), which is an expression of satisfaction [57]. More compliments will be given to reward good service [58, 59], which is consistent with the equity theory [60]. In this study, we focus on two different and important types of compliments: psychological compliments (thank you note writing) and material compliments (virtual gift giving). We propose that there are positive relationships between service qualities in the multistage process and patients’ compliments, leading us to the following hypotheses:

H1a. The effect in the current stage: Presale service quality positively affects patients compliments.

H1b. The effect in the current stage: During-sale service quality positively affects patients compliments.

H1c. The effect in the current stage: After-sale service quality positively affects patients compliments.

The two-factor theory suggests that a favorable process condition magnifies the impact of a favorable outcome condition on consumer behavior [46]. Service quality for the process through which the service is delivered to consumers could affect service quality perception for service outcome [3]. Moreover, based on the hysteresis effect [61], there may be a delay between service receipt and compliments. Gratitude has both short- and long-term effects on individuals [37] and has against stress and depression [41], and predict social integration, prosocial behavior, and life satisfaction [42].

On the basis of the sequence of services, we argue that service quality at one stage may influence the relationship between service quality at its subsequent stage and patients’ compliments. That is, service quality at one stage enhances the effect of service quality at its subsequent stage on patients’ compliments.

H2a. The effect in the later stage: Presale service quality enhances the impact of during-sale service quality on patients’ compliments.

H2b. The effect in the later stage: During-sale service quality enhances the impact of after-sale

service quality on patients’ compliments.

## 3.3. Moderating effects of service price

As medical service deals with life and wellness, price is a better heuristic cue than quality [30]. Because of their lack of medical knowledge and their health concerns, patients often use price as an important indicator for doctors’ medical service quality, so they often seek doctors charging higher prices in major hospitals.

On the basis of the framework of medical resource scarcity, high-quality medical resources are highly desirable but scarcely available [32]. Hence, patients often flock to large hospitals in major cities [62], clogging the health system with medical experts treating minor diseases [63]. The market-driven pricing mechanism on OHCs leads to an equilibrium of supply and demand and helps filter insincere patients. Moreover, the pricing mechanism on OHCs forms a market that helps filter out inferior doctors and leads to higher pricing for high-quality doctors. As a result, doctors on OHCs can price their services based on their service quality and demands. The price mechanism helps distinguish doctors with different abilities and encourages them to improve the quality of their service. In addition, the price mechanism leads to increased variability in prices and thus influences patient decisions. As several previous studies show, the signals have an additive effect with more signals (here service quality and price) making consumers more certain about their decisions [64], leading us to the following hypotheses:

H3a. Service price enhances the positive impact of presale service quality on patients compliments.

H3b. Service price enhances the positive impact of during-sale service quality on patients’ compliments.

H3c. Service price enhances the positive impact of after-sale service quality on patients compliments.

3.4. Compliment heterogeneity in the transaction cycle

Compliment is a social emotion that signals individuals’ recognition of the things others have done for them [65] and is a voluntary behavior given after services have been rendered. Consistent with the theory on equity motivation, there is a positive relationship between customer tips and service evaluation [44]. Based on the equity theory, consumer compliments are rewards for excellent service [60]. Specifically, people feel anxious in an inequitable relationship and will attempt to obtain equity even if they incur costs when doing so [58]. Compliments not only enhance social relationships, [66] but also signal to others that the two sides are fair partners [67].

Although both material compliments and psychological compliments can bring doctors tangible and intangible benefits, the public views them differently, which leads to different choices for expressing their gratitude. Material compliments such as gift giving have a long history and have been considered an acceptable human activity, but material compliments coming from patients may raise some concerns that gifts or payments are generally considered to have some purposes such as desiring new and continuous care in the future rather than expressing gratitude [68]. Therefore, when doctors accept these gifts, they may feel responsible to reciprocate by providing services [69]. Specifically, in the healthcare sector in China, gift giving is always a sensitive and controversial topic [80], virtual gifts on OHCs have a knock-on effect [70]. Virtual gifts on OHCs have been questioned as they may force patients to buy doctors gifts for their peace of mind rather than expressing gratitude, which has provoked misgivings from both doctors and patients [70]. As a result, many doctors on OHCs would not even withdraw money from virtual gifts [70].

For psychological compliments, there is a standard format in writing thank you notes on OHCs experience. To write a thank you note, a patient needs to recall detailed past interaction experience with his or her doctor and mentally organize each piece of information, perception, and evaluation into a detailed thank you note. Therefore, these thank you notes can provide other patients with valuable information on a physician’s service quality [3]. As a result, the doctor’s chance of being understood and chosen is much greater [3, 5, 15]. A thank you note with detailed information helps patients express their thanks and provides other patients with details that facilitate their decision-making. By comparison, gift giving is a simple action, and it is difficult to gauge the degree of a patient’s gratitude.

The influence factors of patients’ choices on different compliments mainly come from two aspects: a) What do doctors want? As there are doubts about the motivation for gift giving, patients also worry about the possible side effects of gift giving on doctors, especially on doctors with high-quality service. Besides, there are other more meaningful ways to express gratitude [71], so patients can avoid this controversial compliment. In practice, patients are encouraged to post service feedback (e.g., treatment experiences or thank you notes) online, which not only provides information for other patients but also motivates doctors to improve their service quality and establish an online reputation.<sup>1</sup> The word of mouth (i.e., psychological compliments) brings more powerful benefits than tips or rewards (material compliments) to doctors [13]. Therefore, the compliments doctors wanted to receive most are thank you notes [72]; b) What do patients want? Different from other e-services, medical service deals with life and wellness, so patients deeply care about doctors’ service quality, including diagnosis and treatment abilities, which directly relate to the likelihood of correct disease diagnosis and treatment and affect their health and safety. Therefore, patients would like to cognitively retrieve their knowledge and evaluation of the service from their memory to give a psychological compliment, which is brimming with gratitude for high-quality service.

On the basis of the transaction cycle framework, after having selected a service provider, a consumer usually has to wait for a response from someone, similar to an invisible queue of incoming telephone calls [33]. Presale medical service emphasizes doctors’ response time to patients’ orders. The services and associated products [33], including the diagnosis and treatment activities, are mainly provided at the during-sale stage, and those related to after-sale services such as problem

##

solving [34], disease recurrence and maintenance, are provided at the after-sale stage. Consumer behavior in the follow-up stage are influenced by the activities received in the service system [33]. In healthcare, these activities are doctor-patient interactions. There are direct interactions between doctors and patients at the during- and after-sale stages by telephone or text. Therefore, because the core services for pre-, during-, and after-sale stages are different, consumers value them differently. Compared with during and after-sale service stages, there is no doctor-patient communication at the presale service stage. Hence, it is difficult for patients to write a thank you note that contains detailed interaction experience about the diagnosis and treatment process, the patient’s feelings and experience for expressing their gratitude.

On the basis of the above discussion, in the medical transaction cycle, we hypothesize that there exists compliment exchange heterogeneity by a two-step process as follows. First, considering the characteristics of psychological/material compliments and doctors’/patients’ preferences [72], patients would be more willing to give doctors psychological compliments than material ones when they have received high-quality service. Second, considering the core services and doctor-patient interactions at the different stages, service quality during the three stages would have different impacts on patients’ compliment behaviors. Patients’ feedback behaviors are influenced by the activities received [33], namely how patients interact with doctors in one stage. Specifically, there is detailed and direct communication about diseases at the during- and after-sale stages but not in the presale stage. The presale stage includes activities prior to medical service delivery [26]. However, psychological compliments contain more information disclosure about doctor-patient communication than material compliments. Therefore, first we hypothesize that a high presale service quality is negatively related to patients’ willingness to give psychological complements than material ones. Patients take communication with doctors (only happens in during and after-sale stages) seriously as it deals with life and wellness, which stimulates them to write a thank you note with detailed information to express their gratitude after receiving high-quality service. Therefore, secondly we hypothesize that high during- or after-sale service quality is positively related to patients’ willingness to give psychological compliments than material compliments. Hence, we have:

H4a. Presale service quality is negatively associated with patients’ increased use of psychological compliments than material compliments.

H4b. During-sale service quality is positively associated with patients’ increased use of psychological compliments than material compliments.

H4c. After-sale service quality is positively associated with patients’ increased use of psychological compliments than material compliments.

Comparing these two compliments, although both compliments represent the recognition of doctors’ work and competence, improve doctors’ perceived benefits, and satisfy their social needs such as self-esteem and being valuable to others [73], they have different emphases. Specifically, a [19-21], and a material compliment can be converted to a cash equivalent and bring economic benefits [20, 21]. Therefore, based on the different functions of different compliments, patients may have heterogeneous choices to express their gratitude according to their doctors’ preference. For example, a higher service price may be perceived by patients as the doctor’s preference for monetary reward, which may lead to him gaining more economic compliments from patients. Hence, we have:

H5. A higher service price is associated with patients’ increased use of material compliments than psychological compliments.

## 4. Methods

In this section, we describe our research context, transaction cycle, compliments on OHCs, our data collection process, and the variables, and empirical models.

## 4.1. The research context

We focus on the Good Doctor (www.haodf.com), the largest and most professional medical service website in China. Good Doctor was established in 2006 and now has 142,000 registered doctors from 7,216 hospitals across China. Multiple services are provided to patients, including photo-text consultation, telephone consultation, video consultation, and outpatient appointments.

Good Doctor allows patients to provide feedback on the service they receive. Negative feedback are few compared with positive feedback in healthcare for three main reasons. First, in recent years, doctor-patient conflicts occur frequently in China with some even involving violent attacks. Social media platforms often filter extremely negative or illegal comments on doctors, such as those with personal attacks, threats, and obloquy [74]. Second, patients are unwilling to post negative comments even when they are dissatisfied for the fear of retaliation from doctors. Third, the feedback system helps regulate doctors as they are concerned about the consequence of negative word of mouth. Consequently, we rarely find negative feedback on OHCs. On the basis of the above considerations, examining patient complaint behaviors (negative feedbacks) on OHCs may be biased, whereas unbiased results about compliment behavior can be investigated.

Information technology (IT)-enabled OHCs are ideal for the study of the effects of service quality on patients’ compliments for several reasons. First, in healthcare, there are no tangible products provided to patients, thus service qualit mes vital to patients. Second, medical service has a transaction cycle, like most products, where pre-, during-, and after-sale service form a complete medical service. In our research, t hree stages occur on the very same website. Third, doctor information, especially service information, is missing in the traditional channel. Online communities have facilitated the sharing and delivering of information publicly, thus significantly reducing the degree of information asymmetry between doctors and patients. Finally, the healthcare industry has been transformed by technology and online communities in profound ways, so that understanding patients’ behavior in this context will help us understand and further improve the healthcare industry as well as other service industries.

## 4.3. Compliments on the Good Doctor

Good Doctor provides a forum for patients to express their gratitude publicly through two different ways. First, patients can write thank you notes (psychological compliments) akin to a “silk banner.” Thank you note writing is free, but patients need to spend time to describe their experience with the doctor and whether they are satisfied or not. If a patient chooses to write a thank you note, he or she has to fill out the relevant information and is asked to write at least 30 Chinese characters (see Fig. 6). Thank you notes help enhance doctors’ self-efficacy and self-worth [19-21]. Second, patients can give doctors virtual gifts (material compliments), akin to “red packets” with a small amount of money or tipping in other service fields such as the catering industry. Virtual gifts are sold on Good Doctor and used to express gratitude to doctors (see Fig. 7). Patients need to pay extra more convenient by saving time and effort. The most expensive gifts on the Good Doctor are valued credited to the doctor’s research fund. Thus, they are a kind of extrinsic or economic benefit [20, 21].

## 4.4. Sample and data collection

This study quantitatively explores the effects of the service quality of different subprocesses on patients’ compliments in the online medical service. Identifying the effects using observational data are challenging because of the selection bias. The assignment of patients with different health conditions to doctors with different service quality is unlikely to be random, which poses a challenge to researchers in uncovering the true association between service quality and compliments. The nonrandom assignment online mainly comes from the patient side, namely patients with serious illnesses may be more likely to seek those doctors with high service quality. In addition, patients with different health conditions may have different compliment behaviors. To address the potential selection bias, we selected one specialty of doctors as the study’s subjects: doctors who treat hypertension. First, hypertension has a high morbidity rate and low mortality rate<sup>2</sup>. As a common disease (measured by the morbidity rate), patients’ knowledge on hypertension is greater than that about rare diseases, and there is less information asymmetry [50]. Low-risk diseases (measured by mortality rate) reduce the tendency of patients to blindly pursue high service quality. Second, there are standardized tests for diagnosing patients with hypertension, and the disease is a commonly consulted health condition on OHCs. Third, as after-sale service is not a necessary or compulsive stage in the transaction cycle, patients can voluntarily accept or not. Daily health management is important for hypertensive patients who need long-term tracking and treatments. Choosing hypertensive patients for our research thus helped minimize the possibility that patients do not accept after-sale service.

As a doctor may treat multiple diseases, we determined his or her primary specialization using the disease for which the doctor received the most votes from the patients. We developed a longitudinal design, to explore the impact of a doctor’s service qualities in the multistage process (pre-, during-, and after-sale) on his patients’ compliments. We collected detailed transaction process and content for each patient. On Good Doctor, all transactions are recorded on the doctor’s homepage. We first collected data, including service price, the number of thank you notes, the number of virtual gifts, and other information for each doctor who treated hypertension on October 28, 2018. We deleted those doctors missing important information, such as service quality and service price. Moreover, to ensure that the doctor was active on Good Doctor, the last login time had to be within three days<sup>3</sup>, yielding a sample of 4,335 doctors. Second, we collected two web pages with 60 patients for each doctor. The inclusion criteria were: a) transaction records were collected on 4,335 doctors obtained from the first data collection stage; b) the most recent 60 transactions completed within 30 days were identified and included. As there may be a delay between the service and review [75], we used the 30-day interval<sup>4</sup> to give patients enough time to post their feedback on the doctors’ service. We crawled the entire interaction contents for 260,100 (4,300\*60) patients. The crawling process lasted three weeks (from October 29, 2018 to November 19, 2018) with 647,328 items (an item represents a question/reply from a patient or doctor) included. We randomly picked 100 interaction processes from our sample and found that all the compliments were given within two weeks after the service was finished. Hence, we believe compliments are unlikely to take place after 30 days.

We integrated the data for each doctor by averaging the data (for pre-, during-, and after-sale periods, respectively) from 60 patients to overcome two difficulties. First, patients’ information is hidden on Good Doctor, thus important patient characteristics such as age, gender, education, job, income, health status, and individual preference that influence patients’ compliment behaviors are unobservable. By combining data from multiple patients, we hypothesize that the comprehensive characteristics of patients for each doctor are similar. Second, after-sale service is voluntary and free, thus this period is missing for some patients. On the basis of our dataset, the percentage of patients without after-sale service is about 7.21%. Therefore, we cannot capture after-sale service quality for these patients. We compared the information based on patients’ disease description and interaction process between doctors and patients. By comparing the whole interaction process for 100 patients from our dataset, we did not find obvious differences in disease descriptions and doctor-patient interaction processes between patients with and without after-sale service. The choice on the after-sale stage service may be mainly influenced by external environmental factors. Thus, by averaging data on patients, these cases can be neutralized. That is, all pre-, during-, and after-sale service quality are available. By using the above method, we can avoid subjectively deleting patients from the sample.

## 4.5. Variables and models

Compliments. There are two ways for patients to express their gratitude to the doctors on the Good Doctor: thank you note writing (psychological compliment) and virtual gifts giving (material compliment). We use different methods to measure these two variables: a) the increment of thank you notes/virtual gifts from the last 60 patients (ΔTN and ΔVG); b) the ratio of the increment of thank you notes/virtual gifts to the increment of patients (ATN and AVG). After-sale service is provided for both online and offline patients, though the latter has not received pre- and during-sale service. Therefore, we removed these offline patients from further analysis based on the public transaction contents. The increment of patients is measured based on these retained patients for each doctor; and c) the ratio of the increment of thank you notes to the increment of virtual gifts (RTNVG):

$$
R T N V G = \frac {\Delta T N}{\Delta T N + \Delta V G}
$$

RTNVG is used to examine the impact heterogeneity of antecedents (i.e., service qualities and service price on patients’ choices of different compliments).

Service quality. On the basis of the frameworks of the customer processing operation and transaction cycle, we included a doctor’s service qualities for different subprocesses into our model. Based on the 647,328 items collected from 4,335 doctors, we calculated the presale, during-sale, and after-sale service quality for each doctor. The average response rate in 24 hours for the first questions of patients was calculated to measure presale service quality (P-SQ). The length of reply based on the average word count of a doctor’s replies at the during-sale period/after-sale period is used to measure during-sale service quality (D-SQ)/after-sale service quality (A-SQ). The longer text often includes more product or service details and helps reduce consumers’ quality uncertainty [76]. The length of the text can measure text depth [76] and is regarded as a measure of quality [77]. Therefore, a more detailed reply includes more information and helps patients understand their situations and treatment methods better. On the other hand, insufficient and poor quality doctor-patient communication often is the crux of many conflicts [78]. Two main reasons behind the phenomenon are a) limited time. Short service time is an important reason that causes doctor-patient conflicts. Doctors have to treat traditional patients within normal working hours in the hospitals (e.g., about 60 patients within 4 time to time [78]; b) lack of humanistic care. Patients often complain doctors behave like robots without listening and empathy [78]. The indifference hinders doctor-patient trust building and decreases patient satisfaction. A detailed reply promotes doctor-patient co unication. Therefore, we believe the length of replies measured by word count can re lect a doctor’s service quality.

Service price. A doctor can price his services on the Good Doctor. We calculated the average price based on all online services for each doctor as the service price (SP) variable.

Control variables. We controlled for other factors such as the doctor’s medical title, hospital level, the doctor’s years of experience on OHC, and economic level of the city where the doctor works. In China, doctors have a medical title, which is evaluated and issued by government health departments and measures their education and work experience. The medical title has several types, including the chief doctor, associate chief doctor, attending doctor, and resident doctor. Because the number of resident doctors is very small, we combined it with the attending doctor category and used two dummy variables, MTITLE1 and MTITLE2, to measure medical titles. Hospital level, which is also evaluated and issued by government health departments, reflects a hospital’s functions, equipment, technology, etc. There are three levels: A, B, and C, with A being the best. As the number of level C hospitals is very small, we combined level C with level B and used one dummy variable HLEVEL to measure the hospital level. The level of the city (CL) where the hospital is located is also included with a value of 1, if the doctor comes from top tier cities. The doctor’s experience in using OHC was measured by the number of days since the doctor first joined the OHC (WA). The consumer conformity theory argues that consumer psychology and behavior may be influenced by doctor without the compliments from the 60 recent patients as a control variable (Stocked DV). Fig. 9 shows an example of a doctor’s page on Good Doctor.

Model estimation. We tested our hypotheses on the effects of online service qualities in the multistage process on patients’ compliments. The multiple regression model is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {Compliment} _ {i j} = f (\beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} P - S Q _ {i} + \beta_ {2} D - S Q _ {i} + \beta_ {3} A - S Q _ {i} + \beta_ {4} P - S Q _ {i} \times D - S Q _ {i} \\ & + \beta_ {5} D - S Q _ {i} \times A - S Q _ {i} + \beta_ {6} S P _ {i} \times P - S Q _ {i} + \beta_ {7} S P _ {i} \times D - S Q _ {i} \\ & + \beta_ {8} S P _ {i} \times A - S Q _ {i} + \beta_ {9 \sim 1 4} X _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i}) \quad j \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5 \} \end{array}
$$

where f (·) is a link function depending on the dependent variable and empirical model used. Let $i =$ 1, …, n indexes the doctor, j = 1, …, 5 shows five types of compliment variables, including ΔTN, ΔVG, ATN, AVG, and RTNVG. Coefficients $\beta _ { I } , \beta _ { 2 } ,$ and $\beta _ { 3 }$ measure the effects in the current stage, and $\beta _ { 4 }$ and $\beta _ { 5 }$ measure the continuous effects of service qualities on patients’ compliments. $\beta _ { 6 } , \beta _ { 7 } ,$ and $\beta _ { 8 }$ measure the moderating effects of the service price. X is a vector of covariates and $\varepsilon$ is the error term. All independent and control variables are calculated using the data collected at time t. The service quality in the past should not have been influenced by the current patients’ behaviors.

Fourth, in countries in central Asia, patients’ offering money or gifts to the doctors as a gesture of respect and appreciation is a tradition [80]. However, over time this voluntary tradition has been “complemented or even replaced by the payment demand on the part of the health-care provider, as a precondition for ensuring the treatment,” [80] which may lead to positive results. Therefore, we checked our results by considering the motivation of compliments. Based on the above statement, we needed to distinguish the motivation of virtual gifts giving: whether patients bought doctors gifts for getting better service or just to express their appreciation. We collected all the doctor-patient interaction content for each doctor and identified each gift based on its timing [81]: if the giving behavior happened immediately after a patient asked a question and before the doctor’s answer (an example is shown in (a) in Fig. 10), it was regarded as a gift for getting better service rather than compliment; on the other hand, if the giving behavior happened after the doctor’s answer (an example is shown in (b) in Fig. 10), we treated it as a compliment. We evaluated the motivation of each gift that a doctor received from the last 60 patients. We deleted all gifts that were given for getting better service and only included the gifts given for expressing gratitude. The results are shown in Table 8. We find that patients buy doctors gifts for getting better service on the Good Doctor, but the proportion is small (less than 10%). We obtained results consistent with our main results even after having considered the motivations of gift giving.

## 6. Discussion and implications

To the best of our knowledge, our study is among the first that tests the effects of service quality in both the current stage and later stage on psychological and material compliments from the transaction cycle perspective. Our findings can help people understand patients’ compliment behaviors in online communities.

## 6.1. Result analysis

Using data from an OHC in China, we find strong and robust evidence on the influences of service quality on patients’ compliments. Table 9 shows the summary of our results with most of our hypotheses supported. Our results confirm that service qualities have impacts on subsequent compliments: psychological and material compliments. We find high service quality improves patients’ propensity to post feedback, which is consistent with prior studies [2]. Moreover, our empirical study generates several interesting results.

There are significant effects of service quality in both current and later stages on compliments

High during-sale and after-sale service qualities improve patients’ propensity to express their gratitude to doctors. However, the influence of presale service quality is only significant for virtual gift giving. In addition, we also find that there is significant continuous effect of service quality through the significant moderating effects between the three service qualities at different stages. Specifically, presale service quality enhances the positive impact of during-sale service quality on patients’ compliments, and during-sale service quality strengthens the positive effect of after-sale service quality on patients’ compliments. The pleasant experience at one stage has a spillover effect on the patient’s experience at a subsequent stage.

Positive moderating effects of service price are found

Our results suggest service price only significantly moderates the relationship between during-sale service quality and compliments. One possible reason is that the service price is charged for during-sale service rather than for pre- and after-sale services.

Compliment shows heterogeneity in the transaction cycle

We further find that when barraged with two compliment methods, patients are more likely to buy virtual gifts than writing thank you notes for doctors when they get high pre- and after-sale service qualities. Meanwhile, they are more likely to give thank you notes than gifts when receiving high-quality during-sale service. There are two possible explanations. First, compared with the during-sale service (for a fee), the presale and after-sale services (free) are additional services that [82]. Second, patients may deeply care about the service quality in the during-sale stage as the first doctor-patient communications occur in this stage, and the core services (diagnosis and treatment) are provided. Diagnosis and treatment quality directly relate to the likelihood of correct disease diagnosis and treatment, and affect the health or safety of patients. Therefore, patients would like to cognitively retrieve their knowledge and evaluation of the service from their memory to give a psychological compliment, which is brimming with gratitude for high-quality service in the during-sale stage.

More importantly, service price eliminates the positive relationship between during-sale service quality and RTNVG. There are two possible explanations. First, doctors charging a high service price give their patients the impression that they tend to seek economic benefits. Considering the doctors preference, patients are willing to pay for more economic compliments to a doctor who appreciates wealth, while more psychological compliments will be granted to a doctor who appreciates reputation [83]. Gifts can be converted to a cash equivalent and are more likely to be chosen by patients for doctors charging a high service price. Second, patients who choose doctors charging a high service price may have high income and have the ability to pay extra money to buy doctors gifts.

The negative outcomes for antecedents (e.g., hospital level, doctor title, and city level) in Tables 4 and 5 can be explained by the expectation-disconfirmation theory [84]<sup>6</sup>: Patients will have high expectations on these doctors with high titles, in high-level hospitals, and cities. Therefore, they are more likely to feel disconfirmation when they receive the service [2], which decreases their propensity to give compliments.

## 6.2. Implications and limitations

On the basis of the transaction cycle framework, this research studies the impacts of pre-, during- and after-sale service qualities in both current and later stages on patients’ compliments from a transaction cycle perspective using data collected from an OHC in China. Our study contributes to knowledge in several ways.

First, our work provides evidence on patients’ behavior from the whole transaction process

##

perspective and enriches knowledge on OHCs. To date, there have been many studies on OHCs (e.g., [2, 83, 85, 86]), especially those on the role of service quality on OHCs (e.g., [85]). However, they mainly focus on the during-sale process and fail to capture the whole transaction process (pre-, during-, and after-sale processes). The effects of service qualities of different stages on patients behavior are different. By distinguishing three periods (pre-, during-, and after-sale processes), we are able to evaluate which stage of service is the most important to consumer decision-making. In addition, most prior studies are based on general indicators that can be found on websites (e.g., [2, 83, 85, 86]), which cannot be matched to the act of a person (e.g., these compliments may not actually come from these patients). The one-to-one correspondence design in our study helps reveal the true effects of service quality on patients’ behavior by measuring the service quality experienced by these 60 patients correspondingly.

Second, our study extends our knowledge of patient behaviors by exploring compliment behavior overlooked in existing studies. Prior studies mainly recognize the importance of complaint behavior (e.g., [9]), and neglect the more valuable profit-generating element – consumer compliments [13]. From the current state of the medical environment and health issues in China, conflicts. Doctors usually need to be identified and respected by patients. For doctors, compliments represent the recognition of their work [3, 14], which influence their enthusiasm. Moreover, as a kind of reputation, compliments can help doctors attract more patients. However, compliments in traditional hospitals are costly and cannot be easily seen by other patients. Moreover, material compliments such as red envelopes are forbidden by many hospitals. OHCs provide the compliment mechanism, which makes the gift-giving behavior justified by controlling its amount. These compliments are public on Good Doctor and easily visible and are effective motivation for doctors to improve their service quality. Therefore, understanding the driving forces of patients’ compliment behavior is vital in encouraging doctors to actively and consistently engage in high-quality service on

OHCs, and may further positively influence their attitude in physical hospitals.

Third, our study also enriches our knowledge of exchange heterogeneity in the whole transaction process. First, although interaction behaviors in the online healthcare environment have been widely studied (e.g., [19, 83, 87, 88]), they often only focus on one stage in a transaction (mainly the during-sale stage). By distinguishing different stages (pre-, during-, and after-sale stages) and different compliments (psychological and material compliments), we find that the effects of service quality in different stages on compliment behavior are different, and we also reveal that compliment heterogeneity exists in the transaction cycle. Specifically, service qualities in different stages lead to patients’ different choices on different compliments. Second, as prior studies only include exchange behavior in one stage or integrate different stages as a whole (e.g., [19, 83, 87, 88]), they failed to consider the continuous effects. We examine the continuous effects of service quality equent stage and compliments. Third, different from existing studies (e.g., [19, 89]), we also consider the moderating effects of price in the exchange. By including the moderating effects of service price, the findings are robust and provide deeper insights into exchange heterogeneity in the whole transaction process.

This study helps doctors better understand how to gain compliments from patients by improving service quality and has several implications for practitioners, doctors, hospitals, and OHC providers. First, in this study, we reveal the role of service qualities in three stages in promoting patients compliment behavior. Doctors can obtain patient recognition by improving their service quality, thus enabling doctor-patient interaction to achieve harmonious development. In addition to during-sale service quality, pre- and after-sale service qualities are also very important and need to be improved. Second, our study indicates that service qualities in the three stages influence patients’ use of different compliments. Thus, doctors can focus on their service in different stages to improve their service quality based on their goal to either maximize revenue or psychological satisfaction. Doctors who already have a high reputation and prestige should pay more attention to the reputation return from patients to ensure that the existing reputation would not be damaged. For doctors with lower professional titles, their income is relatively low, and they can get material rewards by improving their online service quality.

For hospitals, compared with complaints, consumers show less willingness to give compliments even when they are satisfied with the service, and we appeal to hospitals to focus more on the improvement of fundamental functions to enhance compliments rather than passively deal with complaints. Patients often do not know the effective ways to express their feeling in physical hospitals. Thus, they tend to do nothing rather than to express compliments. Hospitals should create diverse and easy interactive channels for patients when they feel like showing their appreciation to doctors or hospitals. In addition, our research helps hospitals become better at managing multistage service quality and effective training programs by analyzing the essential differences in complimentary behaviors between material and psychological compliments.

For website developers and managers, our results indicate how an OHC supports doctors with different service qualities to obtain benefits and to encourage the sustainability of doctors on OHCs. First, an effective pricing mechanism can be developed to assist doctors in pricing their service appropriately based on their ability, reputation, and popularity among patients to promote better doctor-patient interaction. Second, website managers can improve the compliment reward mechanism. For example, the platform can introduce more compliment types to encourage doctors to use online medical communities more actively and improve the quality of medical services. OHCs can also limit the price of gifts. High price not only presents patients with a financial burden but also leads to price comparison behavior among patients/doctors. Third, website developers can develop a reminder mechanism to ensure doctors reply quickly and establish a follow-up reminder system for doctors. By providing after-sale service, doctors can obtain more patient compliments. For example, after the during-sale service has been completed for a certain period, the reminder system can send the doctor a message encouraging him or her to check in on the patient again. Fourth, more direct indicators about service quality can be calculated based on the doctors’ replies and shown on doctor homepage publicly, which can help other doctors to learn and improve their service quality on OHCs.

This research has several limitations and future directions. First, although we collected data at the patient/transaction level, we integrated the data at the doctor level because of the lack of patient data such as sociodemographic characteristics. Second, potential endogeneity issues due to unobservable doctor characteristics cannot be solved based on the dataset in this research. Third, our findings may not be generalizable to other OHCs because we only collected data from one OHC in China. Fourth, approximately 93% of the doctors come from level A hospitals, which is consistent with the Good Doctor’s market positioning—to provide high-quality professional service. Future studies can validate our results in other service markets using the panel data.

## 6.3. Conclusions

Although researchers have conducted many studies on the impact of service quality on consumer behavior, there is little evidence on the effects of service quality of different subprocesses on consumer compliments. To address this research gap, we measured pre-, during- and after-sale service qualities from a transaction cycle perspective and tested a theoretical model to explore the influence of service qualities in both current and later stages on patients’ psychological and material compliments on OHCs. Our empirical shows that there are significant effects of service quality in both current and later stages on patients’ compliments, and service qualities at different stages have different effects on patients’ compliments. Furthermore, this study also investigated the moderating effects of the service price and observed several interesting effects. The results support the proposed models. This research a) provides evidence on patients’ behavior from the whole transaction process perspective, and enriches our knowledge on OHCs; b) extends our knowledge on consumer behavior by exploring compliment behavior, which is overlooked in existing studies; and c) enriches the knowledge on exchange heterogeneity in the whole transaction process. We consider three transaction stages and two types of compliments and investigate impact heterogeneity. We also consider the continuous effects and the role of price in exchange. Our results provide suggestions to doctors on how to improve their service quality on OHCs.
