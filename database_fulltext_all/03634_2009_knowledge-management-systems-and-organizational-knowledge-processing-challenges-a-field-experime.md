Keywords: Knowledge exchange Decision support systems Knowledge management systems (KMS) Task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF)

## a b s t r a c t

This paper investigates the appropriateness of knowledge management system (KMS) designs for different organizational knowledge processing challenges. Building on the theory of task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF), we argue that different KMS designs are more effective for different knowledge tasks. An exploratory <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment was conducted in the context of Internet-based knowledge sharing services to provide empirical support for our hypotheses. The results of our experiment show that a KMS designed to support the goal GENERATE is more appropriate for divergent type knowledge problems because of its affordances for iterative brainstorming processes. Conversely, for convergent type knowledge processing challenges, a KMS with the goal CHOOSE that supports the ability to clarify and to analyze is more effective.

## 1. Introduction

Today's competitive business environment is characterized by uncertainty and dynamism [10,31,41]. Competing in such an environment requires <sup>fi</sup>rms to leverage intangible knowledge assets rather than tangible assets such as capital or labor [42]. Indeed, knowledge is now recognized as a key organizational resource [26]. A variety of perspectives suggest that the ability to marshal and deploy knowledge dispersed across the organization is an important source of competitive advantage [1,25,35,43,44]. As a result, organizations have recently begun to pursue knowledge management initiatives by making substantial investments in information technology (IT) in deploying knowledge management systems (KMS) [7,8,16,27].

Despite the considerable investment and organizational energy devoted in knowledge management initiatives, many of such initiatives end in less than desirable outcomes possibly due to a mismatch between the KMS and the kinds of knowledge work the KMS was deployed to solve [11,33]. This mismatch may be due to the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of designing KMS because knowledge work processes are inherently abstract and unstructured, and knowledge worker roles are unstructured, complicated, dynamic and unpredictable [30]. As a consequence, knowledge workers must deal with a broad range of knowledge tasks that require very different approaches for successful completion [29]. For instance, the task of obtaining market and customer information from another branch of<sup>fi</sup>ce can be quite different from effectively developing a new marketing campaigns based on prior successful experiences. The former essentially involves the exchange of factual information whereas the latter requires developing and setting priorities that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the current context. However, although knowledge workers are provided with access to various KMS to support such knowledge tasks, the problem lies not in the lack of available tools to support the knowledge workers. Instead, it is the lack of guidance for choosing among the various tools that will best <sup>fi</sup>t their task at hand. Therefore, it is important to understand which type of KMS will best <sup>fi</sup>t the different knowledge tasks, especially as knowledge workers are frequently overwhelmed with different KMS [17].

While the academic research and business practitioner communities have found it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to understand how best to deploy effective KMS, we observe several prominent Internet-based services in practice which are characterized by knowledge sharing processes. Online services such as Google Answers (http://answers.google.com/) and Yahoo! Answers (http://answers.yahoo.com/) are gaining popularity and broad acceptance from users around the world who use these websites for sharing knowledge. It is interesting to note that despite the common goal of supporting knowledge sharing, these two services are designed in quite different ways. These two distinct designs and the apparent effectiveness of these online services lead us to ask the following research questions: How does the design of knowledge sharing systems impact the process of knowledge sharing? Is there a particular design that is more effective than another? Or does the effectiveness of a particular KMS design depend on the characteristics of knowledge tasks?

To address these questions, we draw upon the literature on Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) and the theory of task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF) to develop hypotheses about the appropriateness of different KMS designs. These hypotheses are tested via an exploratory <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment in the context of Internet-based online knowledge sharing services. This paper contributes to the KMS literature in the following way. While the theory of TTF posits that the performance will be better with KMS if the features supported by the technology ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the needs of the task, it fails to provide much guidance on selecting among various KMS alternatives given that different KMS could have similar supporting features. Our extension to the theory of TTF and supporting empirical results suggest that in order to better understand the implications of KMS design, it is necessary to consider the match between the behavior induced by the design of the KMS and knowledge processes required to solve the knowledge challenges.

This paper is organized as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst review the literature on Decision Support System (DSS) design, organizational knowledge problems and the theory of task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t as the theoretical foundation to develop a contingency theory of the effectiveness of KMS design for different knowledge tasks (Section 2). Details of the research context and the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment methodology are presented in Section 3. The results of the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment are reported in Section 4. Finally, we conclude with discussions of contributions, limitations and venues for future research (Section 5).

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses

The theory of task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF) is a general theory of information systems that posits that information technology is more likely to have a positive impact on performance if the characteristics of the information system match the tasks that organizations must perform [14]. While the propositions of TTF are quite intuitive, we note that TTF cannot be readily applied in our context of KMS. First, given that the objectives of different KMS are common (i.e., to share knowledge), the functionalities and features of the system are also essentially the same. Consequently, TTF would not be able to distinguish between the different types of KMS. A critical review of the literature reveals some of these shortcomings of TTF, which has mainly focused its discussion on the level of <sup>fi</sup>t between tasks and technology features at a general level. For example, Goodhue and Thompson [14] use high-level characteristics such as non-routineness, interdependence, and job title to describe tasks, while the particular systems used and the department in which the system is deployed is used to describe technology characteristics. Similarly other studies use TTF to postulate whether performance will be better with or without an information system when the system itself supports the task at hand. For example, Zigurs and Buckland [51] categorized tasks based on task complexity and argued that organizational performance will be enhanced with a Group Support Systems (GSS) having technology features supporting communication, structuring and information processing.

However, the prior literature does not provide much guidance with respect to choosing an appropriate information system from a set of systems that provide a similar set of technological features. With a plethora of technology alternatives offered in practice, the main practical concern has shifted from designing an effective system to selecting an appropriate system. This is because various systems could provide similar features, which are implemented in different ways. For example, two systems could support the communication among group members, where one system supports the group members to brainstorm anonymously at the same time while another allows the members to communicate via email exchanges. Obviously, group decision making would be enhanced with either of these systems than without any system at all [e.g., 51]. However, which one of the two will be more effective is not such an obvious problem. In this paper, we extend TTF to delve deeper into thinking about the notion of fit in task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we propose the mechanism of <sup>fi</sup>t in TTF, which aids us in developing <sup>fi</sup>ner-grained insights about the appropriateness of technology at the design level (for a given family of information systems).

In a nutshell, given that different tasks are best solved with different knowledge processes and that these are in turn induced by different KMS designs, we argue that different KMS designs will be more appropriate for different knowledge tasks. In order to develop a theory about KMS design, we <sup>fi</sup>rst look to the DSS/GDSS literature, as KMS can be regarded as a particular type of DSS [19,20,21,32], to identify the salient design features of DSS and GDSS that can be applied to our understanding of KMS design. In particular, we <sup>fi</sup>rst de<sup>fi</sup>ne the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the KMS design features (i.e., the technology) based on the literature on DSS/GDSS design and the knowledge challenges (i.e., the tasks) from the literature on organizational problems. Then, we develop our hypotheses based on our extended theory of TTF.

## 2.1. DSS/GDSS design

Decision support systems (DSS) are information systems designed to support decision-making. However, due to the complexity of the decision task and the bounded rationality of the decision makers, important decisions are often made in groups [5,48,49]. Making decisions in groups also has additional bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. For example, including the stakeholders (i.e., those who will ultimately implement the decision or be impacted by the decision) in the group decision process has been shown to help ensure commitment and acceptance of the <sup>fi</sup>nal decision [28,31,41]. Group decision support systems (GDSS) are an extended concept of DSS that speci<sup>fi</sup>cally cater to such group decision processes. The main research issues in GDSS research focus on identifying the obstacles to effective group decision making that exist in the group decision processes, so that GDSS can increase the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and quality of group decisions [9,21,44,46].

In order to better understand how best to design GDSS to support group decision processes, prior research has conceptualized the general design space for GDSS [38,51]. DeSanctis and Gallupe [9] proposed a multidimensional taxonomy for GDSS with three major dimensions that have been shown to impact group decision processes. First, the size of the decision making group (i.e., small vs. large group) has been shown to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the dynamics of the group decision process [e.g., 13]. Second, whether group members are collocated (i.e., face-to-face) or dispersed also changes how group members collaborate [18,45]. Finally and most relevant to this study, the nature of the group decision task was shown to determine the type of group decision process that was most effective [e.g., 51]. Planning tasks require the generation of action-oriented plans whereas creativity tasks require generation of novel ideas. Intellective tasks require the selection of the correct alternative whereas preference tasks require the selection of an alternative for which there is no objective criterion for correctness. Finally, cognitive conflict tasks involve resolving con<sup>fl</sup>icting viewpoints whereas mixed motive tasks require the resolution of con<sup>fl</sup>icting motives or interests. The six tasks above can be categorized based on what is to be accomplished and the processes through which these task goals are accomplished. The major task goals are GENERATE (for planning and creativity tasks), CHOOSE (for intellective and preference tasks) and NEGOTIATE (for cognitive con<sup>fl</sup>ict and mixed motive tasks) [9,34]. To support the above three major task goals, DeSanctis and Gallupe [9] proposed several possible GDSS features. For example, to support the task GENERATE, possible support features are anonymous input of ideas, pooling and display of ideas, and electronic brainstorming [9]. To support the task CHOOSE, features such as synthesis and display of rationales for choices, aid to <sup>fi</sup>nding the correct answer can be helpful [9].<sup>1</sup>

## 2.2. Organizational knowledge problems

Different knowledge processing problems or challenges require different knowledge processing capabilities to solve them [49,50]. Zack [49] points out that there are four generic categories of knowledge processing challenges: complexity, uncertainty, ambiguity and equivocality. Complexity re<sup>fl</sup>ects “… the numerosity and diversity of situational elements and the intricacy of their relationship…” To deal with problems of complexity, organizations can either increase its information processing capacity or reduce the level of complexity by ignoring some aspects of the situation. Uncertainty problems are characterized by “… a lack of information to chose from an exhaustive and well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned set of possible states, even if that set is not complex …” An organization can reduce uncertainty by acquiring additional information or knowledge and/or by improving its ability to predict (i.e., use estimates or projections without acquiring additional information). Ambiguity represents “… an inability to interpret or to make sense of something…” Organizations may reduce ambiguity by acquiring contextual or explanatory knowledge via learning or experience. Finally, equivocality problems involve “… multiple meanings for or interpretations of the same thing [even if] each interpretation is individually unambiguous …” [49]. Organizations can reduce equivocality via cycles of interpretation, discussion and negotiation.

Although the four types of organizational knowledge processing challenges are conceptually distinct, it seems that there exist commonalities especially when we think of the prescribed solution processes. Dealing with uncertainty and complexity requires facts or additional analyses to clarify and to simplify the problematic situation. A well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned “correct” answer is usually derivable via careful analyses and clari<sup>fi</sup>cation. Following the discussion in [4,40], we will refer to these two types of problems as convergent type problems in the following analysis because “convergent problems deal with distinct, precise, quanti<sup>fi</sup>able, logical ideas that are amenable to empirical investigation. Convergent problems are solvable problems, and as they are studied more rigorously and more precisely, answers tend to converge into a single accepted solution” [4]. Conversely, In order to tackle knowledge processing challenges of ambiguity and equivocality, repetitive discussions and sharing of ideas are needed followed by interpretations and generation of novel ideas [49]. The brainstorming and iterative idea contribution processes will help clarify these ideas thereby reducing ambiguity and equivocality. Multi-faceted opinions and experiences from a diverse group of members will usually create a better solution [e.g., 6,22,37]. Obviously, there is no one clear answer for these types of knowledge challenges, rather a good solution is developed through sense making [47]. Following the discussion in [4,40], we will refer to these two types of knowledge challenges as divergent problems because “divergent problems … are problems that are not easily quanti<sup>fi</sup>able or veri<sup>fi</sup>able and that seem not to have a single solution. The more rigorously and precisely they are studied, the more the solutions tend to diverge, or to become contradictory and opposite” [4].

## 2.3. Appropriateness of KMS design

So far, we have de<sup>fi</sup>ned two distinct design approaches for KMS (i.e., CHOOSE vs. GENERATE) and two broad types of knowledge tasks (i.e., convergent vs. divergent tasks) that knowledge workers confront. In this section, we draw on the theory of task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF) to argue that for different knowledge processing challenges (i.e., the task), different design features (i.e., the technology) will be more effective in inducing the appropriate knowledge process to solve the problems and consequently lead to higher quality of decisions [3,14,23,51,52].

Given that different knowledge processing challenges are best solved via different knowledge processes and that these are in turn induced by different KMS designs, we posit that different KMS designs will be more appropriate for different knowledge tasks. In particular, for a KMS design to be more appropriate, the usage behaviors induced by the design features must match the normative processes for accomplishing the task. Accordingly, based on the discussion in Sections 2.1 and 2.2, KMS design features supporting the goal of GE-NERATE should induce the generation of ideas and viewpoints or the provision interpretive schemes for decision making. Consequently, this idea generating process should better assist knowledge workers deal with divergent type knowledge problems (i.e., ambiguity and equivocality). On the other hand, KMS designed with the goal of CHOOSE should assist in the process of selecting alternatives. Accordingly, such systems should be more appropriate for dealing with convergent type knowledge problems (i.e., uncertainty and complexity). Thus, we state the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1. Knowledge management systems (KMS) supporting the goal of CHOOSE will be more effective for solving convergent type knowledge challenges than for solving divergent type knowledge challenges.

Hypothesis 2. Knowledge management systems (KMS) supporting the goal of GENERATE will be more effective for solving divergent type knowledge problems than for solving convergent type knowledge problems.

We report the results of a <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment that were designed to gain further insights into whether different KMS designs can be more effective for solving different types of knowledge problems. Two online KMS currently used in practice—Google Answers and Yahoo! Answers— are representative instances of the two different types of KMS (i.e., CHOOSE vs. GENERATE) that we have previously described. Google Answers is illustrative of the CHOOSE type KMS, whereas Yahoo! Answers shows characteristics of the GENERATE type KMS. Accordingly, we hypothesize that Google Answers will be more effective for solving convergent type knowledge challenges, whereas Yahoo! Answers will be more effective for solving divergent type knowledge challenges.

## 3. Research methodology

In this section, we present the details of our research context and the exploratory <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment that was conducted to examine whether the performance of KMS is contingent on the types of knowledge processing challenges as proposed in the research hypotheses.

## 3.1. Research context

We conduct a <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment in the context of two websites— Google Answers (http://answers.google.com/) and Yahoo! Answers (http://answers.yahoo.com/), which can essentially be considered as KMS for knowledge sharing and transfer [1,36,39]. Although the overall purpose of the two websites is the same (i.e., sharing information and knowledge), how it is accomplished is different.

Google Answers is an online service for knowledge sharing provided by Google. On Google Answers, any registered user can post questions in pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned topic categories such as Arts and Entertainment and Business and Money. When a user posts a question, she speci<sup>fi</sup>es a monetary amount that she is willing to pay for the answer to her question. The questions have a duration beyond which it becomes expired. The default expiration duration is one month. Before the question expires, any of the approximately 500 researchers can respond to it and receive the monetary reward. These “researchers” are independent contractors that Google Answers has screened and approved through a stringent process in order to ensure high quality responses. However, these researchers are not necessarily domain experts but rather are experts at searching and locating hardto-<sup>fi</sup>nd information on the Internet and other of<sup>fl</sup>ine resources. Before a question is of<sup>fi</sup>cially answered, researchers as well as other users (who need not necessarily be researchers) can post comments and help clarify the question. Nevertheless, only one researcher may provide a <sup>fi</sup>nal answer and secure the monetary reward [12,15].

Operated by Yahoo!, Yahoo! Answers, is a similar online service for knowledge sharing but with a sharing process that is quite different from Google Answers. Similar to Google Answers, Yahoo! Answers allows any registered user to post questions in pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned topic categories. However, Yahoo! Answers does not institutionalize any monetary rewards for answering, nor does it restrict answering privileges to a group of pre-approved researchers. Rather, Yahoo! Answers operates more in the spirit of a knowledge sharing community where users ask and answer questions from one another. A typical process unfolds as follows. After a user posts a question in one of the prede<sup>fi</sup>ned topic categories, other users can post answers or comments to that question. All responses are (potentially) acceptable as answers. However, the question asker can select one of the responses as the “best answer” or ask other users to vote for the “best answer”.

An interesting consequence of Yahoo! Answers' setup is that since multiple users can provide answers or opinions to a particular question, users can build on prior answers and opinions of others. For instance, it is not unusual to see such statements as “… I think the previous response by [username] is right, but I would like to add two more things …” Consequently, Yahoo! Answers' setup affords a conversation-like process that can lead to a diversity of opinions and perspectives. If a user seeks opinions, she can receive a plethora of responses from a wide variety of perspectives. Notwithstanding, a potentially negative aspect of this is that she may have to <sup>fi</sup>lter through a large number of irrelevant and/or redundant information in order to <sup>fi</sup>nd information that is truly valuable. Often, valuable information is scattered among many responses provided by different users so it is up to the question asker to combine all the information to construct her personal answer. Furthermore, even if the question asker is able to coherently integrate all of the contents from all the responses, there is no guarantee that the combined answer will be complete.

## 3.2. A field experiment

A <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment was conducted at these two websites: Google Answers and Yahoo! Answers. Ideally, effective comparison across these two KMS would require that they are subject to the same set of knowledge tasks so that the characteristics of the responses, such as the response time and the quality of response, can be directly measured and compared. There are two possible ways to achieve this goal, each with potential drawbacks. One way would be to generate a set of questions that can then be posted to both KMS after which responses can be collected and analyzed. This approach, however, is problematic since it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to determine an appropriate price for the questions on Google Answers, which could potentially affect the response time and the quality of the response or even result in no response. An alternative approach would be to identify matching questions from actual question/answer histories of the two websites. However, it is unrealistic to expect that a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient set of matched questions can be found on both KMS as they operate in a real-world environment. Furthermore, given that our research focus also scrutinizes the impact of knowledge processing challenge, measuring response quality can also be problematic since objective answers (i.e., factual or yes/no type response) are by de<sup>fi</sup>nition not possible for equivocality and ambiguity knowledge challenges [47]. In order to resolve these issues, we took a hybrid approach wherein actual questions that have been posted and answered on Google Answers were sampled to create a realistic set of questions that comprise of various knowledge processing challenges (i.e., complexity, uncertainty, ambiguity and equivocality). These questions were then posted on Yahoo! Answers so that users from Yahoo! Answers could provide responses. We also posted the original response from Google Answers on Yahoo! Answers so that those responses generated from Yahoo! Answers could be compared with the response from Google Answers through the voting mechanism provided by Yahoo! Answers. Additional details of the experimental procedure are presented later in Section 3.2.2

## 3.2.1. Experimental materials

Adhering to the general experimental paradigm outlined above, we created a sample of questions for our experimental materials as follows. Four topic categories that have a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient amount of activity in terms of question asking and answering in both websites were <sup>fi</sup>rst chosen. The topic categories selected were Love and Romance, Health and Beauty, Entertainment and Music and Computer and Internet, which, when combined, have over 2,000,000 resolved questions. From these topic categories within Google Answers, we randomly sampled 120 questions. Only the questions that conform to the following criteria were retained. First, the questions must have been answered on Google Answers, otherwise it would not be possible to compare the response with those from Yahoo! Answers. Second, the questions must be unambiguously categorized into one of the four knowledge challenge types (i.e., complexity, uncertainty, ambiguity and equivocality).

Two independent coders categorized the 120 questions into the four knowledge processing challenges using a coding scheme developed based on [49]. Appendix A presents a summary of the coding scheme used. Inter-rater reliability using Cohen's kappa was deemed acceptable (κ=0.87). In case of coding discrepancies, the coding results from the <sup>fi</sup>rst coder were consistently selected.<sup>2</sup> The frequency of sample of questions by knowledge challenge type and topic category is summarized in Table 1.

## 3.2.2. Experimental procedure and data collection

The experimental procedure is summarized in Fig. 1. We posted each of the questions from our sample on Yahoo! Answers while keeping the posting time consistent with that of the original posting on Google Answers. For example, the question “Antivirus software on multiple computers?”, which was posted in the “Computer and Internet” category on Google Answers on Dec. 16, 2005 (Friday) at 7:26pm (PST) was posted on Yahoo! Answers on a Friday at 7:26 pm (PST). The question had received an answer on Dec. 16, 2005 (Friday) at 8:06 pm (PST) on Google Answers, so this answer was also posted on Yahoo! Answers on the same Friday at 8:06 pm (PST). We used a separate user ID for posting the Google Answers' response so that it seemed like it was posted from another user from the Yahoo! Answers community.

As explained earlier, we used Yahoo! Answers' voting mechanism to compare the quality of the responses from the two websites. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when the question expired on Yahoo! Answers, we asked the Yahoo! Answers community to vote for the best answer among all of the responses that were posted (which included the original response from Google Answers as well as any additional responses from Yahoo! Answers. In other words, if one of the responses from Yahoo! Answers was voted as the best answer then we can infer that Yahoo! Answers produced a higher quality response than Google Answers. Conversely, if the response from Google Answers that we posted was voted as the best answer, then this would suggest that Google Answers performed better than Yahoo! Answers. This procedure ensured realism of context while at the same time allowing us to directly compare across the two KMS.

There were, however, two issues that needed to be resolved. The <sup>fi</sup>rst relates to situations where no additional responses are posted on Yahoo! Answers. Obviously, if a question does not generate any responses in addition to the answer from Google Answers that we posted, voting would be irrelevant as there is only one response to choose from. In such cases, we did not subject the question to a vote and simply noted that the response from Google Answers was voted as best. The second issue relates to the difference in expiration time between the two websites. Google Answers sets its default expiration time to one month while Yahoo! Answers sets it to one week. As a result, it is possible that answers on Google Answer were posted after one week. In which case, we would not be able to maintain the exact same response time on Yahoo! Answers as the question would have expired before we can post the response from Google Answers. Two questions in our sample were problematic. For these two cases, we arbitrarily set the response time for posting the response from Google Answers to three days (i.e., middle of Yahoo! Answers' one-week expiration time).

In summary, the following data was collected from the experiment. For each question, we counted the number of responses that were received. We also counted the number of votes for each question as well as for each response to each question. Third, using the voting procedure outlined above, we noted the relative performance comparisons between the two KMS for each question. Finally, we also stored the textual content of all of the responses for further content analyses. Taken together, the data collected allows us to gain a rich understanding of the process through which the questions were answered as well as the outcome of the knowledge sharing process.

## 4.2. Detailed process results

While we <sup>fi</sup>nd empirical support for our research hypotheses, it is important to further scrutinize the results to gain a deeper understanding of why the different KMS showed differential performance for different types of knowledge processing challenges. In other words, while our hypotheses were based on an extended concept of TTF, we need to verify that our extension to TTF—that fit between task and technology results from induced behaviors—is the causal mechanism driving the performance outcomes. In order to do so, we investigated the response generation process using content analysis. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we compared the patterns of how new information is accumulated via each of the responses.

The textual content of the responses was used to measure the amount of new information that each response provided above and beyond the content from prior responses. In order to measure the amount of new information, the textual content from each response was segmented into discernable information units. Each information unit was categorized into argument types (i.e., who, what, where, when, why or how based on the role of the information unit within the response). For example, if a response contained two distinct reasons, this was coded as two units of whys. If an information unit had already been supplied in a prior response, this was not double-counted so that only new information is captured. If a response includes information that is irrelevant to the question posed, these were not counted as new information units. The content analysis was also performed by two independent coders (see Appendix B for the coding scheme and examples). The inter-rater reliability was deemed acceptable for both divergent and convergent problems (Cohen's κ=0.72 and 0.74 for divergent and convergent problems, respectively). Again, if there were any discrepancies between coders, the categorization from the <sup>fi</sup>rst coder was used. This process allows us to investigate whether there are any substantial differences in terms of how answers arrive—whether each response is more informative (i.e., contains more information), whether the total amount of information accumulated across all responses is greater etc. across the two KMS and/or across different knowledge processing challenge types. The results of the content analysis are summarized in Table 3. To make the comparison meaningful, the analysis excludes the questions that did not receive any responses from Yahoo! Answers.

The results show that each additional response from Yahoo! Answers provides on average 1.05 pieces of relevant new information. We also see that the average amount of new information in each response is greater for convergent type problems compared to divergent type problems (t = −1.55, pb0.10). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, each additional response contributes on average 1.30 pieces of new information for divergent type problems and 0.89 for convergent type problems. However, we did not observe any statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in the amount of new information within divergent and convergent problem types (ambiguity vs. equivocality: t=−0.27, ns; uncertainty vs. complexity: t=0.02, ns).

These results are interesting when we consider the prior analysis concerning the number of responses generated. Earlier, we observed that divergent type problems generated a greater number of responses compared to convergent type problems (see Panel A of Table 2). We also checked whether the patterns of information accumulation across knowledge processing challenge types would be indicative of the characteristics of the responses that were voted as the best answers. Building on the above observation of how information is accumulated through responses, we compared the amount of new information in the response from Google Answers with those from Yahoo! Answers. The results suggest that the amount of new information in the response from Google Answers is typically greater than the responses from Yahoo! Answers, irrespective of knowledge challenge type. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, 32 of 50 cases (64%) for divergent type problems and 32 of 47 cases (68.1%) for convergent type problems, the responses from Google Answers contain a greater amount of information units than any other response from Yahoo! Answers. However, if we match the voting results with the results relating to amount of information content, we see that although 87.5% of the responses from Google Answers (28 of 32 responses) was voted as the best answer for convergent type problems, only 34.4% (11 of 32 responses) was voted as the best for divergent type problems. Similarly, we investigated whether those responses with a proportion of information content greater than 70% of all the information units received for that question. The results show that such informationladen responses were voted best 90.48% of the time for convergent type problems (i.e., 19 out of 21 occurrences), but only 55% of the time for divergent type problems (i.e., 11 out of 20 occurrences).

We further validate the above results by scrutinizing the cases which were inconsistent with our hypotheses, i.e., the cases where the response from Google Answers was voted as best answer for divergent type problems, or those cases where a response from Yahoo! Answers was voted as best answer for convergent type problems. In 64% (7 of 11 cases) of the cases where the response from Google Answers is voted as best, the process of information accumulation is similar to the gradual increasing pattern shown in Fig. 2a. Similarly, in 74% (14 of 19 cases) of cases where a response from Yahoo! Answers was voted as the best answer for a convergent type problem, these responses follow a pattern of information accumulation that is similar to those shown on Fig. 2b, where one of the responses clearly dominates the contribution of information.

To further verify these results, we ran a logistic regression to test whether the amount of information content in a response is associated with the probability that the response is voted as the best answer. The results show that for convergent type problems, the proportion of new information in a response is positively associated with the probability of being voted as best answer $( \beta = 5 . 6 9 $ pb0.001), whereas for divergent type problems, the amount of information content is actually negatively associated with being voted as best answer $( \beta = - 2 . 3 5 , p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$

Overall these results suggest that, what is important for solving different types of knowledge processing challenges (i.e., divergent vs. convergent problems) is the process through which these problems are resolved. A gradual process of collecting diverse perspectives is more effective for solving divergent problems whereas merely gaining access to an informative answer seems to be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for solving convergent problems. It just so happens that the two KMS were more effective for solving different types of knowledge problems because they were more likely to induce the kinds of information gathering processes that are more appropriate for the respective problem types. In other words, the Yahoo! Answers (i.e., the GENERATE KMS) was found to be more effective for divergent type problems because it induced the kinds of processes that assemble a diversity of perspectives. However, Google Answers (i.e., the CHOOSE KMS) was more effective for convergent type problems because it induced answerers to provide informative and de<sup>fi</sup>nitive responses.

## 4.3. Robustness checks

We conducted several additional analyses to ascertain the robustness of our results. One of the main concerns relates to our use of the voting results as a proxy for response quality. First, to rule out the alternative explanation that monetary incentives may have impact on the results, we examined whether questions with higher rewards on Google Answers were consistently voted as being better. However, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between questions' ask prices and voting outcomes. Second, to check the appropriateness of using the voting outcomes as a proxy for response quality, we conducted a follow-up experiment where we replicated the same experiment for a set of 20 questions from Google Answers where the question asker requested for a refund. Google Answers allows question askers to request a refund if she is not satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the response generated. In other words, these responses can be deemed as unsatisfactory and thus of low quality. The voting results from Yahoo! Answers show that these “low quality” answers (from Google Answers) were never voted as best regardless of question type and regardless of the amount of information content provided in these responses. Third, since Yahoo! Answers typically generates multiple responses, the random chance that one of these is voted as best answer over the one from Google Answers is greater. To rule out this explanation, we tested whether the number of responses had an impact on the likelihood of a question being voted as the best answer from Yahoo! Answers. At best, we <sup>fi</sup>nd a weak statistical relationship between response count and the likelihood of any answer from Yahoo! Answers being voted as the best. Finally, to verify whether the voting procedure would result in identifying the response of highest quality, we asked a domain expert (i.e., a relationship counselor) to select best answers from our dataset in the one of the topic categories (i.e., the Love and Romance category with 49 questions). The expert's opinion was largely consistent with the voting outcomes with a correlation of about 65% $( t = 1 . 6 8 , p { < } 0 . 1 0 )$

## 5. Conclusions and discussion

This paper investigates the effectiveness of KMS design for different types of organizational knowledge problems. Building on the theory of task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF), we hypothesize that a KMS with the purpose of GENERATE will be more effective for divergent type questions while a KMS with the goal of CHOOSE will be more appropriate for convergent type problems. Using data collected from an online experiment involving two online knowledge sharing services, Google Answers and Yahoo! Answers, we empirically examine and <sup>fi</sup>nd support for these hypotheses.

Ultimately, the decisions made through the use of KMS will have implications for organizational performance. In order to ensure that KMS support business objectives, the choice of KMS to deploy should be based on an understanding of the nature of organizational knowledge needs [1]. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, although the theory of TTF provides general guidelines on how to align the goal of KMS design with the knowledge tasks, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that designing KMS (or choosing among different KMS designs) is more complex than merely going through a checklist of required functionalities. Managers need to understand what kinds of information gathering processes the design of the KMS will induce, as it is the induced usage behaviors that are indicative of the performance outcomes.

Our study has several practical implications. Given that organizations nowadays are facing a complex and dynamic environment, it is inevitable that knowledge workers need to perform multiple tasks which may exhibit different characteristics in terms of the different nature of the knowledge processing challenges. However, it is not clear what kind of KMS these knowledge workers should use. Obviously, one system will not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient. Rather, depending on the characteristics of the tasks, knowledge workers will more effective if they use a system that induces the behavior that matches the procedures required for the tasks.

Our results also have direct implications for how to approach the design of common knowledge management applications [1,16,24]. The <sup>fi</sup>rst family of knowledge management application is for internal benchmarking [1,36]. Based on our results, a KMS with the goal of CHOOSE is best suited for transferring internal best practices since identifying best practices is more likely to be characterized by convergent type challenges. For example, this can be applied to multi-national or multi-division corporations that need to share their best practices across organizational units. Another common knowledge management application is to create electronic communities of practice that enable knowledge sharing between members [1,39]. The inability to share knowledge could affect the allocation ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of corporate resources, which in turn might undermine business performance. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that there may be a better way to solve the various problems depending on the type of problem the organizational members are faced with. In other words, managers should choose the appropriate design for the knowledge challenges at hand. Clearly, a “one size <sup>fi</sup>ts all” approach to KMS will not work. For convergent problems, a KMS will be more effective if the design is able to effectively connect seekers and speci<sup>fi</sup>c answer providers or experts. In contrast, for divergent problems, a KMS design that can bring people with similar interests together will be more effective.

Any research study inevitably has limitations, and our study is not an exception. First of all, due to the inherent nature of the questions in each topic category, it was not possible to derive a balanced sample of questions for each of the four different knowledge processing challenges (i.e., complexity, uncertainty, ambiguity and equivocality) across each of the topic categories. For example, our sample does not include any ambiguity and equivocality type questions for the category “Computer and Internet”. Most questions in that category were in fact uncertainty or complexity problems. Second, although the reliability of the content analyses and coding was acceptable, it was marginally so, especially with respect to divergent type problems. This was due to dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in content coding given the extensive lengths of the responses and the complex nature of the knowledge problems. However, since we are not comparing the semantic meaning of the information in detail, the reliability issue should not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact our results. Finally, our analyses implicitly assume the populations of the two online knowledge sharing websites are homogeneous. Under this assumption, the voting results can be used as a proxy of response quality. However, if this assumption is relaxed, we would need to control for user preferences and behaviors which would be a dif<sup>fi</sup>cult endeavor, especially given our experimental setting that attempts to maintain control while maximizing realism of context.

As a result, there is much room for future research. First of all, through our data collection process, we noticed that the mechanism used by Yahoo! Answers could be further explored to understand how the non-monetary awards affect usage behavior [e.g., 2]. Second, as mentioned previously, our results raise possible design concerns when employing centralized knowledge repositories. An effective design will depend on the nature of knowledge a <sup>fi</sup>rm seeks to share across units. Third, this study attempted to measure quality of responses objectively via community voting process. However, since the value of knowledge really depends on how the initial question asker perceives the response, it would also be possible to obtain feedback on the quality directly from the original question askers via surveys. By doing so, it would be possible to obtain a subjective measure of perceived quality. Lastly, this study used data from Google Answers and Yahoo! Answers. Replicating the study with real GDSS usage data from organizations would help us generalize the <sup>fi</sup>ndings to an organizational context, which is the target context of our research.

In conclusion, the results from the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment suggest that task performance is determined not only by a simple <sup>fi</sup>t between task and technology, in terms of whether a technology provides the various functionalities required for a task, but also by how the design of the technology induces the appropriate behaviors that match the processes required to perform the task. Overall, our study provides a useful extension to the theory of task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t by offering a theoretical explanation for when and why the <sup>fi</sup>t between task and technology leads to enhanced performance.
