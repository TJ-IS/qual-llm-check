# An empirical analysis of open source software developers’ motivations and continuance intentions

Chorng-Guang Wu <sup>a</sup>, James H. Gerlach <sup>b,\*</sup>, Clifford E. Young

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Systems, The Business School, University of Colorado at Denver, Denver, CO 80217-3364, USA <sup>b</sup> The Business School, University of Colorado at Denver, Denver, CO 80217-3364, USA

Received 1 March 2006; received in revised form 28 November 2006; accepted 23 December 2006 Available online 30 January 2007

## Abstract

We investigated open source software (OSS) developers’ intentions to continue their involvement in future projects. The research goal was to analyze the motivations of OSS developers systematically and identify those factors that influenced their continuation. Awork motivation model for OSS developers was proposed and a research model was empirically validated using data from a field survey of 148 OSS participants. The results showed that OSS developers’ feelings of satisfaction and their intentions to continue with OSS development was influenced by both helping behavior and economic incentives and also that adequate motivators existed, though OSS developers did not benefit equally and there was substantial room to improve their experiences as OSS developers.

Keywords: Open source software; Expectancy-value theory; Expectancy; Valence; Satisfaction; Continuance

## 1. Introduction

The open source community has produced a number of highly successful software products such as Linux, Apache, Perl, and Sendmail. Gartner [7] has predicted that Open Source Software (OSS) products will be well established by 2010 in at least 75% of mainstream enterprises, operating alongside traditional COTS solutions. The favorable acceptance of OSS products by business and the direct involvement of major IT vendors in OSS development have apparently transformed OSS from a public good to a mainstream, commercially viable form [11]. In contrast to traditional software, adopters of OSS seek lower cost solutions that they can modify or use as software components to build new or extended applications [1,20].

OSS is developed by loosely organized communities of participants located around the world and working over the Internet. Remarkably, most participants contribute without being employed, paid, or recruited by the organization. Although a recent trend is for companies that benefit from OSS to have some employees work on OSS, surveys have shown that the majority of participants are volunteers [22]. Central to this is the question: Why do some OSS developers voluntarily contribute their time and skills freely? The answer to this was the topic of our research.

The open source community is often described as a gift instead of an exchange culture. Essentially, gift culture refers to behavior which contains acts of altruism and reciprocity: in lieu of tangible rewards, givers receive psychological benefits such as the satisfaction of helping or living up to a moral commitment [35]. Others assert that intrinsic rewards such as boosting one’s ego, enjoyment, and community identification provide intrinsic motivation to those engaging in OSS development.

However, economists argue that this is unlikely to explain the enormous contributions of some participants, since there are no obvious similar patterns of behavior in most other areas of economic activity. Many beneficiaries are well-to-do and could afford to pay OSS developers. Furthermore, altruism or reciprocity has not played a major role in other industries, so it is necessary to explain why individuals in the software industry are apparently more altruistic or reciprocal [36].

Exploratory empirical research on the motivations of OSS participants has been made: Hars and Ou [16] performed a simple correlation analysis, finding a weak correlation between effort and altruism and strong correlations between effort and both building human capital and signaling employers. Likewise, Hann et al. [15] found that high-ranking OSS contributors were paid high salaries by their employers, suggesting that rank conveyed sought-after skills. The Floss Survey [6] identified many other reasons why developers were involved in OSS development, including becoming part of the open source community, promoting the open source mode of development, supporting the idea of free as an alternative to proprietary software, gaining a reputation, and having fun. The report recognized that additional research was needed to understand how these motivators influenced OSS developers.

Such arguments have not been tested rigorously using work motivation theory. Furthermore, there has been little research focused on understanding OSS developers intentions to engage in future open source projects once they have become involved. The sustainability of an open source community relies on developers’ continuous voluntary contributions. By utilizing a work motivation model based on expectancy-value theory, we tested a framework that identified factors that generally influenced OSS participants’ intentions to continue their involvement in future open source projects.

## 2. Motivators for participation

The set of possible motivators of OSS developers is extensive, containing both extrinsic and intrinsic aspects. Extrinsic motivators are environmental factors brought by the organization to an individual, while intrinsic motivators or internal motivators are related to the needs satisfying the individual [27]. The set of all possible motivators is too extensive to include in one study. Therefore, we focused on those that appeared to have the strongest effect: the intrinsic motivator helping and the extrinsic motivators enhancing human capital, career advancement, and personal needs.

## 2.1. Helping behavior

Many open source researchers have argued that altruism is the primary intrinsic incentive for participants. ‘‘Altruism. . . is a natural part of human nature [and]. . . is exhibited in some manner by everyone’’ [32]. Based on this viewpoint, developers make OSS contributions because they would like to lend a hand to others and simultaneously give something back to those who have assisted them [30]. In such a gift culture setting, given the abundance of resources, social status is determined not by what one has but by what one gives away, such is the case in the OSS community [33].

## 2.2. Enhancing human capital

A number of economists and researchers regard enhancing human capital as one of several extrinsic incentives that motivate participants to engage in OSS projects. From the standpoint of labor economics, human capital as a determinant of productivity refers to personal skills, capabilities, and knowledge. Human capital involves the accumulation of investments in people through their education and on-the-job training [28].

According to Ye and Kishida [42], OSS development provides examples of excellent programming to less skilled developers. However, the learning environment involves much more than the study of excellent craftsmanship. OSS communities possess the essential qualities that Wenger and Snyder [41] identified as ‘‘communities of practice,’’ which are formed by people, mostly practitioners, who are ‘‘informally bound together by shared expertise and passion for a joint enterprise.’’ As a result of sustained learning over time, the members develop a shared repertoire of experience, tools and ways of addressing recurring problems. Consequently, less experienced open source participants can develop skills that are useful when they enter/reenter the labor market.

## 2.3. Career advancement

Lerner and Tirole suggested that participation in OSS development may advance a person’s career in two ways: by demonstrating (or signaling) their capabilities and skills to potential employers and by using OSS involvement to acquire access to venture capital, acquire shares in commercial open source-based companies, or to launch a business endeavor.

## 2.4. Satisfying personal needs

Many OSS projects start because the people promoting them have not been able to find a program needed to perform a particular function. They arise to satisfy a work-related demand: to ‘‘fill an unfilled market’’ [5]. In many cases, OSS developers have the ability to modify OSS to satisfy personal or business requirements or help by knowing how to incorporate OSS into other products. The software and the knowledge to utilize it provide extrinsic value to the developer.

## 3. Theoretical framework

The proposed research model for our study was based on expectancy-value theory (EVT). This is a behavioral version of the rational choice model in economic decision making; it states that behavior is determined by conscious choice [38]. According to Lynd-Stevenson [26], EVT is a cognitive–motivational theory that relates an individual’s level of motivation to the expectations and value/valence (positive or negative) held by the individual on reaching a goal. Research with EVT can be found in the fields of social and applied psychology; e.g., in evaluating the relative strength of intrinsic versus extrinsic valences in the motivational process [31] and for exploring the behavior of individuals alone or in a group setting [8]—key requirements for investigating the motivations of OSS developers.

## 3.1. Expectancy and valence

According to Vroom [40] and others [2,9], work motivation is conceptually defined as a momentary belief on the part of an individual that acting in a particular way would be followed by a given outcome. The level of motivation for a specific behavior can be predicted by the strength of the individual’s expectancies toward particular outcomes and the valences (i.e., subjective desirability/undesirability) the individual places on those outcomes. The mathematical product of expectancy and valence can be used to predict the need (force) for a person to perform a particular act. Vroom’s theory has been used for the prediction of occupational choice, valence of good performance, effort on the job, and job satisfaction [3].

While these researchers regarded expectancy as the estimation of action–outcome behavior, we proposed a variant definition of expectancy in our model. In the open source setting, we intended to examine developers’ confirmation of the relationship between action and outcome in order to understand the perceived value of the experience. Post-expectancy is thus important, because future expectations would be heavily influenced by past experiences, and, over time, approach steady-state equilibrium as they become more realistic and entrenched in observed behavior [10]. Therefore, in our model, we interpreted expectancy as an individual’s belief that a given action had led to some outcome.

Based upon Lewinian force theory [23], Vroom argued that behavior of a person was the result of a field of forces each of which had direction and magnitude. Following his idea, we suggested that OSS developers post-action expectancies and valences could be combined in a multiplicative way to predict their satisfaction and intention to continue participating in OSS projects.

## 3.2. Satisfaction and continuance

The concept that satisfaction is a function of an expectation and some perceived discrepancy from the initial reference point was based on research in the fields of social and applied psychology [19,37] and has been used to analyze a variety of human conditions, including job satisfaction. Locke [24] defined satisfaction of job performance as a pleasurable or positive emotional state resulting from the appraisal of one’s job. Based on Vroom and Locke and Latham [25], the degree of satisfaction will be a joint function of the degree of fulfillment of the value and its importance to the person. Therefore, OSS developers’ satisfaction level should depend upon their post-participation expectancies against their perception of valence for their performance (i.e., their emotional reaction to their own motivations).

According to Reilly [34], low job satisfaction could lead to disruptive behaviors and sabotage. In contrast, high job satisfaction can enhance organizational commitment and citizenship [21]. Locke and Latham argued that satisfied people will be more likely to remain with the organization and accept its new challenges. Thus, in the open source setting, we suggested that a satisfactory participation experience including pleasure and/or tangible benefits would strengthen developers’ continuance intentions.

Hypothesis 3d. Developers’ motivation to satisfy their needs will have a positive effect on their intention to contribute to future OSS development.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Instrument development and sampling

To test our research model the six constructs were measured using multiple-item scales that were based on existing measures and reworded appropriately for our study. Satisfaction items were measured using sevenpoint semantic differential scales. All the other remaining scale items used seven-point Likert scales that ranged from 1 (very unimportant/strongly disagree) to 7 (very important/strongly agree). The survey instrument and sources are shown in Appendix A.

The sample was randomly drawn from participants of current OSS projects working in three communities: SourceForge.net, Debian.org and OpenWebmail.org. The first is the world’s largest OSS development website, providing support for 1 million members and 100,000 projects. Debian, developed by more than a 1000 open source programmers, is a free operating system that uses Linux as its kernel, but most of the basic software utilities come from the GNU project. Open Webmail is a mail system that was implemented by hundreds of participants world-wide. Participation was requested via an electronic mailing which described the purpose of the study, a request for voluntary participation, a statement of risk to the participant, and a hyperlink for connecting to an online survey form.

Following a single round of data collection with a response rate of 12%, 148 usable responses were gathered. The OSS developers’ position in their project was determined and is given in Table 1. The sample contained 143 males and 5 females. Among them, 127 OSS developers were volunteers and 21 were paid employees. The respondents’ average age was 29 years, with an average of 4 years of OSS development experience, involvement in 5 OSS projects, and 7 years of IT experience. Seventy-eight percent were employed full time. Almost all reported roles that are central to the development of OSS; thus, we refer to them collectively as developers.

## 4.2. Data analysis

In assessing the research model, construct reliability, validity, and hypothesis testing were measured by using AMOS, a covariance-based structural equation modeling system, developed as a statistically equivalent tool to LISREL. Such techniques are well suited for the analysis of models containing variables with measurement error, facilitating a transition from exploratory to confirmatory analysis [13]. In our study, we used AMOS 5.0 Graphics Version.

## 4.3. Measurement model assessment

In the research model, each indicator variable in a motivational construct was formed by calculating the square root of the product of the ‘‘expectancy’’ item and the ‘‘valence’’ item. Just as in the calculation of a correlation coefficient, taking the square root returns the results to the same scale level as the items in the ‘‘satisfaction’’ and ‘‘continuance’’ constructs in order to reflect the relationships between the constructs. Appendix B shows the descriptive statistics of the indicator variables. All items revealed a reasonable distribution across the ranges.

Prior to analyzing the reliability and validity of the measurement model, we first examined whether the indicator variables composing a particular construct could operate equivalently across different populations (the sample consisted of both volunteers and employees). Two groups, one with all respondents and the other containing only volunteers, were used to test for multigroup invariance.

## 4.4. Structural model assessment

The structural model that specified the hypothesized associations was evaluated for the measurement model. According to estimated outcomes, there were four insignificant paths: enhancing human capital to satisfaction, satisfying personal needs to satisfaction, helping to continuance, and career advancement to continuance. Therefore, Hypotheses 2b, 2d, 3a and 3c were not supported. In the interest of parsimony, a final model was determined by removing the four insignificant associations. A nested $\chi ^ { 2 }$ of 1.27 with four degrees of freedom demonstrated no violation of data by eliminating the paths. Fig. 2 shows the standardized path coefficients and levels of significance for the final model, as well as the overall fit indexes.

Based on the final model, continuance intention $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ is well predicted by satisfaction $( \beta = 0 . 4 4 )$ That is, OSS participants’ continuance intention increased as their level of satisfaction rose, supporting Hypothesis 1. Motivation on helping $( \beta = 0 . 3 1 )$ revealed a strong and significant positive relationship with satisfaction $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , and it had an indirect positive effect on continuance intention $( \beta = 0 . 1 4 )$ through satisfaction as well. This result supported Hypothesis 2a and indicated that an OSS participant’s satisfaction was dependent upon motivation on helping. Similarly, motivation on career advancement $( \beta = 0 . 2 5 )$ demonstrated a direct, statistically significant positive relationship with satisfaction $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). It implies that OSS developers’ satisfaction level increased as their level of motivation on career advancement rose, supporting Hypothesis 2c. In addition, motivation on career advancement also had an indirect effect on continuance intention $( \beta = 0 . 1 1 )$ ) through satisfaction. Motivation on enhancing human capital $( \beta = 0 . 2 4 )$ and satisfying personal needs $( \beta = 0 . 1 7 )$ showed direct and statistically significant effects on continuance intention $( p < 0 . 0 5$ and $p < 0 . 1$ respectively), supporting Hypotheses 3b and 3d. The outcomes suggested that both motivation on enhancing human capital and motivation on satisfying personal needs directly influenced continuance intention.

## 4.5. Limitations

The results may be influenced by non-response biases. Most respondents were positively inclined to continue OSS development, and the survey only solicited current OSS contributors and did not take non-continuers into consideration. Thus, a response bias is likely since they probably had a dissimilar viewpoint from continuers. Overall, the study focused on continuance behavior and therefore we cannot draw any conclusions about the reasons for lack of continuing to participate.

## 5. Discussion

A major contribution of our study was the development of a work motivation model, based on EVT, for studying the motives of OSS developers. It improves understanding of motivation by measuring both the subjective importance of the motive and confirmation of the outcome expectancy.

According to our results, satisfaction with participating in OSS projects has the strongest influence on OSS participants’ intentions to participate in future projects, followed by their motivation on enhancing human capital and satisfying personal needs. These associations demonstrated that satisfactory experience and recognition of the benefits of enhancing human capital and satisfying personal needs directly influenced OSS participants’ continuance behavior. It also demonstrated that OSS developers directly benefited through the acquisition of OSS for which they have a personal or job related use.

Our findings supported the idea of a gift culture: that people will receive help and support given that previous contributions have created a tacit reciprocity agreement. Results showed that helping indirectly influenced continuance intention through satisfaction. Likewise, we found that career advancement opportunities that were derived from OSS development, enhanced satisfaction, and indirectly influenced continuance intention.

In conclusion, we determined that participants believe that OSS development is instrumental in their career advancement, in enhancing human capital, and in acquiring specialized software.

## 5.1. Implications

Our study confirmed that intrinsic and extrinsic motivators are significant to OSS volunteers. The existence of economic benefits provides strong indications that the open source community has created an exchange arrangement between OSS developers and organizations that benefit from OSS. The OSS community is likely to attract new talent and retain current developers as long as this economic partnership between OSS developers and OSS adopters holds.

Although the results of our study showed that adequate motivation existed, not all OSS developers benefited equally. The raw survey data showed that 14% rated their experience below satisfied; 28% rated their experience below pleased; 43.5% rated their experience below contented; and 51% rated their overall experience below delighted. Of those surveyed, 41.3% indicated that participating in open source projects did not make it easier to get a better job; 43% did not experience career advancement; and 37.5% had not found participating to be an important part of their job. However, only 10.1% rated advancing their skills in developing software neutral or less.

## 6. Conclusions

The goal of our study was to identify salient determinants of OSS developers’ intention to continue making OSS contributions. Toward this goal, EVT was adapted to form a theoretical model of OSS developers satisfaction and continuance behavior. The findings showed that OSS developers are motivated by both helping and economic incentives related to enhancing human capital, career advancement, and personal requirement in software development.

However, subjects’ responses indicated that not all OSS developers benefit equally: a significant number indicated lower than desired levels of overall satisfaction and career advancement opportunities. Open source communities rely upon an unusual mix of intrinsic and extrinsic motivators to attract and retain developers, thus an understanding of the recruitment and retention of volunteer developers is of importance to them.
