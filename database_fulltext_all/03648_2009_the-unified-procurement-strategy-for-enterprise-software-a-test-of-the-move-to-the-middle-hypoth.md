Abstr act: To increase their firms’ competitiveness, information technology (IT) managers are adopting a strategy that many deemed risky in the past. Recent IT advances combined with certain firm and industry characteristics are prompting firms to move toward a unified procurement strategy for enterprise software solutions. Unified procurement occurs when a firm elects to purchase all compatible products and services from a single vendor. Key benefits of unified procurement involve motivating managers to alter their procurement strategy. At the top of the list of benefits is transferred risk, which includes risks that can be transferred from one participating party to another during a transaction. In the case of unified procurement, they include technology risks and integration costs that are transferred from the procurement firm to its vendor. Firms have been shifting toward a unified procurement strategy for enterprise software solutions. We discuss the evolution of procurement practices in an industry that exemplifies a manifestation of Clemons et al.’s [10] “move to the middle” hypothesis predictions. The adoption of unified procurement is being driven by changes in IT, firm, and industry structure. We explore the “move to the middle,” transaction cost economics, and industry clockspeed theory to explain this phenomenon. We present a series of propositions that extend the prior theory to the more specific setting of enterprise software procurement—an example of middle range theory development— and use mini-cases to validate the various perspectives that we offer.

Key wor ds and phr ases: competitive strategy, enterprise software, industry structure, IT procurement, middle range theory development, “move to the middle” hypothesis, opportunism risk, software stacks, unified procurement.

Since 2000, the enter pr ise softwar e industry has experienced many changes as it has matured. A bellwether sign is the pace of its consolidation. According to Thomson Financial, software dominated all technology sectors for acquisitions, accounting for 50 percent of the \$306 billion in deals in 2005, and approximately 40 percent of the \$298 billion in technology mergers and acquisitions (M&A) deals that were done in 2006 [64]. One outcome is the emergence of software stacks, a strategy created by software vendors to deliver new value [20]. A software stack is a set of application programs that form a complete system. The recent emergence of single-vendor software stacks has created an opportunity for firms to pursue a unified procurement strategy for enterprise software solutions.

Unified procurement occurs when a firm elects to purchase all compatible products and services from a single vendor.<sup>1</sup> The unified procurement strategy differs from multivendor procurement strategy in that it offers a firm different capacity to transfer certain transactional risks away from itself to its vendor.<sup>2</sup> The opportunity to reduce risk has motivated some information technology (IT) managers to alter their procurement strategies [56, 57]. As a result, firms are choosing to increase outsourcing while dramatically decreasing the number of vendor partners. As firms shift toward the application of unified procurement strategy for enterprise software solutions, it prompts us to explore this industry setting, where there appears to be a relatively strong and interesting manifestation of Clemons et al.’s [10] “move to the middle” hypothesis.

Some IT managers deem vendor exclusivity a risky endeavor. Researchers have explored the risks associated with relationship-specific investments. Williamson [67] asserts that contracts allow firms to reduce opportunism risk and achieve lasting commitments between buyer and supplier, thus affecting economic organization and institutional choices. However, Klein et al. [29] argue that quasi-rents pose a threat to contracts; under conditions of high specificity, a firm is more likely to pursue vertical integration. It is not uncommon for an IT manager’s evaluation of the value of an IT investment to extend beyond support of day-to-day business operations to include its strategic value [6]. Firms alter their IT procurement strategies to increase competitiveness [13, 26]. IT, firm, and industry structure changes have all contributed to the shift in procurement strategy, with IT as the primary enabler. Clemons and Reddi [7] point out that customer decisions to use fewer suppliers gives the suppliers the incentive to improve product quality. As part of their strategy to establish themselves as strong partners for unified procurement, vendors have begun to preintegrate their products, absorbing systems integration costs. Preintegration eases a firm’s integration efforts by mitigating issues of compatibility [47]. Another advantage lies in the simplification of the principal–agent relationship via one-vendor accountability, which helps to reduce external coordination and transaction costs. Early works have emphasized the role in which these costs affect a firm’s outsourcing decisions [8, 69], organizational structure [23, 34], and size [4]. Such external coordination costs or market transaction costs capture operational and contractual costs [23]. Transaction costs, in contrast, include coordination costs, operations, and opportunism risks [10].

Bakos and Brynjolfsson [2] indicate that a reduction in the number of suppliers can lead to an increase in noncontractible advantages, which include collaborative innovations, relationship-focused responsiveness, and information sharing involving the suppliers. While they refer specifically to traditional procurement of physical products, noncontractible advantages can also exist for software stack procurement. A reduction in suppliers enables a firm to establish greater ex ante incentives and ex post bargaining power as a result. Exclusivity between the principal and the agent enables the two to establish a more effective partnership. In the enterprise software industry, this strengthened partnership provides a chance for the buyer firm to help drive innovation and contribute ideas to its vendor’s research and development (R&D). It can also lead to a stronger level of responsiveness and support from the vendor since the vendor’s success is heavily dependent upon the customer’s success. Growth in the number of customer software users implies predictable revenue growth for the vendor from future license expansions and the additional up-sell of new products. In an industry where maintenance fees can account for at least 50 percent of a vendor’s revenue [51], they are critical to a vendor’s long-term success. All of this helps to reduce uncertainty risks for both parties. Noncontractible relationship-specific investments from the supplier depend on two conditions: the goodwill of the buyer or its own ex post bargaining power to reap a share of the benefits from its investments [2].

A common counterargument to unified procurement is that the strategy places a firm in a position to experience high opportunism risk resulting from the execution of a relationship. However, Clemons et al. [10] argue that changing characteristics of IT, such as standards, reduce the relationship specificity of IT investments and consequently allow firms to use the market as if it were part of an extended hierarchy. Managers are concerned that the loss of competitive motivation for the vendors will lead to future challenges in price and performance, common issues associated with vendor lockin. Also, firms run the risk of limiting their competitiveness and the growth of their business to the technologies supplied by their vendor. Despite the counterarguments that suggest unified procurement may be risk bearing, we nevertheless have seen an increase in the adoption of unified procurement for firms in specific industries.

What theories enable us to explain this phenomenon? In addition to the foundational “move to the middle” hypothesis perspective, we will explore two additional theories to help shed light on the adoption of unified procurement strategy. Transaction cost economics theory [69] offers insights into transferred risk, a critical factor that affects the procurement decision making of firms. Under what conditions do firms seek to transfer risk? Why is it that the vendors are more able and willing to bear the technology risks and integration costs required to offer single-vendor software stacks? Industry clockspeed theory [40, 41] predicts the velocity of change in the business environment to help set management’s expectations related to the pace of innovation that a firm should aspire to in the refinement or transformation of its internal operations and products.

Unified procurement, like any strategy, is not “one size fits all,” but under certain conditions there seem to be persuasive reasons to expect to see higher adoption. Recent IT advances combined with certain firm and industry characteristics are prompting firms to alter their procurement strategies. As vendors continue to strengthen support for this procurement strategy and more firms see the benefits of single-vendor software stack adoption, the number of firms that adopt unified procurement will rise. This has not yet occurred on a large scale, but it offers powerful current-day evidence of Clemons et al.’s [10] “move to the middle” hypothesis, which was presciently written in the early days of the development of outsourcing of IT services to the market. We employ exploratory research methods in support of related theory development and to assess what prior theory offers for interpreting the current market environment.

Our primary goal is to analyze the theoretical rationale for a shift to firm-level execution of unified procurement strategy. We ask: What theoretical perspective can explain firms’ shift toward unified procurement? What are the IT advances and firm and industry characteristics that combine to promote the adoption of unified procurement? What outcomes do we expect to observe associated with firms’ movement toward the unified procurement strategy? What evidence can we supply for our proposed theory of the observed shift in procurement strategy?

## The “Move to the Middle” Hypothesis

For nearly two decades, firm-level procurement behavior for IT has been characterized by the “move to the middle” hypothesis. Clemons et al. [10] defined this as a move toward increased outsourcing but with fewer suppliers, and the establishment of close and long-term partnerships with these suppliers. Firms have been increasingly outsourcing, and the trend remains strong today. For the enterprise software industry, there is a changing trend though. Firms that work with enterprise software vendors are not only decreasing the number of vendors they use, some are electing to partner exclusively with one. As a result, we observe an industry that appears to illustrate an interesting manifestation of the “move to the middle” hypothesis. The “move to the middle” hypothesis focuses on the role that IT has been playing in lowering coordination costs without increasing associated transaction risks. Sources for the risks include relationship specificity, the number of potential suppliers for the product, and loss of resource control. What arguments offered by the “move to the middle” hypothesis support firms’ shift to a unified procurement strategy?

We will evaluate why the unified procurement strategy is chosen in the presence of coordination costs, opportunism risk, and operations risk. Clemons et al. [10] define transaction risk as the sum of coordination cost, opportunism risk, and operations risk. Coordination cost refers to the cost a firm incurs from coordinating with firms actually or potentially producing the product. Opportunism risk stems from bargaining power risks associated with the execution of a relationship. It also reflects the risks that stem from differences in goals among the participating parties and which are supported by information asymmetries. Operations risk, in contrast, involves more random elements, and they rarely arise due to intentional behavior on the part of a business partner in a procurement relationship. We next consider coordination costs and opportunism risk in greater detail.

## Coordination Costs

A firm’s coordination costs are affected by IT in many settings. The adoption of unified procurement leads to a decrease in coordination costs under most circumstances. Today there are just a few software vendors with the capital and resources to offer a comprehensive software stack. This helps to reduce vendor uncertainty and vendor screening costs, issues that Snir and Hitt [55] have addressed. The search time required to locate a unified procurement vendor is also less when compared to trying to sort out vendors for a multivendor strategy. The ongoing cost of maintaining partnerships with a single vendor is less, too. A project involving three vendors requires more effort to reach agreements prior to execution than a project with just one vendor, so multivendor procurement management drives up coordination cost.

## Opportunism Risk

By limiting the number of its vendors to just one, a firm will run a high risk of experiencing opportunistic behavior intentionally perpetrated by the focal vendor. Clemons et al.’s [10] study reveals that IT changes such as standards can minimize opportunism risk though. IT can also lead to tighter interfirm coordination, as well as cause shifts in bargaining power, sometimes giving the procurement firm more power [9]. Although ideally a firm would like to have an upper hand, Bakos and Brynjolfsson [2] argue that in some cases, increasing the bargaining power of a firm’s supplier can improve the buyer’s situation. Depending on the volume of assets purchased, a firm can proactively minimize such opportunism risk and incentivize its vendor to offer noncontractible benefits. The unified procurement strategy is designed such that a vendor expects a firm to purchase multiple products from the software stack. So, a firm’s negotiation power will be positively correlated with the size of its investment. The advantage that this provides to smaller firms is that it gives them a chance to establish a more equal footing with the vendor. A smaller firm that practices unified procurement and invests \$2 million on a software stack from the vendor, for example, is no less important than a large customer with an equivalent dollar value investment that has no intention of adopting a unified procurement strategy. The long-term commitment from the smaller firm makes it a stronger partner for the vendor, especially if the firm is growing.

Another form of opportunism risk involves withholding information from participating firms in a transaction. The differences in goals among participants often generate challenges [17]. With enterprise software vendors, they often do not reveal information related to technology road maps or product-related issues. However, this behavior can change if a customer is also a partner, because the long-term cost of withholding such information can lead to project failures and distrust. Adoption of unified procurement should thus lead to a decrease in opportunism risk, since common goals between the firm and its vendor play a role in solidifying the long-term partnership. The stakes for the firm and the vendor are high due to their long-term investment and commitment to one another right from the start. Indeed, both parties run the risk of increased costs, loss in revenue, and decreased control. Because the firms become dependent on one another for their success, we expect to observe fewer examples of shirking behavior. For the vendors, they are counting on future licenses and product expansions, and the firms are relying on the vendors for ongoing innovation and support.

## Transaction Costs Theory

In addition to the benefits related to lowering a firm’s transaction costs, transferred risk appears to play a key role in driving a firm to explore a unified procurement strategy for enterprise software solutions. To more fully understand transferred risk, Williamson’s [69] transaction cost economics theory is especially helpful. It indicates that the decisions of managers are bounded by rationality and firms are focused on profit maximizing through cost minimization. Williamson identifies three dimensions to transactions: “uncertainty, the frequency with which transactions recur, and the degree to which durable, transaction-specific investments are incurred” [67, p. 239]. All three are relevant when dealing with enterprise software solutions, and they help to explain why firms are motivated to offload risks. Uncertainties and risks are generally high for software projects. Since software solutions are commonly an integral part of a firm’s business, ongoing transactions with the firm’s vendors become necessary. Moreover, software solutions are highly specialized assets, thus firms are motivated to vertically integrate. All of a sudden, a strategy that enables a firm to transfer some of its risks to its vendor has become an attractive option. Williamson declares that there are three benefits to a bilateral exchange: “common ownership reduces the incentives to suboptimize; internal organization is able to invoke fiat to resolve differences; and internal organization has easier and more complete access to the relevant information when dispute settling is needed” [68, p. 559].

Transferred risks include risks that can be transferred over from one participant to another during a transaction. For unified procurement, transferred risks include those transferred from the procuring firm to its vendor. This can include technology risks, vendor management risks, integration costs and project failure costs, loss or gain of bargaining power, and so forth. Prior to the existence of single-vendor software stacks, customer firms poured historically large investments into constructing integrated architectures. They had to first evaluate many vendors, along with their products, to identify the appropriate solution. Then they had to determine if the selected products could be properly integrated. With unified procurement, the vendors are now taking on the responsibility of constructing software stacks for their customers. They absorb the costs and risks of finding the right products for the software stack, and may need to try a few different and costly avenues before they find a broadly workable solution. Large vendors are better able to bear these risks due to having the central limit theorem on their side, meaning not all projects are late or over budget. For smaller firms, a single project failure could be lethal. Further, some vendors that support unified procurement have also taken on the responsibility of preintegrating their products. The costs and risks linked to product integration and future product compatibility are transferred from firm to vendor. The single-vendor approach allows contractual agreements to be designed so that a vendor bears a heavier responsibility in the event of project failures. These advantages have prompted firms to partner exclusively with a single vendor.

## Industry Clockspeed Theory

The option to pursue unified procurement of enterprise software stacks did not emerge overnight. From early 2000 to present, two activities were occurring in parallel that can be interpreted with reference to industry clockspeed theory [40, 41]. They are the consolidation of the enterprise software industry and other targeted industries for IT service industries, including financial services, retail, communications, and health care. They experienced fast-paced changes in part due to technological innovations that have been pervasive in their business environments, but also due to cost and quality competition in their services. As a result, many of the firms in these industries have had to adapt to compete in the new environment, by redesigning their business models and strategies.

The emergence of open source software vendors has also intensified the competition for large enterprise software vendors. It has become increasingly challenging for the vendors to compete on the basis of just a few core products. Moreover, many of their products were built on proprietary standards. It became more difficult for IT managers to justify bearing the high cost of these solutions, as the emerging open source solutions were often less expensive and offered similar functionalities, if not identical support levels and complementarities. Due to the high level of competition, large enterprise software vendors had to find new ways to compete. Their first step was to create software stacks for targeted industries for IT services. By targeted industries for IT services, we mean industries in various sectors that exhibited strong IT demand and spending patterns over the years, and that had a clear need for sector-specific software stacks. Vendors needed to recoup their investment costs, so identifying and selling to the targeted industries was vital. As more enterprise software vendors began to offer unified procurement as an option, the vendors worked to differentiate themselves by offering preintegrated software stacks.

## The Shift to a Unified Procurement Strategy

What factor s ar e motivating fir ms to move away from a multivendor procurement strategy? Unified procurement introduces a critical element—transferred risk—which offers key benefits to a procurement firm. The amount and types of risks that are present during an IT procurement transaction is affected by changes in IT, firm, and industry structure. IT has the largest effect since IT evolves at a much faster pace than firm- or industry-level changes; however, firm and industry characteristics also have an impact.

Middle range theory [42] refers to theory that involves some abstraction, has a limited scope, and can easily lead to testable hypotheses in specific contexts [22]. It enables researchers to focus on a specific phenomenon through the combination of theory and empirical research, moving away from radical or narrow empiricism. Levine and Rossmoore [32] apply middle range theory development methods in their case study on the impact of power and politics on IT implementation. We leverage the same technique on unified procurement by using real-world observations in our theory development and the formulation of our hypotheses. A contrasting approach called the structured-case method, described by Carroll and Swatman [5], pertains to the development of theory in the interpretivist paradigm and shares a focus on obtaining explanations that are connected to the key themes of the research and contextual data from fieldwork.

## Open Versus Proprietary Standards

Software solutions have moved from proprietary standards to open standards. With publicly available standards and various associated uses, software solutions have become free for all to implement and adapt to their own needs. The vendors’ shift to standards-based software solutions has helped to mitigate IT managers’ fears of lock-in, since standards reduce switching costs and opportunism risk [10]. They allow firms the flexibility to introduce new software products or replace existing ones in their IT infrastructure. Brian Simmermon, CIO of Subaru and an adopter of Oracle’s Application Integration Architecture, offered his view on standards-based solutions: “By leveraging an open, standards-based architecture, we will have the flexibility to make discrete changes without disrupting the entire business process” [45].

## Vendor-Supported Versus Buyer-Supported Integration

Traditionally, buyer firms made complementary investments to integrate different software products that they either built in-house or procured externally. This increased the duration of their software projects, along with the concomitant costs and risks. Vendors are expected to preintegrate their products and maintain upgrades to ensure the ongoing value of their solutions. With unified procurement, firms are more likely to gain access to preintegrated solutions, because a vendor does not need to put forth as much effort to integrate its software assets as to partner with another vendor to achieve similar results. The industry press has reported that “[s]oftware vendors are increasingly offering packages intended to facilitate simpler integration, [with productized integration] as a ‘third’ piece of software to mediate between ongoing and legacy products” [59].

## Presence Versus Absence of Software Stacks

Prior to the emergence of single-vendor software stacks, firms had to build their IT infrastructure with products from various vendors, which required a lot of search time and cost. In the past five years, software solutions have increasingly been sold as software stacks, allowing buyers to take advantage of bundled pricing and other benefits. The presence of software stacks favors unified procurement. So, for example, from the “supply chain and operations support perspectives, buying from one vendor will likely result in easier contract negotiations, one maintenance contract for all products from that vendor and potentially some reduction in general management overhead” [24].

## The Firm Level of Analysis

Organizational characteristics represent one of the five contextual factors that impact the initiation and adoption of IT [30]. We have used the words more complex firm to describe a firm that is rigid and has a large IT operation and a heterogeneous IT infrastructure. A firm with these characteristics is less likely to transition to a unified procurement strategy. (See Table 2.) A less complex firm, in contrast, is more agile and has smaller IT operations and a uniform IT infrastructure. A firm with these characteristics is a stronger candidate for unified procurement adoption, since it often will have the need and nimbleness to make the transition.

## Agile Versus Rigid Strategic Capabilities

Agile firms exhibit more flexibility, which allows them to adapt quicker to change. They are constantly in a process of reinventing themselves and are more likely to embrace new strategies. Shifting from multivendor to unified procurement under such circumstances may require alterations to a firm’s existing IT, methodologies, and processes. Future benefits make it worthwhile, because IT can serve as a key enabler in increasing a firm’s agility [49].

## Small Versus Large IT Operations

Some firms prefer to work with leaner IT operations, since smaller-scale operations help to reduce IT costs. For firms that adopt unified procurement, one of their objectives could be to achieve optimal efficiency with the fewest resources possible by leveraging their vendor’s resources versus expanding their IT department. Cooperative relationships allow firms to increase resource utilization [8]. Under a multivendor setting, it becomes more difficult for a firm to scale down due to the large number of employees and diverse skills required to maintain disparate systems and vendors.

## Uniform Versus Heterogeneous IT Infrastructure

Many firms today still run on a heterogeneous IT infrastructure made up of multiple architectures and various products from different vendors. It is hard for these firms to alter their strategy due to high switching costs. Still, more and more firms are seeking to reduce their IT complexity, and a way to achieve this is to reform their IT infrastructure [36]. A firm that would like to rework its IT infrastructure is drawn to unified procurement since the strategy is founded on the uniform architecture from one vendor.

A challenge of unified procurement at the firm level is that it becomes easy for a firm to run the risk of relying too heavily on its vendor for resources and product expertise. Also, fuller software stacks are typically offered by the larger vendors, so there could be some residual complexities involved in vendor management.

## The Industry Level of Analysis

Software vendors support unified procurement of enterprise software solutions for mature industries. A mature industry often works with industry standards, is consolidated, and has sizable IT spending. A young industry shows the opposite characteristics: the absence of industry standards, unconsolidated operational capabilities, and limited IT spending. (See Table 3.) So how do these characteristics influence a firm to select a particular procurement strategy?

## Presence Versus Absence of Industry Standards

Mature industries have standards that help to stabilize industry IT use [43]. Software solutions are stable when their core features do not differ much by vendor [19]. Firms that pursue unified procurement need to know that pursuing one vendor will not leave them at a competitive disadvantage. For industries that have standardized solutions, their risks are lower since standards guide IT vendors in their definition and expansion of software stacks. For the home mortgage industry, Wigand et al. [65] discovered that industry standards lead to widespread adoption since the opportunity to reduce costs will motivate firms to use packaged software from vendors to conform to standards.

## Consolidated Versus Unconsolidated Industries

Mature industries evidence consolidation. A strategy employed during consolidation is reducing or retiring assets. This includes recognizing that unnecessary IT complexity adds costs, reduces effectiveness, and stalls innovation [14]. IT consolidation helps solve this problem. During consolidation, a firm can elect to move to unified procurement by reducing its IT assets to only those from one vendor. Also, consolidation creates an opportunity for firms to modify their procurement strategy appropriately.

## Sufficient Versus Limited IT Spending

Mature industries have higher IT spending since firms from these industries are generally experienced technology users. Furthermore, multiple software solutions are often required in order to support their business. Gartner’s Web site has reported that mature industries have been increasing their IT spending relative to other firms: “[their high level in IT spending] is perceived as a significant indicator of the alignment of an IS organization with the business units” [48]. Since mature industries continue to show strong IT spending, it becomes easier for firms in these industries to attract the attention of technology vendors and incentivize them to build stacks to support their industry. Moreover, industries with sufficient IT spending can exert stronger influence over the vendors, thus enabling a more equal partnership.

At the industry level, firms run the risk of vendors redirecting their industry focus due to changes in the market. The presence of transferred risk in unified procurement has led firms to alter their procurement strategies. The amount of risk transferred is directly affected by changes in IT, firm, and industry structure. (See Table 4 for a summary of our thinking.)

Proposition 1a (The Standards-Based Software Stack Strategy Proposition): Since managers have viewed single-vendor procurement as bearing higher opportunism risk, standards-based software stacks are more likely to result in a faster move to unified procurement than proprietary-based software stacks.

## Mini-Case 1a: Novell Meets Customer Demand with an Open, Standards-Based Software Stack

In 2006, Novell announced a new offering, the Integrated Stack for SUSE Linux Enterprise, which is built on open standards and a combination of mixed source software from IBM [44]. Industry reports show that there is an increasing desire from customers for standards-based solutions. For example, Carlos Montero-Luque, Novell’s chief technology officer (CTO) for identity-driven products, asserts that the industry is moving toward standards-based products—an idea that guides his own managerial actions at his firm. “[S]tandardization is critical and becomes a vendor problem. If the vendor cannot give you a good story for interoperability then the vendor is asking you to work around them” [21].

In order for vendors to motivate firms to pursue unified procurement, opportunism must be controlled, consistent with the predictions of the “move to the middle” hypothesis [10]. Montero-Luque’s assessment suggests the importance of preintegration as a key differentiator between the two procurement strategies. Others have suggested the same: “Increasingly it will not be a question of feature richness that differentiates one vendor from the next, it will be how strong their partner strategy is, the availability of published APIs and pre-built integration, and evidence of their ability to integrate into an organization’s broader IT environment, that will give them the edge” [63]. Vendors that wish to stay competitive must be able to satisfy their customers’ demand. Thus, we propose our next proposition:

Proposition 1b (The Software Stack Preintegration Proposition): Increased investment by vendors to build preintegrated software stacks will lead to increased adoption of the unified procurement strategy.

## Mini-Case 1b: Oracle Launches Preintegrated Software Suites

The demand for one-vendor accountability and ease of integration has created a new opportunity for large enterprise software vendors. Oracle’s chief executive officer (CEO), Larry Ellison, has commented that Oracle’s pursuit of acquisitions was in part “fueled by requests from partners and customers, such as General Electric, that wanted to hold a single company accountable for their applications and also ease the integration process” [27]. (See Appendix Table A 1 for the consolidations.) In April 2007, Oracle [45] announced the launch of Oracle Application Integration Architecture, offering prebuilt integrations across multiple Oracle applications, including enterprise resource planning (ERP), customer relationship management (CRM), and industry applications.

In the past, vendors focused on developing a number of core products and partnered with other suppliers to deliver a comprehensive solution. However, unified procurement has led vendors to expand their product portfolios to offer comprehensive software stacks. For supply-chain solutions, we observe that IT managers want breadth as well as depth in their software solutions [61]. Rather than a piecemeal approach as we have seen in the past with technology procurement, software solutions will increasingly be purchased in the form of bundles with unified procurement. This prompts us to state the third proposition:

Proposition 1c (The Software Solutions Bundle Proposition): More and more software solutions will be supplied to the market in the form of bundles by software vendors, increasing the likelihood of adoption of the unified procurement strategy.

## Mini-Case 1c: IBM Creates Software and Services Bundles for the Financial Industry

In 2004, IBM announced it would provide industry-specific bundles of software and services tailored for financial services companies [31]. IBM prices software bundles based on their construction. Doug Brown, IBM’s Director of Industry Marketing, has commented that the bundle approach “represents the capabilities of all IBM’s technologies in a way that the customer understands, because it maps right into a project that an IT executive has likely already been asked to deploy” [31]. The bundling strategy has allowed IBM to compete with other software vendors, including Oracle and SAP, that also offer vertical industry bundles.

Alterations in how software solutions are packaged and delivered to customers will impact the rate of adoption of unified procurement. Vendors will move away from building proprietary-based software stacks to offering standards-based software stacks. They will increase their investments in creating preintegrated software solutions, too. We also expect to see a boost in software solutions offered in the form of bundles. From a procurement perspective, these changes allow the procuring firm to transfer technology risks away from itself to the vendor, making it the vendor’s responsibility to construct the offerings that its customers will seek and allowing firms to best leverage for high-value returns on their investments.

## How Firm-Level Changes Support Unified Procurement

Hender son and Venk atr aman [25] have indicated that organizational competitive advantage can be obtained through dynamic processes of strategic alignment involving business strategy, IT strategy, organizational infrastructure and processes, and IT infrastructure and processes. The unified procurement strategy enables firms to achieve higher agility with their IT infrastructure because it allows them to upgrade future software stack products at a faster rate by eliminating challenges of product compatibility and multivendor coordination. More complex firms that wish to leverage the benefits of unified procurement will strive to make such a transformation. This leads us to the following proposition on firm-level effects of unified procurement:

Proposition 2a (The Competition-Driven Agility Proposition): Firm competition will drive firms to strive for greater agility in their IT infrastructure through the adoption of a unified procurement strategy for the acquisition of their software stacks.

Mini-Case 2a: JetBlue Increases Competitiveness by

Standardizing on One Vendor

The airline industry has been moving toward single-vendor data centers, as Benaroch et al. [3] have reported. With single-vendor data centers, airline firms have identified uniformity as a way to reduce their internal costs in an extremely competitive industry. An example is JetBlue Airways, which decided to go with Microsoft as its single-vendor supplier for all of its software programs, except their customer reservation system. Tony Lams, Vice President and Lead Analyst of the Port Chester Consulting of New York, has noted the key reason behind the move: “JetBlue claims standardization on one system has helped them gain on the competition” [56]. Jeff Cohen, JetBlue’s chief information officer (CIO), said that moving to a Windows platform resulted in cost savings and a 50 percent reduction in head count for the company [66].

Another benefit of unified procurement is that it allows firms to effectively and efficiently scale during a project by leveraging its vendor’s resources, thus removing the need to expand its existing IT operations [15]. Moreover, fewer skill sets are required to support a single-vendor software stack due to a vendor’s common platform for all of its technologies [56]. All of this contributes to an overall reduction in the IT department for firms that adopt unified procurement. Knowing this allows us to offer the next proposition:

Proposition 2b (The IT Operations Size Reduction Proposition): Firms that seek to reduce the size of their IT operations will be drawn to a unified procurement strategy for enterprise software solutions, increasing the likelihood of its marketwide adoption.

## Mini-Case 2b: MedAire Achieves Smaller IT Operations Through Microsoft Adoption

We learned from the JetBlue mini-case that unified procurement adoption has resulted in head count reduction. Firms in other industries have been able to achieve similar results. For example, MedAire, a large Tempe, Arizona-based provider of health care services, moved to a single-vendor software stack as long ago as 2003. At the time of its implementation, the IT department had four staff members. Two years later, it was still operating at this same number. Doug Payne, CIO at MedAire, commented: “Our goal was to increase employee productivity without increasing IT staff and we did that” [38].

Adoption of unified procurement for a firm’s software stack allows it to achieve a uniform architecture. When a firm pursues a multivendor approach, it increases the heterogeneity of its IT infrastructure without necessarily creating the ideal basis for cost control going into the future, but unified procurement enables a firm to achieve consolidation, leading to a uniform and simplified IT infrastructure [11]. Thus, we further propose:

Proposition 2c (The Uniform IT Infrastructure Proposition): Firms that strive to achieve a uniform IT infrastructure will be more likely to adopt the unified procurement of software stacks.

## Mini-Case 2c: Fidelity National Achieves IT Infrastructure Uniformity Through IBM

Fidelity National Financial originally had an IT infrastructure with client/server applications from IBM, Microsoft, and Oracle. Moving to purely an IBM IT infrastructure allowed Fidelity National to reduce the complexity and heterogeneity of its IT environment. Despite the millions of dollars spent to make the transition, management believed that it was investing in the firm’s future. Joe Nackashi, CIO of Fidelity National, noted that his firm’s investment will allow it to “move away from the complexities of a client/server distributed world” [37].

The factors that drive a firm to adopt unified procurement thus will include the motivation to become more agile, an opportunity to reduce the size of its IT operations, and a chance to establish greater uniformity across its IT infrastructure. How then is risk being transferred in this case? Although the procuring firms have become more agile using this approach, the vendors have grown quickly with each new acquisition of their software solution. With multivendor procurement, a firm must manage several vendors, taking measures to make sure that they work well together. With unified procurement, the vendor takes on a different task. It is to ensure that the software solutions that populate the acquiring vendor’s software stack integrate smoothly into the organization so as to deliver a consistent, high-quality experience to the customers. Although the size of the IT department of the firm that adopts a single-vendor software stack is shrinking, the IT department of the vendor is likely to be expanding as its customers and technology assets increase. Moreover, firms that adopt the unified procurement strategy typically expect their vendor to help them keep their IT infrastructure uniform. This means that the vendor will need to take on the added responsibility of locating solutions that fit well with its existing software stack.

## Industry-Level Effects and a Move to Unified Procurement

Vendor s pr efer to suppor t industr ies with stable technology and process standards for various reasons. The industry community has established that standards lead to wider adoption and increase the demand for technologies that support them [65], motivating vendors to develop new ones. This leads us to state:

Proposition 3a (The Technology and Process Standards Proposition): Industries with technology and process standards will move to unified procurement of software stacks at a faster rate than industries without them.

## Mini-Case 3a: Oracle Adopts Communications Industry Standard, eTOM

Development of industry standards such as the enhanced Telecom Operations Map (eTOM) for the communications industry enables service providers and vendors to identify where the gaps are in their software stack. As noted by Martin Creaner, current president of the TeleManagement Forum (TM Forum): “When service providers can’t summarize in their own words the process gaps that exist, they pull out eTOM and ask which of the vendor’s products will fill that gap in the eTOM hierarchy of processes” [52]. Oracle has been able to leverage TM Forum’s eTOM framework during its product development process [46].

Unified procurement offers vendors the ability to block out the competition by making it difficult for vendors that do not offer relatively complete software stack capabilities to compete. This leaves small vendors with just a few options to yield the appropriate complementarities that will make the adoption of their software solutions attractive. They can either strive to be acquired or they can merge with other vendors to create a stronger, complementary software stack. Acquisitions by large vendors have created a software war among leading vendors [58]. When this occurs, the selection of vendors from the industry will quickly narrow. We propose:

Proposition 3b (The Vendor Industry Consolidation Proposition): Industry consolidation and the diminution of the number of software solutions providers that support a given industry represent another force that is driving the move toward unified procurement of enterprise software solutions.

## Mini-Case 3b: Oracle’s Acquisitions Shrink Software Vendors for Vertical Industries

Starting in 2005, Oracle went on a shopping spree and made several strategic acquisitions. In essence, the company was pouring capital investment into building its software stack. For example, Oracle acquired PeopleSoft in January 2005 for \$10.3 billion, Retek in March 2005 for \$670 million, Siebel Systems in January 2006 for \$5.85 billion, Hyperion in March 2007 for \$3.3 billion, Agile in May 2007 for \$495 million, and more recently, BEA Systems in January 2008 for \$8.5 billion. Liedtke [33] reports that Oracle had completed about 30 acquisitions at a total cost of about \$25 billion by June 2007. A more concrete example is Oracle’s move into the communications industry. Oracle took over several communications enterprise software vendors, including HotSip, Net4Call, Telephony@Work, Portal Software, and MetaSolv Software [70]. (See Appendix Table A 1 for additional details of the many acquisitions of Oracle.)

Vendors essentially have two options when it comes to developing a software stack. They can develop the products internally or obtain them through various acquisitions. Either choice requires a sizable up-front investment. As such, vendors must have confidence that they can recoup their costs. This leads the vendors to pursue industries with larger IT spending budgets, leading us to the final proposition on industry effects that supports unified procurement:

Proposition 3c (The Vendor Reaction to IT Demand Proposition): Vendors will have a stronger motivation to support the unified procurement strategy for industries that have more IT demand and larger IT spending budgets, increasing the likelihood of unified procurement in the high IT demand industries.

## Mini-Case 3c: SAP and Microsoft Build Software Stacks for Targeted Industries for IT Services

In 2003, the Massachusetts-based IT strategy and operations consultancy, IDC, reported strong projections for IT spending in vertical markets, with the highest spending in banking and manufacturing. Anne Lu, a senior analyst at IDC, indicated that on “the IT-services spending front, banking, insurance, and utilities industries will be the best opportunities for vendors pitching to fast-growth markets, and will account for [\$]162.3 billion in technology purchases” [28]. According to SAP [50] and Microsoft [54], starting in about 2003, enterprise software vendors began to pour heavy investments into building industry-specific software stacks. Their main targets were banking, communications, financial services, health care, retail, and manufacturing.

At the industry level, specific factors are driving firms within targeted industries for IT services to move to a unified procurement strategy of software stacks: the presence of standardized software solutions, a decrease in the number of industry software solutions providers, and vendors’ motivation to support the unified procurement strategy for these industries. As we have observed, software vendors are willing to take on additional technology risks given the right industries. As a result, firms in the targeted industries for IT services are more likely to be able to leverage the unified procurement strategy to their software stacks to help them decrease their risks.

## Conclusion

Vendor suppor t of the unified pr ocur ement str ategy does not seem to be slowing down. A 2007 report by Forrester Research, as cited by Wailgum [62], showed that the four largest vendors—IBM, Microsoft, Oracle, and SAP—had 35 percent of the global market for software. As more firms begin to show value from the adoption of unified procurement and the total number of adoptions increases, vendors will make even more investments to expand their software stacks, leading to higher adoption in the future.

## Implications

Because the unified procurement strategy is still relatively new in the enterprise software industry, we have yet to witness the long-term effects. Our current observations nonetheless have led us to predict that when modern IT design approaches are used and a firm is in a more mature industry, then less complex firms, as we have defined them, will increasingly be observed to adopt the unified procurement strategy. The opportunity for a firm to transfer some of its risks to its vendor is making unified procurement a more attractive option when compared to multivendor procurement. As indicated by Clemons and Row [8], IT can lead to stable, tightly coupled relationships among firms by decreasing costs and risks of explicit coordination. Our study of this strategy has led us to present a number of propositions that represent new middle range theory related to the effects of IT, firm, and industry structure changes on the adoption of unified procurement of enterprise software solutions. We believe that the new ideas we have presented will generalize well, once they are subject to further tests in other contexts.

Regarding developments in the IT area, it seems that standards-based software solutions will achieve a faster rate of adoption via unified procurement. A positive relationship appears to exist between vendors’ development of preintegrated software stacks and adoption of the unified procurement strategy. In our view, more and more software solutions will be sold in the form of bundles by software vendors that are interested in offering unified procurement of software stacks and seek to strengthen their support of the approach. Market competition, meanwhile, will drive firms to strive for greater agility through the adoption of the unified procurement strategy for acquiring their software stacks. There is evidence that may support an inverse relationship between observations of different firms’ adoption of unified procurement and the size of their IT operations, too, as we noted with our discussion of some mini-cases. Also, firms that strive to achieve a uniform IT infrastructure will be more likely to adopt unified procurement, reflecting a shift from greater heterogeneity in the elements that comprise their software solutions.

Finally, our evidence suggests that adoption and diffusion of the unified procurement strategy will occur at a faster rate for industries with technology and process standards. The enterprise software industry’s movement toward unified procurement seems to be partly driven by the shrinking number of software solutions providers. For this and the other reasons that we have presented, it appears that vendors’ support of the unified procurement strategy will occur at a faster rate for industries that have higher IT demand and larger IT spending budgets.

## Complicating Factors

Despite the strategy’s benefits, there are several factors that can impede the adoption and spread of unified procurement. If changes in IT, firm, and industry structure make it so that the transferred risks we proposed will move to the vendors under multivendor procurement strategy, then we may see less impetus for unified procurement adoption.

Modifications in transaction costs can easily affect the economic organization of firms [69]. The value that is achieved from IT is heavily dependent on internal and external factors [39]. We next consider some of the impending threats.

On the IT side, we are seeing competing changes and effects that are at work. Recently, for example, there has been a push in the industry to move toward adoption of service-oriented architecture (SOA). It is consistent with a multivendor strategy. Service-oriented architecture is “a technical architecture, a business modeling concept, a piece of infrastructure, an integration source, and a new way of viewing units of automation within the enterprise,” according to Erl [18, p. 476]. Firms that have adopted SOA believe it is the strategy to help them reduce costs. With market demand for SOA, vendors that support unified procurement will have to show that they can support SOA or be able to demonstrate value beyond it.

Strategies in their early stages of launch are always fragile. So far, most of the reported success stories have been small and medium-sized enterprises. A possibility is that large firms may already have obtained several of the benefits associated with unified procurement. In order to encourage them to transition to a different strategy, vendors must be able to demonstrate additional value. Otherwise, lack of adoption from large firms may limit widespread acceptance of unified procurement. Additionally, we must not forget the skeptics. There are IT managers who continue to question the strategy [16]. This puts intense pressure on the vendors; those that support unified procurement need to work closely with their customers to ensure success. Being able to measure the potential and realized value of IT investments is critical [1].

One of the assumptions we made is that the targeted industries for IT services will continue to consolidate, creating opportunities for firms in the industry to consolidate their IT assets with those of one vendor, or migrate their legacy environments onto single-vendor platforms. Cost pressures in the current economy, particularly among certain industries, will make it difficult for these firms to make the transformation we have described. Without investments from industry firms, the vendors’ ability to strengthen this model for certain industries will be hindered.

## Contributions and Limitations

We provided a new theoretical interpretation of procurement related to the firm’s software stack. We reexamined the predictions of the “move to the middle” hypothesis of Clemons et al. [10] in light of the emergence of unified procurement strategy for enterprise software solutions, a growing phenomenon that has arisen in the presence of changing IT, firm, and industry structure. To date, unified procurement of enterprise software appears to offer one of the most extreme manifestations of “move to the middle” hypothesis. We offered our own new theoretical perspectives on why an increasing number of IT managers are electing this procurement strategy in the study context. A primary motivation for this shift is the chance for a firm to transfer some risks it bears to its vendor. We revealed its importance in affecting firms’ procurement choices as illustrated by the adoption of unified procurement. We further showed how the amount of risk transferred is influenced by the changes in IT, firm, and industry structure, and used mini-cases to provide support for our theoretical interpretations.

Our analysis has led us to develop and present propositions on the emerging effects of the adoption of unified procurement strategy. This knowledge is critical for IT managers who are involved in making software stack design and procurement decisions. Understanding the potential effects will allow them to make more well-informed choices. From the vendors’ perspective, our study helps to reveal what issues they will need to consider as they move forward with their own technology strategies. Although we studied the enterprise software industry, unified procurement is relevant for other IT industries as well. A good example of this is Cisco Systems [53], which focuses on a unified procurement strategy for the computer networking industry. Ray Jaksa, CIO of the Mansfield Independent School District, provides his reasons for selecting a unified procurement strategy: “By incorporating everything into one package, we really got a great cost reduction in our overall expenses over time. We’ve really, really experienced a lot of good things from the Cisco product since we put them in” [35].

Since unified procurement strategy for software stacks has only begun to generate interest within the past five years due to changes in the enterprise software industry, we were limited to validating our ideas via exploratory methods using vendor mini-cases, industry articles, and press announcements. While exploratory research methods may be subject to criticism, we nevertheless have been able to deliver significant value by offering early insights on an emerging technology phenomenon. As for future research on the topic, we hope to conduct more in-depth case studies with firms that have adopted unified procurement to gain further insight. We also will explore how vendors should bundle and price their software solutions under this setting and evaluate the effects of adoption on future procurement contracts. Undertaking empirical work in this area will be strictly limited by how quickly diffusion occurs.
