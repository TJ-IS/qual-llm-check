# Online Reverse Auctions and the Dynamics of Trust

MOHAMED HÉDI CHARKI AND EMMANUEL JOSSERAND

MOHAMED HÉDI CHARKI is an Associate Professor in the Department of Markets, Management, and Humanities at the EDHEC Business School in Lille, France. He received his Ph.D. in Management Information Systems from Université Paris Dauphine. He has also been a consultant for a leading software company. His research focuses on business-to-business electronic markets; online reverse auctions; and interfirm trust, distrust, and ethics within information systems.

EMMANUEL JOSSERAND is a Professor at HEC, University of Geneva. He has taught strategic management at various universities and business schools in Europe, Australia, and Africa. He has a Ph.D. from, and was formerly an Associate Professor at, and is currenty an associate researcher at Université Paris Dauphine. He was also a professional associate at a leading consulting firm in France. His research interests are organization and strategy, more specifically, cooperation within and between organizations.

ABSTRACT: This research explores the effect of the introduction of online reverse auctions (ORAs) on interorganizational trust between buyers and suppliers in the retail industry. Building upon the notion of the spirit of the technology and the organizing vision, we shed light on the “equivoque” nature of ORAs. In an integrative model, we show how the desocialization associated with the introduction to ORAs can lead to distrust. Our findings show specifically the importance of the role played by technical problems and rumors.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: distrust, online reverse auctions, online trust, opportunism, rumor, trust, unethical behavior.

THE ESTABLISHMENT AND SAFEGUARD OF TRUST within and between organizations can improve business performance and competitive achievement in the business environment [8, 47, 48]. Interorganizational trust is believed to have several economic advantages, such as facilitating the coordination of economic activities, encouraging the open exchange of information, and smoothing interorganizational conflicts, which contribute to a significant reduction of transactions costs [61].

Nevertheless, intensified competition and the dynamic nature of business connections between firms can blur common assumptions in the relationship, thereby weakening trust and increasing perceived uncertainty [34]. The proliferation of advanced e-commerce systems, such as the electronic marketplace, can change the nature of competition and the dynamics of business relationships [32]. More particularly, online reverse auctions (ORAs) have emerged in the last decade as a significant technological tool with great promise for efficiency and economic savings for firms in the business of purchasing [19, 57]. ORAs have become a popular mediation tool [56, 57] because they offer considerable cost reduction opportunities for buyers [55].

Buyers tend to consider ORAs as an attempt to create a pure and transparent market [62]. They also argue that this technology represents an opportunity for suppliers to penetrate new markets [55, 56]. However, the effects of ORAs are double-edged. In fact, suppliers claim that the inappropriate use of ORAs is a source of conflict and threatens trusting relationships [29, 31]. ORA use can trigger defensive behavior that may lead to distrust in buyer–supplier relationships [20]. This, in turn, can harm the buyer because suppliers might adopt opportunistic behaviors such as reducing the quality of their products [31]. Hence, more research is needed on the effect of information technology (IT) on relationships [50], especially in the context of ORAs [31, 57].

In this paper, we improve our understanding of the influence ORA use has on interorganizational trust. This understanding can help organizations safeguard trust when using ORAs. The paper is based on an in-depth case study about the effects inferred by the use of ORAs between a major player in the French retail industry and its suppliers. Data were collected through a series of 70 in-depth interviews with stakeholders. Our analysis of the data led us to develop an integrative model of the effect of ORAs on the trust–distrust dynamic and the consequences for the buyer–supplier relationship.

## Literature Review

## Online Reverse Auctions: Structural Features and Spirit

WE PRESENT ORAS FOLLOWING DESANCTIS AND POOLE’S [16] distinction between structural features and the spirit of the technology. The structural features of technology are defined as the specific types of rules and resources or capabilities offered by the system. DeSanctis and Poole define how information can be gathered, manipulated, and otherwise managed by users [16, p. 126]. ORAs represent a technological resource hosted in the virtual environment of electronic marketplaces on the Internet. ORAs allow buyers to gather information about market prices and supplier offerings. More precisely, the buyer begins the process by requesting a traditional quote from a wide number of suppliers. Suppliers—generally a mix of incumbent(s) and new supplier(s) [19, 30]—are then selected and invited to bid during the ORA. The buyer then gives a virtual appointment to the selected suppliers at a precise date and time. The process starts by fixing a starting price, the lowest price offered by suppliers at the preselection stage [19]. Suppliers then propose decreasing prices. The bidding time restarts with each new quote from each supplier until a price is reached beyond which no supplier will make a new bid [30, 55].

The spirit of the technology encapsulates the goals, values, and philosophy that underlie the structural devices. It provides legitimacy to the technology by supplying the normative frame of appropriate behaviors in a specific context [16]. ORAs are presented by their designers as a valuable tool that can enhance buyers’ negotiation performance by increasing the chance of obtaining better prices from suppliers, optimizing the negotiation process [19], and decreasing administrative and supply-chain management costs [19, 57]. From the supply side, ORAs—as with any auctions—are presented as a means of increasing transparency [6, 37] by revealing the current market prices [55], enhancing chances to penetrate new markets [55, 57], offering empirical benchmarks with global competitors [15], and, as a result, improving business practices [19] and production planning [56]. Core to the spirit of ORAs is the principle that selected suppliers are qualified, trustworthy, and able to honor their bid [30]. For this, buyers require samples to prove that products are eligible, and conform to schedules of conditions. This is necessary to ensure the possibility of savings [55, 56, 57], to reduce transaction costs for both players [55, 57], and to enable worldwide sourcing horizons for buyers [19].

ORAs are a popular tool among industrial firms because, in general, they provide more attractive prices, a less cumbersome negotiation process, and an optimization of buyer’s productivity [31, 57]. Eng [22] found—in an extensive survey in the retail sector—that ORAs are the most used tool among electronic marketplace functions for supply-chain management [22, p. 101]. Recent professional research has provided further evidence of the use of ORAs: Accenture states that “companies as diverse as General Electric, Sun Microsystems, British Aerospace and many others have staged large-scale eAuctions with select vendors or buyers—and seen remarkable results.”<sup>1</sup> In the same vein, a study by Purchasing reveals that reverse auctions become a prime sourcing tool because they gather valuable pricing information.<sup>2</sup>

## The Ambivalent Effects of ORAs Put Trust at Risk

The effects of ORAs are double-edged; this creates an ambivalence that can jeopardize trust. Trust can be defined as a psychological state integrating the acceptance of vulnerability based on expectations of positive intentions and behavior from the other party [49, 52]. This implies the expectation that the parties will negotiate in an equitable way, even if the possibility of opportunism exists [47, 48].<sup>3</sup>

To start with, the effects of ORAs on idiosyncratic investment are not straightforward. The perceived threat associated with using ORAs might induce suppliers to carry out idiosyncratic investments with the intention of discouraging the buyer from using the technology [29], or it might have no effect on idiosyncratic investments [31]. Nonetheless, suppliers will also be less inclined to carry out such investments because they have no guarantee of the buyer’s continued commitment [56, 57]. This can have a negative effect for the buyer.

ORAs are also perceived as impersonal and dehumanizing, depriving valuable business relationships of their emotional content [31]. This can lead to a situation where price becomes the only decision criterion [56, 57]. ORAs can lead to an imbalance in the power/dependence equilibrium [61], and can thus be considered by suppliers as an additional form of pressure to obtain more concessions [56, 57], which are transformed into an increase in buyers’ profits [20, 21, 31, 59]. This imbalance, in turn, increases the perception of risk and consequently leads to the erosion of trust. Beyond the idea of power, suppliers accuse buyers of being opportunistic by only considering their own interests to the detriment of those of the supplier [20, 21, 31, 59]. Also, suspicions of distorted bidding can lead to distrust, for instance, when suppliers suspect that the deal has been negotiated before the ORA took place [57] or that it is only a negotiation ploy toward the preferred supplier [57]. Other opportunistic behaviors include using ORAs to find out about market prices without having the intention of awarding a contract [20, 56] or inviting nonviable suppliers to bid in order to oblige reliable ones to reduce their bids [20, 31].

This could explain why Howard et al. [28] present the erosion of the suppliers trust as a serious barrier to the adoption of ORAs. Indeed, ORAs have been described as damaging interorganizational trust [20, 31]. The use of ORAs is perceived as a serious threat to trust [29], especially where past successful business relationships existed [56].

ORAs are thus a good illustration of the ambivalence of technology. Weick [64, p. 148] used the concept of “equivoque,” something that allows several plausible interpretations and therefore can be esoteric, subject to misunderstandings, uncertain, complex, and recondite. Making sense of ORAs and distinguishing the positive and negative effects is neither easy in theory nor in practice. Actors attribute different meanings to ORAs depending on what they use them for. ORAs can therefore be perceived as effective for buyers and suppliers, but also as a source of distrust that can have negative effects for both parties. This is partly due to the fact that actors suspect that the spirit of the technology is not respected by the other, but it can also result from their various interpretations of the technology.

Such a risk was highlighted by Swanson and Ramiller [60] when they explained how collective and cognitive views of the interorganizational community create and employ an organizing vision which is constituted by the “focal community idea for the application of information technology in organizations” [60, p. 460]. Swanson and Ramiller recognize the inherent heterogeneity of the organizing vision and consider that stakeholders involved in the use of technology must reach a consensus in order to support the adoption of the technological innovations.

Unfortunately, this consensus seems hard to reach in the case of ORAs because the technology is inherently double-edged and can thus lead to ambivalent representations by the actors. This ambivalence may well be blamed for diminishing trust but we still know little about the actual representations of the technology by the actors and about the nature and circumstances of the influence of ORA use on interorganizational trust and distrust.

## Trust Deterioration and Its Effect on Business Relationships

Our review of the literature has shown that the introduction of ORAs harms trust in business relationships. This is significant for the implementation of ORAs. Indeed, trust is a success factor in electronic auctions in both the business-to-consumer [43] and the business-to-business (B2B) sectors [31]. It is also considered the bonding element that characterizes the majority of the productive [26] and successful buyer–seller relationships [39]. More broadly, it is a key part in the emergence of all social interactions [23, 51] and a determinant element of successful business [47, 48] and social relationships [2, 39]. Trust also has an impact on the economic aspects of a relationship in terms of transaction costs [8, 61] and risk reduction [47], and also because it improves value creation [49].

Trust can be understood as developing gradually over time due to the accumulation of positive experiences [47]. But trust is fragile and easily destroyed [7] even if we grant it has a certain amount of resilience [46]. Trust can resist erosion due to opportunistic behaviors up to a point, but a rapid decline is to be expected after a certain threshold [9]. A trusting relationship can thus devolve toward distrust—distrust being defined as the expectation of assured negative behavior by the other party [36] and the apprehension that the other party will harm the interests of the party that created the trust [5, 25].

It is only recently that researchers began exploring the process of trust deterioration as an interorganizational phenomenon [5, 10] even if a number of notable endeavors had already been undertaken within organizations [13, 36, 52, 53]. To describe this process, Slovic [54] explains that negative events, including breach of trust, are felt more keenly and therefore perceived more readily than positive events. In this regard, Deutsch [17] presents suspicion as being a central element of distrust and describes it as a psychological state where individuals actively maintain several potentially rival hypotheses for the other party’s behavior. Distrust is harmful because it makes the fruitful exploration of the environment difficult and discourages potentially positive behavior [27].

It is thus especially important—considering the double-edged effect of ORAs described in the literature, and the possible negative effect on trust—to better understand the influence of ORA use on interorganizational trust and distrust.

## Research Methodology

OUR APPROACH FOLLOWS THE PRINCIPLES OF the “ensemble view” that focuses on “the dynamic interactions between people and technology” [40, p. 126]. We integrated the IT artifact perspective according to which IT is applied to “enable or support some task(s) embedded within a structure(s) that itself is embedded within a context(s)” [11, p. 186]. We designed the research to highlight the issues related to the intended and unintended consequences of using a technology such ORA, while taking into account the dynamic interactions between people and technology.

## Rationale and Setting

Our research design is a single case study with a qualitative approach. Benbasat et al. [12] suggested three reasons to justify the use of case studies. First, the case study provides an analysis of interorganizational information systems in their natural state, and provides a basis for the generation of theory founded on practice. It also offers a better understanding of the nature of processes by answering “how” questions [66]. Last, it is very helpful when there are few research studies on the subject and when the study relates to real events. Indeed, the exploratory case study approach is particularly necessary in new situations [66]. In fact, given the lack of previous systematic research dealing with the effects of ORAs on business relationships [55], and more particularly in the absence of an information system conceptual basis surrounding the emergent literature on the topic of ORAs [31, 55], we consider it essential to conduct and adopt an inductive approach based on the study of a single case.

The domination of positivist thought in the study of managerial issues relating to IT has not prevented a growing interest in qualitative research [63]. Kaplan and Maxwell [33] estimate that data quantification is likely to harm the understanding of the phenomenon from the point of view of the participants, and the institutional and social context. Conventional survey research settings are static and, consequently, ill-adapted to examining dynamic and sensitive phenomena like the effects of ORAs on interorganizational trust. Also, qualitative research settings have been identified as being well-adapted to analyzing dynamic, sensitive, and nonstatic events [35]. Moreover, the qualitative approach allowed us to acquire a thorough understanding of the case and revealed unexpected clues to help us develop a theoretical contribution that results from the object of study being positioned closer to the surface and therefore being easier to detect [18, 45]. The present study is therefore based on a qualitative, in-depth, contextually rich case study.

## Data Sources

We studied the effect of ORAs on the relationships between a French retailer and its suppliers. The unit of analysis is that of the elementary buyer–supplier relationship. The retailer used ORAs for its retail brands range only, and on a wide range of products—food and nonfood items. This case study is of special interest considering the scarcity of studies about the retail industry in the emergent literature about ORAs [62].

Data were collected through 70 semistructured interviews. Sampling was aimed at obtaining a rich and contextual understanding of the representations of ORAs by the different categories of actors that constitute the interorganizational community described by Swanson and Ramiller [60]. The actors we interviewed fall into three main categories: originators of the spirit of the technology, buyers, and suppliers.

We organized a first set of exploratory interviews with the manager of B2B technological applications (our key informant), and the general manager of the B2B relationships, the ORA coordinator, and the sales manager for B2B applications (our main interlocutors). These meetings aimed to gain a thorough understanding of the context and establish the research agenda and sampling. Our main interlocutors’ knowledge of the community and various actors was key in selecting the first set of interviews for the first two categories of actor—originators of the spirit of the technology and the buyers. The position of our interlocutors also helped the process by giving us easy access to selected interviewees. For the originators of the spirit of the technology, we drew up an exhaustive list of people who were involved in the conception and deployment of the technology. And, for the buyers, we drew up an exhaustive list of firms that had run at least one ORA with their suppliers. We interviewed all of the actors listed for both categories. For the third category of actors—suppliers—we asked the buyers to indicate at least one or more suppliers with whom they had had ORAs. We continued to interview suppliers until we reached a saturation point [24, 41]—that is, when no new considerations emerged from the data collection [24].

We thus interviewed 18 originators of the spirit of the technology, 20 buyers, and 32 suppliers. The interviews lasted from one hour to two hours and 15 minutes. Informants for the spirit of the technology were found mainly inside the retail company, but we also interviewed external members. The main informants were the manager of B2B technological applications, the sales manager responsible for B2B applications, the retailer’s information systems director, the ORA coordinator, the electronic marketplace general manager, and the electronic marketplace key account manager. The sample of suppliers is presented in Table 1.

In order to adapt our perspective to the position of the three categories of actors, we developed three interview guides. Based on the literature review, the three guides were specifically designed in order to capture the actors’ perceptions of both the outcomes of the use of ORAs and the current and future influence of ORAs on trust more broadly, in the supplier–buyer relationship. We conceived the guide so as to ensure theoretical saturation [35, p. 70].<sup>4</sup>

Given that the interviews could deal with confidential or sensitive aspects of business relationships, we assured the participants that all interviews would be recorded under cover of anonymity, and that suppliers’ answers in particular would not be transmitted to their respective buyers. In addition to the interviews, we conducted two nonparticipant observations of ORAs at the retailer’s headquarters.

## Data Analysis

All of the interviews were recorded and then transcribed verbatim. We did the retranscription progressively after each interview and carried out a code-based content analysis.

Originally, data were coded in categories corresponding to sets of themes drawn from the literature review [38]. A case study analysis entails examining, classifying, and selecting or recombining facts [66]. We examined each text through a comprehensive line-by-line exploration in order to become highly familiar with each informant’s way of interpreting events [18]. At the end of each content analysis, we drew up a short summary of the main information contained in each interview transcript in order to facilitate the elaboration of our contribution. We analyzed the data by locating the topics identified in the literature. These topics were analyzed in relation to our research questions based on an iterative process respecting Miles and Huberman’s [38] recommendations. ## Results

BY CONTRASTING THE PERSPECTIVES OF THE three categories—originators of the spirit of the technology, buyers, and suppliers—we were able to integrate the various effects of the use of ORAs into a global perspective that improves our understanding of the ambivalence of ORAs, the impact of such ambivalence on trust, and the resulting effects. For each step of this reasoning, we present our data in the form of typical quotations from the three categories.

## The Ambivalence of ORAs

There is a fundamental opposition between the originators of the spirit of the technology and the buyers, on one hand, and the suppliers, on the other. The opposition is not in the vision but rather the stakes that they associate with the use of ORAs. It is very clear for the three categories that ORAs result in price reductions and thus a reduction of suppliers’ margins, and is very efficient in doing so. The first obvious benefit is thus one for the retailer. In their description of ORAs, the originators of the technology and the buyers mention cost savings whereas the suppliers complain about the reduction of their margin. The technology is seen as ambivalent in its capacity to offer some positive prospects for the suppliers to consider.

A first positive aspect that seems to be shared among three categories of actors is a reduction of transaction costs associated with the negotiation process. In effect, the three interviewed parties confirm that ORAs accelerate negotiations, decrease faceto-face meetings and business traveling, and save time. Still, some suppliers see in ORAs an additional step to the classical negotiation process.

Next, there are two elements more specific to suppliers—access to new markets and the opportunity to benchmark. On both items, there seems to be a consensus about the possibility offered by the use of ORAs. Even suppliers who were suspicious about this new “imported” tool find that it constitutes a way to challenge incumbent suppliers. More particularly, ORAs would be more worthwhile to suppliers whose business models enable them to increase their production volume while supporting the drop in their prices. Still, the question raised by suppliers is that of the balance of advantages and disadvantages, especially as the advantages can be eroded by the margin reduction: “Even if we gain new markets, reverse auctions don’t generate enough margins for profitability. It’s a disguised victory.” In the same vein, another supplier indicated: “ORAs were an advantage at the beginning because we could penetrate the market . . . but today there are no advantages, only inconveniences since margin levels are too low to interest our firm.”

For suppliers, the balance between positive and negative effects of ORAs is delicate and the technology is thus perceived as extremely ambivalent. If they assess the situation as being disadvantageous, suppliers make the decision to reduce their participation in ORAs: “Our top management rules were clear; we are going to reduce our participation in ORAs considerably because we believe that it is a risky system whose aim is solely the buyer’s interest.” Both buyers and suppliers also recognize that the impact on margins leads to a deterioration of the relationship, as illustrated by the following quotation:

The 50/50 win doesn’t exist, if you make savings, it’s to the detriment of suppliers, there is obviously a big winner and a small one or a small loser and a big one, so it’s clear that if in the end the auctions allow you to still optimize the purchase price even more than a classic negotiation, yes, that’s going to devalue the relation. (Buyer)

Simple technical problems can be a first point for eroding trust. This was identified mainly by the originators of the technology. Suppliers tend to attribute the interruption of an ORA to unethical behavior—that is, a behavior that does not respect the spirit of the technology. This is, of course, harmful to the relationship and subsequent participation in ORAs. This is well illustrated by the following quotation:

they give you ten minutes, or a quarter of an hour, a half hour and they disconnect you, that’s the reality. . . . I’m telling you this type of behavior creates distrust and at some point when you have two or three calls for tender per auction like that you say to yourself, so what do we do? And we at the group level, after five or six years of experience in this type of auction, we really question it. (Supplier)

We also found evidence in the three categories that ORAs have suffered from opportunistic and unethical behavior of other retailers. Among them, we noted practices such as the selection of nonvalid suppliers, buyers’ phone calls to the supplier during the bidding process, excessive recourse to ORAs, or even the use of fake suppliers. This, of course, leads to the deterioration of the relationship and, ultimately, to distrust, a phenomenon acknowledged even by the originators of the technology:

I heard that some of our competitors used fake buyers. I think that it’s a big mistake to do that, because for a system to work there really needs to be trust, that is, the people who participate in it need to have confidence in the fact that the system is going to be fair and that’s important. (General Manager of B2B Relationships)

It also directly threatens future usage of ORAs, as it generally means reverting to classical negotiations: “with another retailer in England, I took part in an ORA where some abuses had been noticed; the buyer didn’t achieve expected savings, so he withdrew from the auction and asked us to revert to classic negotiations” (Supplier).

Technical incidents and opportunistic behavior tend to taint the perception of transparency associated with ORAs. It is interesting to note that the three groups share the vision that ORAs can, under certain conditions, improve interorganizational transparency about contract-awarding decisions. One of the key elements of this transparency is the fact that all of the selected suppliers visualize live price evolution. Thus, an ORA is described as a process that can remove distortions and suspicions, formerly associated with classic negotiation, of systematic contract awarding to the incumbent supplier. Still, even buyers acknowledge the fact that this opinion is not shared by all suppliers: “Some suppliers have the feeling that it is destructive without transparency and this is completely wrong” (Buyer).

Suspicion is heightened by the fact that the relationship between buyer and supplier is intermediated by the IT tool and is thus perceived as devoid of human content. Both originators of the spirit of the technology and buyers recognize that ORAs constitute a radical and restrictive system that ignores all of the “nonquantifiable” criteria of business relationships. As a consequence, such undersocialization leads suppliers to interpret the ORA as an unfair system because it is blind to suppliers’ investments and efforts; it does not acknowledge successful past experiences. The strength of the possible impact on the relationship is well illustrated by the following quotation:

before, there used to be trust but it is nonetheless fragile, and then the ORA was the straw that broke the camel’s back, resulting in distrust. . . . it destroys everything, and there are no more human relationships, buyers are now in front of their screen. (Supplier)

In such a context, rumors are prone to emerge across the industry. Typical examples include, of course, the case of buyers presenting themselves as suppliers and bidding during the auction process. In effect, unethical behavior that emanates from other French or European retailers risk extending distrust that occurs in a given relationship across the whole industry. A typical quotation on the subject is:

I reckon that this type of inappropriate and unethical use by certain big European retailers can harm the overall image of the auction so it’s not the auctions themselves that are responsible for the possible deterioration of the climate of trust but the poor use by certain retailers at the European level and this can explain the spirit of distrust of certain providers vis-à-vis the auction mechanisms. (Supplier)

## The Impact of ORAs on Supply Relationships

We identified three major effects that emanate from the supply side as a reaction not only toward ORAs but also as a result of distrust. Table 5 presents the data for this section.

First, there is agreement among the three groups of actors about the expected decrease in suppliers’ idiosyncratic investments. Suppliers are not likely to take the risk to invest in a given relationship that is governed only by the price criterion. In effect, because suppliers do not feel any commitment from the buying side with ORAs, notably when systematized, they are less likely to make idiosyncratic investments, for instance, in research and development. Interestingly, if the buyers perceive this risk, the originators of the technology tend to associate idiosyncratic investments with investments in general and underline the risk of systematic underinvestment from the supplier. Terminating the business relationship after a period of idiosyncratic investment from the supplier is perceived as a breach of trust:

there you can’t speak of trust with this company because when you work hard for two years of projects and after two or three years they put you in competition with companies that can for some reason reduce the price, it’s a huge frustration and you know. . . . Competition is okay but not when you see on the Internet things less than 20 percent of our price because there is something wrong; we saw that last year. (Supplier)

Second, all members of the interorganizational community are conscious of the risk of opportunistic behavior from the suppliers. A first possible opportunistic behavior is revealing sensitive information, such as the price of the ORA, to the retailer’s competitors. The buyers declare that suppliers’ opportunism is more likely to take place in the initial stages of the process during the response to the request for quotation. Suppliers indicate that the systematic use of ORAs for negotiation in the same market in a relatively short period of time risks pushing suppliers to undertake undeclared decreases in the product quality in order to endure continuous drops in prices. More certainly, they limit their accommodating behaviors if unpredicted situations arise. In certain cases, suppliers tried to organize market collusion and thus share the retailers market. A typical response is found in the following quotation: “suppliers risk cheating on raw materials, such as one who diluted lubricant with water in order to achieve such low prices” (Supplier).

## Discussion

IN THIS PAPER, WE IMPROVE THE UNDERSTANDING of the influence of ORA use on interorganizational trust and distrust through the study of the introduction of ORAs into the relationship between a major French retailer and its suppliers. Our findings corroborate some of the early literature’s first findings, suggest new factors, and combine them into an integrative model. This knowledge can help organizations safeguard trust and its related beneficial outcomes when using ORAs.

The findings of this research validate a number of the initial results suggested by the emergent literature about ORAs. At the same time, they expand the understanding of the way such a sophisticated technological application influences trust across organizational boundaries. Our study covered the perception of the three actor categories constituting the interorganizational community: the originators of the spirit of the technology, the buyers, and the suppliers.

We first underline the ambivalence of ORAs. This ambivalence stems from the economic impact of ORAs on suppliers. We confirm the importance of the main effects suggested by the emerging literature on ORAs: reduction of transaction costs [55, 57], price reduction [19, 31, 55, 57], benchmarking for suppliers [15], and supplier access to new markets [19]. A consensus exists on the effects on price and thus on margins and, with the exception of a few suppliers, on the transaction costs savings. Nonetheless, suppliers tend to question the openness of new markets and benchmarking advantages because these are inseparable from the reduction of their margins. We thus gained a unique and detailed understanding of the perception of such effects by the three groups of actors, which allowed us to understand the dynamic of adoption of the technology. These findings confirm Weick’s vision of technology as “equivoque” [64].

Figure 1. An Integrative Model of the Effects of the Use of Online Reverse Auctions

We then presented a description of the dynamic leading from this perceived “equivoque” of the technology toward distrust [64]. First, we exposed the fact that in such a context, technical dysfunctions can be detrimental to trust. This factor is not addressed in the literature. We also found confirmation of the important effects of unethical behaviors from the suppliers, behaviors that were observed by the three actor categories. Thus, as suggested by the trust literature, we found that opportunistic behaviors lead to distrusting relationships [10]. Our study reveals the following behaviors that were not found in the literature on reverse auction: buyers’ phone calls to the supplier during the bidding process, and asking the winning supplier to get back to classic negotiation if no significant economic savings occur from the ORA. Such incidents and unethical behaviors are detrimental to the perception of transparency associated with ORAs by the three categories of actors and the emerging literature [31, 55]. Such effects echo those revealed in forward online auctions where the violation of what Pavlou and Gefen [44] called a psychological contract leads to a decrease in the level of trust.

We also confirm that the undersocialization created by ORAs was detrimental to trust. The final factor at the origin of distrust is rumor, a factor also not present in the literature on ORAs. Rumors have been identified as responsible for distrust propagation through business relationships, especially in uncertain settings [17]. Our results show that rumors can lead to distrust even in the absence of unethical or opportunistic behavior. It can result, for instance, from a mere technical problem. This nuances the literature on trust that assumes that opportunistic behaviors are necessary antecedents to distrust [5, 10].

Finally, we showed that the level of trust could have three major effects. First, we underlined that the introduction of ORAs, when associated with a decrease of trust, leads to a reduction of suppliers’ idiosyncratic investments. This is coherent with earlier findings by Smeltzer and Carr [57] and with more general findings on business relationships within the marketing channel [1]. We found no evidence of the opposite [29] or neutral effect [31] on idiosyncratic investment. The degradation of trust can also lead to opportunistic behaviors from the suppliers. This includes classical consequences of trust deterioration such as doing less work [13] or reacting in kind [3] by delivering lower-quality goods and services in the case we studied, or limiting accommodating behaviors [31]. The risk of fraud—notably, by not delivering the product—had already been identified as one of the psychological contract violations in forward auctions [44]. The buyers’ refusal to set up future ORAs corroborates the result of Pavlou and Gefen [44], who identified that the psychological contract violation can prevent parties from taking part in other transactions. Other effects not underlined by existing literature include the setting up of illegal agreements between suppliers or even divulging sensitive information about the buyer (such as prices obtained) to its competitors. These behaviors have an impact on the adoption of ORAs by both buyers and suppliers. The divulgence of sensitive information can be seen as an act of revenge subsequent to the violation of trust, similar to what Bies and Tripp [13] observed in intraorganizational settings. We thus contribute to extending the scope of application of their contribution to interorganizational settings.

More broadly, this paper shows the importance, especially considering the doubleedged effects and thus ambivalent perception of the technology, of the strict need to respect the spirit of the technology. It underlines how rumor can ruin the emergence of a consensus regarding the usage of the technology. Our observation shows the importance of considering the extent to which new technologies are presented as being parallel technologies involving a technology at the head and a technology on the floor [64, p. 158]. In other words, it is because ORA users (people on the floor) do not respect and/or neglect the spirit of technology rules (people who design the technology at the head) that negative effects prevail. This adds evidence to the perception that it is the nonrespect of the initial spirit of the technology of ORAs—notably, through unethical behavior—that causes distrust. That is, that “the effects of advanced technologies are less a function of the technologies themselves than of how they are used by people” [16, p. 122].

Our integrative model underlines the fact that a use of ORAs that does not respect the spirit of the technology could be detrimental to both suppliers and buyers. We provide below practical ways of minimizing the negative effects of ORAs—technical reliability and enhanced ethics, relationship management, and the introduction of transparent qualitative criteria.

The first step in order to improve the benefits of ORAs is to reduce incidents that can damage trust. The technology must, of course, be reliable, but if some technical problem occurs, immediate measures must be taken in order to inform the suppliers and make sure nobody is prejudiced by the interruption. Also, the development of behaviors either unethical or not conforming to the spirit of the technology must be eliminated in order to improve trust. To reach this aim, we found interesting potential solutions within forward online auctions literature. Thus, the use of feedback systems can be applied in order to enable participants (suppliers and buyers, in this context) to rate the extent to which they were satisfied and feel that their trading partners were trustworthy [4, 42]. Besides, the establishment of a third-party monitoring system—through the electronic marketplace—can assure that the ORA process is performed as expected [42, 58]. At the same time, a first step can be made at the company level, but efficient action would require the involvement of institutional actors—namely, the government and legislators at the European level. These endeavors would improve suppliers’ trust thereby facilitating their willingness to use the online auction [43].

A second possible step would be improved relationship management in order to limit the sense of undersocialization. Several actors underlined the importance of relationship management before and after the ORA. Such management could involve compensating suppliers who made idiosyncratic investments, took part in the auction, and lost by giving them access to other markets. Buyers could also call them to follow up and help consider their situation.

Finally, the introduction of qualitative criteria could reduce the degradation of trust and also the limitation of idiosyncratic investments. Such criteria would allow taking into account positive past experiences and antecedents of trust such as satisfaction, accommodating behaviors, and idiosyncratic investments.

Our research is exploratory by nature. It thus offers a theoretical contribution that remains to be further developed and assessed in other contexts. Hence, future studies of the effect of ORAs on trust could involve quantitative efforts to corroborate our results in various contexts. Also, the process we describe could be observed as it unfolds over time through a longitudinal design that would allow for a finer understanding of the respective roles played by the different variables we identified at the successive stages of the introduction of ORAs.
