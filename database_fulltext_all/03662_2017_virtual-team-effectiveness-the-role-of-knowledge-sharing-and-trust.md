## Abstract

Organizations utilize virtual teams to gather experts who collaborate online to accomplish organizational tasks. The virtual nature of these teams creates challenges to effective collaboration and team outcomes. This research addresses the social effects of knowledge sharing on virtual teams. We propose a conceptual model which hypothesizes a relationship between knowledge sharing, trust, collaboration, and team effectiveness in virtual team settings. The findings suggest that knowledge sharing positively influences trust and collaboration among virtual team members. The findings also suggest that while trust positively influences virtual team collaboration, it does not have a significant direct effect on team effectiveness.

Keywords: Virtual team, Knowledge sharing, Trust, Collaboration, Team effectiveness, Knowledge Based Theory of the Firm.

## Introduction

Teams are used by firms to organize employee resources to support the completion of complex and non-routine tasks. If designed and managed properly, teams can contribute to organizational value and competitive advantage (Alavi and Tiwana, 2002). Organizations have shifted to teambased work over the past several decades, with advances in information and communication technologies (ICT) enabling and further motivating this shift. Team design has moved beyond a configuration of same-location employees to now include members from different geographic locations and from different organizations (Berry, 2011). The result is the creation of virtual teams with new types of work patterns, decision making styles, and relationships. Together, these changes pose unique challenges to theory, practice, and research on team effectiveness (Berry 2011; Tannenbaum et al., 2012; DeOrtentiis et al., 2013). Especially as traditional team effectiveness factors may not apply or be less effective in a virtual team setting (Berry 2011).

#

In a knowledge-based view of the firm (Nonaka, 1994), knowledge is seen as an organizational asset that can be used to create and sustain a competitive advantage for both organizations and individuals. Organizations, as knowledge-based systems, must recognize the significance of knowledge as a valuable asset that has the potential to impact prosperity, maximize economic value, and improve effectiveness (Gold et al., 2001; Alavi and Leidner, 2001). As knowledge is distributed among organizational employees and other non-affiliated individuals, the use of teams can facilitate the sharing and integration of knowledge to improve performance and outcomes.

Organizations establish teams to bring together individuals with the necessary expertise and skills to collaborate on organizational tasks (Hoegl and Gemuenden, 2001). The advantage of the team structure is that it integrates the knowledge that is distributed among team members, which facilitates synergies and the achievement of more effective outcomes (Lam, 2000). However, knowledge sharing is dependent on the willingness of individual team members to share the unique knowledge they possess (Bock et al., 2005) and unfortunately, a common perception is that sharing knowledge may lead to a loss of knowledge ownership and relaated loss of power. This can lead to the hoarding of knowledge by employees and a reluctance to sharing (Kankanhalli et al., 2005). As knowledge sharing and idea exchange is crucial for virtual team collaboration, an effective virtual team requires its members to put the success of the team ahead of their tendency to hoard knowledge.

Trust is a key factor for addressing these challenges and is central element that can affect team effectiveness and success (Jarvenpaa and Leidner, 1998; Zolin et al., 2004; Henttonen and Blomqvist, 2005). However, building trust among team members in a virtual team setting is a complex task. Virtual team members are unable to observe physical behaviors which traditional face-to-face team members rely upon to establish and sustain trust. Virtual team members are reliant upon on different behaviors, unique to virtual settings, to assess trustworthiness and compensate for the lack of physically observed behaviors. We see knowledge sharing as a key behavior that virtual team members can observe and rely upon to build trust within their team.

In this research we investigate the role of knowledge sharing and trust in enabling virtual team as well as their impact on team effectiveness. Using the Knowledge Based Theory of The Firm, Social Capital Theory, and Social Exchange Theory, we propose a novel conceptual model which explains the relationship between knowledge sharing, trust, and team effectiveness, taking into account the unique characteristics of virtual teams. We test this model using data generated by a survey of virtual members working on a random set of projects in an organizational setting. The results of structural equation modeling analyses indicate that knowledge sharing positively influences trust and collaboration among virtual team members. The findings also suggest that trust positively influences virtual team collaboration, but, does not have a direct effect on team effectiveness, instead the impact of trust on team effectiveness is fully mediated by collaboration.

Our research extends the Knowledge Based Theory of the Firm by improving our understanding of how knowledge sharing impacts trust, collaboration, and virtual team effectiveness. It is one of the first studies to examine the role knowledge sharing plays in building trust and collaboration in virtual team settings. Our research also contributes to a recent call for the investigation into socio-emotional processes in virtual settings to better understand what contributes to team effectiveness (Berry, 2011; DeOrtentiis et al., 2013; Pinjani and Palvia, 2013).

## Background

In the 1990s, organizations typically established virtual teams to work on temporary projects with short life cycles. These teams were mainly established based on a need to quickly gather necessary knowledge and expertise to solve complex or non-routine problems (Squire and Johnson, 2000). The temporary nature of early virtual teams afforded limited opportunities to form social relationships (Jarvenpaa et al., 1998). Today, however, organizations are increasingly establishing virtual teams to work on ordinary routine tasks. In addition, organizations are allowing employees to work virtually from the location of their preference. The resulting mix of teams in an organization, some completely distributed while others a mix of co-located and distributed, is deemed as the degree of team virtualness (Griffith et al., 2003)

Due to the powerful combined intellectual capital of team members who benefit from each other’s knowledge and expertise, virtual teams have the potential to generate innovative and valuable results (Pinjani and Palvia, 2013). As such, investigating virtual teams is essential for learning how firms can deploy resources and perform tasks for the maximization of performance, especially when compared to traditional face-to-face team settings (Potter and Balthazard, 2002).

Beyond the technology used by teams for communication and collaboration, a major influence on virtual team performance is the social interaction and social capital among team members (Potter and Balthazard, 2002; DeOrtentiis et al., 2013; Pinjani and Palvia, 2013). According to Social Presence Theory, “communication is effective if the communication medium has the appropriate social presence required for the level of interpersonal involvement required for a task” (Sallnas et al., 2000). Organizations are social systems of individuals who are required to work collaboratively on accomplishing a common task (Alavi and Tiwana, 2002). Collaboration is an essential part of team work and an effective collaboration leads to an effective team outcome (Aram and Morgan, 1976). Virtual team members are chosen and assigned to tasks based on the need for their unique knowledge and expertise. However, recruiting expert members does not guarantee effective collaboration (Kudaravalli and Faraj, 2008; Hemetsberger and Reinhardt, 2009). Adopting elements of Aram and Morgan (1976), we define collaboration in an organizational setting as “…the presence of mutual influence between persons, open and direct communication and conflict resolution, and support for innovation and experimentation…” (Page 1). Team effectiveness has to do with group-produced outputs, and to collaborate effectively the knowledge that is distributed among team members must be properly

#

and adequately integrated (Gray, 2000). Thus, we propose that for virtual teams to collaborate effectively, team members must establish open communication and effectively share knowledge among each other.

Adopting elements of Alavi and Leidner (2001), we define knowledge as, “… a justified belief that increases an entity’s capacity for taking effective action…” (Page 14). In relation to a firm, knowledge can be possessed by employees or by the organization as a whole (the entity) and is used, through skill or capability, to complete individual or organizational tasks (the action). Knowledge is an organizational asset that comes in tacit and explicit forms, and should be treated as a significant organizational resource that can contribute to an organization’s competitive advantage (Nonaka, 1994; Alavi and Leidner, 2001).

The origination of knowledge takes place at both the individual and group levels (Nonaka 1994); individuals create knowledge independently whereas teams create a social knowledge through the interaction and collaboration of team members (Alavi and Leidner. 2001). Transforming individual knowledge into social knowledge is a complex task as it depends on employee willingness to share and contribute individual knowledge (Bock et al., 2005) given the tendency to hoard the knowledge for preservation of value within an organization (Gilmour, 2003; Bock et al., 2005). The reluctance of employees to share knowledge has the potential to hinder team collaboration and rresult in missed objectives (Van den Bosch et al., 1999). Virtual teams could amplify this problem due to their complex characteristics and the remote location of its members.

Virtual teams have an impact on organizational structure (Jarvenpaa et al., 1998; Alavi and Leidner, 2001; Berry, 2011) and bring unique challenges (Powell et al., 2004). For example, virtual team members are distributed among different geographical locations and any communication and collaboration most be mediated by technology. While IT can improve efficciency, many challenges remain including time difficulties, feedback delays, misinterpretation, cultural barriers, scheduling conflicts, lack of communication, and delayed responses (Fussell et al., 1998; Jarvenpaa and Leidner, 1998; Alavi and Tiwana, 2002; Powell et al., 2004). These challenges, if not addressed and managed appropriately, can affect the performance of virtual teams (Piccoli et al., 2004).

One such challenge is the building of trust among virtual team members. Trust is a crucial factor in forming and maintaining social relationships and is key for cooperative relationships and effective teamwork (Jarvenpaa et al., 1998; Zaheer et al., 1998; Jarvenpaa et al., 2004; Powell et al., 2004; Brahm and Kunze, 2012). Trust includes elements of risk, vulnerability, and uncertainty which team members must overcome in order to work collaboratively (Mayer et al., 1995). In co-located organizational settings, research shows that trust has many benefits such as increasing team productivity, facilitating resolution of conflicts and disagreements, and improving effectiveness (Earley, 1986; Hagen and Choe, 1998; Zaheer et al., 1998). As organizations become more distributed, concerns about how to build trust among team members has grown (Kirkman et al., 2002). The absence of observable behaviors, which members of traditional face-to-face teams rely upon to establish and maintain trust, makes building trust among virtual team members a complicated issue, increasing performance uncertainty (Riegelsberger et al., 2003; Ferreira et al., 2012).

Research needs to investigate virtual team specific factors, behaviors, and standards that will allow team members to compensate for the lack of physically observed behaviors in the establishment of trust. Kanawattanachai and Yoo, (2002) argue that virtual team members rely more on cognition-based trust than on affect-based trust, basing their trust decision on perceptions of evidence of trustworthiness not on genuine caring and emotion. We propose that knowledge sharing is a significant behavior which virtual team members observe, measure, and rely upon to build cognition-based trust.

## Research Model and Hypotheses Development

Organizations are distributed knowledge systems and the ability of the organization to identify knowledge resources, leverage them, and make them available for its employees can lead to a distinctive competitive advantage (Tsoukas, 1996; Davenport and Prusak, 1998; Alavi and Tiwana, 2002). As illustrated in our theoretical model (Figure 1), we propose that knowledge sharing positively affects trust, collaboration and team effectiveness in virtual team settings. In addition, trust moderates the relationship between collaboration and team effectiveness, with higher levels of trust positively influencing the impact of collaboration on team effectiveness.

The Knowledge Based Theory of The Firm states that a firm is a knowledge creating entity (Nonaka, 1994; Nonaka et al., 2000). Grant (1996) emphasizes the role of the individual within the organization in creating knowledge and argues that the role of the organization is to integrate, store, and apply the knowledge created by the individuals. Organizations assemble virtual teams of knowledgeable and skilled individuals who are expected to utilize their knowledge to perform organizational tasks. Teams perform better when they contain members with knowledge relevant to the task at hand (Malhotra and Majchrzak 2004; Gardner 2012; Pangil and Chan, 2014). As

#

with traditional teams, virtual teams perform better and produce more effective outcomes when they are able to locate, access, and apply organizational knowledge (Civi, 2000; Gardner et al., 2012). In a virtual setting, the knowledge must be exchanged and shared amongst team members through the use of connecting information technologies given location diffferences.

This knowledge exchange can be viewed through the lens of Social Exchange Theory where human behavior is seen as taking place in a social exchange (Blau, 1964). Individuals within a social system exchange favors with a general expectation of some future, but unclear, return. Long-term relationships obtain where individuals have enough time to collaborate and exchange favors (Blau 1964; Molm et al., 2000). With these assumptions, knowledge sharing can be seen as a form of generalized social exchange, where individuals share their knowledge without any clear expectations of a return other than the promise of a long-term mutual relationship (Fulk et al., 1996).

Social Exchange Theory posits that people behave in ways that maximize their benefits and minimize their costs (Molm et al., 2000). The main cost of sharing knowledge, especially in a virtual team setting, is the loss of personal relative advantage from the release of unique knowledge. The main benefit of knowledge sharing is effective collaboration and integration of diverse resources to reach new insights. To motivate proper behavior, organizations are implementing reward systems to encourage team members to contribute and share knowledge while in some organizations, punishing employees if they refuse to share the knowledge they possess (Bartol and Srivastava, 2002).

To collaborate effectively, virtual teams require distributed knowledge to be adequately shared and integrated by team members (Xiao and Jin, 2010; Pinjani and Palvia, 2013; Pangil and Chan, 2014). Otherwise, virtual teams will be less efficient, suffering higher costs associated with infformation search, communication failure, information misunderstanding and misinterpretation, and inadequate decision making due to missing data (Gray, 2001; Pinjani and Palvia, 2013). Knowledge sharing by member experts enables effective team outcomes by ensurring all pieces of an information puzzle are available for task performance and appropriate decision making. This allows the team, regardless of location, to accomplish its work requirements and contibute to an organization's goals. Thus, we hypothesize:

## H1: In virtual team settings, knowledge sharing has a positive influence on collaboration.

Virtual team characteristics influence the way team members work together and have the potential to hinder team success and effectiveness. For example, without the benefit of face-toface interaction, virtual teams tend to take longer to reach common ground and to collaborate effectively (Holton, 2001; Potter and Balthazard, 2002; Kirkman et al., 2004; DeOrtentiis et al., 2014; Pangil and Chan, 2014).

However, virtual teams are able to overcome such challenges. As suggested by Interdependence theory, individuals within a team will naturally work collaboratively in order to achieve a

#

common goal, ultimately affecting team effectiveness (Kelley and Thibaut, 1978; DeOrtentiis et al., 2014). For example, virtual team members tend to express their opinions more freely and openly regardless of any social or managerial constrains (Duarte and Snyder, 1999). Consequently, virtual team members are able to assess each other more accurately based on performance and contribution. Virtual team members also show less bias compared to those in traditional teams when evaluating each other’s performance and contribution (Weisband and Atwater, 1999).

The result is a collaborative team effort where, despite having to overcome challenges associated with disparate locations, virtual teams exchange more ideas, share more knowledge, and effectively coordinate tasks among each other (Berry, 2011; Mathieu et al., 2008). This tighter integration, enabled by modern technologies, affects team outcomes. We thus hypothesize:

## H2: In virtual teams, collaboration among team members has a positive influence on team effectiveness.

The type of trust which develops in virtual settings is cognitive-based trust (Kanawattanachai and Yoo, 2002). This is because: 1) information technologies are not successful in transferring feelings and emotions which affect based trust depends upon; and 2) trust decisions are often built based on team members ability, integrity, and benevolence which, requires virtual team members to provide solid evidence of their trustworthiness before other team members will trust them (Mayer et al., 1995; Kanawattanachai and Yoo, 2002).

Forming trust in new virtual teams is difficult as members may lack a history of working together (Jarvenpaa et al., 1998). Especially as virtual teams are located in separate sites, lack of face-to-face interaction, work in different time zones, etc., reducing key observable cues available to traditional teams in the evaluation of team member trust (Kanawattanachai and Yoo, 2002; Pinjani and Palvia, 2013). With the lack of physical interaction which takes place in collocated team settings, virtual team members need to demonstrate different and unique behaviors to their teammates in order to prove their benevolence and integrity. Sharing knowledge is one such behavior. By sharing the knowledge they possess virtual team members demonstrate their willingness to act with the group in mind, that they are dependable, and can be relied upon to contribute to the collective task. Knowledge sharing provides evidence of benevolence and trustworthiness of team members. Thus, we hypothesize:

## H3: In virtual team settings, knowledge sharing has a positive influence on trust among team members.

A virtual team is a social system of individuals who are expected to collaborate on a common organizational task, where the act of one team member affects all others on the team (Hoegl and Gemuenden, 2001). Social Capital Theory explains how changes in relations among individuals in a social system facilitate action, coordination, collaboration, and resource exchange (Coleman,

1988, Adler and Kwon, 2002; Chiu et al., 2006). In the social capital process, as Coleman describes,

“If A does something for B and trusts B to reciprocate in the future, this establishes an expectation in A and obligation on the part of B. This obligation can be conceived as a credit slip held by A for performance by B…Unless the placement of trust has been unwise and these are bad debts that will not be repaid” (Coleman, 1988).

Coleman’s (1988) argument suggests that in order to build social capital in social systems, two components are necessary: action and trust among the individuals. For each action that takes place, a trust decision is made, whether the action confirms that trust is warranted or suggests that trust is misplaced. In social systems like teams, trust is reported as a key factor in reducing risk, complexity, and uncertainty, enabling a positive atmosphere of collaboration among individuals within the system (Kollock, 1994; Jarvenpaa et al., 1998; Baba, 1999; Paul and McDaniel, 2004).

The role of trust in an organizational setting can be modeled in two ways (Dirks and Ferrin, 2001). The most dominant model depicts trust as having a direct effect on team outcomes (Paul and McDaniel, 2004; Peters and Manz, 2007; Muethel et al., 2012; Pangil and Chan, 2014), with the second depicting trust as having a moderating effect on team outcomes (Jarvenpaa et al., 2004; Brahm and Kunze, 2012). Conducting a comprehensive review of research utilizing each trust model, Dirks and Ferrin (2001) find research utilizing model 1 (trust as a main effect) has inconsistent, non-robust results, especially when trust is hypothesized to have positive effects on workplace behaviors and performance outcomes (Zaheer et al., 1997; Dirks, 1999). On the other hand, research adopting model 2 (trust as a moderator) demonstrates that trust provides a necessary condition for better team outcomes and reveals more consistent findings (Hwang and Burgers, 1997; Dirks and Ferrin, 2001). As highlighted in Figure 1, we adopt model 2 propose that trust as a moderator of the collaboration-effectiveness relationship.

Team collaboration is the backbone which supports and drives team success and effectiveness. However, the ability to collaborate depends heavily upon trust to facilitate sharing of information and knowledge across the team (Scott, 2000; Paul and McDaniel, 2004; Koehne et al., 2012). Without the appropriate information and know how, individuals and teams cannot make effective decisions. Moreover, without trust, members may exert a less than optimal collaborative effort towards the team outcome. A team will still produce output, however when trust levels among members are low, virtual team production may be minimized (Aubert and Kelsey 2003; Jarvenpaa et al., 2004; Pangil and Chan, 2014). Thus, we hypothesize:

H4: (a) In high trust contexts, there will be a positive association between collaboration and team effectiveness. (b) In low trust contexts, the association between collaboration and team effectiveness will be significantly less strong.

Methodology

#

In this quantitative, positivist research, we test our proposed research model analyzing primary survey data. To avoid sampling bias, we focused on a specific industry, administering a questionnaire to virtual team members who work in an organization setting, in the information technology industry (e.g. software engineers, software developers, etc.). To acquire a representative sample, the sample frame in this study was acquired from LinkedIn.com, a leading professional social network.

The data was collected in two phases. In the first phase, we identified individuals and groups who best represented our population of interest, targeting those profiles containing a major IT firm’s name (e.g. Microsoft, Google, IBM … etc.). This is possible as in LinkedIn, members post detailed resumes which include education, past experience, and current employment information. LinkedIn also provides suggestions of other people who work for the same company, affiliated companies, and similar companies. These features enabled us to target appropriate subjects for our study. Emails were then sent to both group administrators and directly to group members. A brief description of the research was given along with a link to access the survey online. Over 1,000 invitations to participate were sent. A total of 193 subjects completed the survey, representing an approximate 19.3% response rate. However, as group administrators were encouraged to send the invitation on their team members, a true response rate could not be calculated.

The survey items were derived from previously published research. The variables of interest are knowledge sharing, trust, collaboration, and team effectiveness. The measures for knowledge sharing is adopted from Phang et al. (2009). The measures for trust were derived from Mayer et al. (1995), a widely cited and reference research project on the topic (e.g. Jarvenpaa and Leidner 1998, Jarvenpaa et al. 1999, Dirks and Ferrin 2001). Measures for collaboration were derived from Aram and Morgan (1976), while measures for team effectiveness were derived from Lurey and Raisinghani (2001). The latter measures virtual team effectiveness in terms of team performance and team member’s satisfaction. Our survey questions are shown in Appendix A.

## Demographics and Descriptive Statistics

Respondents were asked to indicate their gender and age, and to answer a number of questions concerning factors that potentially could impact team effectiveness. These factors include how long an individual has (1) worked in virtual team settings, (2) been members of the same virtual team, (3) participated in pure virtual environment or in both virtual and face-to-face environments, (4) been members of global virtual teams, (5) been a virtual team leader, (6) worked for the same organization, and (7) if sharing knowledge is mandatory or voluntarily (Table 1). A Multi-group analysis is conducted to verify if any of these factors have an effect.

Internal consistency is evaluated by a construct’s composite reliability score (Table 3) which shows adequate reliability scores for all constructs.

The analysis shows that all items load higher on their respective construct than the other constructs in the research model. Likewise, the square root of the AVE in the diagonal for each construct is higher than the inter-construct correlations on the same row and the same column as illustrated in Table 4. ## Post-hoc Analysis

The structural model analysis results suggest that trust does not have a significant moderating impact on the relationship between collaboration and team effectiveness (H4 rejected). This is in contrast to prior literature which highlights trust as having a moderating effect (e.g. Dirks and Ferrin, 2001). To examine the role of trust further, we conducted a series of post hoc analyses that examined the different ways trust might influence overall team effectiveness. We conducted three different PLS analyses on adjusted models; in the first analysis we tested for a direct effect of trust on collaboration (figure 4), in the second analysis we tested for a direct effect of trust on virtual team effectiveness (figure 5), and in the third analysis we tested for a direct effect of trust on both collaboration and team effectiveness at the same time (figure 6).

The results of these analyses suggest that a statistically significant direct effect of trust on collaboration, as seen in both figures 4 and 6. However, the relationship between trust and team effectiveness is not statistically significant in either analysis that examined the direct impact of trust on team effectiveness, see figures 5 and 6.

## Discussion

Our results have significant implications for both research and practice. For research, it provides new insights regarding the role of knowledge sharing in virtual teams. We also identify the need for future research to better understand the impact of trust on virtual teams’ outcomes. For practice, this research emphasizes the significance of knowledge sharing to virtual team effectiveness, and recommends that organizations support knowledge sharing in virtual settings on both technological and social levels.

This research investigated the relationship between knowledge sharing and trust, and how this relationship ultimately affects virtual team effectiveness. The researchers utilized the Knowledge-Based Theory of the Firm and Social Exchange Theory to frame hypotheses. Results

#

provide support for the theoretical model and qualified support for most all but one hypothesized relationship. The results suggest that knowledge sharing has a significant influence on both trust and collaboration in virtual team settings. This provides support for hypotheses H1 and H3. Knowledge sharing in virtual team settings could be a significant element in establishing social capital and social exchange among virtual team members. Consistent with the Knowledge-Based Theory of the Firm and Nonaka’s (1995) concept of “ba”, a virtual team is considered a place where members share knowledge and transform their environment to reach new insights. Furthermore, this research extends the literature on virtual teams to argue that sharing knowledge is crucial for virtual team members to collaborate, trust each other, and to be effective.

The results illustrate that the relationship between collaboration and team effectiveness is significant, supporting hypothesis H2. The Sobel test of mediation indicates that collaboration mediates the relationship between knowledge sharing and team effectiveness. This provides additional support for the significance of knowledge sharing to the effectiveness of virtual teams.

Contrary to our expectations, hypothesis H4 was not supported, indicating that trust does not have a significant moderating impact on the relationship between collaboration and team effectiveness. This is consistent with contradictory and inconclusive results of related research (Dirks and Ferrin, 2001; DeOrtentiis et al., 2013). Aubert and Kelsey (2003) find that the level of trust among virtual team members does not have a significant direct impact on team performance. This is consistent with our results and suggests that the impact of trust may have a limited direct influence on virtual team effectiveness. However, the post-hoc analysis suggests that trust does have a direct effect on collaboration which in turn impacts virtual team effectiveness. Jarvenpaa et al. (2004) argue that trust effects are sensitive to the context of the virtual team, which might suggest that our results could be limited to the population of our study. Nevertheless, trust remains a complex phenomenon and the characteristics of virtual teams add to this complexity.

Peters and Manz (2007) argue that building trust among virtual team members depends on two factors, the first factor is personality-based trust and the second factor is institution-based trust. The latter allows team members to trust each other’s behavior based on the norms and rules of the organization. The findings of Pangil and Chan (2014) suggest that institution-based trust affects virtual team effectiveness. Institution-based trust was the center of discussions the researchers had with several virtual team members who responded to the study’s survey, in order to understand why hypothesis H4 was not supported. Those respondents argued that trust among virtual team members is not built based on personal factors; mostly virtual team members trust each other’s capabilities and competencies to perform the organizational task at-hand. A virtual team member for over 10 years stated that in his organization, a team leader uses a system which matches employees’ profiles to the objectives of the project. The leader then interviews candidates to decide whom should be assigned. These profiles are made available for team members before they begin working together, which means that team members establish institution-based trust at a very early stage of the project life. As a whole, future research is

#

needed to investigate the impact of trust on virtual teams’ collaboration and effectiveness in order to reach conclusive findings.

The multi-group moderation effect results were negative for all items except for global virtual team members’ perception of trust. This result may indicate that trust is higher among virtual team members who have similar characteristics (i.e. culture, language). The impact of culture on trust in virtual teams has been examined in the literature given that team members of different cultures are likely to join the same virtual team (Zakaria et al., 2004; Henttonen and Blomqvist 2005; Verburg et al., 2013). Culture is fundamental in interpreting communication and behavior (Zakaria et al., 2004), which explains why having members of different cultures in the same virtual team could affect the level of trust among them. Future studies are needed to investigate ways to enhance trust among global virtual team members and how mechanisms to enhance trust may differ for global teams when compared to teams formed from a single country.

## Limitations

Our research investigates knowledge sharing in teams independent of technology and task. This is a potential limitation as the type of technology used may affect the formation of trust and the knowledge shared by team members. For example, knowledge management systems (KMS) used by organizations can vary from simple blogs and discussion boards, to more sophisticated software applications designed especially for organizational knowledge needs. KMS related research suggests that these systems can indeed influence the quality and quantity of knowledge shared (Alavi and Leidner, 2001; Kankanhalli et al., 2005). In addition, research has shown there is a relationship between task complexity and the type of technology needed to facilitate virtual team collaboration; higher task complexity requires more synchronous, tightly coupled interaction than low task complexity (Riopelle et. al., 2003). Future research could include an examination of different types of technologies used by teams, like KMSs, as well as the complexity of virtual team tasks to better understand the interplay between task-technology fit and the social aspect of sharing knowledge in virtual teams.

Another limitation of our research concerns the measure used for virtual team collaboration. It is possible that the measure, originally designed for co-located team settings (Aram and Morgan 1976), may affect the point estimates of our results. It is possible that the nuances of online collaboration may lead to differences in team collaboration than what takes place in face-to-face settings. For example, prior research has found that there is a need for technical training to empower team members to function in virtual environments, in addition to traditional training on developing good interpersonal skills (Kirkman et. al., 2002). Future research could develop and use measures specifically designed for virtual team settings to provide additional insights into the balance and influence of technical and interpersonal skills on virtual team collaboration.

Finally, our study utilized virtual team members who worked in the information technology industry as the source of our data measures. As technical skills have been found to be an

#

important factor for virtual team success (e.g. Kirkman et. al., 2002), future research should examine data samples from virtual team members in non-technical industries in order to improve the generalizability of our results. It is possible that different backgrounds may affect the estimated collaborative relationships.

## Conclusion

Organizations are increasingly using virtual teams to take advantage of employee skills and knowledge regardless of geographical location. Yet, little is known about how sharing knowledge impacts the relationship between virtual team members, their collaboration, and team effectiveness. This research proposed a conceptual model which hypothesized a relationship between knowledge sharing, trust, collaboration, and team effectiveness in virtual team settings. The theoretical model was developed, based on the Knowledge Based Theory of The Firm, Social Capital Theory, and the Social Exchange Theory. PLS analysis results support three hypotheses, indicating that knowledge sharing influences the formation of trust within virtual teams and contributes to virtual team effectiveness. The study finds the relationship between knowledge sharing and team effectiveness is mediated by collaboration.

The study raises important questions for future research. First, it is unclear why trust does not seem to directly influence team effectiveness. Instead the results of the post hoc analysis suggest that trust has a direct impact on collaboration and it is that collaboration that impacts team effectiveness. Prior research has suggested that the impact of trust in virtual teams is sensitive to the virtual team setting. Additional research is necessary to determine if the importance of trust on team effectiveness is higher in other virtual team environments (e.g. for online communities of practice outside the organization). Future research should also examine the impact of different team building strategies on trust, collaboration and team effectiveness, especially in global virtual team contexts.
