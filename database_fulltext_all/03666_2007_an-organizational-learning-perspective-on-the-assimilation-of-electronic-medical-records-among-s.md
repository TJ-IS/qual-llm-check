# An organizational learning perspective on the assimilation of electronic medical records among small physician practices

## Abstract

Small physician practices play an essential role in the healthcare delivery system but are least likely to adopt health information technologies such as electronic medical records (EMRs). Factors contributing to low adoption include investment cost, productivity loss, and lack of financial incentives. However, these factors do not explain why some small practices, which face similar challenges nonetheless assimilate EMRs, while others do not. We investigated the assimilation of EMRs from the theoretic perspective of organizational learning in a survey of small physician practices and evaluated whether characteristics associated with organizational learning barriers are related to EMR assimilation. We found that learning-related scale, related knowledge, and diversity were positively associated with small physician practices’ stage of assimilation of EMR technology. Our findings suggest that some small practices are able to overcome the substantial learning barriers presented by EMRs but that others will require support to develop sufficient learning capacity. We consider implications for practice from this research and areas requiring further research. European Journal of Information Systems (2007) 16, 681–694.

## Introduction

High costs, medical errors, variable quality, administrative inefficiencies, and poor coordination are often cited as major problems within the U.S. healthcare system (Kohn et al., 1999; Porter & Teisberg, 2004; WHEP, 2004). Various healthcare constituencies have detailed how IT might be used to address these problems (Institute of Medicine, 2001; Loomis, 2002). In particular, the U.S. Government has outlined a plan that seeks to ensure that most Americans have electronic health records by the year 2014 (WHSOU, 2004, 2005, 2006). The feasibility of achieving this ambitious goal is questionable (Ford et al., 2006; Ferris, 2007; McGee, 2007). Despite high expectations for the value of IT in healthcare, healthcare information technology (HIT) use in the U.S. is low in the number of adopters as well as in the extent of actual use (Johnston et al., 2002; Blumenthal et al., 2006).

In this research, we are concerned with one type of HIT and one type of healthcare setting, that is, electronic medical records (EMRs)<sup>1</sup> use among small physician practices. Whereas recent estimates of EMR adoption indicate that the actual number of adoptions has increased in recent years, this increase still represents less than 25% of physician practices in the U.S. (see Table 1). Moreover, physicians in small practices are least likely to use any IT in clinical practices (Menachemi & Brooks, 2006; Reed & Grossman, 2006). The highest levels of IT use for patient care are found in staff- and group-model health maintenance organization (HMO) practices, followed by medical school faculty and large group practices. Although overall rates of IT adoption have increased across all groups, the gap between EMR use among small practices and large healthcare organizations remains and may be increasing (Grossman & Reed, 2006). Given that half of all U.S. physicians in 2004–2005 worked in solo and two physician practices (32%) or small groups of three to nine physicians (19%), it appears that many physicians may be left behind, even as EMR adoption accelerates, impeding national goals for pervasive use of electronic health records (Ford et al., 2006), and contributing to disparities in the quality of care among patients of small practices (Grossman & Reed, 2006).

A number of factors contribute to low levels of EMR adoption: cost of EMR software and hardware, lost physician productivity and hence revenue during lengthy implementation projects, and lack of financial incentives for physicians to adopt (Miller & Sim 2004; Valdes et al., 2004; Gans et al., 2005; Ford et al., 2006). These factors help explain the low level of EMR adoption overall, but, because small physician practices face similar financial and environmental challenges, they do not explain why some small practices have adopted EMR technology while most have not. A better understanding of these differences may help us to understand organizational barriers to EMR assimilation among small physician groups and help policy makers design effective interventions to assist these practices to adopt and use EMRs.

In this research, we investigated possible explanations using the theoretic perspective of organizational learning barriers to diffusion of complex organizational technologies (Attewell, 1992; Fichman & Kemerer, 1997). We considered whether small physician practices differ in terms of organizational learning barriers and whether differences help explain variations in EMR assimilation among these micro-sized organizations. We found that learning-related scale, related knowledge, and diversity of knowledge are useful indicators to predict the likelihood that a practice will initiate and sustain assimilation of an EMR. These findings suggest implications for policies and practices to promote EMR adoption and extend the theoretic research space of IS theory on innovation diffusion to EMR technology and small healthcare organizational settings (Berthon et al., 2002).

In the next section, we outline the theoretic foundations for our study in the organizational learning and innovation literature and its applicability to EMR assimilation. We then describe our research design and methods. We present the findings and analysis of a survey of small physician practices in the U.S. State of Hawaii in which we assessed our theoretic model. We conclude with a discussion of the implications for practice and future research.

## Theoretic foundations

Surveys of physicians in the U.S. illustrate what might be termed an EMR adoption gap between small physician practices and large-group or institutionally based practices. Table 1 summarizes key statistics from recent studies. Some surveys found that variation in adoption rates was, in small measure, related to medical specialty, geographic region, practice location, physician age or gender, whereas other studies did not. However, practice size consistently emerged as the clearest differentiator between adopters and non-adopters. This gap in adoption rates is not surprising, because large organizations are in general more likely to adopt innovations (Rogers, 1995). It is, however, problematic, given the importance of small physician practices in the U.S. healthcare system (Burt et al., 2007) and policy makers’ goals for HIT use across healthcare venues (WHSOU, 2004).

Surveys of U.S. physicians have also identified barriers to the adoption of EMR technology among U.S. physician practices, notably the up-front investment costs in software, hardware and training, loss of productivity during system implementation, lack of financial incentives for practices to invest in EMRs, immaturity in the EMR software market, and insufficient support services for practices implementing EMRs (Gans et al., 2005; Ford et al., 2006; Simon et al., 2007). Financial incentives to adopt EMRs and lower capital investment costs will require changes in government or industry policy to address these issues. Overcoming financial barriers is a necessary step, but it is insufficient to facilitate widespread EMR adoption, because organizational factors such as lack of expertise and access to support resources also present substantial barriers (Simon et al., 2007, p. 115). In particular, Gans et al. (2005, p. 1331) noted, ‘the transition from computer-based administrative information systems to fully implemented EHRs is a major undertaking that creates dislocation among the clinical staff and is more complicated, more difficult, and more expensive than we or most practices expected. The majority of practices are finding the transition difficult even if the physicians and nurses are fully supportive.’

A better understanding of how organizational characteristics influence EMR adoption is essential to develop policies and programs to help alleviate these barriers. That is, descriptive studies are useful to assess overall adoption rates and to highlight the types of physician organizations that need special attention, but they provide limited explanations for why the EMR adoption gap developed and persists, and they lack predictive analysis.<sup>2</sup> To-date, organizational studies of HIT generally have examined large organizations such as hospitals (Ash et al., 2002; Doolan & Bates, 2002; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005; Garets & Davis, 2006; Davidson & Chismar, forthcoming) or perceptions and use of HIT at the individual level (Blumenthal et al., 2006; Dykes, 2006). Little theoretically motivated research has addressed small physician practices at the organizational level of analysis (Davidson & Chiasson, 2005).

To investigate organizational factors influencing the assimilation of EMR technology, we viewed EMR systems as a type of complex organizational technology. Citing work by Attewell (1992), Fichman & Kemerer (1997, p. 1346) define complex organizational technologies as ‘technologies, that, when first introduced, impose a substantial burden on would-be adopters in terms of the knowledge needed to use them effectively.’ Reports of EMR implementation (Miller et al., 2003; Gans et al., 2005) demonstrate that EMR systems have a profound effect on organizational workflow and practices, and members of a physician practice experience a steep learning curve for effective use. Attewell (1992) posited that the organizational know-how and technical knowledge required to utilize a complex organizational technology creates knowledge barriers to adoption; and, diffusion may be delayed until institutional resources such as consulting firms or user groups develop to help organizations overcome such barriers. In an empirical study of the diffusion of business computing in the United States, Attewell (1992) re-conceptualized the diffusion of technology in terms of organizational learning, skill development, and knowledge barriers.

Drawing on Attewell (1992), Fichman & Kemerer (1997) investigated the influence of organizational learning barriers in the assimilation of complex organizational technologies. They posited that differences in organizational learning barriers could help predict which organizations have a greater propensity to initiate and sustain the assimilation of complex organizational technology, in their case, software process innovations. They hypothesized that organizations that have a greater scale of activities over which learning costs can be spread (learning-related scale), more extensive existing knowledge related to the focal innovation (related knowledge), and a greater diversity of technical knowledge and activities (diversity) are more likely to assimilate complex organizational technologies than organizations lacking such organizational attributes. They demonstrated these relationships in an empirical study of object-oriented programming languages (OOPL).

Fichman & Kemerer (1997) also found that large organizations had greater learning-related scale, related knowledge, and diversity, and thus were more likely to assimilate this complex organizational technology. Whether small organizations have sufficient learning capacity to similarly overcome learning barriers is of practical and theoretic import. Davidson & Chiasson (2005) suggested that larger healthcare organizations are more likely to have staff to dedicate to tasks such as software selection, customization, and ongoing training and adjustment, whereas small clinical organizations generally do not. That is, micro-sized organizations likely face substantial organizational learning barriers related to EMRs.

To investigate this issue, we drew on Fichman & Kemerer’s (1997) model to assess the assimilation of EMRs among small independent physician practices. Figure 1 depicts the research model that guided our study. Fichman & Kemerer (1997) suggested focusing on a narrow range of technologies to produce predictive models; they thus defined and operationalized learning constructs in terms of the specific technology studied (OOPL). Our challenge was to determine how to apply the concepts of learning-related scale, related knowledge, and diversity to EMRs and small physician practices. This required a conceptual translation of the theoretic concept to a different technology and different organizational setting. We next outline this conceptual translation and summarize the operational definitions in Table 2.

Assimilation stage reflects an organization’s earliness of initiation and speed of assimilation activities as well as absence of rejection, stalling, or discontinuance of an innovation (Meyers & Goes, 1988). Researchers have used a variety of categorical definitions to describe the linear progression through stages from general awareness to full implementation of an innovation.<sup>3</sup> We modeled our assessment of assimilation stage following Fichman & Kemerer (1997), with modifications to better reflect the target population (small physician practices). For example, physician practices tend to vacillate in their decision to proceed with adoption when they are actively involved in software evaluation (Davidson & Heslinga, 2007), suggesting that this stage may be iterative rather than a simple linear progression. Our operational definition of assimilation stage thus categorizes a practice’s movement towards adoption and use of an EMR as follows: awareness, that is, having little or limited knowledge of EMRs or interest in pursing adoption; interest in evaluating an EMR in the near future; pursuing adoption, by evaluating vendors, viewing demonstrations, or piloting one or more systems; limited deployment involving some training, limited features in use, and limited implementation throughout the practice; and, full deployment of many EMR capabilities throughout the practice.

Learning-related scale is the scale of activities over which learning costs may be spread (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997, p. 1350). Scale is important because it may motivate an organization to make the investments necessary to adopt an innovation, since learning costs can be spread over many activities. Analogously, we posited the ‘installed base’ for a physician practice is the patient panel (assigned or regular patients) and their associated medical records, and that learning related scale is associated with growth in this base.<sup>4</sup> For example, if a practice’s patient panel increases, the number of new medical records and potentially administrative staff would likewise increase, indicating greater learning-related scale. Generally, we posited that practices that are growing in numbers of patients, participating physicians, number of office locations, and so on have greater opportunities to spread learning-related costs across the operation. At one extreme, a new practice has no ‘installed base’ and could apply an EMR in all activities. At the other extreme, a long-established but declining practice would have less motivation to invest in an EMR system. We considered both growth, stability, or decline in the size of the practice (in terms of change in patient panel size) and the number of factors contributing to changes in the patient panel, such as changes in the number of practice locations, physicians, specializations, or certifications, participation with healthcare provider networks, chronic care management programs and thirdparty insurance programs, and patient referrals to the practice.

Physician practices routinely provide patient data to and receive data from other healthcare organizations. We posited that physician practices that have a greater need to process and exchange clinical patient data might also have greater learning related scale, since EMRs are used to collect and recall patients’ clinical data. For example, physicians who maintain hospital admitting privileges are more likely to exchange patient data with the hospital than are physicians who do not admit patients. Thus, we considered that learning-related scale might also increase with the number of different activities that a practice engages in, which require processing and exchange of clinical patient data.

To summarize, physician practices that possess greater learning-related scale should, ceritus peribus, be more likely to initiate and sustain assimilation of EMR technology due to a more timely and assured return on the investment in the learning associated with the innovation. This leads to our first hypothesis:

## H1: Learning-related scale is positively related to assimilation stage of EMRs by small physician practices.

Related knowledge is defined as the extent of abstract knowledge, know-how, and skills possessed by an organization in areas related to the focal innovation (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997, p. 1351). Related knowledge helps an organization to obtain new knowledge or decreases the total quantity of knowledge required to utilize an innovation; it includes abstract principles and skills related to the innovation, expertise in specific technologies embedded in the innovation, and knowledge of technologies that cluster round the innovation (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997, p. 1351). Fichman & Kemerer (1997) construed related knowledge in terms of an organization’s experience with various programming languages associated with OOLP. We posited that the small physician practice’s experience with and use of computerized practice management (PMS) applications represent a critical area of knowledge related to EMRs. A PMS may include patient appointment and referral authorization functions; patient demographic and insurance information; and encounter coding and billing systems. Practices that use computerized PMS extensively should have more knowledge related to EMRs, including general computer literacy, familiarity with computerized patient records, and understanding of how to maintain the operation of a computerized system (such as the need for system backup) than practices that use paper-based or mixed paper and computerized PMS. Moreover, some functions of practice management and EMRs overlap, such as maintaining the patient demographic information and coding patient encounters for billing purposes, further contributing to related knowledge. This leads to our second hypothesis as follows:

## H2: Related knowledge is positively related to assimilation stage of EMRs by small physician practices.

Diversity is defined as the degree of heterogeneity of organizational knowledge and activities in areas related to the focal innovation (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997, p. 1352). Diverse knowledge is important in overcoming learning barriers because organization members may find unique ways to overcome problems with the targeted innovation in the diverse knowledge base. In terms of OOLP, Fichman & Kemerer (1997) assessed the variety of programming languages, runtime platforms, and application platforms used in the organization. We posited that the variety of information technology applications and hardware as well as network infrastructure used in the practice provides a base of diverse IT knowledge, which would be useful when evaluating, adopting, or using an EMR system. To assess diverse knowledge, we assessed the following: (1) the practice’s use of a variety of communications and software technologies; and, (2) IT infrastructure maintained in the office (see Table 2). Unlike the IT organizations Fichman & Kemerer (1997) studied, physician practices may outsource IT support services. Nonetheless, staff in practices that use a diverse range of technologies must possess sufficient IT knowledge to contract with service providers, initially diagnose operational problems, and communicate with service providers about problems and future requirements. When implementing an EMR, a small practice typically finds its IT support requirements greatly increase (Miller et al., 2003). Thus, we posited that having a reliable IT support network, whether in-house, outsourced, or mixed, would provide a diverse knowledge base to facilitate EMR adoption and implementation. This leads to our third hypothesis:

H3: Diversity is positively related to assimilation stage of EMRs by small physician practices.

## Research methodology

To test our research model, we conducted a mailed survey following procedures outlined in Dillman (2000). Our process included an initial invitation letter, signed by the physician chair of the quality improvement committee; the first round of mailings a week later; a reminder post card in 2 weeks; and, a second round of surveys mailed after 2 weeks. We utilized the mailing list for the State of Hawaii’s largest independent physicians association (IPA). The association leadership’s endorsement and sponsorship of the survey was critical to assure a good response rate, as physicians are typically non-responsive to surveys (Olson et al., 1993). The population we sampled is not statistically representative of physicians practicing in Hawaii or elsewhere, but its membership is typical of physician practices we wanted to focus on: small practices operating in community settings, rather than hospitals, HMOs, or institutional settings, with the majority of members in primary care practices but also including a variety of specialists. The IPA physician members practice in urban, suburban, and rural settings.

Our unit of analysis was the practice organization, which included one or more physicians and staff members such as nurses, medical assistants, office manager, biller, or other office administrative staff. The 780 individual physician members of the IPA were grouped into 567 practices. To determine which physicians practice together as an organization we utilized information in the practice association database (the same address and phone number) and online sources of licensing information (such as http://www.upinregistry. com) and calls to office staff to verify practice arrangements.

Table 3 summarizes response rate information for the survey. Among responders to the survey, 68% of practices were solo practices, 12% were practices with two physicians, and cumulatively, 90% of practices were three or fewer physicians. The most represented practice areas were Internal Medicine (20%), Pediatrics and OB/GYN (8% each), and Family Practice and Ophthalmology (7% each). Of responders, 54% reported practicing in an urban setting, 32% in suburban, and 16% in rural settings; some practices operate two or more offices in different locations. The IPA does not maintain this type of member data, so we were unable to compare responders to non-responders. We were comfortable, however, that the demographic profile of this group is not exceptional in ways that would influence the theoretic constructs of interest.

To develop operational measures of each construct and verify our measurement model, we applied successive stages of theoretical modeling, statistical testing, and refinement (Straub, 1989; Zhu et al., 2003). New measures for learning-related scale, related knowledge, and diversity relevant to our construct definitions (summarized in Table 2) were needed, as these organizational characteristics have not been measured previously in this setting. As others researchers who have surveyed physicians have done (Andrews et al., 2004; Simon et al., 2007), and following Fichman & Kemerer’s (1997) approach, we developed measurement items on the basis of a comprehensive literature review and interviews with subject matter experts including the executive director of the IPA, manager of a physician management services organization and former group practice manager, physicians, nurses, and other healthcare clinical and administrative personnel.<sup>5</sup> Each of these individuals completed the survey and helped us to refine overall content and

To validate the measurement of the variables representing learning-related scale, related knowledge, and diversity, we utilized confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Through an iterative process, discussed in the findings section, we refined the measurement model. Since our dependent variable is ordinal, we selected ordinal regression for analysis of the causal model in Figure 1. Ordinal regression allows for building models, generating predictions, and evaluating the importance of predictor variables. Questions related to the dependent variable of assimilation stage were organized into five groups for ordinal regression analysis consistent with the construct definition: (0) limited awareness; (1) interest; (2) actively pursuing; (3) limited deployment; and (4) full deployment. ## Findings and analysis

We verified the measurement model for the independent variables using CFA (Comrey & Lee, 1992). The literature section and Table 2 outline our theoretic assumptions. Using communality estimates of one, we ran a principal axis factor analysis using SPSS (version 14) to determine the legitimacy of the underlying structure of the organizational learning constructs. An initial rotated factor matrix yielded factor scores above 0.400 for all items except for the variable practice change (activities). We reexamined the data for survey questions related to this item and our index measure (a count of activities requiring data handling) and concluded that the questions did not elicit reliable responses. Thus, we dropped this item and ran a second CFA analysis of the independent variables.

A potential threat to validity is reverse causation. Reverse causation implies that practices may have acquired knowledge needed to assimilate EMRs as a result of using EMRs. To assess this threat to validity a model was estimated that excluded practices already using EMRs (assimilation stages 3 and 4). The resulting CFA and ordinal regression on the reduced data set showed that both learning-related scale and related knowledge was not statistically significant in determining assimilation stage (Sig. ¼ 0.434 and 0.736, respectively). Thus, reverse causation cannot be ruled out with respect to learningrelated scale and related knowledge. One explanation is that some practices upgraded or acquired a PMS system in conjunction with the EMR system, and related knowledge from PMS use developed simultaneously with EMR adoption. Likewise, a practice may have determined a need to increase either staff or patient panel to accommodate the change in workload gleaned after adopting an EMR. Reverse causation was not an issue with diversity which was statistically significant at Sig. ¼ 0.000.

## Methodological limitations of this study

Dillman (2000) cites four general sources of potential error in survey research: measurement, sampling, coverage, and non-response. To reduce the potential for measurement error we obtained advice and feedback on survey questions from professionals in the field. However, the statistical failure of the variable practice change activities as a measure of learning-related scale in the CFA analysis appeared to be due to measurement error. All members of the sample frame (the IPA) were included in the survey, so that sampling error was not relevant. We addressed coverage errors by screening the physician association database to identify each individual physician with a unique practice. Non-response error occurs when a survey fails to collect complete information on all units in the selected sample. By using an accurate mailing list, pre-survey notification communications from the physician association’s leadership, and multiple mailings to non-responders we obtained an overall survey rate of 32% and an effective sample rate for this analysis of 23%. This difference was due to incomplete responses on some surveys; in written comments on incomplete surveys, respondents complained about its length and complexity. The response rate is reasonable for the target population (physicians)<sup>10</sup> and is of concern primarily if there are theoretically relevant differences between responders and non-responders. The rate of EMR adoption reported by the respondents (24%) suggests a slight bias towards EMR adopters, compared to recent national surveys of EMR adoption rates among small practices. Our research goal was to develop and test a theoretically informed model to help explain variations in EMR assimilation among small practices. For this purpose, achieving a sufficient number of responses with variation across the categories of theoretic interest is critical, and we had sufficient variety in the dependent variable to support the statistical analysis of the hypotheses (see Table 4).

Method bias is a concern in survey research such as this, because the independent and dependent variables are measured at the same time. Given that the target population (physician) is difficult to survey, a second survey to address method bias was not deemed feasible. There was no evidence as we cross-validated responses to questions, such as complementary questions about assimilation activities and stages, to indicate methods bias in our data.

## Discussion and implications

In this research, we investigated the assimilation of EMRs as a process improvement technology and variations in assimilation among small physician practices from the theoretic perspective of organizational learning and learning barriers (Attewell, 1992; Fichman & Kemerer, 1997). As predicted by our three hypotheses, the organizational learning constructs of learning-related scale, related knowledge, and diversity were positively associated with higher levels of assimilation of EMRs among the small physician organizations surveyed. Our findings suggest that physician practices that are growing and expanding (learning-related scale), using PMS systems (related knowledge) and using a variety of non-clinical IT in their offices (diversity of knowledge) may more readily overcome learning barriers related to assimilation of EMRs and are thus more likely to have adopted or to progress towards adoption of an EMR.

## Implications for practice and future research

The likelihood that learning barriers inhibit assimilation of EMRs among the population of small physician practices and thus contribute to the well-documented adoption gap between small-sized and larger healthcare delivery venues is of practical concern. Facilitating EMR use among small physician practices is a key to the development of electronic health records that span healthcare settings and the realization of expected benefit from HIT integration (Davidson & Heineke, 2007). Moreover, small practices are an important component of the healthcare delivery system in the U.S. (Ford et al., 2006). Understanding why some small physician practices are able to overcome learning barriers while others are not may help policy makers formulate more effective programs to facilitate and expedite EMR assimilation among this critical population.

Lowering investment costs and providing financial incentives is a critical step to promote EMR adoption. Our findings suggest that small physician practices may respond in different ways to financial incentive programs aimed at encouraging EMR adoption. These steps may be effective among small practices with sufficient organizational learning capacity to overcome the learning barriers that EMRs present. However, small practices in which organizational learning barriers are high may be unable or unwilling to respond to financial incentives. $\mathrm { O r } ,$ they may purchase an EMR but use it in a minimal way or even discontinue its use if learning barriers are insurmountable (Miller et al., 2003; Gans et $a l . ,$ 2005). For these organizations, taking incremental steps towards EMR adoption, for example, computerizing aspects of the practice management system that are paper-based, introducing communication IT such as e-mail, or using stand-alone clinical IT applications such as electronic prescribing may help the practice develop related and diverse knowledge essential for later EMR use.

Government policy makers, third-party payers, charitable organizations, and others interested in promoting EMR use should consider ways in which learning barriers might effectively be lowered through the development of shared community resources, such as IT support programs, EMR learning labs, and user groups. Vendor training in use of a particular EMR software package will not be sufficient to help the practice assimilate the innovation, in most cases. Instead, intervention programs aimed at assisting physician practices to overcome learning barriers to EMRs may be needed. Davidson & Heslinga (2007) describe one such program. Programs to develop community-based knowledge and resources could complement financial incentive programs and might increase their efficacy.

Our theoretic model suggests that some small physician practices might not be good candidates for EMR adoption, at least until community-wide support resources are developed and deployed to effectively lower learning barriers for most small practices. In this regard, mandating EMR use, as the U.S. Medicare system and several U.S. states have considered doing, may be counterproductive. Organizational learning barriers due to related knowledge and diversity might be lowered through a combination of financial incentives and support services. However, barriers related to learningrelated scale cannot be influenced in the same way. In particular, it may not make sense for practices that are declining in size and scope, either because the physician is nearing retirement or there are changes in market conditions, partnerships and so on, to make the financial and organizational commitment to EMRs. Moreover, although mandates may motivate practices that are stable (neither expanding nor contracting) to invest in EMRs, they may not be needed to motivate practices that are expanding. Thus, we suggest that policies mandating EMR use be approached with caution.

Although our study has, we believe, provided useful insights on organizational barriers to EMR assimilation, it has limitations that must be addressed through further research. Lee & Baskerville (2003) note that an empirical study may be generalized as theoretic insights, as we have done here, but cannot be generalized to other empirical settings without retesting. Of note here, we conducted our study in one U.S. state (Hawaii). In doing so, we held constant environmental and market factors that might influence assimilation of EMRs, such as market competition among small practices and between independent practices and HMOs, state-level regulation, policies of third-party insurers to promote EMR use, and proximity of EMR technical support resources and vendors. This design allowed us to narrow in on the theoretic constructs of interest and to detect their effect in a homogeneous context, but it does not inform us about the relative importance of institutional or market based influences on EMR assimilation patterns. Studies of the influence of organizational learning barriers in diverse settings, particularly where financial incentive programs have been implemented, would help determine the robustness of our findings.

Our measure of assimilation begins with awareness and ends with full deployment of the EMR. It does not, however, assess the ways in which the organization might change its practices to become more efficient or to improve the quality of care delivered to patients. Although reductions in healthcare costs and improvements in quality are generally assumed to follow from EMR adoption, there is little evidence that such benefits actually result. In fact, recent studies suggest there is little correlation between EMR use and the application of quality care standards in ambulatory care (Linder et al., 2007; Welsh et al., 2007). This finding is not surprising, as substantial changes in organizational practices and social institutions may be necessary to achieve substantial benefits from HIT use (Davidson & Chismar, forthcoming). Facilitating the availability and adoption of EMRs are only the first steps; organization-level and industrylevel changes in work and incentives focused on cost and quality improvements will be needed as adoption levels increase (Davidson & Heineke, 2007). Intensive field studies within practices that adopt EMRs, as well as community and industry-level research, will be needed to understand the variety of organizational, social and institutional barriers to their effective use.

## Conclusions

In this research, we drew on organizational and information systems theories to investigate a persistent and troublesome problem, the adoption gap in assimilation of EMRs among small physician practices. Our study highlights the role of organizational learning barriers as a contributor to this gap and outlines policy implications for promoting EMR adoption. Although our focus has been on issues related to health information technologies, our research also contributes to general knowledge about the diffusion and assimilation of complex organizational technologies. By applying Fichman & Kemerer’s (1997) model of organizational learning barriers to a different type of technology (EMRs) and a different organizational context (small physician practices), we extended knowledge into a new research space (Berthon et al., 2002).
