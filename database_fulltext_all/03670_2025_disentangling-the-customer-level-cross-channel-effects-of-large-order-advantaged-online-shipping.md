# DISENTANGLING THE CUSTOMER-LEVEL, CROSS-CHANNEL EFFECTS OF LARGE-ORDER-ADVANTAGED ONLINE SHIPPING POLICIES<sup>1</sup>

Vamsi K. Kanuri Department of Marketing, Mendoza College of Business, University of Notre Dame Notre Dame, IN, U.S.A. {vkanuri@nd.edu}

Andrew T. Crecelius Department of Marketing, Ivy College of Business, Iowa State University Ames, IA, U.S.A. {acreceli@iastate.edu}

Subodha Kumar Department of Statistics, Operations, and Data Science, Fox School of Business, Temple University Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A. {subodha@temple.edu}

A key challenge in e-commerce retail is identifying a shipping fee policy that will incentivize more online orders and sales. To achieve this, retailers occasionally alter their shipping policies. While information systems research has extensively examined e-commerce channel strategies and their interplay with offline channels, it has yet to explore the online and offline implications of changes in e-commerce shipping policies. Against this backdrop, we studied a shipping policy change designed to incentivize higher-dollar orders, specifically, a large multichannel retailer’s shift from a tiered online shipping policy to a flat-rate policy. Using rich customer-level panel data and a regression discontinuity in time approach, we demonstrate that a flat-fee online shipping policy, counterintuitively, shifts sales away from the online channel and toward the offline channel—generating 23% more offline sales across 21,028 customers in five states. Evidence from additional analyses corroborated an account based on two mechanisms: an online order aggregation effect, wherein flat-fee shipping encouraged shipping fee-sensitive shoppers to aggregate purchases into larger orders, and an offline store interpurchase effect, wherein order aggregation generated longer interpurchase periods, during which customers met their needs for smaller purchases by visiting offline stores. Thus, a flatrate shipping policy can serve as an unexpected lever for driving multichannel behavior. These findings contribute to the e-commerce channel interplay literature within information systems research and have important implications for legacy retailers seeking to leverage their brick-and-mortar investments to fend off competition from online-native retailers such as Amazon.com.

Keywords: E-commerce, multichannel, shipping policy, brick-and-mortar, regression discontinuity in time

## Introduction

The past decade has witnessed a sea change in e-commerce. Demand for convenient online purchasing options continues to expand rapidly, with Amazon capturing 41% of the \$900 billion online market (Droesch, 2021; U.S. Census Bureau,

2021). Meanwhile, legacy rivals with large brick-and-mortar footprints have either rushed to enhance their e-commerce operations, as in the case of Macy’s, or faced bankruptcies and store closures—as with J Crew, Neiman Marcus, Pier 1 Imports, Revlon, and Sears (Peterson, 2020; Thomas, 2022; Wahba, 2021). Information systems research has investigated how retailers can thrive in this new landscape by optimizing e-commerce strategies, especially strategies for managing the online channel alongside traditional brick-and-mortar channels (e.g., Samuel et al., 2020; Scherer et al., 2015).

Yet a key strategy for managing e-commerce channels has received little attention in extant information systems (IS) research: online shipping fee policy changes. According to a recent study, 52% of U.S. retailers apply a shipping fee on some online orders, while an additional 30% apply a fee on all online orders (Parcel Labs, 2021). Shipping fee policies are critical because they influence consumers’ e-commerce transaction costs and resulting purchase decisions (Rosen & Howard, 2000; Sun et al., 2022). Few studies have explored shipping fee policies; for example, Sun et al. (2022) analytically assessed the implications of retailers offering free shipping for upstream channel coordination, while Boone and Ganeshan (2013) designed an inventory-based optimization model for online shipping promotions. However, no study has empirically examined this e-commerce strategy in the context of a multichannel (i.e., online and offline) environment. This is a critical distinction because, as prior IS research has shown, strategies in one channel can dramatically impact other channels; for example, Kumar et al. (2019) showed how the presence of brick-and-mortar stores can increase e-commerce sales. Hence, the objective of the present research is to examine the effect of online shipping fee policy changes on shoppers’ multichannel (brick-and-mortar and e-commerce) purchasing behaviors. We studied this phenomenon in the context of a retailer’s shift from a tiered policy (i.e., higher fees for higher-dollar orders) to a flat-rate policy (i.e., the same fee for all orders) and explored the underlying mechanisms driving the outcomes.

## Motivation to Study Flat-Rate Online Shipping: Large-Order-Advantaged Policies

In the early days of e-commerce, retailers viewed shipping online orders as a cost driver and followed the intuition of logistics providers; they charged higher fees for larger orders to recover their costs since higher-dollar orders tended to be more expensive for the retailer to ship due to their weight and/or insurance cost (Lewis et al., 2006). As a New York Times report explains: “e-tailers agree that the best approach is to simply pass along the shipping costs to buyers … so many online retailers charge on the basis of an order’s cost” (Tedeschi, 2001).

Although some retailers, such as World Market, persist in this strategy, many others have shifted their perspective of online shipping from a cost driver to a revenue driver; that is, the right fee structure can encourage online purchasing (Yao &

Zhang, 2012). Specifically, modern shipping fee policies are often designed to incentivize high-dollar purchases by providing a cost advantage to shoppers placing larger orders (Huang & Cheng, 2015) (see Figure 1 for a comparison and examples of shipping policies). One such contemporary example is flat-rate shipping policies. Because flat-rate shipping encourages large orders by penalizing small orders, the shopper may feel that the fee is “wasted” unless they buy more (Koukova et al., 2012). Therefore, we conceptualize flatrate shipping policies as large-order-advantaged (LOA) shipping policies: e-commerce shipping fee schedules that delineate a lower percentage shipping cost when the consumer places a higher-dollar order. Under a \$8 flat-rate policy, for example, a \$25 order faces a 32% fee (\$8/\$25), while a \$50 order is subject to only a 16% fee (\$8/\$50).

As Figure 1 illustrates, flat-rate shipping and threshold-based free shipping policies can both be considered LOA shipping policies (Panels A and B)—in contrast to tiered shipping policies, which increase with the cost of the order (Panel C). We focus specifically on flat-rate shipping, as the impact of such policies can be more cleanly attributed to a large-order advantage and remain understudied in the literature.<sup>2</sup> Threshold-based free shipping, on the other hand, produces more complex psychological effects due to the impact of the specific threshold (the “breaks” in the dotted line in Panel B); for example, it may nudge shoppers towards specific items that exceed the threshold (Huang et al., 2019; Koukova et al., 2012). Hence, studying a shift from a tiered to a flat-rate policy is ideal for isolating the impact of the large-orderadvantaged nature of the policy.

From a strategic perspective, it is unclear whether LOA shipping policies are advantageous for multichannel retailers. On the one hand, they are designed to boost online customers’ spending by spurring more high-dollar purchases (Yao & Zhang, 2012). As mentioned earlier, with a flat-rate shipping policy, the percentage of the purchase represented by the shipping fee decreases as the size of the order increases (compared to a tiered policy), incentivizing customers to place larger orders. On the other hand, LOA policies might discourage buying at all on occasions when the customer is considering a low-dollar purchase (Forrester, 2011). In a multichannel environment, it is also unclear how LOA policies might affect incentives to visit the store. These ambiguous potential outcomes are further complicated by the possibility of heterogeneous customer responses with respect to their prior purchase behaviors. Hence, retailers need guidance about the impact of LOA shipping policies in a multichannel environment.

## Focal Online Shipping Fee Policy Change

Our research focuses on the shipping policies of multichannel retailers—those with both an e-commerce channel and a brick-and-mortar footprint. We studied LOA shipping policies against the backdrop of a typical multichannel retailer: a large, established, upmarket seller of apparel, shoes, and home goods. We examined the effect of an e-commerce shipping policy change on existing customers’ buying behavior. We focused on existing (rather than new) customers in order to zero in on the heterogeneous impact of the policy change on individual buying behavior and hence constrained ourselves to customers where such before-and-after effects could be observed.

Before the policy change, the retailer offered tiered shipping with higher fees for higher-dollar orders (similar to Figure 1, Panel C, and retailers like AutoZone, World Market, and BatteriesPlus). For example, a \$10 order would incur no fee, a \$30 order would incur a \$7 fee, and a \$100 order would incur a \$20 fee.<sup>3</sup> The policy change provided shoppers with a relative cost advantage for larger orders: shoppers paid a flat shipping fee (\~\$7) on all e-commerce orders, such that the shipping cost represented a lower percentage of high-dollar orders. This policy is comparable to the flat-rate policies now used by retailers like BoxLunch, White House | Black Market, and Saks. The retailer’s policy change is an ideal context to examine the LOA shipping phenomenon because (1) it was a discrete change from a non-LOA to an LOA shipping policy with no advance notice to shoppers, and (2) the retailer made no other policy changes (e.g., to return shipping policies, assortment strategy, etc.) in the period surrounding the change.

## Research Questions and Contributions

Our first research question (RQ1) is: How does a retailer’s shift to a large-order-advantaged (LOA) shipping policy affect online and offline purchasing behavior among shoppers who use the online channel? As noted above, it is unclear, a priori, whether purchasing across online and offline channels would increase or decrease in response to such a shipping policy change and how these effects might vary across customers. Most prior work indicates that flatrate shipping is associated with higher online spending (e.g., Koukova et al., 2012). However, while LOA shipping policies might boost some customers’ online spending by spurring more high-dollar purchases, it could also limit spending from other customers by discouraging small purchases (Forrester, 2011). Further, if customers are incentivized to increase their online spending, this could come at the expense of sales in offline stores as customers might simply shift their constrained budgets online.

To resolve this uncertainty, we applied a regression discontinuity in time (RDiT) framework, which is commonly applied when a policy change affects all units at a distinct point in time (Lee et al., 2018; Qiu et al., 2022). We leveraged the policy change as a discrete shock to customer behavior to isolate the effect of the retailer moving from a non-LOA shipping policy to an LOA shipping policy. Countering the commonly understood intent of these policies, we found that flat-rate shipping did not induce higher online spending, on average. While total purchasing increased, online spending decreased from 39% to 30% of purchases following the policy change, as customers shifted their purchasing away from the e-commerce channel and toward the brick-and-mortar stores, generating 23% more offline sales across five states.

This counterintuitive result led to our second research question (RQ2): What are the mechanisms for the effect of shifting to an LOA shipping policy on customer behavior? Prior literature offers multiple explanations for why shippers may shift purchasing from the online toward the offline channel (e.g., a distaste for online channel policies or a need to return online purchases offline). We evaluated various behavioral mechanisms within the context of a flat-rate shipping policy change. By analyzing multiple outcomes and considering customer heterogeneity based on prior purchasing habits, we found that a combination of two explanations is likely operative when retailers move to a flatrate shipping policy. Specifically, first, we found evidence of an online order aggregation effect. Shoppers with budget constraints aggregated their orders after the policy change, i.e., they placed fewer online orders for higher amounts. This shift, while visible for most shoppers, was most extreme for shoppers whose past behavior indicated a strong tendency to avoid high shipping fees and was minimized for shoppers without this tendency. Second, the results support an offline store interpurchase effect, wherein shoppers visited the offline store to fulfill shopping needs that arose during the longer interpurchase times between aggregated online orders. As a result, offline purchasing increased following the flat-rate policy change, especially for smaller purchases. Once again, we found this effect to be especially pronounced for shoppers who previously demonstrated a preference for lower shipping fees (i.e., before the enactment of the flat-rate shipping policy).

Our research makes three primary contributions to the literature and practice. First, to the best of our knowledge, our research is the only study to date studying the impact of ecommerce shipping policies on multichannel purchasing. We thus contribute to the growing domain of IS research on the interplay between e-commerce and traditional channels. Second, we extend the channel evaluation framework in the IS literature (Trenz et al., 2020) to incorporate the concept of shocks (e.g., Maier et al., 2021); in so doing, we enable a clearer understanding of how retailers’ policy changes influence subsequent reevaluations of existing online and offline channels. Third, we offer timely insights about an increasingly popular e-commerce strategy, LOA shipping policies (i.e., flat-rate policy), and provide results that run counter to practitioner intuition regarding this strategy. While LOA shipping policies do not boost sales in the online channel as retailers might expect, they can be a creative lever to nurture multichannel purchasing.

## Relationship to Extant Literature

Our investigation builds on two research domains: (1) the channel interplay literature and (2) the online shipping policy literature.

## Relationship to Channel Interplay Literature

IS studies on e-commerce strategies are myriad. Some wellestablished research areas include price transparency (e.g., Soh et al., 2006), dynamic pricing (e.g., Hinz et al., 2011), and product recommendations (e.g., Oestreicher-Singer & Sundararajan, 2012). A growing number of studies (see Table 1) have investigated e-commerce strategies from a channel interplay perspective by assessing interdependent, spillover, or joint impacts across online and offline channels. Broadly, these studies fall under three high-level topics.

First, some research has studied the effects of integrating information, promotions, or other strategies between offline and online channels. For example, Trenz et al. (2020) showed that various channel integration strategies, including in-store pickup and in-store returns for online orders, increase the perceived convenience and reduce the perceived risk of the online channel, while Fang et al. (2023) found more ambivalent effects of in-store pickup and the integration of information (on product availability) on sales growth. Similarly, Luo et al. (2020) tested the effect of offline promotions that encourage online purchasing and found that the effectiveness of such promotions depends on the consumer’s distance to the store. Analytical work has likewise examined omnichannel integration strategies; for example, Jia et al. (2022) designed methods for costeffectively fulfilling online orders using in-store inventory.

Second, a closely related stream has examined the factors leading to channel migration, or the movement of customers between offline and online channels. For instance, Langer et al. (2012) showed that movement toward the e-commerce channel depends not only on relative prices but also on buyer characteristics and inertia in channel choices. Li et al. (2017) further showed that shoppers’ experience with competitors online channels spurs greater activity in the focal retailer’s e-commerce channel. Likewise, Yi et al. (2015) examined migration in the opposite direction and found that engaging online product displays can encourage interaction with products in the offline channel.

Finally, several papers have examined the impact of strategies devised for the online (or offline) channel on other channels. For example, Bar-Gill and Reichman (2021) found that creating an engaging website decreased offline sales for an automobile dealer. Kumar et al. (2019) showed that opening offline stores can increase online sales, as physical stores boost engagement. Similarly, Shriver and Bollinger (2022) examined how a shopper’s distance to a physical store affects online purchases,<sup>4</sup> and Zhou et al. (2020) found that physical outlets boost online banking transactions over time. A related stream has viewed these cross-channel impacts from a competitive angle: physical stores serving as “showrooms” that can boost both the retailer’s and/or its competitors’ online sales (Bell et al., 2018; Gu & Tayi, 2017; Samuel et al., 2020).

Our research fits neatly into this third category: the crosschannel effects of e-commerce strategies. Yet, to our knowledge, no work in this domain has considered the crosschannel implications of e-commerce shipping fee policies, let alone the effects of flat-rate shipping policies. In the broader domain of e-commerce literature in IS, only the aforementioned analytical work by Sun et al. (2022) touches on shipping policies. We build on this literature by empirically examining the customer behavior impacts of an understudied e-commerce strategy, online shipping fee policies, with a focus on both same-channel and crosschannel outcomes.

## Relationship to Online Shipping Fee Literature

A sparse literature, mostly in the marketing and supply chain domains, has examined the implications of shipping policies in the online or catalog channel on same-channel outcomes (see Appendix Table A1). Schindler et al. (2005) provided laboratory evidence that some consumers prefer catalog shipping charges to be bundled into the purchase price while others do not. Koukova et al. (2012) used experiments to examine perceptions of online shipping fees, finding that consumers view fees more positively when they are presented as arising from delivery costs. Both Huang and Cheng (2015) and Huang et al. (2019) assessed features of threshold-based free shipping based on surveys and experiments, finding a preference for item-based (rather than dollar-based) thresholds and a perception that these policies can be seen as self-serving. Boone and Ganeshan (2013) designed an analytical model for optimizing online channel outcomes from threshold-based free shipping policies. Along similar lines, Sun et al. (2022) built a game theoretical model to assess the implications of retailers’ free shipping for upstream channel coordination.

Field studies have also examined the implications of shipping policies. Lewis (2006) found that higher online shipping fees reduced orders for an online grocery retailer, while tiered policies suppressed large orders. Lewis et al. (2006) came to similar conclusions while noting that these responses differ considerably across customer segments. More recently, Shehu et al. (2020) examined the effects of free online shipping with a diversified online retailer, finding that free shipping promotes purchasing but also increases subsequent returns of unneeded products. Most recently, Guo and Liu (2023) tested the effects of membership-free shipping for another diversified online retailer, finding that it increases revenue for light buyers but decreases it for heavy buyers. Notably, each of these studies focused on same-channel outcomes, that is, the effects of online shipping policy on online sales, leaving managers of multichannel retailers with an incomplete view of whether, when, and how these policies are beneficial. We, therefore, build on this literature by examining the effects of a shift to a large-order-advantaged shipping policy (i.e., from a tiered policy to a flat-rate policy) on both online and offline purchasing.

## Theoretical Framework

Disentangling the effects of shifting to an LOA shipping policy requires an understanding of online shoppers behavioral reactions. Early research viewed shopper perceptions of channels through a relative advantage framework, with some channels viewed as more beneficial for certain tasks (Choudhury & Karahanna, 2008). Recent IS research has expanded this perspective into the multichannel evaluation framework, in which shoppers assess the retailer’s offline and online channels along dimensions of convenience and risk (Trenz et al., 2020). We extend this theory by incorporating discrete behavioral shocks (Morgeson et al., 2015) to understand the response when a retailer shifts to an LOA shipping policy. In Figure 2, we depict our framework across three stages: status quo ante, the time of the policy change, and after the policy change. For ease of exposition, the figure focuses on the typical online shopper and illustrates how, across these three stages, the shopper’s purchases will evolve based on our theorizing and prior literature.

## Shipping Policy Change as a Behavioral Shock to Shoppers

The retailer’s shift from a non-LOA (tiered) shipping policy to an LOA (flat fee) shipping policy disrupts online shoppers. Individuals’ previous shopping habits, in terms of the size and price of orders, may be less costly or more expensive after the policy change. Hence, we view this shift as a discrete shock experienced by shoppers.

![](/api/attachments/PC4HBY89/fulltext/images/e593fd71f50b1268b4f8b2760c6bbf4e35feb64da4df67c67d8e8f8854ee4e92.jpg)

Shocks have been studied in many domains (e.g., market-wide demand shocks, Tokar et al., 2014). The shocks most relevant here are those felt in the minds of individuals; for example, prior research has studied career journey shocks (e.g., company mergers) that can lead to turnover (e.g., Maier et al., 2021). Morgeson et al. (2015) define these and other shocks to individual behavior as “events”: “discrete, discontinuous happenings, which diverge from the stable or routine features of the ... environment.” These events prompt a reevaluation of the individual’s environment and behaviors. In our case, the environment and behaviors concern the retailer and the individual’s shopping habits.

Hence, after a shipping policy change, consumers will be shaken out of complacency and reevaluate how they shop with the retailer. We apply the multichannel evaluation framework (Trenz et al., 2020) to understand how this fresh evaluation affects consumers’ subsequent shopping behaviors. Under this framework, consumers first assess the convenience and risk of the online and offline channels; based on this assessment, they choose whether and how to shop in each channel. For example, the speed and flexibility of e-commerce encourage online channel use, but the risk of an unsatisfying transaction hinders it (e.g., Devaraj et al., 2002).

## Channel Reevaluation in Response to the Shipping Policy Shock: Shopper Reactions

Therefore, shifting to an LOA shipping policy is a shock that prompts a channel reevaluation, which could change customers’ perceived convenience and risk of online and offline channels. Based on this reevaluation, we anticipate a realignment in behavior following a shift to the flat-rate shipping policy. This realignment may be summarized according to two mechanisms: an online order aggregation effect and an offline store interpurchase effect.

## Online Order Aggregation Effect

The ability for shoppers to order as many items as they like without incurring a higher shipping fee improves the convenience facet of the online channel due to the reduced hassle of transaction costs for large orders. This may especially be the case for shoppers who are sensitive to paying high fees and were hence previously reluctant to place large orders. Conversely, shoppers who are not concerned about shipping fees will experience a less dramatic reevaluation. As a result, we posit that customers will be more likely to place high-dollar orders after the policy change to benefit from the convenience of online shopping and that this change will be more dramatic for shoppers who have demonstrated a sensitivity to shipping fees.

However, shoppers are budget-constrained; they have a fixed spend to allocate across offline channels, online channels, shipping fees, and other costs (Shriver & Bollinger, 2022). The policy change does not increase or otherwise affect the shopper’s budget. Hence, placing higher-dollar online orders will also typically lead the shopper to group their planned purchases into fewer online orders. We call this behavioral outcome the online order aggregation effect.

## Offline Store Interpurchase Effect

Nevertheless, the convenience of flat-fee, high-dollar orders may have an unintended side effect for the online channel: the risk of not adding an item to the order that the shopper might want later. In other words, this is the risk of incurring a flat shipping fee multiple times to place multiple, smaller orders—or in threshold-based free shipping schemes, the risk of incurring the shipping fee by not meeting the threshold on a future, small order. This risk is exacerbated depending on the severity of the order aggregation effect; placing fewer, larger orders under a fixed budget necessarily extends interpurchase duration, meaning the time between orders (Zhang et al., 2021). More time between orders means more uncertainty about a future need for a small purchase arising, necessitating a shipping fee under an LOA policy.

Offline stores are often viewed as an important alternative to the e-commerce channel, in part due to their lack of shipping fees (Samuel et al., 2020). Awareness of the risk of small orders under an LOA policy, along with the behavioral shock of the shipping policy change, may also elevate the salience of the offline channel’s convenience as an outlet for small purchases without a shipping fee. In other words, consumers could use offline store visits to mitigate the risk of paying excessive shipping fees for small orders during lengthier interpurchase intervals. This could result in increased purchasing in the offline channel. We call this behavioral outcome the offline store interpurchase effect, a potential knock-on effect of order aggregation. Jointly, the order aggregation effect and store interpurchase effect would imply that the LOA policy shift leads to fewer, larger online orders and a shift of small orders toward the offline channel.

## The Role of Shopper Heterogeneity

As the discussion above implies, sensitivity to online shipping fees should be an important source of heterogeneity in the effect of shifting to an LOA shipping policy on shopping outcomes. This heterogeneity plays a key role in our analyses. Another source of relevant shopper heterogeneity is multichannel behavior. Specifically, customers may be categorized as (1) online-only, (2) hybrid (online plus offline), or (3) offline-only. The theorized mechanisms above can only manifest for onlineonly and hybrid customers—that is, online order aggregation, and shifting small purchases away from the online channel, can only occur if the customer already has a baseline level of online purchasing. Hence, we focus our tests of the flat-rate shipping policy change on online shoppers, i.e., groups (1) and (1). Group (3) may experience other, more intuitive effects of the policy change—for example, the publicity from merely announcing the policy could lead some offline shoppers to experiment with the online channel. Given that similar effects are already established in the literature (e.g., Shriver & Bollinger 2022), we set aside offline-only customers and examine them in a post hoc analysis.

## Empirical Context and Data

The empirical context of our study is legacy retail, meaning large retailers with longstanding brick-and-mortar footprints, such as Macy’s, Belk, and Kohl’s, which are continuously threatened by online-native competitors. We focused on a collaborating retailer that is representative of this context. Virtually all of this U.S.-based retailer’s annual revenue of over \$10 billion is derived from selling products via two channels: an older brick-and-mortar store channel and a newer, fastgrowing e-commerce storefront.

The retailer offers products across numerous categories, the largest of which are shoes, women’s accessories, women’s apparel, children’s apparel, men’s apparel, and home goods. These products mostly occupy the mid-to-high end of the quality spectrum and are aimed primarily (though not exclusively) at upmarket consumer segments.

## The Shipping Policy Change

Prior to the shipping policy change, this retailer offered a tiered shipping fee structure wherein higher-dollar online orders required the customer to pay a proportionally greater shipping fee. As noted earlier, a \$10 order, for example, would incur no fee, a \$30 order would incur a \$7 fee, and a \$100 order would incur a \$20 fee.<sup>5</sup> Motivated by a desire to expand purchasing among customers active in the online channel, the retailer enacted a shift to a flat-rate shipping policy wherein customers would pay the same online shipping fee (\~\$7) regardless of the order size.

Importantly, the retailer made no other alterations to its shipping policy, its returns policy, or its promotions calendar during or surrounding the observation window. Throughout the observation window, customers could return any product online or in-store within 90 days of purchase. Also, the entire window preceded COVID-19 and did not overlap with any recession.

In addition, the shipping policy change was not publicly announced prior to its implementation throughout the U.S., such that consumers could not adjust their purchase timing to plan for the shift (e.g., by holding off on a large purchase). The shipping policy change occurred at the beginning of the eighth month of a 16-month observation window, with seven months preceding and nine months following. This extended observation window was chosen to allow sufficient time for consumers’ channel reevaluations to play out (later, we demonstrate the stability of effects over the course of the post-shift months).

## Variables and Measures

We obtained a rich dataset including multichannel purchasing behavior of the retailer’s customers from a sample of five states in the U.S. over a 16-month observation window.<sup>6</sup> We focused on 21,028 shoppers active in the online channel (online-only and hybrid) since they were the target of the policy change and, as noted, because the theorized mechanisms cannot hold for offline-only shoppers. The retailer tracked and recorded individual customer behavior in both the offline and online channels over time. This was accomplished using industry-standard identification vectors: loyalty program numbers for the minority of loyalty members and a combination of other factors (phone numbers, credit cards, email addresses) for nonmembers. The retailer aggregated this data to an unbalanced customer-month panel (n = 131,898) prior to sharing it with us.

Variables provided by the retailer for each customer-month include: total sales dollars (TotalSales), percentage of sales in the online channel (PercentOnlineSales), average dollar amount of online orders (OnlineOrderSize), number of online orders (OnlineOrderFrequency), average dollar amount of offline orders (OfflineOrderSize) and offline returns dollars (OfflineReturns). To account for preference heterogeneity across categories, we account for the number of times a customer bought from each of the six online product categories and six offline product categories. Finally, we used the unique customer identifier to implement shopper fixed effects in all analyses. We provide definitions in Table 2 and descriptive statistics in Table 3.

## Model, Identification Strategy, and Results

We developed an identification strategy at the intersection of available methods and the data environment while attending to the necessary assumptions of our chosen strategy (Mithas et al., 2022a, 2022b) (Table 4). Ideally, one would be able to conduct a randomized controlled trial in which the retailer would randomly assign the new shipping policy to some shoppers in order to demonstrate the causal effect of the policy change on shoppers’ purchasing behavior. However, such randomization is not feasible as the collaborating retailer would likely find it unfair and discriminatory and refuse to participate.

Quasi-experimental approaches relying on a separate group of shoppers that are unaffected by shipping policy changes could also be considered. If such a group existed, approaches such as difference-in-differences (DiD), matching, or synthetic control could potentially be applied. For example, if a retailer were to deploy a shipping policy change to shoppers who joined a membership program only (as in Guo & Liu, 2023), we could treat nonmembers as the “control” group, correct for the nonrandom treatment assignment, and estimate the average treatment effect on the treated with a DiD design. However, this was not the case with our retailer since the policy was deployed to all shoppers at the same time. Further, offline-only shoppers, who by nature cannot experience online order aggregation (as they have no online orders to aggregate), may also be affected (e.g., they might begin to explore the online channel) by such policy changes.

Hence, the key challenge in this environment is the lack of an obvious counterfactual in the absence of the policy change. Table 4 outlines why this challenge rules out several alternative identification strategies and summarizes the strategy we deployed. Specifically, we employed a regression discontinuity in time (RDiT) framework that leverages the temporal discontinuity arising from the sudden shock of the policy change experienced by all online shoppers.

RDiT is a special case of the regression discontinuity design in which time is the running variable. The advantage of RDiT is its ability to estimate treatment effects when (1) there is no between-unit variation in treatment status (i.e., all online shoppers are affected by the shipping policy shift) and (2) the treatment can be isolated to a distinct cutoff time (Hausman & Rapson, 2018). This model is ideal for testing within-unit, before-and-after effects when a suitable control group does not exist. For example, Lee et al. (2018) exploited the discrete release date of a film (an event that potentially affects all Twitter posts) as a discontinuity in time when estimating effects on Twitter sentiment. Likewise, Qiu et al. (2022) viewed the implementation of the Hospital Readmission Reduction Program (part of the Affordable Care Act, affecting all hospitals) as a discontinuity in time when assessing hospitallevel readmission and mortality rates. In our context, the change to a flat-fee online shipping policy, which was simultaneously announced and rolled out to all online customers at the same time, functions as a discontinuity in time with respect to shopping behavior.

## Threats to Identification

The main threats to identification in an RDiT design concern contemporaneous temporal trends and unobserved shocks, which could lead to a correlation between the error term and the post-treatment dummy (PostPolicy<sub>t</sub>) and hence confound the treatment effect (Anderson, 2014). To mitigate this threat, RDiT models incorporate flexible polynomial time terms, allowing for nonlinear temporal effects that can differ between the pre- and post-treatment periods (Hausman & Rapson, 2018). Later, we conducted multiple additional analyses, including augmented local linear analysis and donut RDiT analysis, to further assess and alleviate the threat of temporal confounds and to test the robustness of our results.<sup>7</sup>

## RDiT Specification

We addressed our research questions in two parts. First, to examine RQ1, we assessed the impact of the shipping policy change on overall multichannel purchasing using the baseline specification described in this section. Second, to investigate RQ2, we conducted analyses of multiple shopping basket outcomes and shopper heterogeneity, as detailed in the subsequent section.

A basic regression discontinuity in time (RDiT) estimation measures the effect of the online shipping policy change on customer-level TotalSales and PercentOnlineSales (for simplicity and ease of exposition, we use Purchases<sub>it</sub> to refer to these metrics). These two focal outcomes capture the overall multichannel purchasing behavior of each shopper:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {Purchases} _ {i t} = & \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \text {PostPolicy} _ {t} \\ & + \sum_ {p = 0} ^ {\bar {p}} \alpha_ {2, p} \times \text {Duration} _ {t} ^ {p} + v _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t, 1}, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where PostPolicy, our coefficient of interest, represents whether (=1) or not (=0) month t belongs to the post-flat-rate shipping policy implementation period; it captures the discontinuous change in purchasing behavior at the time of the policy change (i.e., the treatment effect). Duration is the continuous time variable centered at the month of the policy change, such that it takes negative values in the pretreatment period; its higher-order polynomial terms allow nonlinear temporal effects before and after the treatment. In line with prior research (e.g., Pu et al., 2020; Qiu et al., 2022), we tested various polynomial orders of Duration based on AIC and BIC and determined that a value of 3 (3rd order polynomial) had the best fit. The vector v<sub>i</sub> includes shopper fixed effects and incidence of online and offline categories while $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is the idiosyncratic error term for customer i in month t. To account for repeated observations, we specified a covariance structure that assumed correlation among observations from a given shopper.

## Results

Our results from the estimation of the econometric model (Table 5) largely mimic the model-free evidence. TotalSales experienced a modest increase $( b = I 8 . 9 8 3 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . In contrast, PercentOnlineSales shows a marked decline (b = -0.089, p < 0.01). The drastic purchasing shift in a single month—with the average online shopper shifting roughly 9% of their spending toward the offline channel, per the model estimates—supports the premise of our theoretical framework: The policy change is a shock to customer behavior that triggers channel reevaluation (Figure 2). Further, we estimated the same specification with offline sales dollars as the outcome of interest (see Appendix Table A2) and confirmed that the shock resulted in increased offline purchasing.

To ensure that this shock is robust to alternate model specifications, we undertook a series of tests (reported in the next section), including establishing whether the findings are sensitive to TotalNetSales and PercentNetOnlineSales outcomes. Subsequently, we elaborate on the mechanisms and demonstrate the heterogeneity in the effects of the shipping policy change.

## Alternate Shopping Outcomes

In our main analysis, we established the effect of the shipping policy change on TotalSales and PercentOnlineSales. One concern with these results is that they could be an artifact of customers buying unwanted products to maximize the flat fee’s value and subsequently returning unwanted products offline. If so, the results could be inflated. Hence, we evaluated the effect of the shipping policy change on TotalNetSales and PercentNetOnlineSales. The results in Table 6 indicate that the new analyses fully replicate prior findings. Hence, we can rule out customers’ intent to order unwanted products as an alternative explanation.

## Donut RDiT Test

Since the shipping policy change was implemented at the same time that it was announced, customers should not have been able to plan for the change (e.g., by waiting to place a large online order). Nonetheless, if company insiders leaked information to friends and family, for example, individual customers might have gotten advance notice. To eliminate the possibility that such leakage drove the results, we followed Hausman and Rapson’s (2018) recommendation and applied a donut RDiT strategy. Specifically, we reran the model, removing the months immediately preceding and following the policy change, such that strategic shifting of purchases before and after the cutoff time could not drive the results. The results held in sign and magnitude under this specification (see Table 7).

## Why Did the Flat-Fee Shipping Policy Affect Purchasing?

Beyond the impact on the overall purchasing behavior across the two channels, the story of the shipping policy change is incomplete without addressing RQ2 and exploring the mechanisms. Further, prudent managerial application of our results requires an understanding of why these effects occur. In this section, we model purchasing baskets as behavioral proxies for the mechanisms. We also assess the effects of prior shipping fee payment, a form of shopper heterogeneity that should interact with the underlying mechanisms.

## Customer Heterogeneity Analysis: Prior Shipping Fee Payment

To examine the mechanisms, we considered how heterogeneity in shopping behavior might provide evidence of the order aggregation effect and store interpurchase effect. Although these effects should result in changes in purchasing baskets, their foundations are the economies created by the flat-rate shipping fee (compared to the prior, tiered fee). Under this view, the shopper’s motive is to minimize the risk of paying excessive shipping fees; for example, they are likely to aggregate online orders to avoid paying for repeated flat-fee orders. It stands to reason, then, that shoppers who are strongly motivated by this risk will most dramatically demonstrate the order aggregation and store interpurchase effects. If the order aggregation and subsequent store interpurchase effects hold, then the discontinuous change in shopping behaviors at the time of the policy change should be stronger for customers with an especially strong desire to avoid shipping fees.

Hence, we posit that susceptibility to the order aggregation and store interpurchase effects is strongly influenced by prior shipping fee payment (PriorShippingFees) or the average monthly shipping fees paid by shoppers prior to the shipping policy change. High-fee shoppers (those with a higher value of this continuous variable), who paid extensive shipping fees under the tiered-rate structure, already demonstrated a willingness to place orders regardless of high shipping fees; low-fee shoppers, who paid lower shipping fees under the tiered-rate structure, would be more likely to place orders that minimize shipping fees. Therefore, because the order aggregation effect (and subsequent store interpurchase effect) is based on shoppers’ cost-saving efforts, it should manifest more strongly for low-fee shoppers and weakly for high-fee shoppers. We tested this form of heterogeneity using a moderated RDiT specification (Qiu et al., 2022):

Therefore, conversely, the interaction effects of PriorShippingFees with OnlineOrderSize and

OnlineOrderFrequency are negative $( b = - 8 . 0 8 6 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ and positive $( \mathbf { b } = 0 . 0 1 0 , \mathbf { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ , respectively. The order aggregation effect was lower for higher-fee shoppers. To investigate the range of prior shipping fees where the effect held, we conducted marginal effect analyses (Figure 4). As the plots show, the effect on online order frequency held through the entire range of average monthly prior shipping spend. The effect on average order size held for the average shopper’s monthly prior shipping spend (mean = \$11.43 prior shipping fee; left-skewed [skewness = -0.766]); it disappeared for the high-fee shoppers.

Taken together, the purchasing basket analyses in Table 11 tell a nuanced story of why the shift towards the offline channel occurs. For shoppers that incurred lower shipping fees under the previous, tiered structure, the order aggregation and store interpurchase effects were evidently driving wallet-shifting away from the online channel. For high-fee shoppers, these effects were largely absent, in line with the earlier heterogeneity analysis, demonstrating that these shoppers were less likely to shift their spending away from the online channel following the policy change.

While Table 11 provides strong evidence of the order aggregation effect, the store interpurchase effect is only implied. We see that shoppers shifted dollars towards the online channel; combined with evidence of order aggregation (and longer interpurchase durations), this provides compelling indirect evidence that the theorized interpurchase effect was occurring.

How can we provide more direct evidence? Recall that the store interpurchase effect was theorized to particularly motivate purchases of smaller (i.e., less expensive) items in the offline channel. As the shopper’s needs arise during extended interpurchase intervals, the smallest items would be least likely to prompt an online purchase (due to a desire to avoid wasting the flat fee on a small item) and most likely to motivate a visit to the offline store.

Therefore, if the offline store interpurchase effect holds, we would expect to see an increased tendency to buy small items offline following the policy change. To assess this tendency, we constructed six additional outcomes: (1) %OfflineItemsLess Than\$30, (2) %OfflineItemsLessThan\$20, (3) %OfflineItems LessThan\$10, (4) %OnlineItemsLessThan\$30, (5) %OnlineItems LessThan\$20, and (6) %OnlineItemsLessThan\$10. Each variable is defined as the number of items purchased below the price threshold (\$30, \$20, or \$10) divided by the total number of items purchased in that channel (offline or online) in that month. We included three different price thresholds to avoid an arbitrary cutoff in defining a “small item.” As shown in Table 12, the results are supportive of the store interpurchase account in each case. After the policy change, shoppers exhibited an increased likelihood to buy small items (whether defined as <\$10, <\$20, or <\$30) in the offline channel, accompanied by a decreased likelihood to buy these items online. Hence, the store interpurchase effect shifted the shopper’s tendency to buy small items towards the offline channel.

## Tests of Alternative Mechanisms

The analyses strongly support the order aggregation and store interpurchase effects. Nonetheless, it is worth highlighting two other mechanisms that have compelling theoretical arguments and assessing how the evidence aligns with these mechanisms.

## Distaste Effect

Shipping policies that are perceived as manipulative are not well-received by shoppers (Huang et al., 2019; Koukova et al., 2012). An LOA policy creates a new set of transaction cost incentives that may be perceived as such. For example, consumers may be uncomfortable with the feeling of “wasting” a flat shipping fee on a small order and may resent the retailer for this discomfort, making them less likely to place orders. This distaste would imply a reduction in the perceived convenience of the online channel and, if it holds, would imply a reduction in total purchasing following the LOA policy change. We call this potential mechanism the distaste effect.

Under the distaste effect, the dominant result we would expect to see is shoppers withdrawing from the retailer— that is, reduced sales. Broadly speaking, we did not observe a pattern of reduced sales across the various analyses. On the contrary, across most of the analyses, we observe increased sales. Therefore, the evidence does not align with the distaste mechanism.

## Exploratory Purchasing Effect

In the presence of a flat fee, shoppers may feel freer to add more items to their cart and trial a variety of products; the monetary risk of incurring a shipping fee for unwanted items will not increase with more items in the order, as it would under tiered-rate fees. As past work speculates (Enders & Jelassi, 2009; Shehu et al., 2020), shoppers trying more products, in turn, might return more products to the offline store. Visits to offline stores allow shoppers to uncover other benefits and conveniences of that channel (e.g., personal relationships with frontline employees and in-store services). Such a pattern would imply a reduction in the perceived risk of the online channel and, potentially, a concurrent increase in the perceived convenience of the offline channel. It would also align with media reports about rampant retail returns (Cain, 2018). If this pattern holds, the retailer should see an increase in in-store returns following the LOA policy change. We label this potential mechanism the exploratory purchasing effect.

Under this mechanism, channel-shifting will occur because of the convenience of in-store returns. If we do not see an increase in in-store returns following the shipping policy change, the mechanism will be unsupported. We report the analyses of returns in Table 13. Contrary to this view, the shipping policy change decreased OfflineReturns (b = -0.182, p < 0.01). Even when considering shopper heterogeneity, lowfee shoppers exhibited decreased returns (simple effect of PostPolicy; b = -0.152, p < 0.01) and the interaction with PriorShippingFees was not significant; high-fee shoppers also returned fewer items offline. Hence, the results do not support the exploratory purchasing account.

## Additional Support for the Practical Importance of the Effects

We present two further analyses related to the managerial impact of shoppers’ shift towards the offline channel. First, we test whether and how the shipping policy change affects customers who only purchased offline prior to the change to assess whether effects on that group might have offset effects on the online customers. Second, we conducted an analysis of short- versus longer-term effects to test whether the flat-fee shipping policy’s impact was stable over time.

## Offsetting Effects: Impacts on Offline Customers

The flat-fee shipping policy change was targeted towards increasing purchasing among online and hybrid shoppers. Hence, they were the focus of our analyses. Yet it is possible that the policy change influenced the behaviors of offline customers as well, meaning those who had not used the online channel before the policy change. If the shipping policy change affected offline customers in ways (e.g., reduced purchasing) that undermined impacts on online customers, the overall impact of the policy change (and the managerial importance of our findings) could be lower.

To test this possibility, we replicated our main RDiT analysis among the offline customers. That is, we estimated Equation (1) with this alternate sample; we report our results in Table 14. We found that, for offline customers, the online shipping policy change had a very modest, positive effect on TotalSales $( b = 7 . 4 1 0 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ ; the multichannel effects on online customers were not undermined by effects among offline customers. Interestingly, the policy change also exerted a small, significant effect on PercentOnlineSales among offline shoppers $( b = 0 . 0 2 3 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , likely due to the mere shock of the policy change and the reevaluation of the two channels.

Yet, in this case, the magnitude of the shift—about 2.3% of the average wallet during the 9-month post-policy change period—was substantively negligible. Therefore, the shift to flat-fee shipping had an appreciable impact on multichannel behavior among online-active customers only.

## Stability of the Effect: Short-Term and Long-Term Impacts of the Shipping Policy Change

Although the reported effects support the order aggregation explanation, we also sought to explore the persistence of the effect. For example, if shoppers initially shifted purchases toward the offline channel but then shifted back—or if online shoppers were continuously shifting offline, regardless of policy changes—the managerial importance of our findings would be diminished.

Regression discontinuity designs are not well-suited to assessing treatment heterogeneity with respect to the running variable. The logic of RDiT is that observations just before the treatment serve as counterfactuals for observations just after it; quantifying the treatment effect many periods later runs counter to this logic. Nonetheless, we followed prior work (Lee et al., 2018) and tested for heterogeneous time effects within the post-policy change period. To identify the time effects in the presence of the polynomial order time trend, we separated the nine post-policy change months into three quarters (i.e., Quarter1, Quarter2, and Quarter3):

## Additional Sources of Heterogeneous Impacts: Product Categories

We have thus far focused on prior shipping fee expenditure as the main source of heterogeneity in the impact of the shipping policy change on shopping outcomes due to this variable’s close connection with the theorized mechanisms. But other sources of heterogeneity could be managerially relevant—for example, product categories. We estimated six separate RDiT models for interactions of the PostPolicy treatment with prior online purchasing in each of six product categories (home goods, shoes, women’s accessories, women’s apparel, children’s apparel, and men’s apparel) and repeated these six models for offline purchasing in these categories; the results are reported in Appendix Table A3. We found that the treatment effect on sales was strongest for those with greater online purchasing of women’s accessories, women’s apparel, and children’s apparel and greater offline purchasing of children’s apparel.

## Conclusion

Large-order-advantaged (LOA) online shipping fee policies such as flat-rate shipping are believed to incentivize greater purchasing in the online channel. Yet, in response to RQ1, we found that a large retailer’s shift to a flat-rate policy caused shoppers to decrease online purchasing but increased offline purchasing. These effects amounted to, on average, \$242K less monthly online purchasing across the five states in the study—an 11% reduction—but \$975K in new offline purchasing each month for these previously online-only shoppers—a 23% increase. The end result is an increase in total sales of \$733K each month, an increase of 12%.<sup>9</sup>

For managers of legacy retailers such as the focal firm in our study, our findings show that an investment in an LOA shipping policy can be viewed as a creative tool to inculcate multichannel behavior among online customers. We observed a shift toward the retailer’s brick-and-mortar channel that amounted to \$733K of monthly offline purchases across five states after the change to the flat-rate online shipping policy. We show that even though this shift resulted from a discrete shock (the shipping policy change), the effect persisted in the nine months following the change. Moreover, behavioral changes among offline customers did not offset these effects on online customers, suggesting that managers should not ignore the multichannel impacts of shifting to an LOA online shipping policy.

In response to RQ2, we conclude that the changes initiated by the introduction of a flat-fee shipping policy were achieved by creating an incentive for shoppers to aggregate their online orders and save money on shipping fees—the online order aggregation effect. When customers aggregate their online purchasing into larger orders without concomitant increases in their budgets, the time between orders expands. As a result, needs that arise between these expanded interpurchase periods—especially for small items that would incur disproportionate shipping fees—create pressure for the online customer to find alternative modes of purchasing. This pressure, combined with the policy change’s discrete shock on customers’ channel evaluations, leads online customers to begin shifting purchases toward the offline channel. We call this knock-on phenomenon the offline store interpurchase effect. We found that this wallet-shifting was focused on lessexpensive items, consistent with the order aggregation and store interpurchase account. Also consistent with this account, the effects were most dramatic for customers who were most susceptible to order aggregation, namely, those who had minimized their shipping fees under the prior, tiered-rate online shipping policy.

In general, our findings run counter to managers’ intuition that LOA shipping policies will generate increased purchasing in the online channel and imply more broadly that managers evaluating policy changes within the online channel should carefully monitor outcomes in the offline channel. Metrics to assess the effectiveness of shipping policies, for example, should focus equally on online and offline sales outcomes. Relatedly, we demonstrate that online shipping policies represent an unexpected but actionable managerial lever for nurturing multichannel behavior. In our study, after the shipping policy change, online customers began to visit offline stores and become multichannel customers.

This shift to multichannel behavior is critical because extant work emphasizes that retail strategy is converging towards multichannel shopper relationships (Bharadwaj et al., 2013). Retailers are learning that multichannel customers are more loyal and spend more on each transaction (Timoumi et al., 2022). As such, retailers are continually seeking ways of leveraging their brick-and-mortar investments toward profitable offline and online engagement (Luo et al., 2020). Hence, the wallet-shifting effects of flat-fee shipping policies are desirable for retailers.

Our findings extend knowledge in three literature streams. First, past literature on shipping policies has focused on outcomes in the online channel. In contrast, we provide the first examination of a shipping policy’s effect on customers’ multichannel behaviors. Second, extant work on channel interplay effects in e-commerce has considered the crosschannel impacts of various strategies, such as store openings (e.g., Chan et al., 2021; Kumar et al., 2019). We broaden this domain by demonstrating online shipping fee policies, a vital but understudied e-commerce strategy lever, as a vector for cross-channel impacts and a viable means of encouraging multichannel behavior. Third, we extend the literature on multichannel evaluations (Kanuri et al., 2017; Trenz et al., 2020) by incorporating discrete shocks, which trigger channel reevaluations. Our findings suggest that enhanced convenience evaluations in the online channel lead to order aggregation and longer interpurchase times. Combined with the shopper’s reevaluation of the offline channel, this creates fresh opportunities to experience that channel by fulfilling smaller purchase needs.

This research is subject to some limitations, each of which offers directions for future research. We focus on one specific type of shift toward an LOA shipping policy—specifically, from a tiered-rate to a flat-rate fee structure. Such a shift may manifest in other forms, for example, a move from tiered-rate shipping to threshold-based free shipping. A flat-rate fee structure shares key features with threshold-based free shipping—reducing percentage shipping fees with order size—while avoiding some of the idiosyncrasies of threshold-based policies, such as reference price effects of the threshold. Nonetheless, future research could build on our initial investigation and identify conditions under which differential implications might emerge for various flavors of LOA shipping policies. In addition, other retailers might experience different impacts from shifting to a LOA shifting policy than the focal retailer in this study. For example, situational factors like stockouts and changes in inventory availability, shifts in store promotions, and the extent of online competitors offering similar products (and their shipping policies) could drive heterogeneous shipping policy outcomes. Lastly, to test the effects on customer-level purchasing behavior, we focused on existing customers. Future research could examine the implications of online shipping policy changes for customer acquisition.
