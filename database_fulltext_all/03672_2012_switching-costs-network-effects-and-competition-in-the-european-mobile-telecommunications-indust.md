# Switching Costs, Network Effects, and Competition in the European Mobile Telecommunications Industry

Lucio Fuentelsaz, Juan Pablo Maicas, Yolanda Polo

Research Group Generés, Marketing and Strategy, Department of Management and Marketing, Universidad de Zaragoza, 50005 Zaragoza, Spain {lfuente@unizar.es, jpmaicas@unizar.es, ypolo@unizar.es}

his paper empirically analyzes the joint effect of switching costs and network effects in determining the Tlevel of competition in the European mobile communications industry. Theoretical reasoning argues that switching costs and network effects may confer some market power that firms can strategically exploit to reduce competition and thus increase profits. Theoretical predictions are completely confirmed by the empirical evidence and important asymmetries between the market structures in the different European countries can be observed. These asymmetries are clearly related to the levels of switching costs and network effects—the greater their importance, the lower the rivalry in the market. This suggests that the recent efforts of policymakers to reduce the negative consequences of switching costs and network effects have not been successful enough and these efforts must be strengthened, at least in several countries.

Key words: network effects; switching costs; mobile telecommunications industry; competition History: Sanjeev Dewan, Senior Editor. Chris Forman, Associate Editor. This paper was received June 10, 2008, and was with the authors 7 <sup>1</sup> months for 2 revisions. Published online in Articles in Advance November 18, 2010.

## 1. Introduction

Information technology (IT) industries have received increasing attention in the academic literature in recent years (Shapiro and Varian 1998, Farrell and Klemperer 2007). Both theoretical and empirical work usually suggest that profitability in these markets is strongly conditioned by the size and stability of an installed customer base, which is mainly determined by network effects and switching costs (Farrell and Klemperer 2007). The existence of these forces usually allows firms to exert some market power that leads to prices above marginal costs and thus gives them the possibility to obtain abnormal returns.

Farrell and Klemperer (2007) postulate that the two elements arise when consumers value different forms of compatibility. Nevertheless, the nature and consequences of switching costs and network effects are slightly different. In the presence of switching costs, compatibility has a longitudinal dimension because users look for compatibility between consecutive purchases of goods or services to preserve their specific investments. In a context with network effects, compatibility acquires a horizontal nature, given that users are interested in consuming products or services that provide higher utility when the number of customers who buy them increases. Their different natures also condition the strategies followed by firms when one of these two elements is present. The literature in economics has concluded that when switching costs are high, firms will initially set low prices for their products with the aim of attracting a high number of customers who will later become locked in.<sup>1</sup> Similarly, firms may also strategically manage network effects, whose presence conditions users’ choices because the higher the network size, the higher the utility perceived by their customers. Under these conditions, preannouncement strategies to influence users’ expectations about an installed base are also very common.

The presence of network effects increases consumers’ willingness to pay, given that people will be ready to pay a higher price for a product that is compatible with a large network of users. On the other hand, switching costs can be understood as a renouncement: users accept higher prices because they were incurred in previous investments that would be lost if they changed providers.

Despite having the same origin—compatibility— and similar implications for market dynamics (Farrell and Klemperer 2007), switching costs and network effects have usually been analyzed separately. However, it has been argued that it is the interaction of the two forces that leads to the well-known lock-in effect (Shapiro and Varian 1998, Amit and Zott 2001, Farrell and Klemperer 2007). In markets where both are present, their independent analyses could bias their interpretations or their impacts on market dynamics. Recent theoretical literature (Viswanathan 2005, Lee and Mendelson 2007, Chen and Forman 2006)

has also established that the combination of switching costs and network effects may have an amplifying impact on IT industries. Surprisingly, empirical research in the management and information systems fields about the joint impact of these two elements is still scarce and inconclusive.

Our article tries to contribute to this literature by analyzing how the joint effect of switching costs and network effects influences the level of competition. We develop a model that integrates the two variables and is empirically tested in the context of European mobile communications. This industry is especially suitable for this study for two reasons. First, the level of both switching costs and network effects is especially high (Gruber 2005, Grzybowski 2008, Maicas and Sese 2008). Second, there is a great homogeneity among the different countries in the environmental conditions in which the firms compete. European regulators decided from the beginning to establish a unified technological standard global system for mobile communications (GSM in the case of the second generation—2G—and universal mobile telecommunications system (UMTS) in the third generation—3G) and the market was liberalized at approximately the same time. As a consequence, the level of competition within the different countries—and, thus, within the observed market structure—is expected to be similar. Nevertheless, although the level of service is relatively comparable and the technology employed by the operators is identical, competition in, say, the United Kingdom is unanimously considered intense while in, say, France, Portugal, and Spain, the first firms that entered the market retain privileged positions. Within this context, the question that arises is what can explain the asymmetries observed in the European mobile communications industry.

From a theoretical point of view, one possible explanation of the differences observed among countries could emerge from asymmetries in the level of network effects and switching costs. As Klemperer (1995) points out, in a market with switching costs, market shares tend to converge in the long run especially if the market growth is abrupt as in IT industries. Additionally, Shi et al. (2006) maintain that in a market with network effects, a reduction of switching costs might precipitate the market to a winner-takes-all outcome. The pattern we observe in the European mobile industry does not match any of the two extreme situations that we have just described. Most likely, the different structures that we observe are a consequence of different combinations of switching costs and network effects. Thus, a model that only considers one of these two elements will provide us with a biased picture of the competitive situation that emerges in a given market.

We extend existing research in two important ways. First, though prior research on the mobile communications industry has analyzed how switching costs and network effects separately influence prices and competition (Shi et al. 2006, Grajek 2009, Grzybowski 2008), ours is the first attempt that jointly considers both. Because switching costs and network effects are simultaneously present in the mobile communications industry and have similar implications for market dynamics (Farrell and Klemperer 2007), to not consider both at the same time would tend to positively bias the implications of the other for market competition.

Second, we examine how switching costs and network effects can help us to understand different levels of competition in our intercountry data set. In contrast to previous studies on the mobile communications industry, our research setting is based on data from a cross-section of countries over time.<sup>2</sup> The benefits of our approach could come from (1) direct focus on whether country effects may explain variations in performance; (2) understanding how the regulator can influence the level of competition; and (3) the ability to deal with new issues that are uniquely addressed with international country-level data (Dewan and Kraemer 2000, p. 549).

Our findings suggest significant differences among countries with respect to the level of competition, which seems to be partially determined by both switching costs and network effects. The general rule derived from our results is that the higher the magnitude of switching costs and network effects, the lower the competition in the market. These results have important implications for both operators’ strategies and regulators’ activities. On one hand, the countries with higher network effects and switching costs on average will confer higher market power to the operators. On the other hand, regulators should pay special attention to markets where operators enjoy privileged positions because this may be harmful from a social welfare point of view.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents a brief review of the empirical literature on switching costs and network effects within the context of the mobile communications industry. In §3, we identify the main sources of switching costs and network effects on mobile communications. Section 4 provides some evidence about the current structure of the industry in Europe. Section 5 develops the theoretical model that will be empirically tested, and §6 describes the methodology and estimation strategy we follow in the empirical tests. Section 7 presents the data and variables that will be used in the empirical analysis. In §8, we offer the estimation results and propose some extensions to the basic model. The work finishes with a discussion in §9 of the main conclusions from and the policy implications of our research.

## 2. Empirical Literature on Switching Costs and Network Effects in the Mobile Communications Industry

Switching costs and network effects have been analyzed in depth from a theoretical perspective, mainly in the economics literature. Surprisingly, the ample theoretical literature has not had its parallel in empirical research (Stango 2002, Kim et al. 2003, Viard 2007, Grzybowski 2008, Chen and Hitt 2007). This section analyzes the empirical literature on switching costs and network effects. Particularly, we focus our attention on the mobile communications industry, whose development in recent years has been especially important.

Within this context and on the network effects side, Grajek (2009) acknowledges that, in spite of being a crucial element to understanding mobile communications, the empirical literature has almost completely ignored the impact of network effects on the level of industry competition. Grajek (2009) estimates price elasticities in the Polish market and concludes that if we do not consider network effects, the elasticity of demand could be substantially overestimated. Very similar results can be found in other studies about diffusion in mobile communications. For example, Doganoglu and Grzybowski (2007) show that network effects played a significant role in the diffusion of mobile services in the German market and that ignoring them might significantly bias the estimated price elasticity of demand. Birke and Swann (2006) explore the role of network effects in consumer choice between mobile providers in the United Kingdom. Their findings suggest that even in the absence of price differences between on-net and off-net calls, there is still a form of direct network effects. Furthermore, Birke and Swann (2006) find that individual choice is heavily influenced by the choices made within the individual’s household. Maicas et al. (2009a) analyze the role of local network effects in determining mobile users’ choices. They conclude that the probability that a customer selects a provider increases with the number of members of her social network already subscribed to that provider. Corrocher and Zirulia (2009) also study local network effects in mobile communications services and conclude that users are highly heterogeneous with respect to their evaluations of personal network effects when choosing mobile providers, and that this heterogeneity is basically related to specific characteristics such as individual innovativeness and patterns of mobile phone usage. Overall, this stream of research has focused its attention either on the impact of network effects on mobile communications diffusion or on the role of network effects on the understanding of how users make their mobile communications choices.

On the switching costs side, Lee et al. (2006) analyze the impact of switching costs (measured through number portability) on competition in the Korean mobile telecommunications market. Their results show that switching costs decrease as a consequence of the introduction of number portability, but their impact is still positive after the implementation of this measure. Kim (2005) also analyzes the effect of number portability on switching costs in Korea and concludes that switching costs differ among operators and that switching behavior has to do with both user profile and operator characteristics. Grzybowski (2008) empirically estimates switching costs in mobile communications in the United Kingdom and finds that users face significant switching costs that vary according to the mobile communications providers. Shi et al. (2006) analyze how mobile number portability affects market prices, consumer welfare, and market concentration. Their results show that prices decrease but at very different rates between operators.

To sum up, we can observe that there is a growing literature that analyzes the causes and consequences of switching costs and network effects. It mainly helps us to understand mobile communications diffusion in different markets, in consumers’ choices, and even in the evolution of market prices. However, although switching costs and network effects are considered to be two of the main important forces that govern mobile communications, there has been no serious attempt (to our knowledge) to measure their joint impact on market competition.

These two factors coexist in many real situations (Amit and Zott 2001, Farrell and Klemperer 2007). Their joint analysis is particularly important because switching costs and network effects have similar market consequences (Farrell and Kemplerer 2007). The simultaneous presence of the two elements may favor the well-known lock-in effect (Amit and Zott 2001) that can transform competition toward the winnertakes-all outcome that we may observe in many high tech industries (Arthur 1996, Viswanathan 2005). In other words, switching costs and network effects allow the reduction of market rivalry. If we accept that the two elements are reinforcing each other, then not considering them simultaneously could underestimate their impact on market competition. Therefore, any effort to consider them together would constitute a valuable contribution to the empirical literature, especially in information-intensive markets.

## 3. Sources of Switching Costs and Network Effects in Mobile Communications

This section tries to identify the most important sources of switching costs and network effects in mobile communications. Among the most cited switching costs, number portability and locked handsets are of special interest for our research.<sup>3</sup> Network effects (especially tariff-mediated network effects) are important per se. Price or tariff-mediated effects (Laffont et al. 1998) appear when there are differences between on-net and off-net calls.

Number portability gives the users the right to keep their phone numbers when switching from one service provider to another. The lack of this possibility has been considered as one of the most important costs within the industry, given that users have to inform all their contacts of number changes and they take the risk of missing calls from friends or clients (NERA 2003). To avoid this inconvenience, in September 1998 the European Parliament passed Directive 98/61, which forces operators belonging to member states to allow customer portability between operators. Number portability has previously been analyzed in the context of telecommunications (Buehler et al. 2006, Grzybowski 2005, Viard 2007, Lee et al. 2006, Maicas et al. 2009b) and its absence clearly constitutes an important exit barrier. Nevertheless, it is necessary to highlight that even though the European Union regulated number portability in 1998, its introduction took place at different times in each country (see Table 1) (see also Buehler et al. 2006 for a detailed analysis of this process). Moreover, more important than the date of introduction is the effective implementation of this measure (NERA 2003, Grzybowski 2005, Lyons 2006). By way of example, portability was introduced into Spain in November 2000 but only from 2004 onward was it truly effective (in the latter year, almost 5% of the total population changed their mobile provider).

Another important switching cost has to do with the impossibility of unlocking handsets. Handsets were often locked by manufacturers at the request of mobile operators. To fight against this switching cost, the European Parliament sent a warning letter to operators, handset manufacturers, and the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI) urging them to unlock handsets (under certain conditions). Otherwise, they would be guilty of anticompetitive behavior.

Network effects appear when the utility that a user obtains from the consumption of a good increases with the number of other agents consuming it (Katz and Shapiro 1985). In the case of mobile services, network effects would mainly be expected to affect the network as a whole and not individual firms. Theoretical reasoning has predicted that as roaming agreements allow compatibility between operators, the introduction of an additional consumer in the market benefits the other users independently of whether they belong to one operator or another.

Nevertheless, empirical evidence in mobile communications (Kim and Kwon 2003, Birke and Swann 2006) shows some signs of firm-level network effects, even when the networks are perfectly compatible. These effects are reinforced by the pricing policies followed by the operators, which discriminate between on-net and off-net calls and which generate important tariff-mediated network effects. These policies give advantages to on-net calls because they are cheaper than off-net calls.

## 4. The European Mobile Communications Industry

The European mobile communications industry has grown impressively in recent years. Its average penetration rate increased from around 30% at the end of 1998 to slightly over 100% at the end of 2005. This growth places the industry in its mature phase, passing from the introduction stage to maturity in less than a decade. Gruber and Verboven (2001a, b) have analyzed this process all over the world and postulate that this rapid diffusion can be attributed to the setting of a single digital standard (which implies a substantial reduction in costs, with the subsequent effect on prices), as well as to the introduction of competition in the second generation technology. Doganoglu and Grzybowski (2007) also point out that this fast development has had an additional driver: the presence of a technology with substantial network effects. Market entry is especially valuable for a user once the number of customers in this market has reached a certain threshold,<sup>4</sup> which in turn stimulates further entry of users, establishing a positive feedback in mobile communications diffusion.

Economies of scale are also important in the industry, given the big investments needed to develop infrastructures. The joint effect of network effects and economies of scale leads to market structures in which the leading firms obtain a clearly advantageous position (this configuration is usually known in the literature as “winner-takes-all” or “winner-takes-most”) (Arthur 1996, Shapiro and Varian 1998, Liebowitz 2002). Moreover, radio spectrum is considered a scarce resource and operator entry is regulated by governments. As a consequence, telecommunications markets are expected to be highly concentrated with a relatively low level of competition. Table 2 presents the market share of the most important operators in a representative sample of European countries.

The information included in Table 2 confirms the picture just described. The number of firms that operate in each country is small (usually three or four with the exception of only the United Kingdom, where there have been five rivals since 2003) as a result of the constraints imposed by radio spectrum and by entry regulation. Moreover, the market shares of the different firms show high levels of variation both within and between countries. Indeed, it is very unusual to find countries where the different operators have similar market shares (the United Kingdom, with four firms of approximately the same size, is again the only exception to this pattern—Hutchison, the fifth competitor, entered the market recently). Very often, the former state monopolists dominate the market with shares close to 50%. A second operator captures about one third of the customers and the remaining 15%–20% is distributed between one or two further competitors. France, Portugal, Spain, and Sweden are examples of this behavior. Finally, in several countries (Germany, Greece, and, to a lesser extent, Italy), two firms of approximately the same size capture more than 70% of the customers.

When we analyze the longitudinal evolution of the market shares, it is necessary to admit that most of the countries show a reduction in the differences between firms. The last two rows of each country in Table 2 calculate the difference between the share of the first firm established in the industry (which is listed first) and that of the second and the third entrants. These differences show a slight convergence that is especially noteworthy in the United Kingdom and Italy. Nevertheless, the former state monopolist is still the market leader in all the countries with the exceptions of the United Kingdom and Greece. This behavior is somewhat surprising because we are analyzing markets with high growth rates, where the policymakers have adopted several measures directed toward enhancing competition (as mentioned in §3, number portability or unlocked handsets are clear examples of these measures). The expected outcome in these markets would be a high level of convergence (Klemperer 1995) that does not match the observed picture. Probably the main explanation of this situation is the presence of switching costs and network effects that reduce the desired level of competition. Not surprisingly, the reduction in the differences between firms is more acute in the last two to three years, when number portability has been really effective.

## 5. Model Setup

To assess the level of competition in a context with switching costs and network effects, we borrowed a simple theoretical model proposed by Kim et al. (2003). Basically, the model provides a methodology for determining both the magnitude and significance of switching costs and network effects and their impacts on market shares and margins. We closely follow Kim et al. (2003) with respect to the inclusion of switching costs, and their model is complemented by incorporating direct network effects. The main advantages of this framework are that it does not require much information and that it allows us to use aggregated data (at the firm level), which facilitates the empirical estimation.

The model considers an oligopoly of n firms that compete in prices. The service offered by each firm is unstorable. The main consequence of the presence of switching costs and network effects is that the demand becomes inelastic. As usual, consumers make their choices with the aim of maximizing their own utility. The service characteristics appreciated by the users are modeled along two main dimensions: switching costs and network effects. On the one hand, all consumers compare the prices of the different firms, keeping in mind that switching between them is costly. On the other hand, they incorporate firms’ sizes into their utility functions as a proxy of the presence of network effects.

The proposed model relies on three main assumptions. The first explicitly considers the presence of switching costs. Following Kim et al. (2003), user behavior is modeled through the concept of transition probability, which can be defined as the probability that a user maintains his relationship with the current provider or, alternatively, switches to a competitor.

Transition probabilities are characterized as follows:

$$
\operatorname * {P r} _ {i \to i, t} = f \{p _ {i, t}, \mathbf {p} _ {i R, t} + \mathbf {s}, n _ {i, t - 1}, \mathbf {n} _ {i R, t - 1} \},\tag{1}
$$

$$
\operatorname * {P r} _ {j \to i, t} = f \{p _ {i, t} + s, \mathbf {p} _ {i R, t} + \mathbf {s} _ {j}, n _ {i, t - 1}, \mathbf {n} _ {i R, t - 1} \},\tag{2}
$$

where $\mathrm { P r } _ { i \to i , t }$ is the probability that a consumer who previously bought from firm i will continue to purchase from this firm in period $t \colon \mathrm { P r } _ { i \to i , t }$ is the probability of a consumer switching from firm j to firm i in period $t ; ~ p _ { i , }$ <sub>t</sub> is the price of firm i in period $t ;$ $\mathbf { p } _ { i R , }$ is the vector of prices of competing firms; s is the vector of switching costs; $\mathbf { s } _ { j }$ is an $( n - 1 )$ vector of switching costs in which each of the elements equals s except for the jth element, which is zero; $n _ { i , t - 1 }$ is the size—number of users—that firm i had in the previous period; and ${ \bf n } _ { i R , t - 1 }$ is the competitors’ vector of sizes.

In aggregate data, we are not able to observe transitions. Therefore, we have to formulate the probability of switching to firm i unconditional on the rival’s identity:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \operatorname * {P r} _ {i R \to i, t} = \sum_ {j \neq i} \bigg (f \big \{p _ {i, t} + s, \mathbf {p} _ {i R, t} + \mathbf {s} _ {j}, n _ {i, t - 1}, \mathbf {n} _ {i R, t - 1} \big \} \\ \cdot \frac {n _ {j , t - 1}}{\sum_ {k \neq i} n _ {k , t - 1}} \bigg), \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

where $\mathrm { P r } _ { i R  i , t }$ is the probability that a rival’s user switches to firm i and $n _ { j , t - 1 } / \sum _ { k \neq i } n _ { k , t - 1 }$ is the probability that a randomly selected rival’s user is one who previously purchased from firm j.

Following Kim et al. (2003), we apply a first-order (linear) approximation on the transition probabilities. Linearity converts the transition probabilities into functions of the price charged by the firm $p _ { i , t } ,$ the average price charged by the rival firms $\bar { p } _ { i R , t } ,$ the average rivals’ sizes $\bar { n } _ { i R , t - 1 } ,$ the number of firms $n ,$ and the cost of switching between firms $s . { } ^ { 5 }$ We characterize this as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \operatorname * {P r} _ {i \to i, t} = \alpha_ {0} ^ {i} + \alpha_ {1} p _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {2} (\bar {p} _ {i R, t} + s) \\ + \beta_ {1} n _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} \bar {n} _ {i R, t - 1}, \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \operatorname * {P r} _ {i R \to i, t} = \alpha_ {0} ^ {i} + \alpha_ {1} (p _ {i, t} + s) + \alpha_ {2} \bigg (\bar {p} _ {i R, t} + \frac {n - 2}{n - 1} s \bigg) \\ + \beta_ {1} n _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} \bar {n} _ {i R, t - 1}, \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

where $\alpha _ { 0 } ^ { i }$ are firm-specific intercepts capturing firm heterogeneity,<sup>6</sup>  measures the sensitivity of the transition probability to the firm’s price, $\alpha _ { 2 }$ represents the cross-price sensitivity, $\beta _ { 1 }$ measures the sensitivity of the transition probability to the network’s size, and $\beta _ { 2 }$ represents the cross-size sensitivity.

Under the assumption of an inelastic total demand, a small increase in $p _ { i , t }$ should have the same effect on the transition probabilities as that of a decrease of the same magnitude in the rivals’ average price $\bar { p } _ { i R , t } .$ The same reasoning is applied to a variation in size. Thus, we restrict $\alpha _ { 2 } = - \alpha _ { 1 }$ and $\beta _ { 1 } = - \beta _ { 2 }$ . The resulting transition probabilities are

$$
\operatorname * {P r} _ {i \to i, t} = \alpha_ {0} ^ {i} + \alpha_ {1} (p _ {i, t} - \bar {p} _ {i R, t} - s) + \beta_ {1} (n _ {i, t - 1} - \bar {n} _ {i R, t - 1}),\tag{6}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \operatorname * {P r} _ {i R \to i, t} = \alpha_ {0} ^ {i} + \alpha_ {1} \bigg (p _ {i, t} - \bar {p} _ {i R, t} + \frac {s}{n - 1} \bigg) \\ + \beta_ {1} (n _ {i, t - 1} - \bar {n} _ {i R, t - 1}). \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

In Equations (6) and $( 7 ) , \alpha _ { 1 }$ measures the sensitivity of the transition probability to price differences and $\beta _ { 1 }$ measures the sensitivity of the transition probability to network size differences. Given the usual characteristics of the demand function, we would expect $\alpha _ { 1 }$ to be negative and $\beta _ { 1 }$ to be positive.

As can be derived from Equations (6) and (7), switching costs and network effects can simultaneously favor and harm a firm’s interests. On one hand, the higher s, the higher the probability for the firm of maintaining current users but the lower the probability of capturing customers from other firms. On the other hand, the higher the intensity of network effects $\beta _ { 1 } ,$ the higher the proportion of the company’s installed base that will choose not to switch to other rivals but the lower the probability of users switching from a competitor to that firm.

Our second assumption establishes that demand can be obtained by aggregating individual transition probabilities. The demand for each operator (Equation (8)) is given by two terms. The first includes customers the firm had in the previous period and who are currently continuing with the firm. The second considers new users captured from other firms.

Consequently, the demand function can be expressed, using the transition probabilities defined in Equations (6) and (7), as the sum of the demand in the previous period $( n _ { i , t - 1 } )$ times the probability of maintaining it $( \mathrm { P r } _ { i  i , t } )$ and the demand for other competitors in $t - 1 \ ( n _ { i R , t - 1 } )$ multiplied by the probability of switching to the focal firm:

$$
n _ {i, t} = n _ {i, t - 1} \cdot \operatorname * {P r} _ {i \to i, t} + n _ {i R, t - 1} \cdot \operatorname * {P r} _ {i R \to i, t}.\tag{8}
$$

The model also considers new users that enter the market (market growth) between periods t −1 and t in the following way: $g _ { t } \equiv \sum n _ { i , t } / \sum n _ { i , t - 1 }$ . If we incorporate this growth into Equation (8), we obtain

$$
\frac {n _ {i , t}}{\sum_ {i} n _ {i , t}} = \frac {n _ {i , t - 1}}{\sum_ {i} n _ {i , t - 1}} \cdot \operatorname * {P r} _ {i \to i, t} + \frac {n _ {i R , t - 1}}{\sum_ {i} n _ {i , t - 1}} \cdot \operatorname * {P r} _ {i R \to i, t},
$$

$$
n _ {i, t} = \left(n _ {i, t - 1} \cdot \underset {i \rightarrow i, t} {\mathrm{Pr}} + n _ {i R, t - 1} \cdot \underset {i R \rightarrow i, t} {\mathrm{Pr}}\right) \frac {\sum_ {i} n _ {i , t}}{\sum_ {i} n _ {i , t - 1}},\tag{9}
$$

$$
n _ {i, t} = \left(n _ {i, t - 1} \cdot \operatorname * {P r} _ {i \to i, t} + n _ {i R, t - 1} \cdot \operatorname * {P r} _ {i R \to i, t}\right) \cdot g _ {t}.
$$

Substituting the transition probabilities into Equation (9), we have

$$
\begin{array}{r l} n _ {i, t} = & \bigg (- n _ {i, t - 1} \frac {n}{n - 1} s \alpha_ {1} + n _ {t - 1} \alpha_ {0} ^ {i} \\ & \quad + n _ {t - 1} \alpha_ {1} \bigg (p _ {i, t} - \bar {p} _ {i R, t} + \frac {s}{n - 1} \bigg) \\ & \quad \quad + \beta_ {1} n _ {t - 1} (n _ {i, t - 1} - \bar {n} _ {i R, t - 1}) \bigg) \cdot g _ {t}. \end{array}\tag{10}
$$

Finally, assuming that $g _ { t } \equiv n _ { t } / n _ { t - 1 }$ and $\sigma _ { i , t } \equiv n _ { i , t } / n _ { t }$ and operating in Equation (10), it is possible to obtain the market share equation<sup>7</sup>

$$
\sigma_ {i, t} = - \sigma_ {i, t - 1} \frac {n}{n - 1} s \alpha_ {1} + \alpha_ {0} ^ {i} + \alpha_ {1} \bigg (p _ {i, t} - \bar {p} _ {i R, t} + \frac {s}{n - 1} \bigg)\tag{11}
$$

where $\sigma _ { i , t } \ \left( \sigma _ { i , t - 1 } \right)$ is the market share of firm i in period t 4t − 15.

Equation (11) establishes the relationship between market share and switching costs and network effects. Several interesting results can be derived from this equation. First, it is important to note that the management and economics literatures give considerable importance to the role of history in firm behavior. In the information economy context, it is relatively common to talk about concepts such as positive feedback, path dependence, or, simply, history matters (Arthur 1989, Shapiro and Varian 1998, Amit and Zott 2001, Farrell and Klemperer 2007). All of these terms implicitly highlight the contribution of switching costs and network effects to competition throughout the industry life cycle, especially when competition is measured in terms of market share as is usual in these scenarios (Klemperer 1995, Shapiro and Varian 1998). The history effect is reflected through the positive effect that the former period market share has on the current market share, as shown here<sup>8</sup>:

$$
\sigma_ {i, t - 1} \left(\beta_ {1} n _ {t - 1} - \frac {m}{m - 1} s \alpha_ {1}\right) > 0,\tag{12}
$$

where we have taken into account that $\sigma _ { i , t - 1 } =$ $n _ { i , t - 1 } / n _ { t - 1 }$

Alternatively, Equation (12) can be expressed as

$$
\beta_ {1} n _ {i, t - 1} - \sigma_ {i, t - 1} \frac {m}{m - 1} s \alpha_ {1} > 0.\tag{13}
$$

According to Equations (12) and (13), the higher the switching costs and the network effects, the greater the importance of history on the purchases made in previous periods.

Our third (and last) assumption is that firms maximize their long-run profits. This assumption suggests that firms have to solve a problem of firm value maximization, so that the firm (at any point in time ) maximizes its profits as follows:

$$
V _ {i, \tau} = \sum_ {t \neq \tau} ^ {\infty} \delta^ {t - \tau} \pi_ {i, t},\tag{14}
$$

where  is the one-period discount factor<sup>9</sup> and $\boldsymbol { \pi } _ { i , t }$ is the firm’s time t profit. $\pi _ { i , t }$ can be defined as

$$
\pi_ {i, t} = n _ {i, t} p _ {i, t} - c _ {i, t}.\tag{15}
$$

Once we solve the problem presented in Equation $( 1 4 ) , ^ { 1 0 }$ we obtain the equilibrium price-cost margin equation

$$
p c m _ {i, t} = \frac {\delta n _ {i , t + 1} \beta_ {1}}{\alpha_ {1}} - \delta \sigma_ {i, t + 1} \frac {m}{m - 1} s g _ {t + 1} - \frac {\sigma_ {i , t}}{\alpha_ {1}},\tag{16}
$$

where $p c m _ { i , t }$ represents the Lerner index $( { p } _ { i , t } - m { c } _ { i , t } )$ and $g _ { t + 1 }$ stands for the market growth in the following period (the other parameters have been previously defined). This equation captures the relationship between the price-cost margin and both switching costs and network effects. The first two terms of Equation (16) $( ( \delta n _ { i , t + 1 } \beta _ { 1 } / \alpha _ { 1 } ) ~ - ~ \delta \sigma _ { i , t + 1 } ( m / ( m - 1 ) ) s \bar { g _ { t + 1 } } )$ include the part of the price-cost margin attributed to the users in period t who are considered as locked in in the following period. These terms correspond to the “bargain, then rip $o f f ^ { \prime }$ strategies aimed at setting lower prices in the current period with the expectation that, because users will be locked in in the future, the firm will be able to charge higher prices. These scenarios are characterized by the presence of switching costs and network effects. Therefore, the higher the potential to lock in new customers in the future, the lower the price currently charged with the purpose of attracting them.<sup>11</sup> We should note that the first and the second terms in Equation (16) are negative. Thus, when switching costs and network effects are important, these terms will be higher, which means that there are incentives to set lower prices because customers will be locked in in the future. The last term in Equation (16) $( \sigma _ { i , t } / \alpha _ { 1 } )$ represents the current oligopoly power of the firm, which increases with its current market share. In fact, in the absence of both network effects and switching costs, the optimization problem is reduced to the conventional case of a oneperiod oligopoly. The higher the market power in $t ,$ the higher the price charged in the current period.

We can observe the trade-off between the first two terms and the third, that is, the “harvest” versus “invest” dilemma. Farrell and Klemperer (2007) argue that firms should find a balance between the incentive for low prices that “invest” in market share and hence increase future profits and the incentive to charge high prices to “harvest” greater current profits. Profitable established firms would be expected to prefer to emphasize the last part of the equation, that is, to obtain a maximum surplus from their current users. On the other hand, firms in the first stages of the industry life cycle or firms without a consolidated position in the market would choose strategies directed at acquiring new customers that will become profitable in the future.

## 6. Methodology and Estimation Strategy

The equations that will be empirically estimated and were derived in the previous section follow.

(i) Market share equation (Equation (11)):

$$
\begin{array}{c} \sigma_ {i, t} = - \sigma_ {i, t - 1} \frac {n}{n - 1} s \alpha_ {1} + \alpha_ {0} ^ {i} + \alpha_ {1} \bigg (p _ {i, t} - \bar {p} _ {i R, t} + \frac {s}{n - 1} \bigg) \\ + \beta_ {1} (n _ {i, t - 1} - \bar {n} _ {i R, t - 1}). \end{array}
$$

(ii) Margin equation (Equation (16)):

$$
p c m _ {i, t} = \frac {\delta n _ {i , t + 1} \beta_ {1}}{\alpha_ {1}} - \delta \sigma_ {i, t + 1} \frac {m}{m - 1} s g _ {t + 1} - \frac {\sigma_ {i , t}}{\alpha_ {1}}.
$$

One of the main concerns that has to be addressed is the applicability of the model to the mobile telecommunications data that we are analyzing. The model assumptions postulate that we have a service in which early choices condition future ones and thus a user’s behaviour is conditioned by the presence of switching costs and network effects. The mobile communications industry is one of the most important businesses in which switching costs and network effects are present (Shapiro and Varian 1998, Grzybowski 2008, Farrell and Klemperer 2007, Maicas et al. 2009a). Section 3 was devoted to illustrating how these two effects influence mobile communications.

An implicit (and restrictive) assumption of the model is that it imposed a certain degree of service homogeneity. Within the context of the model, differentiation is mainly conditioned through lock in. Gruber (2005) argues that the strategy space for product differentiation in mobile communications expands in three main directions: coverage, prepaid cards, and lock in. With regard to coverage, Gruber (2005) maintains that all firms in Europe tend to provide full nationwide coverage. The scope for product differentiation in terms of coverage is thus limited. The assessment of prepaid cards is very similar. At the very beginning of the digital era, they may have constituted a weapon for differentiation. During our observation period, it is difficult to think about prepaid cards as an element that differentiates between operators. Finally, Gruber (2005) suggests lock in as the most important way of developing product differentiation. In the same vein, Grzybowski and Pereira (2007, p. 205) found within the Portuguese market that “the available mobile telephony subscription products are close substitutes.” Consequently, only lock in seems to be an effective mechanism to differentiate mobile services. Switching costs and network effects are precisely what condition lock in (Shapiro and Varian 1998, Amit and Zott 2001).

Gruber’s (2005) arguments are reinforced by the fact that GSM was established as the standard technology in Europe.<sup>12</sup> Therefore, technology innovation (a usual driver of product differentiation) is also limited and lock in remains the main source of differentiation. However, an exception to this rule can be found in UMTS technology. Some operators are offering bundled services beyond the traditional binomial calls and messages. Additionally, 3G technology has allowed some operators to offer handsets that are unavailable from other companies (e.g., the iPhone from Apple, which is offered only by O2 in the United Kingdom or by Movistar in Spain). In any case, we should not forget that our research window ends in 2005, a period in which UMTS was not truly operating.

The system of equations proposed at the beginning of this section (Equations (11) and (16)) will constitute the starting point of our empirical estimation. Following Kim et al. (2003), the market share Equation (11) will be estimated in first differences to eliminate firm fixed effects $( \alpha _ { 0 } ^ { i } )$ . Moreover, the estimation procedure should take into account the interaction between the market share and the price-cost margin equations. Thus, the system will be simultaneously estimated (performing one individual estimation for each country) using nonlinear 3SLS, which is supposed to offer consistent and asymptotically normal estimates as well as, under some conditions, asymptotically moreefficient-than-single-equation estimates. Additionally, using 3SLS as the estimation procedure allows us to control for potential endogeneity issues. Simple estimators using 3SLS subroutines can be used to handle the endogeneity of the regressors (see Baltagi 1995). We control for the possibility that some of our variables are potentially endogenous, namely, the market share in period $t , s _  i , $ and one period ahead $s _ { i , t + 1 } ,$ and the time differences of the prices $( p _ { i , t } - \bar { p } _ { i R , t } )$ and sizes $( n _ { i , t } - \bar { n } _ { i R , t } )$ . Furthermore, our endogeneity problems could be related to omitted variables, which are those that simultaneously affect the dependent variable and are correlated with one or more explanatory variables being omitted from the regression. In our particular case, the time that the mobile providers have been operating in the market or the condition of being the former state monopolist could be omitted variables.

Because we do not have external instruments, and following the usual methodologies, we use lags of the potential endogenous variables (Geroski et al. 1993). Therefore, our estimations are instrumented by the lagged sizes, lagged prices, and lagged market shares.<sup>13</sup> The models were estimated using TSP 4.5.

## 7. Data and Variables

The data used to estimate the impact of switching costs and network effects in the level of competition in the European mobile communications industry are taken from Global Wireless Matrix (2006), a publication that gathers information for the most important markets in the world. Because of missing data, our final sample includes quarterly data for eight of the most important—in terms of users and diffusion— European countries. Our observation period starts in the last quarter of 1998 and finishes in the last quarter of 2005. The panel contains all significant<sup>14</sup> firms operating in France, Germany, Greece, Italy, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, and the United Kingdom.

Operators’ market share, defined as the percentage of total users, is the dependent variable of the first equation proposed in the previous section. Network effects are proxied through the installed base of customers—number of users—of each operator in the previous period. This variable has been widely used in the literature—see, for example, Brynjolfsson and Kemerer (1996) or Gandal (2002), who argue that although this way of measuring network effects is, to some extent, myopic (it does not contemplate the future expectations of the users and the companies’ sizes), it leads to models that are easier to solve analytically.

Calculating a homogeneous price for the service charged by each mobile operator is a very difficult task. Prices usually include a set of tariffs that differ depending on the final target of the phone call (on-net versus off-net calls), the time of day, or the characteristics of the customer (which are indirectly revealed by the choice of the type of contract with the phone company). To solve this problem, Doganoglu and Grzybowski (2007) use all the available information about tariffs to calculate prices in Germany, but it is very difficult to replicate this procedure in a sample that includes several countries. Grajek (2009) determines the price of each provider as the lowest bill (which reflects the best deal selection) of an average customer. Shy (2002) derives prices from average revenue per user (ARpU) in his calculation of switching costs in mobile communications in Israel. Our choice is similar to that of Shy (2002), but we divide ARpU by total minutes of use by the customer (MoU). This allows us to obtain the revenue per minute (RpM), which is a more precise proxy of the average price. Price-cost margin, the dependent variable in our second equation, is calculated by means of the EBITDA margin, as is usual in information technology industries. Finally, we define market growth on a quarterly basis. The dynamism of the industry advises us to calculate it in this way instead of the usual practice of calculating it on an annual basis.

Because of space constraints, country information is not listed here. As a consequence, Table 3 synthesizes the main descriptive statistics for the whole sample. Several interesting results can be derived from this table. The median operators (given that several firms entered into the market during our observation period, the median is probably more informative than the mean, which can be especially conditioned by extreme values) substantially increase their size, mainly because of the high market growth rates. In the meantime, average and median mobile communications prices have been substantially reduced: The median revenue per minute was 35¢ in 1998, while in 2005 it is only 18¢. The differences between companies have also been reduced—the standard deviation in 1998 is about five times higher than that of 2005.

## 8. Estimation and Results

Kim et al. (2003, p. 42) suggest that before the estimation is performed, it is necessary to decide the adequate length of the period in which switching may take place. This condition must be implemented not only to decide the preferred length of the period to be used in the estimation but also to adjust to the restriction of guaranteeing that transition probabilities lie in the 601 17 interval.<sup>15</sup> Therefore, users’ switching patterns should be determined to select the most reasonable period. In our case, the analyzed service has a shorter maturity cycle than Kim et al. (2003) (who analyze the loan banking market). Thus, switching is expected to be more frequent in mobile communications. We must also take into account that mobile users are mainly of two types: prepaid and postpaid customers. The former have hardly any restrictions to their switching while the latter usually sign contracts that commit them for a period of 12 or 18 months. Thus, our reference horizon should be shorter than the average contract. Finally, we estimated the model with two quarterly lags, although the results are consistent and very similar when three or four lags are considered.

Table 4 reports the results of the nonlinear 3SLS estimation. According to the model, a positive estimation of s implies the existence of customer switching costs.<sup>16</sup> In addition, the demand function is expected to slope downward, which implies that $\alpha _ { 1 }$ must be negative. This condition about $\alpha _ { 1 }$ is required for the validity of the model and is satisfied by all the countries except Italy. To control for autocorrelation, the Durbin-Watson statistic was used. Its value, close to two for all of the models estimated, suggests no serial correlation. We may also say that the model estimated for each country presents a global fit always above 0.75, as measured by the R-squared statistic.

The model predicts that the ex post space for differentiation in products or services that were considered as homogeneous ex ante increases with the intensity of network effects and switching costs. As a result, a direct relationship between demand elasticity and the intensity of switching costs and network effects can be established.

## 8.1. Extensions of the Basic Model

The parameters derived from the previous estimations can be used to characterize further information associated with both consumers and firms (in our case, mobile users and operators). We focus our attention on two main questions: the probability of staying with a particular company at a given point in time, and the strategies that the literature on industrial organization has called “bargain, then rip offs.”

In order to characterize the first question (the probability of staying), we introduce the concept of transition probability. Indeed, transition probabilities constitute a key element of the model we have proposed (§5). They are defined as the probability that a user maintains his relationship with the current provider or, alternatively, switches to a competitor. In our model, this probability is conditioned by the intensity of network effects and switching costs. As a consequence, it would be expected that the higher the intensity of these two forces, the higher the probability of a user maintaining his relationship with his current company. After that, we use the margin equation (Equation (16)) to analyze firms’ pricing strategies.

At this point, it is important to recall that recent literature suggests that the natural inertia of switching costs and network effects frequently leads to lockin outcomes (Shapiro and Varian 1998, Amit and Zott 2001, Farrell and Klemperer 2007). For example, in contexts where switching costs and network effects are particularly high, users will find higher barriers to changing from one supplier to another. In other words, the level of switching costs and network effects is positively correlated with the probability of staying with a particular firm. If we introduce the estimated parameters $\alpha _ { 1 } , \beta _ { 1 } ,$ and s into the transition probability equations, some interesting information can be derived. The results of these calculations are offered in Table 5. We introduce average values of our variables (i.e., prices, rivals’ prices, sizes, rivals’ sizes, and number of competitors). The first column shows the average probability of staying within the same company between periods t and t +1. This probability is extremely high in France (almost 70%) and also in Spain (54%). On the contrary, the probability is much lower for customers from Greece, the United Kingdom, Germany, and Sweden (around 25%). It can be seen that this classification is very similar to the one obtained previously (see Figure 1).

Another important aspect that firms must take into account is the long-term consequences of their strategies, especially in markets where switching costs and network effects are important. In these contexts, a dynamic analysis that considers the whole user life cycle is needed (Farrell and Klemperer 2007). This scenario usually results in strategies by which firms initially establish penetration prices (lower prices than those suggested by the one-period maximization problem), followed by higher prices once the customer has been locked in by the firm. As has been mentioned, this behavior is known in the literature as the “bargain, then rip off” strategy and can be evaluated by means of the price-cost margin derived in Equation (16). As we mentioned in §5, the model allows us to distinguish between two components of the pricecost margin equation. The first reflects the part of the margin that firms sacrifice in the current period to attract new customers who will be locked in later and are thus exploited by the firm through higher prices (Table 5, Column 2). The second part of Equation (16) reflects the current oligopoly power of the firms (last column). For example, the average French firm obtains only 46.4% of the potential margin and waives 53.6% to lower prices and capture new customers from whom it will received further profits in the future.

Future research should complement this analysis (which has been performed in aggregate terms) and incorporate further explanatory variables. Among them, we can mention factors such as the importance of firm size in explaining individual firm behavior, or the evolution over time of the trade-off between strategies that initially set lower prices to build bigger installed bases and strategies that establish high prices from the beginning to extract higher surplus from current installed bases. ## 9. Discussion and Conclusions

Switching costs and network effects have traditionally been considered important sources of market imperfections because of their subsequent effect on firm behavior. Theoretical models predict that one of the main risks in markets where the two elements are present is that firms design their strategies with the aim of benefiting from these failures and gaining abnormal returns.

This article has empirically confirmed the importance of switching costs and network effects in mobile communications in Europe for understanding market structure and thus market competition. Our results are consistent with previous literature that suggests that both elements are crucial in IT industries (Chen and Hitt 2002, 2007; Kauffman et al. 2000). More precisely, the interest of this paper comes from two complementary aspects. First, it integrates switching costs and network effects into one model. Second, the empirical test of our hypotheses is performed on a wide sample of European countries with different market structures, which gives us the possibility of offering an intercountry perspective. Our main result is that the differences between countries are important and are partially conditioned by the level of switching costs and network effects.

We identify two completely different patterns. The first one includes countries such as the United Kingdom, Sweden, Germany, and Greece, where both dimensions are of relatively low levels. Thus, firms that compete in these environments will have to deal with higher degrees of rivalry. On the other hand, countries such as France, Portugal, and Spain have markets with lower levels of competition and privileged positions for the leading firms. A surprising result that derives from our estimation is the Italian case. It does not adjust to the described profiles and does not even satisfy the constraints imposed by the model. As a result, no inference can be made from this country. One possible explanation of this unexpected situation is that the frequent entry and exit of operators—Wind, Blu, and Hutchison—during the period analyzed distorted the results.

One important question that arises in this context is why these differences still remain despite the efforts of European authorities to foster competition within the industry. Recall that the EU is responsible for providing a common European regulatory framework. This manuscript has offered some examples of this common framework such as mobile number portability or the unlocking handsets warning letter, among others. Despite the efforts of regulators, important asymmetries between countries still remain, probably because of the differences in the effective implementation of these measures. Within this context, we can clearly distinguish between supranational regulatory policies, aimed at establishing the same departure conditions for all the EU, and the national application of these policies, which can determine why some countries have lower levels of switching costs and network effects. Within this rationale is the idea that switching costs and network effects are highly determined, directly or indirectly, by the conditions imposed by national regulators.

The question at this point is whether it is possible to identify any shared profile between the countries of the two groups. If we look at the clusters we obtained from our analyses, we can infer some interesting commonalities. The competitive group (the United Kingdom, Sweden, Germany, and Greece) is characterized by the same entry strategy in two dimensions: the number of competitors<sup>19</sup> and the mechanism used to allocate mobile communications licenses. All these countries have at least four companies competing in their respective markets. Furthermore, their regulators decided to use auctions instead of a beauty contest to allocate UMTS licenses. The applicability of these measures was aimed at introducing competition at the early stages of the market. The assumption underlying this logic is that, in markets with network effects, the positive feedback may derive in winner-takes-all outcomes. The introduction of competition in the first stages of the industry may overcome this undesirable outcome. More precisely, if a regulator decides to introduce competition from the beginning, all the operators are in the same conditions when the market starts to grow. The reasoning for the level of switching is very similar. Switching costs tend to be time-dependent—e.g., transactional or contractual switching costs—so if one operator is alone in the market for a long time, it has more opportunities to generate switching costs in its customer base. In this group of countries, either most of the operators entered the market at the same time or the gap between entries was very small. Within this context, operators have fewer margins to deploy consumer switching costs.

The less competitive countries (France, Spain, and Portugal) have also shared very similar regulatory strategies. All of them have three operators in their markets (do not forget that our research window finished at the end of 2005), and they chose the beauty contest as the mechanism to allocate the UMTS mobile licenses. Additionally, it should be noted that the former state monopolist was, in most cases, the pioneer in the mobile communications industry as well as one of the recipients of the 3G licenses. This common policy has had important implications for market competition. Former landline state monopolists still have a privileged position in their respective markets (2005’s market shares: Orange (France) 46.9%, TMN (Portugal) 45.1%, and Movistar (Spain) 46.1%). These operators had the opportunity to both exploit the positive feedback because they were alone in the market for some time and to generate higher customer switching costs.

Our results are not necessarily limited to mobile communications. The evidence that comes from our research is that, in industries in which both switching costs and network effects are present, it is important to adopt proactive behavior to avoid those undesired consequences. Otherwise, the most likely outcome is that one or a small number of firms will dominate the market. This is the case in industries such as operating systems, Internet navigators, or office software, where the outcome has been one company with a market share above 70%. These forces can help us to understand why some firms in IT industries have such strong positions.

Of course, our work has several limitations that may constitute the starting point for future research. Our results provide only a partial explanation about the interaction between switching costs and network effects. The analysis has been performed in aggregate terms and it would be of interest to take into account the specific characteristics of the operators to explain individual firm behavior. Further research would also have to consider the evolution over time of the trade-off between strategies that initially set lower prices (to build bigger installed bases) and those that establish high prices from the beginning (to extract higher surplus from current installed bases). Klemperer (1995) suggests that firms are more interested in the former strategy in the first stages of relationships with their customers. Bigger firms will be more interested in exploiting their current installed bases whereas smaller companies will prefer to invest in the acquisition of new users to enlarge their market shares.

Furthermore, the theoretical model we use does not allow us to easily incorporate new users. Their introduction into the model (market growth) is considered to be exogenous. Thus, we are implicitly assuming that new users have different behaviors from existing customers (Kim et al. 2003). If this is not true, the level of switching costs and network effects might be underestimated. Our logic is that at the departure point of our model, we assume that the transition probabilities depend on the level of switching costs and network effects that users are bearing. In a way, new users are incorporated into the model by addition. This implies that switching costs and network effects do not have any influence on new users before they enter the market. However, anecdotal and theoretical evidence shows that there is some influence of switching costs and network effects on new users. Klemperer (1987) postulates that users can anticipate, to some extent, that firms will charge higher prices afterward because of the existence of switching costs. Additionally, Farrell and Klemperer (2007) postulate that the size of the network increases others’ (in our model, new users) incentives to adopt. This is what they call marginal network effects, as opposed to total or classical network effects. This logic implies that new users are also affected by the size of the network (they incorporate the size of the network they subscribe to—network effects—into their utility functions). Consequently, new users are not totally immune to switching costs and network effects.

Future studies should also collect more detailed information about prices with the aim of distinguishing between on-net and off-net tariffs. In our results, network effects play a minor role in comparison with switching costs in determining market competition. This might be because of not considering on-net and off-net prices. This price differentiation implies that network effects can be not only global but also local (Birke and Swann 2006, Maicas et al. 2009a). Taking into account that the differences between on-net and off-net tariffs are usually high, their introduction into the analysis would facilitate a better understanding of the role of network effects on competition.
