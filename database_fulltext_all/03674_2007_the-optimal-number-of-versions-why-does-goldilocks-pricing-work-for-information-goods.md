# The Optimal Number of Versions: Why Does Goldilocks Pricing Work for Information Goods?

WENDY HUI, BYUNGJOON YOO, AND KAR YAN TAM

ABSTRACT: The literature in general suggests that selling multiple versions is more profitable than selling only a single version. However, how many versions should be offered is not as clear. Classical pricing studies suggest providing as many versions as the number of customer types, whereas some studies in information systems suggest providing only one or two versions. In reality, firms typically provide more than one or two versions, such as three in the case of Goldilocks pricing. This study explains the discrepancies in these results and observations by showing that, although profit increases with more versions, the marginal benefit of an additional version decreases rapidly. Therefore, firms sell few versions even in the presence of very small versioning-related costs such as menu and cognitive costs. This study analyzes the effects of these costs, and shows that cognitive costs have more profound effects on versioning than menu costs.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: analytical modeling, customized bundling, economics of information systems, information goods pricing, pricing, versioning.

A LARGE BODY OF LITERATURE HAS ANALYZED differential pricing strategies for information goods, including versioning [2, 3], pure bundling [1], mixed bundling [10, 11], customized bundling [17], and personalized pricing [8]. These pricing strategies fall into one of three categories of price discrimination: (1) first-degree price discrimination, (2) second-degree price discrimination, and (3) third-degree price discrimination.<sup>1</sup> In situations when personal reservation values are unknown or an external signal for third-degree price discrimination is absent, second-degree price discrimination may become the best differential pricing strategy.

In second-degree price discrimination (or versioning), a firm offers multiple versions to the market, and consumers with different reservation values (unknown to the firm) self-select into the version most attractive to them. Versioning is a vertical differentiation strategy; that is, differentiation is based on quality rather than taste. Typically, it requires the firm to determine a set of $\{ q _ { i } , p _ { i } \}$ , where $q _ { i }$ denotes the quality or quantity of version i and $p _ { i }$ denotes the price of version $i ( 1 \leq i \leq n )$ , where n represents the number of versions.2 The second-degree price discrimination problem is addressed in Maskin and Riley [20] under a monopolistic pricing framework with information asymmetry. However, the extant literature on bundling has not fully addressed the problem of finding the optimal n. Exceptions are the works by Bhargava and Choudhary [2, 3]. In Bhargava and Choudhary [2], the authors found that it is optimal to offer only one version when there are no network externalities and consumers’ marginal utility is constant. In their later work [3], which provides insights to the relationship between network externalities and the optimal number of versions, they found that, in the presence of network externalities and under the constant marginal utility assumption, it becomes optimal for the firm to offer only two versions. This paper discards the constant marginal utility assumption, which, as we later explain, may lead to problematic conclusions about the optimal number of versions that the firm should offer. Instead, we assume marginal utility to be decreasing to better reflect the typical information overload problem customers face today; that is, the marginal utility of an additional piece of news, music, or other information decreases as consumption increases [14]. Similarly, in the context of quality versioning, it is reasonable to assume that different-quality versions of a software product (e.g., Windows Vista Home Basic, Home Premium, Business, and Ultimate) offer decreasing marginal utility in each upgrade. Under this assumption of decreasing marginal utility, we analyze the problem following the procedure in Bhargava and Choudhary [2].

Classical economics research on second-degree price discrimination suggests that when q and consumer taste or demand for quality (often denoted by θ) are both continuous, the firm should offer an infinite number of versions [20].3 If q is discrete (as in the context of customized bundling, which we discuss shortly) and if the number of goods produced by the firm and the number of consumer types are many, then the discrete problem can be approximated by a continuous model, implying that the optimal number of versions in the discrete case should also be many. However, in practice, regardless of whether discrimination is based on quality or quantity, it is unusual for a firm to offer a large number of versions.

One reason to explain the discrepancy between analytical findings in the literature and actual business practices is that too many options could confuse consumers [6, 13, 18]. It also increases the cost of managing the menu [29]. Although determining the optimal number of versions has not been fully addressed in the literature, guidelines based on consumer psychology exist. Shapiro and Varian [22] suggest that firms should adopt Goldilocks pricing, in which three versions (low, medium, and high) are offered. People would choose the medium version because of extremeness aversion (i.e., most people avoid choosing versions at the extreme, whether it is “extremely high” or “extremely low”). It is still not clear, however, why the firm should not offer four or five versions, while expecting only the middle versions to be effective.

This paper provides a rigorous approach to determine the optimal number of versions for information goods and offers an explanation to the discrepancies in results reported in the existing literature. It further takes into consideration both menu cost and cognitive cost, which are versioning-related costs. Menu cost here is defined as the cost of managing the different bundle offerings, consistent with Wu and Anandalingam [29]; and cognitive cost refers to the cost imposed on the consumers when they evaluate bundle offerings to determine the bundle size that yields the highest consumer utility. While the model is developed in the context of customized bundling, the results are applicable to the general quality versioning problem (where version quality could be defined by other dimensions such as speed, physical size, response rate, etc.).

We focus our analysis on customized bundling, where a monopolistic firm offers a menu of different-sized bundles (versions), which serves as a self-selection device to sort consumer types. Constrained by the bundle size they self-select into, consumers pick items they most prefer among all information goods produced by the firm into the bundle. ## Literature Review

## Product Versioning

THE AIM OF VERSIONING IS TO LET CONSUMERS self-select into different versions according to their willingness to pay so that the high-consumer types purchase the high versions and the low-consumer types purchase the low versions. In order to induce self-selection, the firm often deliberately reduces the quality of the lower versions so that the higher types do not find them too attractive. For example, IBM deliberately degraded the performance of LaserPrinter Series E, which was functionally identical to the standard LaserPrinter but operated at only half the speed [22]. Consider a simple case in which there are only two types of consumers. In order to satisfy the self-selection or incentive compatibility constraints, the firm must make sure that the surplus a high-type consumer receives from the high version is at least as good as that he or she receives from the low version. Therefore, unlike first-degree price discrimination, the firm cannot extract consumer surplus completely with versioning.

The optimal versioning problem is harder to solve than the first-degree or third-degree price discrimination problems. This is because the incentive compatibility constraints can easily become intractable when the number of types is large. Moorthy’s study [21] represents one of the earliest studies to solve for the optimal versioning problem in a market with an arbitrary number of consumer types. It provides an algorithm to solve for the optimal number of versions, under the assumption that consumer utility is linear in quality and that production cost is convex in quality.

Bhargava and Choudhary’s [2] and Varian’s [26] studies consider versioning of information goods in a market with two types of consumers. Varian [26] shows that versioning is optimal whereas Bhargava and Choudhary [2] show that versioning is never optimal. To explain these apparently contradictory findings, a follow-up paper of Bhargava and Choudhary [4] shows that introducing a lower version is optimal only when low-type consumers have a greater ratio of valuations than high-type consumers.

The optimal versioning problem is also closely related to the nonlinear pricing problem. The aim of nonlinear pricing is to encourage people with a high demand for a single good (e.g., electricity) to consume more. Quantity discounts are often the means to offer such incentives so that the low-demand consumers continue to consume little but the high-demand consumers are encouraged to consume more. It is not hard to see the parallels between nonlinear pricing and versioning as they both require solving a set of incentive compatibility constraints. In fact, insights from classical nonlinear pricing papers [20, 24] can be readily extended to the customized bundling problem.

## Customized Bundling

The aim of customization is to better serve customers’ needs by providing personalized goods and services. In customized bundling, a monopolistic firm offers a menu of different-sized bundles, which serves as a self-selection device to differentiate different consumer types. Chen [7] attempts to analyze customized bundling by mixed-integer programming. Brooks et al. [5] study the problem under the name of “nonlinear pricing” by continuous approximation and simulations. Hitt and Chen [17] determine the conditions under which a general bundling problem (addressed in Hanson and Martin [16]) can be reduced to a customized bundling problem. This paper makes reference to the equivalence of the customized bundling and nonlinear pricing problems under the Hitt and Chen conditions [17]. While result 3 of Hitt and Chen characterizes the optimal customized bundling solution for “multiple customer segments” (equivalent to multiple consumer types defined by θ in this paper), the rest of their results (results 4 to 7) focus on the analysis of a single segment (or type). The rationale behind the analysis of a single segment is that the firm is assumed to be able to offer tailored bundles to each segment, eliminating the need to consider incentive compatibility constraints between segments [17]. In other words, the firm can consider each segment (or type) separately. The current study may be considered as an extension to Hitt and Chen [17] by considering a more complex situation where there are multiple segments (or types) and consumers are free to conduct personal arbitrage. Thus, the firm’s problem is to design the most profitable incentive-compatible pricing scheme such that all consumers will reveal their types truthfully. ## Few Versions Are Enough to Extract Most of the Consumer Value

P1 is an important result of this paper and has a significant business implication: it implies that few versions are needed to extract most of the consumer value that can potentially be earned by second-degree price discrimination. To illustrate this, we first note that the total consumer value in this market is given by

$$
\int_ {0} ^ {Q} V (\theta , q) f (\theta) d \theta = \int_ {0} ^ {Q} \frac {v _ {0} \theta}{2} \frac {1}{Q} d \theta = \frac {v _ {0} Q}{4},\tag{2}
$$

where $f ( \boldsymbol { \theta } )$ denotes the density of θ. Equation (2) is the profit the firm can earn if it has perfect knowledge of each consumer’s type and adopts first-degree price discrimination. By comparing Equations (1) and (2), it can be seen that the maximum profit the firm can earn from versioning is when $n  \infty .$ , and the profit so derived is only twothirds—that is, $( 1 / 6 ) / ( 1 / 4 ) \times 1 0 0 = 6 7$ percent—of that of first-degree price discrimination under full information. Versioning cannot earn 100 percent of all consumer value because information asymmetry allows consumers to earn “information rents.” More interestingly, Equation (1) suggests that the firm requires only two versions to achieve almost 90 percent of the potential profit of versioning $( [ ( 2 + 1 ) ^ { 2 } - 1 ] / ( 2 + 1 ) ^ { 2 } =$ $8 / 9 \approx 9 0 \ \mathrm { p e r c e n t } )$ . Obviously, the comparison is likely to be different under a different model setup, but that the marginal benefit of an additional version decreases should still hold. Now, if adding a version entails extra costs, the firm may not want to offer many versions. This explains why firms often offer fewer versions than the existing number of consumer types in the market. For example, there are only three versions for Adobe Acrobat for sale (Elements, Standard, and Professional) and two versions for Adobe Creative Suite (Standard and Premium). Also, the Economist Web-Only Edition offers only the monthly and annual subscription schemes.

## Differences in Findings from Previous Studies

Our model setup so far is very similar to that of Bhargava and Choudhary’s study [2]. However, Bhargava and Choudhary show that it is optimal to offer only one version. The discrepancy in fact comes from the choice of the utility function. In Bhargava and Choudhary [2], marginal utility is constant. This setup leads to repeated roots, that ${ \mathrm { i s } } ,$ all roots coincide at the same point, suggesting that offering only one version is optimal. As an extension to Bhargava and Choudhary [2], they [3] study the versioning problem in the presence of network externalities. The model again employs a constant marginal utility function, and this time it is suggested that the optimal number of versions is two. Bhargava and Choudhary [2] make a very important contribution by setting up a mathematical procedure that subsequent work, including the current study, can follow. Together with Bhargava and Choudhary [3], the two studies [2, 3] suggest that network externalities provide the firm more incentives to version its product. This result offers good business insights for practitioners and market strategists. However, we would like to point out in this paper that some of the results in these studies may be too specific and not generalizable to other settings.

## Effects of Menu Cost on Firms

WU AND ANANDALINGAM [29] CONSIDER THE EFFECTS of menu cost on the optimal number of versions. Menu cost consists of overhead costs of managing a sales menu. For example, if an Internet shop updates its version prices, it has to update the price of each version on its Web site. Effective use of information technology (IT) can drive menu cost down. For example, there are applications that provide direct access to the firm’s database so that Web menus can be updated dynamically [27].

Suppose menu cost is $M ( n )$ , which is assumed to be increasing in $n ;$ the firm’s profit becomes

$$
\Pi^ {*} (n) = \frac {(n + 1) ^ {2} - 1}{(n + 1) ^ {2}} \times \frac {Q v _ {0}}{6} - M (n).
$$

$$
\frac {Q v _ {0}}{3 (n + 1) ^ {3}} M ^ {\prime} (n).\tag{3}
$$

Proposition 2: Menu cost decreases the optimal number of versions offered.

For example, suppose menu cost is given by

$$

$$

As in Wu and Anandalingam [29], Equation (4) specifies that menu cost increases linearly with the number of versions. a is interpreted as the administrative cost associated with managing the menu. A small a represents effective use of IT. The management of a menu of versions is usually assisted by IT and one can avoid the situation where an increase in the number of versions increases menu cost exponentially. Therefore, a convex menu cost function is not assumed. Combining Equations (3) and (4), we derive the optimal number of versions as follows:

$$

The left-hand side of the equation is the derivative of the optimal profit function with respect to n, and it represents the marginal benefit of an additional version (to the firm). Because the marginal benefit of an additional version is always decreasing and approaches zero as $n  \infty ,$ any positive marginal cost would exceed the marginal benefit at some finite n, thus reducing the optimal number of versions. ## Effects of Cognitive Cost

UNIT SALE AND PURE BUNDLING ARE PRICING SCHEMES that involve a one-dimensional price construct—one consisting of a single number (e.g., \$1.99 per unit or \$9.99 per bundle). Versioning, on the other hand, requires the communication of more complex price information using multidimensional prices. Multidimensional prices consist of multiple numbers (e.g., \$3.99 for four units, and \$8.99 for 10 units). In choosing between alterative versions, the consumer has to carry out mental computations to compare their relative values. Estelami [12, 13] provides empirical evidence that consumers find it more difficult evaluating multidimensional prices. Consumer surveys also suggest that consumers in general prefer simpler pricing schemes [23].

Experimental studies [19] have shown that people are more likely to make a purchase when offered a limited array of offers than a wide range of choices. Subsequent satisfaction is also higher if the selection comes from a smaller choice set. These empirical findings are striking because they challenge the fundamental assumption underlying the economic theory of rationality. They suggest that cognitive cost exists when there are multiple alternatives. Furthermore, consumers take cognitive cost as part of the consumption experience; once a selection is made (i.e., when cognitive cost should theoretically become a sunk cost), utility from the consumption of that selection continues to be affected by cognitive cost.

In view of the mounting empirical evidence on cognitive cost, it becomes desirable to incorporate it in our model. As pointed out in Estelami [13], it is reasonable to assume that IT does not help reduce cognitive cost as much as it does menu cost. The current model provides insights on the business implications of cognitive cost, which is modeled here to directly reduce utility, consistent with Iyengar and Lepper [19].

## Theoretical Contribution and Business Implications

## Theoretical Contribution

THIS PAPER ADDRESSES THE IMPORTANT QUESTION OF “how many versions?” in the context of customized bundling of information goods. The current work contributes to the literature of pricing of information goods by offering an explanation to the discrepancies in the results reported in various studies in the literature. We also show that the marginal benefit of an additional version (to the firm) decreases in the number of version. Furthermore, the optimal number of bundles is endogenously determined in the presence of positive menu cost and cognitive cost. Our analytical findings are consistent with general observations of limited bundle offers in the market, providing analytical support of the Goldilocks pricing scheme commonly used by information providers.

Two versioning-related costs identified in the literature—menu cost and cognitive cost—are analyzed. It is shown that cognitive cost has more profound effects on versioning than does menu cost. Both costs decrease the optimal number of versions; however, for a given number of versions, menu cost does not affect the bundle sizes or prices. An increase in cognitive cost, on the other hand, tends to increase sizes and decrease prices.

## Business Implications

The results of this paper have significant business applications. Guidelines are provided to determine the key decision variables in versioning—namely, $n ^ { * }$ and $\{ q _ { i } ^ { * } , p _ { i } ^ { * } \}$ . In the absence of versioning-related costs, it is best to offer as many versions as there are consumer types. However, the marginal benefit of an extra version (to the firm) decreases as the number of versions in the menu increases. The presence of menu cost and cognitive cost associated with versioning reduces the optimal number of versions. The results suggest that, unless menu cost and cognitive cost are sufficiently large, it is always better to version. In other words, pure bundling, which is a single-version strategy, is probably not the best strategy.

Effective use of IT allows firms to reduce the cost of managing a menu of versions. Relative to cognitive cost, menu cost is believed to be less important a factor in reducing the optimal number of versions. Cognitive cost that is borne by consumers is likely to be the key factor that prevents the versioning strategy from being exploited to its maximum potential. The firm should make the version offerings as easy to understand for the consumers as possible. Reducing cognitive cost gives the firm more room to add more versions and earn greater profit.

Take the latest Windows operating system, Windows Vista, as an example. Excluding the Starter Edition (available only for developing areas) and the Enterprise Edition (which is only available through volume licensing with organizations covered by the Microsoft Software Assurance program), there are four versions—Home Basic, Home Premium, Business, and Ultimate. On the other hand, for Windows XP, there are basically two versions based on quality—Home and Professional. The move to more versions is consistent with our findings on decreasing cognitive cost on the consumer side. First, consumers are likely to have already a number of years of experience using Windows products. They probably already know their computing needs and feel confident making a version choice. Second, Microsoft provides consumers assistance in making their version choice [15]. Finally, to further reduce cognitive cost, Microsoft versions its products by selectively enabling and disabling certain features. This approach to quality differentiation leaves little room for ambiguity, and makes it easy for consumers to compare different editions in the form of a table (e.g., [9]).

Some products are complex in nature and are cognitively demanding to evaluate, such as pension schemes, computer hardware systems, and other high technologies. When different versions of these products are offered, it is impossible to keep cognitive cost low. In this case, our results suggest that the firm should improve the version qualities as a means to alleviate the negative effects of cognitive cost on profit.

Finally, it is found that the marginal benefit of an additional version (to the firm) decreases as more versions are introduced. Therefore, a small number of versions are sufficient to capture the majority of the potential profit from versioning. If the optimal number of versions is small and consumers’ extremeness aversion is further taken into consideration, Goldilocks pricing is likely to be a good versioning strategy as suggested in Shapiro and Varian [22].

## Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Research

THE QUESTION OF HOW MANY VERSIONS THE FIRM SHOULD offer has not been fully addressed in the literature. This paper addresses this interesting problem by incorporating menu cost and cognitive cost in a fairly general model. The procedure employed in this paper can also be applied to other model setups that make use of different utility functions, distribution functions, and cost factors.

Our model has a major limitation. We showed that cognitive cost tends to lower the optimal number of versions, increase the quality of each version, and decrease the price of each version; and we used product complexity as an example to show these effects. In reality, cognitive cost can be affected by other factors such as consumer-specific factors, including education, experience with the product category, and analytical skills. These factors are expected to have similar effects on the optimal number of versions, version quality, and version prices (i.e., they will also lower the optimal number of versions, increase the quality of each version, and decrease the price of each version). However, the second-order effects of these factors may differ depending on the functional forms of the cognitive cost. Future research should look into how these important factors affect the optimal versioning strategy, and more importantly, how they interact with each other and how they affect versioning decisions in different markets.

A number of further research directions are also identified. First, like most previous research on versioning, this paper studies a model where SCP is satisfied. Future research should look at how the absence of SCP affects versioning decisions. Second, future research should address how marginal cost of production affects versioning decisions, given the significant role it plays in the pricing of information goods. Finally, complementarities and substitutabilities between information goods, which have the effect of altering the consumers’ utility functions, should be investigated, as in Venkatesh and Kamakura [28].
