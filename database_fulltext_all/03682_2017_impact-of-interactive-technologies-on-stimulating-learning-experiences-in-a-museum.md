Keywords: Technology-mediated learning (TML) Authenticity Cognitive engagement Immersion Museum technologies Interactivity

## A B S T R A C T

This research proposes a model to assess visitors’ learning and engagement with interactive technologies. We test this model with a <sup>fi</sup>eld study conducted at a French museum where 174 surveys were returned. The results indicate that IT dimensions, namely ease-of-use and interactivity, in<sup>fl</sup>uence emotional processes (authenticity and cognitive engagement), which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence learning. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that when users perceive the interaction with technology as being intuitive and interactive, they experience higher levels of cognitive engagement. We also provide recommendations to professionals of the edutainment sector to use interactive technologies to engage their audiences, thereby ensuring positive learning experiences.

## 1. Introduction

Information technologies (IT) have contributed to changing and enhancing the modes of human learning. Consequently, individuals relying on IT for their education tend to experience a higher level of cognition [3,104]. For instance, Alavi [1] showed that students using group decision support systems had higher interest, developed more skills, and reported a higher overall evaluation of their course. When supporting a learner-centered active learning environment, IT can also encourage critical thinking and can facilitate the understanding of educational materials [2].

To the contrary, other studies indicate that technologymediated learning (TML), such as web-based coursework, is not necessarily more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient than traditional teaching [17,79]. In fact, several conditions need to be met for a successful learning experience to occur. For instance, types of knowledge (declarative vs. conceptual) explain why virtual interaction may result in effective learning in certain situations but not in others [17]. Similarly, IT types or IT dimensions differentially in<sup>fl</sup>uence the learning process [45,57]. Although computer-assisted learning produces factual and procedural learning, virtual environments promote conceptual learning [57]. Self-regulated learning strategies can also in<sup>fl</sup>uence e-learning outcomes [97].

Alavi and Leidner [4] advance psychological processes as a key determinant of learning outcomes and called for more research on this factor. As a matter of fact, research in education indicates that learning results from psychological processes such as enjoyment [58,59], authenticity [46,51,72], and more generally, emotions [10,36]. Wan et al. [99] also con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the importance of analyzing psychological processes in TML research. Cahill and McGaugh [16] showed that emotional arousal (linked to emotional events or experiences) activates hormonal and brain systems that regulate long-term memory, the latter being a fundamental step in learning. In a more recent study on music learning experiences, researchers found that emotions such as af<sup>fi</sup>nity or liking help sustain active and continuous learning, especially for young learners [93]. Similarly, Wan et al. [99] showed that psychological processes can lead to learning effectiveness and satisfaction. A study conducted with classroom students also highlights the effects of positive and negative emotions on strategies for learning. One key <sup>fi</sup>nding is that “positive emotions are related positively to metacognitive strategies, elaboration, organization, and critical thinking” [77,p. 99].

Although emotions and hedonic systems in general have gained more prominence in information systems (IS) research in recent years, little attention has been paid to their potential role in the TML environment [4,11,12,98]. Consequently, this research aims at analyzing the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence learning, more precisely, the role of psychological processes and IT dimensions.

Another limitation that can be noted in the literature on TML is that research tends to focus on classroom or workplace contexts [e.g.,3,6,17,79,91,101], thus neglecting other organizational environments [45]. Although e-learning has become an important

In fact, all people today — youth and adults — spend the majority of their lives learning outside the walls of formal classrooms: in out-ofschool programs, workplaces, internships, and other informal learning experiences such as those offered by libraries and museums [56,p. 4].

Why do museums qualify as learning environments? As a matter of fact, learning represents one of the fundamental missions of a museum [54]. In 2014, the Institute of Museum and Library Services [55], the primary source of federal support for American museums and libraries, explained that 52.7% of its grants to museums were dedicated to learning experiences, compared with 33.1% for collection stewardship and 14.2% for community anchors [55]. This stresses the important role of museums in learning and skill development. Besides this, many museums rely on IT to communicate with their public as technologies positively contribute to visitors’ experiences [9,26,47,59,74].

Despite a growing number of studies that highlight the potential of IT for visitors’ experiences, research is still scarce on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c issue of TML in museums. Therefore, it makes sense to determine the role of IT in how visitors learn. This issue is even more important as people who engage in cultural activities try to escape personal monotony and, instead, look for authentic experiences [64,65]. Visitors’ search for authenticity takes different forms. They may want to see genuine things [15] or be reluctant to interact with virtual copies of artifacts or with any other reproduction [7]. Consequently, it is not evident that IT contributes both to higher learning and to a deeper sense of authenticity in the museum context.

Considering these gaps in the literature, the research questions that guide this study are the following:

How do TML environments affect visitors’ psychological processes and learning outcomes in museums? More precisely, what is the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of technology features – ease of use and interactivity – and emotions (i.e., cognitive engagement and authenticity) on learning outcomes?

The current research aims at measuring both visitors’ affective (i.e., entertaining and authentic) and cognitive (i.e., educational) reactions when they interact with technology. As studying human interaction with technologies is at the heart of human–computer interaction (HCI), the questions addressed by this research should be of interest to HCI researchers as well as to researchers who examine TML issues, education, or affective and emotional reactions. Relying on Alavi and Leidner’s framework [4] of TML, we hope to contribute to IS research in three different ways. First, this research extends the generalizability of the results found in the TML literature to a new environment. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of museums can produce new and original <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Second, this research examines the psychological processes that affect learning and introduces an underinvestigated variable, namely perceived authenticity. Third, we also try to assess the role played by IT features by comparing user reactions to two different technologies. This paper provides insights for practitioners as well. In fact, we provide empirical validation on IT effects in enhancing learning and enjoyment, which are two fundamental missions of museums.

This research is organized as follows. First, we consider how the literature has dealt with TML, and we present the recent developments toward a better understanding of its underlying psychological processes. Second, we introduce our research model and its set of hypotheses. Third, we describe the methodology that was implemented to collect data. Fourth, we perform data analysis and report the results. Fifth, we discuss our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, while the last section concludes with limitations and potential contributions.

## 2. TML: toward a better understanding of the psychological processes

## 2.1. IT dimensions and learning

Learning implies “changes in an individual’s mental models or knowledge representations” [6,p. 405]. To measure these changes in mental models or knowledge representations, it is necessary to assess learning outcomes. According to Gagne (1977, cited in [4,p. 6]), “learning outcomes can be intellectual skills, motor skills, verbal information, cognitive strategy and attitude.” As learning increasingly occur through technologies, we will focus on TML de<sup>fi</sup>ned as:

an environment in which the learner’s interactions with learning materials (readings, assignments, exercises, etc.), peers, and/or instructors are mediated through advanced information technologies [4,p. 2].

Although TML research has mainly analyzed web-based courses [17,79] and group decision support systems [3,6][e.g.,3,6], the technologies that are utilized in this mediation are very diverse. Indeed, technologies can range from a simple instructor console to groupware or virtual reality systems [57]. So, what should be the technical characteristics of a good IT-mediated learning environment?

To address this question, we can turn to results found in previous research, which are quite diverse and mixed in their <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Webster and Hackley [101] found that reliability, technology quality, and perceived medium richness are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictors of attitudes toward distance learning but that perceived medium richness is the only factor in<sup>fl</sup>uencing individuals involvement and participation. Media variety was tested in subsequent research, but Marks et al. [66] did not <sup>fi</sup>nd signi<sup>fi</sup>cant results for the following IT features: audio, video, and PowerPoint use. Marks et al. [66] do not think, however, that their results should be generalized, in that their subjects were making little use of these features. In another study, Arbaugh and Duray [8] also report nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant results on the role of IT characteristics. They tested the role of two classic variables of the technology acceptance model (TAM), namely ease of use and usefulness, but they were unable to verify any relationships with learning.

These fragmented results might be explained by the fact that some researchers addressed IT at the feature level, looking at some functionalities such as the presence of video, audio, and brainstorming, [45] while other studies focused on the system level. Instead, Gupta and Bostrom [45] argue that it is “the structural dimension that the technology provides, which in<sup>fl</sup>uence[s] learning effectiveness” (p. 695). Depending on the system under study, these structural dimensions can vary. For instance, if the research deals with communication systems, then interactivity and richness may be salient dimensions; if it is an information processing system, authenticity and feedback may be more appropriate [45].

Furthermore, Alavi and Leidner [4] in their research commentary suggest that future research should focus on the relationship between IT dimensions and learning processes to show how technology in<sup>fl</sup>uences users’ internal processes (see Fig. 1). Despite this decade-old call for research, to our knowledge, very few studies have examined this relationship. One was Benbunan-Fich and Hiltz [12] wherein they proposed a model in which technology characteristics in<sup>fl</sup>uence learning outcomes through the mediation of psychological processes, such as motivation and participation. These IT characteristics include access, functionality, usability, and reliability. But Benbunan-Fich and Hiltz [12] did not test speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the impact of each of these factors on psychological processes. For this reason, the current research will more fully investigate the relationships among IT, psychological processes, and learning outcomes (Fig. 1).

## 2.2. The role of psychological (Emotional) processes in learning

According to Alavi and Leidner [4], internal psychological processes include “the learner’s cognitive and information processing activities, motivation, interests, and cognitive structures (e.g., memory)” [7,p. 4]. Therefore, internal processes refer both to cognitive and affective reactions that individuals experience when interacting with their environment. Although IS research has historically devoted more research to cognitive processes, the number of studies dealing with affective processes has increased, and this trend will surely continue as calls for research on emotions and hedonic systems multiply [48,105].

Emotions or affect has been integrated into many research frameworks, such as the TAM [28,96], the affective response model [106], the Microsoft usability guidelines [2], the cognitive-affective model of perceived user satisfaction [23], and expectation– discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory [29].

The role played by emotions has been mentioned in the literature from several perspectives. First, researchers have tried to assess the impact of IT dimensions on users’ affective reactions [e.g.,33,107]. For instance, Ethier et al. [33] analyzed the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of website design on user emotional reactions, showing that a positive cognitive appraisal of website quality reinforces perceptions of liking, joy, and pride, while decreasing feelings of frustrations and dislike. Second, research has built nomological nets around speci<sup>fi</sup>c emotions, for example, by adding new facets to a construct or uncovering its antecedents/predictors to better understand the emotional process [e.g.,1,38,44,83,90,100].

Overall, the examination of emotions in IT has not been directed toward an explanation of TML even though research indicates that emotions likely play a role in learning. We present some of these studies in Table 1 below.

These relatively few studies indicate that psychological processes might, indeed, explain increased learning. While cognitive engagement, motivations, and enjoyment have been occasionally included in IS research models to explain IT adoption, the role they play in TML deserves more research. Lin et al. [60] considered the role of IT in explaining enjoyment and learning, but their study was at the system level, comparing two technologies. Other previous studies that include IT characteristics such as interactivity or telepresence demonstrated that <sup>fl</sup>ow positively affects students’ online learning continuance intention [44,83]. Although the authors stress the role of emotions in TML context, they did not assess how <sup>fl</sup>ow in<sup>fl</sup>uences learning per se (a change in individuals’ mental models). Furthermore, the concept of authenticity has received limited attention in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, whereas this factor seems to predict enhanced learning.

Authentic learning is based on “complex and sustained scenarios and cases, where students become immersed in problem solving within realistic situations resembling the contexts where the knowledge they are learning can be realistically applied” [50,p. 60]. Consequently, authentic learning should encourage the analysis of real-world problems. It is important that courses deal with real-world situations for learners to become engaged with and to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the situation. To this question, research tends to say yes because technologies have many affordances, such as interactivity and audio/video features that should facilitate (1) the production of real-world scenarios, (2) learners’ immersion, and (3) higher levels of learning [46,51].

The aforementioned characteristics of authentic learning tend to <sup>fi</sup>t the formal context of schools, but some key aspects of authenticity may be different outside the classroom. As a matter of fact, in informal contexts, such as museums, learning is “non linear, self-paced, voluntary, and exploratory” [86,p.109]. Because this is a voluntary experience, visitors are driven by their curiosity or enthusiasm for a topic. Falk and Dierking [34,p. 105] labeled the museum setting as a “free choice environment” to insist on visitors’ freedom and autonomy to learn. Besides, informal learning in museums is driven by personal and individual factors, such as perceptions of enjoyment and motivations. Indeed, in museums, “visitors’ interests are much more general, and a large part of their general agenda is to have fun” [34,p. 141].

These speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics of the museum experience change the nature of authenticity in comparison to understanding it in the education institutional context. For instance, in the cultural heritage <sup>fi</sup>eld, McCannell [64,65] argues that visitors engage in cultural activities during their trips or spare time to escape personal monotony and, instead, to have authentic experiences. Visitors’ search for authenticity takes different forms. They may want to see genuine things [15] or to meet locals and live like them when traveling [21,65]. Visitors of cultural heritage sites seeking authenticity can also be reluctant to interact with virtual copies of artifacts or with any other reproductions. Instead, they will prefer to have real-world experiences or direct interactions with their environment [7]. Consequently, authenticity in cultural heritage experiences is still linked to the idea of real-world relevance, but the concept has a stronger linkage with the genuineness of lifestyle. It is also a feeling and a negotiable process dependent on visitors’ judgment [21,87]. Because research in museums and tourism has focused on authenticity without considering the presence of various technologies, applying TML <sup>fi</sup>ndings in these cultural settings could bring novel and interesting results to this <sup>fi</sup>eld as well.

We conclude this literature review by noting that, in the context of TML, research on IT dimensions and their relationship with psychological processes is still limited. To <sup>fi</sup>ll in this gap in the literature, we propose, in the next section, a research model that includes this relationship.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

## 3.1. Research model

Our research model (Fig. 2) is inspired by the previous research in TML, in that we followed the structure of two of the most important TML theoretical frameworks by Alavi and Leidner [4] and Gupta and Bostrom [45]. These frameworks recommend the inclusion of psychological processes between IT factors and learning outcomes. The <sup>fi</sup>rst part of our model corresponds to IT dimensions that may play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the learning process. These IT dimensions correspond to the inputs or stimuli that will lead to user experiences. In this, we retained ease-of-use and interactivity from previous work. These IT characteristics appear to have a direct effect on individuals’ psychological processes, namely cognitive engagement (composed of heightened enjoyment, curiosity, and focused immersion) and perceived authenticity. Perceived authenticity is also modeled as an antecedent of cognitive engagement, as previous research indicates that enjoyment and immersion are possible emotional consequences of experiencing authenticity [32]. We also hypothesize that these affective factors are direct antecedents of our learning outcome, namely self-reported learning de<sup>fi</sup>ned as people’s perception of their learning. Our model rationale and its eight hypotheses are justi<sup>fi</sup>ed below.

## 3.2. Hypotheses

3.2.1. The impact of the psychological processes on learning outcomes 3.2.1. The impact of the psychological processes on learning outcomes

There are typically two components of visitors’ experience in museums: education and entertainment. Historically, the emphasis on education has been the <sup>fi</sup>rst preoccupation of museums, but more recently, entertainment has become the second. Presently, some museums even treat both on an equal footing to meet visitors’ expectations. Our research model includes two psychological processes to consider this edutainment trend, namely cognitive engagement and perceived authenticity.

Cognitive engagement describes a state of deep involvement and enjoyment for an individual in a learning situation [101]. Webster and Hackley [101] showed that this concept can be assessed with three dimensions: attention focus, enjoyment, and curiosity. As these three dimensions <sup>fi</sup>t especially well within the museum context, we decided to retain this construct as a psychological process. Pointedly, Shaw [88] produced evidence that enjoyment is one of the most important dimensions for people during their leisure time. Given that the present research addresses IT use in a cultural context of leisure, i.e., visiting a museum, perceived enjoyment should also be a salient dimension. Furthermore, enjoyment is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor of learning in informal learning contexts, such as museums [58,59,73]. Similarly, immersion is supposed to re<sup>fl</sup>ect an entertaining aspect of visitors’ experience and immersion also represents a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant determinant of aesthetic experience [26]. Finally, museum exhibitions should aim at arousing interest and curiosity as many visitors are members of the curiosity-driven public who seek to maximize the value of their visit [34,84].

The literature suggests that cognitive engagement is one of the preconditions for a positive learning experience [101]. More precisely, immersion, curiosity, and enjoyment also correspond to three dimensions of the <sup>fl</sup>ow construct, which has been conceptualized as a predictor of learning. Flow is another concept that designates intense engagement in an activity accompanied with a sensation of deep enjoyment [25]. O’Brien and Toms [71] explain that cognitive engagement and <sup>fl</sup>ow share some attributes and, as such, they may have some common variables in their nomological network. Those who studied the consequences of <sup>fl</sup>ow found that increased learning is one of them. For instance, Ghani [43] identi<sup>fi</sup>es the <sup>fl</sup>ow variable (composed of enjoyment and concentration) to be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant antecedent of learning. Indeed, the more users enjoy their experience and feel immersed in their activity, the more likely they are to increase in knowledge. Hoffman and Novak [52] also posit <sup>fl</sup>ow, including enjoyment and immersion, as an antecedent of learning. Skadberg and Kimmel [90] invited their participants to visit a tourism website, and the results of their study indicated that visitors who experienced <sup>fl</sup>ow also gained more knowledge about the online place they visited. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1 (H1). Cognitive engagement positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences self-reported learning.

The second psychological process included in our research model is perceived authenticity. The relationship between perceptions of authenticity and learning in an IT-mediated environment has received little empirical veri<sup>fi</sup>cation. Surprisingly, TV reality programs supply evidence for the primary connection between authenticity and learning. Hall [46] studied viewer reactions to reality programs and discovered that perceptions of authenticity in<sup>fl</sup>uence learning. Indeed, it is the real-world relevance of TV reality programs that supposedly informs viewers and gives them insights about the world (p. 519). In the museum context, Sigala [89] suggests that authenticity can lead to meaningmaking experiences, enabling visitors to better understand cultural content. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 2 (H2). Perceived authenticity positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences self-reported learning.

Perceptions of authenticity tend to positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence visitors’ affective and cognitive reactions. As an illustration, Hall [46] tested the relationship between authenticity and cognitive involvement and her results indicate that perceptions of authenticity encourage the viewers to think harder about the show and even to imagine themselves in place of the cast members. A multimethodology research conducted with visitors of three theme parks also reports on the effect of authenticity on enjoyment and pleasure [67]. Hence, we propose that:

Hypothesis 3 (H3). Perceived authenticity positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences cognitive engagement.

## 3.2.2. The impact of IT dimensions on psychological processes

Two IT dimensions are included in our research model: interactivity and ease-of-use. Interactivity is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the extent to which users can participate in modifying the form and content of a mediated environment in real time” [92,p. 84]. We decided to analyze the role of interactivity because museums are information-intensive institutions, and thus, interactivity represents one of the ways to provide a dynamic experience to visitors instead of a passive reading experience [30,47]. Furthermore, interactivity is a factor that has been included before in TML models [24,29,45], but its potential impact on psychological processes needs further testing.

Several empirical studies show that interactivity is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor of cognitive engagement. As an illustration, Hall and Bannon [47] tested the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of interactive exhibitions, based on ubiquitous computer technologies, at an Irish museum. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that interactivity has a strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence on children’s engagement and participation in the visit. Likewise, in another museum context, the interactivity of a game-based website leads to higher level of enjoyment than with a text-based website [60]. It seems that the more users perceive the medium as being rich and interactive, the more likely they are to be engaged and involved in their experience [52,101]. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 4 (H4). Interactivity positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences cognitive engagement.

Several researchers discuss the potential of technology characteristics to enhance visitor experience of authenticity. Eco [30] believes that tourists prefer hyperreality and simulations rather than reality. This can be explained by the fact that technology sometimes contributes to a more authentic experience, through interactivity, and therefore has the potential to transform inauthentic into authentic [39]. In addition, Reisinger and Steiner [81] assert that interpretive materials such as audioguides or computers can positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence a tourist’s experience of authenticity by adding more interactivity to the experience. Considering the example of the Maori culture, Taylor [95] notes that old media such as brochures or postcards tend to display stereotypes or <sup>fi</sup>xed ideas, such as “ceremonial costumes or cooking scenes” (p. 20). Conversely, Taylor [95] thinks that IT has the potential to display dynamic images, videos, or interactive content that will better present a culture in a more authentic way.

The effects of interactivity on authenticity are not just unsubstantiated notions because design research has validated these claims. Epstein and Vergani [32] relied on the theoretical background of authenticity to develop an IT artifact, a mobile technology named the “History and Unwired Media,” for assisting tourists visiting Venice, Italy. Visitors particularly appreciated the interactivity of this device, an attribute that promotes intimacy with and immersion into the environment, and it also stimulates connections with the Venetian historical characters and overall contribute to perceptions of authenticity. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 5 (H5). Interactivity positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences perceived authenticity.

We selected ease-of-use rather than usefulness, as a second IT dimension, because ease-of-use plays a more important role in hedonic contexts [49,61]. Indeed, in hedonic contexts, individuals are driven by intrinsic motivations such as interest and enjoyment. As museums belong to the leisure industry and clearly differ from workplace and classroom contexts by offering informal learning, measuring ease-of-use of museum technologies is more relevant.

Research provides support for perceived ease-of-use as a predictor of perceived enjoyment [28]. Indeed, it is reasonable to posit that users will better appreciate their experience with technology if the latter is easy to use. Furthermore, Hsu and Lu’s examination [53] of the behaviors of online game users indicate that ease-of-use is a highly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor of <sup>fl</sup>ow, a factor composed of enjoyment and concentration in their research. Consequently, the aforementioned literature warrants the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 6 (H6). Perceived ease-of-use positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences cognitive engagement.

Few studies have examined the link between ease-of-use and authenticity. Featherman et al. [35] analyzed the impact of perceived ease-of-use on arti<sup>fi</sup>ciality. Their study revealed that perceived ease-of-use tends to negatively correlate with arti<sup>fi</sup>ciality. Stated in a more straightforward way, ease-of-use is associated with perceptions of authenticity. Indeed, users interacting with a technology will <sup>fi</sup>nd their experience more natural, vivid, and authentic when the use of the technology is intuitive. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 7 (H7). Perceived ease-of-use positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences perceived authenticity.

Perceived ease-of-use was used as a predictor of interactivity in at least one IS study, which was by Skadberg and Kimmel [90]. They collected 272 questionnaires from subjects who interacted with a tourism website. The results indicate that when users perceive a system as being intuitive to operate, they can better experience the interactive features of the technology. Thus, we hypothesize as follows:

Hypothesis 8 (H8). Perceived ease-of-use positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences interactivity.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Research design

The research design was a <sup>fi</sup>eld study conducted at the National Museum of the History of Immigration (NMHI), a French museum located in Paris. This museum was selected as the setting for our study for several reasons. First, learning and affective experiences are foremost among the objectives of this museum, which aim at educating the public and providing an emotional experience on the history of immigration. As stated by the Director of the Museum [22], “[their] mission is to transmit knowledge, sharing experiences and emotions.” Second, the various theoretical features of authenticity are also addressed by the museum in its approach to present people’s traditions, memories, and history. Hence, the museum goals align well with our research variables. Third, the presence of technologies in the museum setting was requisite to assess visitors’ reactions to IT. The NMHI offers different types of IT for public use, including interactive kiosks, digitally linked audioguides, interactive games, and videos displayed on TV screens.

To gain access to this setting, the director of the IT department was <sup>fi</sup>rst contacted and then met personally for an introduction to the project. An agreement to conduct the study was issued a few weeks later, which included an of<sup>fi</sup>cial badge as a sign of our professional af<sup>fi</sup>liation to the museum. The museum collaboration and support gave more credibility to the study when querying the visitors.

We relied on a cross-sectional survey to test our different hypotheses. Although a longitudinal design would have been more robust, the inability to track visitors over time meant that this design was not possible. Indeed, statistics tend to show that most of the museum public is composed of <sup>fi</sup>rst time visitors. Therefore, examining the evolution of user perceptions at different points in time would have yielded very limited data. Similarly, an experimental design was not considered because it would be too constraining for the visitors. For instance, forcing the visitors to use only speci<sup>fi</sup>c technologies, while touring the exhibition, would have been detrimental to their visit. To let our participants fully experience their museum visit, the survey data gathering technique appeared as most appropriate. This way, the participants were free to use any technology available in the exhibition.

## 4.1.1. Stimulus technologies

While the stimulation was the subjects’ interaction with the technologies available at the NMHI, we did not gather data on visitors’ perceptions on all technologies provided by the museum. We considered that the videos displayed on TV screens were too simple a technology because they are part of everyday life. Thus, we were afraid that variance and inferences could not be drawn from this interaction. Regarding, the three game stations provided at the end of the exhibition, we realized that many visitors were leaving without using them. Consequently, for purposes of comparison, we decided to focus on two different types of technologies represented by: (1) the digitally linked audioguides and (2) the interactive kiosks.

The digitally linked audioguide is the primary technology of the museum because its use is required to hear the content of the videos and audio kiosks. In fact, the permanent exhibition has been designed in a way that gives limited space to text panels explaining the exhibits. The intent of NMHI is to provide an immersive and interactive experience to visitors, so that a large part of the museum content is diffused through the DLA. Furthermore, the DLA appears to be a relevant digital technology because this device combines mobility and interactivity. Indeed, NMHI audioguides are location sensitive and linked via infrared to dispense the content automatically. They also include a stylus that visitors can use to target and launch content of interest.

The second class of technologies that we included in this research is the set of interactive kiosks. These interactive kiosks are available throughout the exhibition and provide more details and information about the history of French immigration. They are also based on a question–answer approach: visitors select a question in the computer menu and obtain an answer (see Appendix A for a more detailed presentation of these two technologies).

It is noteworthy that even though the questionnaire focused on two types of IT, the visitors were free to interact with all the museum technologies, and thus, they had the choice to watch videos, read text panels, listen to audio content, and interact with interactive kiosks, as consistent with the methodological design.

## 4.2. Research instrument

As part of our implementation of the <sup>fi</sup>eld study methodology, we used the data collection technique of the questionnaire [94]. The questionnaire distributed to visitors was composed of existing scales for the primary IS constructs. Learning was measured with the self-reported learning scales of Alavi [3]. Perceived ease-of-use items were originally developed by Davis [27]. Heightened enjoyment, curiosity, and focused immersion were based on Agarwal and Karahanna, [1] as these dimensions are also present in cognitive absorption. Perceived authenticity (PA) scales were adapted from Featherman et al. [35]. Finally, interactivity items were adapted from Skadberg and Kimmel [90]. The instrument can be found in Appendix B.

Special care was taken to include reversed scales in our instrument to ensure visitors paid attention to the scale items. As the questionnaires were self-administered, it was also a good way to ensure that participants carefully <sup>fi</sup>lled in the questionnaire. It is also important to mention that the questions were the same for the entire sample. In fact, even though we used the same items, we surveyed visitors about their interaction with two different types of technologies: (1) the digitally linked audioguide and (2) interactive kiosks. Second, this questionnaire was distributed exclusively in French. It was not translated to other languages because the NMHI principally draws Francophones. This is consistent with the fact that the museum content (video, audio, and text panels) was only available in French.

## 4.3. Sampling procedures

We took the following approach in surveying NMHI visitors. To include the maximum number of persons, a researcher was positioned at the museum entrance where visitors borrow the DLAs. Thus, we stayed behind the desk with the employees in charge of: (a) providing guidance to visitors and (b) of distributing the free audioguides required for visiting the permanent exhibition. This positioning was strategic because visitors had to return to this desk at the end of their visit to return the DLAs. We took advantage of this time to ask visitors to give feedback regarding their interaction with the museum technologies. We followed the same approach to collect data on DLAs and on interactive kiosks because all NMHI visitors had to return to the DLA kiosk before exiting. Surveying the visitors immediately at the end of their visit proved to be a good way to ensure that their experience was still clear and fresh to them. Although the questionnaires were selfadministered, meaning that each participant <sup>fi</sup>lled in the questionnaire by himself/herself, a researcher stayed close to the participants in case they needed assistance.

Sampling was randomized to the greatest extent possible. We conducted the study during both weekends and weeks, so that different types of visitors would be included. During the week, the

## 5. Data analysis

SPSS and SmartPLS [82] were the tools used for data analysis. To conduct the analyses, we had to delete some questionnaires that were unusable because of too many unanswered questions. Hence, our <sup>fi</sup>nal sample consisted of 174 questionnaires. When using PLS, Gefen et al. [41] recommend a minimal sample size of at least 10 times, the number of items in the most complex construct. Our most complex construct, which is “cognitive engagement,” has nine items, so 90 participants would be the minimum sample size. Our realized sample was 174 participants, which is well above this minimum.

In this research, we had not put forth hypotheses about the different effects of technology even though two different types of technologies were involved. However, we could not treat these two independent samples as a unique sample without <sup>fi</sup>rst examining whether the samples were equivalent and, if they were, could be pooled (this methodological approach was also used by Pavlou and Gefen [76]).

To test the difference between the samples, we used the Mann– Whitney test, a test commonly used for ordinal/nonparametric variables. The Mann–Whitney test allows us to test the null hypothesis that two independent samples come from the same population. The test variables were gender, job, place of residence, and time spent in the museum, while the grouping variable was IT type (coded “1 = DLAs” and “0 = interactive kiosks”). Age was not included in these tests because it is a parametric variable, but to consider its potential effects on our dependent variables (DVs), we tested it as a control variable (this test is reported below in the section “Structural Model”). Results of the Mann–Whitney test are shown in Table 2 below. None of the tests were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, meaning that the two groups do not differ on these demographics, and therefore, we can infer that systematic bias is not observed in their responses. Thus, we conclude that we can pool the data.

The descriptive statistics indicate the participation of a larger proportion of females, with 64.4% women compared with 35.6% men. This result is not surprising, though, and is in keeping with statistics on French museum attendance, which also report a feminization of cultural practices [68]. Our results also re<sup>fl</sup>ect our desire to represent the larger public as much as possible with a sample of participants aged from 18 to 73 years. The mean age was 37 years.

Most participants were <sup>fi</sup>rst-time visitors (92%), which also explains why the majority of NMHI visitors spent >1 h in the museum. ## 5.3. Structural model

The structural model refers to the relationships (paths) between the different latent variables [19]. Hence, in this second step, we looked at the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients to determine the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of our hypotheses.

As shown in Table 7 and Fig. 3, seven out of eight hypotheses were validated, providing strong support for our research model. Except for H2, path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 0.05 alpha level. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, cognitive engagement positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences self-reported learning (B = 0.789, p < 0.001), validating H1. As hypothesized, a positive assessment of perceived authenticity has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on cognitive engagement (B = 0.230, p < 0.001); thus. H3 is validated. Nevertheless, perceived authenticity does not in<sup>fl</sup>uence self-reported learning (B = 0.008); hence, H2 is not validated.

Interactivity is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor of cognitive engagement (0.559, p < 0.001) and perceived authenticity (0.409, p < 0.001); thus, H4 and H5 are validated. Similarly, perceived ease-of-use has a positive effect on cognitive engagement (B = 0.198, p < 0.005), perceived authenticity (B = 0.324, p < 0.001), and interactivity (0.453, p < 0.001). Thus, H6, H7, and H8 are validated.

Age was included as a control variable, and we tested its potential effects on our DVs, namely cognitive engagement (B = 0.089), perceived authenticity (B = 0.045), ease-of-use (B = 0.036), and self-reported learning (B = 0.075). As none of these relationships were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, we can conclude that age does not show any effect on visitors’ perceptions. ## 6. Discussion

All our hypotheses are validated except H2. Regarding the role of the two IT dimensions, we can say that both ease-of-use and interactivity of these two technological mediators are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictors of psychological processes. When users perceive the interaction with the technology as being intuitive and interactive, they experience higher levels of cognitive engagement, which means that their involvement in the visit is improved. Similarly, these perceptions of ease-of-use and interactivity contribute to perceived authenticity. Consequently, the study provides evidence that IT dimensions play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in explaining emotional processes, thus supporting Boehner et al.’s [13] thesis. According to Boehner et al. [13], emotions are an interactional phenomenon. In effect, Boehner et al. [13] calls into question the informational stream that views emotions as an objective phenomenon that should be measured in a laboratory. Conversely, the authors believe that emotions are complex phenomena that are socially constructed and that emerge in interactions. This interactional thesis of Boehner et al. [13] suggests that there can be a renegotiation of the role of technologies as they should help individuals express and understand their emotions. The current research also shows that the use of the technologies available in the museum, namely audioguides and interactive kiosks, induces emotional reactions. O’Brien and Toms [71] assert that “successful technologies are not just usable; they engage users” (p. 938).

Although perceived authenticity positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences cognitive engagement, it does not show any effect on learning. This nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant result may be explained by the fact that the scale used to measure authenticity focuses on the genuineness of the experience, which is one of the main expectations of visitors during cultural trips [65]. However, this conceptualization of authenticity may be more related to other experiential variables such as cognitive engagement than to learning factors. Researchers from the educational <sup>fi</sup>eld identify further dimensions of authentic learning, such as problem-based activities and opportunities for re<sup>fl</sup>ection [51]. These other facets of authenticity that are more cognitive oriented may better relate to learning. As such, cognitive engagement is the only predictor of learning in our research. This does support the previous studies, which showed deep involvement and enjoyment in a task are a necessary condition to increase learning [25,90,101].

The use of IT contributes both to the learning and enjoyment of visitors. More precisely, the visitors who interacted with the DLAs

Parry [75] analyzed the evolution of theories in the “museum computing” sub<sup>fi</sup>eld, and he highlighted two different discourses on IT. Although some researchers encourage the presence of IT in museums, others view this technology as a source of inauthenticity and anxiety for the public. This research shows that, au contraire, the use of IT during a museum visit is not incompatible with perceptions of authenticity. Our study adds new counter evidence supporting the role of authenticity and IT in enhancing the visitors’ experience.

## 6.1. Contributions

## 6.1.1. Theoretical contributions

This research contributes in several ways to the body of IS research that investigates TML issues. First, the IT artifact is at the heart of our research, as we analyzed technologies both at the system level (two technologies are involved, audio devices and interactive kiosks) and at the IT dimension level (when we assess the effects of ease-of-use and interactivity). This double level of analysis allows us to shed more light on the critical design factors of IT-mediated environments. Because previous research had inconsistent results regarding the role played by IT features, Gupta and Bostrom [45] invite researchers to look at IT dimensions. This research presents the evidence that IT dimensions in<sup>fl</sup>uence the psychological processes that in turn will in<sup>fl</sup>uence learning. Furthermore, while previous research has principally analyzed web-based platforms or group decision support systems [4,98], the current research examines underinvestigated technologies of the cultural heritage sector, namely digitally linked audioguides and interactive kiosks. These systems contribute to (informal) learning, as curators use them to convey scienti<sup>fi</sup>c content in a more entertaining way to the public.

Second, this research had another ambition, which was investigating the role played by IT dimensions and psychological processes in TML, and thus trying to answer the important research question posed by Alavi and Leidner [5]: “How does technology enhance learning?” (p. 5) Through this question, Alavi and Leidner [5] have called for more research on the relationships among technology and environmental factors, and instructional and psychological processes. Although we were not able to test the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of all these contingency factors, we focused on a number of psychological mechanisms. We included two processes in our model: cognitive engagement, itself composed of three subdimensions, and authenticity. Although authenticity has been addressed in the education <sup>fi</sup>eld [51], researchers are still hesitant to integrate this concept into IS frameworks. Our research provides a <sup>fi</sup>rst step in this direction.

Our research indicates that cognitive engagement is a strong predictor of learning outcomes. More precisely, experiencing immersion, enjoyment, and curiosity lead to a higher level of learning. “Enjoyment of using digital learning” [62]([62] p. 745) is one of the four blue ocean themes identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Liu [62] for investigation in future TML studies. Therefore, by testing the role of heightened enjoyment, our research has resonance not only in IS research but also in educational theory. Recent published studies especially reinforce our argument that emotions deserve further attention in the context of technology-mediated learning [44,83]. More precisely, these two studies demonstrate the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role of <sup>fl</sup>ow (an emotional process) to explain students’ continuance intentions of e-learning solutions.

Third, we think that the concept of authenticity should gain more prominence in IS research with the steady development of virtualization. Systems such as virtual reality or immersive environments are increasingly common, and their use to educate or entertain the general public should continue to grow as companies keep investing in these platforms [31,85]. It is reasonable to say that with higher usage of virtual classrooms, the issue of authenticity will be even more compelling. In addition to 3D systems, many businesses now invest in serious games for end-user training programs. Consequently, future research can apply the concept of authenticity in these settings to see whether users also look for more authenticity in such contexts. Authenticity may explain learning in these contexts, but it can also explain IT usage, IT adoption, or other IT-related variables.

It is noteworthy to mention that our research model, though tested in a museum context, could de<sup>fi</sup>nitely be applied to the classroom setting where authentic e-learning environment has been examined [51]. However, in this research, we relied on Featherman et al.’s [35] scales to measure authenticity as they <sup>fi</sup>t the museum context quite well. In the classroom context, it seems important to reuse scale measurement that match the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of authentic learning [50,51].

Our research highlighted authenticity as a new antecedent of cognitive engagement, thus contributing to IS research on affect and emotions. But heightened enjoyment, curiosity, and focused immersion are also dimensions of <sup>fl</sup>ow [25] and cognitive absorption [1]. Therefore, authenticity probably shares further connections with other emotional processes.

Fourth, several researchers in the HCI <sup>fi</sup>eld call for more studies measuring IT phenomena in a natural, or real-world, context. For instance, Finneran and Zhang [37] encourage more research on the experience of <sup>fl</sup>ow occurring in a naturalistic context. Boehner et al. [13] also urge researchers to assess emotions as they occur in daily life.

Given the pervasiveness of computing technology in our everyday lives and its concomitant societal impact, it is essential that we address people’s actual lived emotional experiences ([13], p. 289).

By surveying visitors in a real museum setting, the present research contributes to: (1) the study of emotions as experienced by visitors and (2) the study of information systems in their context of use. Generally, research assessing visitors’ reactions toward IT has relied on laboratory experiments that simulate user environments. These studies also measure intentions rather than actual behaviors. By surveying visitors who interacted with IT in a natural life context (leisure time), we are very close to real-life experiences. Consequently, this research can contribute to building the IS and HCI research traditions in natural contexts.

## 6.1.2. Managerial contributions

This research has a number of practical contributions. This research examines visitors’ experience with technologies, thus providing museum managers and professionals a better understanding of their customers. More precisely, this research examines both entertainment (heightened enjoyment, curiosity, focused immersion, and authenticity) and learning responses of visitors. According to the International Council of Museums [54], enjoyment and education of the public correspond to the core missions of museums. Therefore, our research focuses on the key levers of cultural institutions. These factors are also important for cultural institutional business, as providing a positive experience to visitors can encourage repeated visits. Even, while the aim of cultural institutions is not to pro<sup>fi</sup>t, they still need to raise money over and beyond expenses to satisfy new goals for ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in the modern era. Our study shows that adequate design and positive reactions to IT dimensions contribute to a positive emotional experience and ultimately to increased learning.

We believe that our research can also bear implications for business, as an increasing number of museums invest in digital strategies, but lack feedback on customers’ perceptions of their offers. First, practitioners of the edutainment sector should consider interactive technologies in their offer. This research suggests that digitally linked audioguides or interactive kiosks are relevant solutions that could be implemented to enhance learning. However, these two systems favor a learner–content type of interaction. Indeed, Moore [69] identi<sup>fi</sup>es three types of interactions: (a) learner–content interaction, (b) learner–instructor interaction, and (c) learner–learner interaction. Because the technologies provided at NMHI neither enable communication with curators or discussion with an instructor nor do they encourage social interactions between visitors, we can consider that the DLAs and interactive kiosks both offer a learner–content interaction.

Second, with the development of social media and mobile technologies, interactivity could be extended and opened. Indeed, online platforms or mobile applications allow professionals to discuss with the public in real time. For instance, professionals can send push messages on visitors’ smartphone to give them directions or offer additional tutorials. With platforms such as Facebook or Twitter, artists or curators can also interact with visitors by answering questions or enriching the presentation of art pieces, thus contributing to a sense of authenticity and increasing visitors’ learning.

## 6.2. Limitations and future research

Although <sup>fi</sup>eld studies do not generally allow for strong causal inference, we measured our DVs immediately after participants had experienced the use of these technologies. Thus, the outcome variables were measured after their antecedent experiences, setting up conditions for, to some extent at least, the DVs succeeding the independent variables (IVs). Even though gathering IVs and DVs through the same instrument can create problems in common method bias, we tested for and ruled out this effect. Therefore, we believe that there is some level of internal validity built into the design of the <sup>fi</sup>eld study.

While using real museum visitors to test our research model certainly added to the external validity of the work, it also adds complexity. Markedly, because the participants were tired at the end of their visit or had to spend precious time to participate in the research, we had to create a shorter instrument than we would have preferred. The result of this was that we were unable to include all the variables identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Chou [20] as playing a role in interactivity. Furthermore, our research model principally accounts for positive psychological processes. However, uses of IT in museums may produce negative outcomes such as stress, anxiety, frustration, or distrust. Future research should investigate this other side of visitors’ experience by including negative reactions and extending the set of variables.

Another limitation that can be mentioned is the content validity of our scales. Content validity is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the degree to which items in an instrument re<sup>fl</sup>ect the content universe to which the instrument will be generalized” [63,p. 5]. To increase the strength of measurement, we kept the items that had the highest loading. This had the consequence that perceived authenticity and focused immersion were measured with only two items. Although research indicates that the measurement of a construct is best achieved with a minimum of three items, other IS studies have also assessed some of their constructs with only two items and sometimes with a single item [42,90].

We chose a previously used IS scale to assess perceived authenticity, but the scales provided by Featherman et al. [35] were not completely aligned of the kind of authenticity experienced by our participants. Future research should try to improve these scales by adding other facets to this construct. Authentic activities can imply the use of immersive scenarios and problembased learning, and they should give the opportunity to learners to re<sup>fl</sup>ect [51]. We could certainly try to investigate this concept of authentic learning further by testing different activities, possibly with an experimental design, and assessing the impact of each activity on learning.

We also point out that our research uses subjective scales to assess learning. Consequently, we did not use objective measures for the outcome variable. However, in situations where only anonymous questionnaires can be collected, self-assessments of learning are justi<sup>fi</sup>ed [11,p. 327]. If learning could be measured through other, and more objective means, that would be very desirable. Perhaps, testing visitors’ knowledge by asking them precise questions about the exhibition would have improved the learning measures.

## 7. Conclusion

In conclusion, this paper proposes a new model to assess visitors’ interactions and learning with technologies in a museum setting. How do museum technologies enhance learning? In answer to this question, we can say that they need to be easy to use and to provide an interactive experience. These technological dimensions, in turn, will elicit emotional reactions such as a sense of immersion, curiosity, enjoyment, and authenticity that will, in turn, lead to a positive learning experience.

This study makes another contribution, in that learning has become one of the most important preoccupations of museum directors. Museum experts try to take advantage of IT development by offering a highly interactive experience to their visitors. As such, more number of cultural exhibitions rely on IT-based materials. The British Music Experience has successfully organized immersive and interactive exhibitions that illustrate the new role devoted to IT in cultural venues. However, little is known about how visitors perceive such exhibitions; thus, research that evaluates IT effects on visitors’ experience is particularly welcome. Our research <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that domains such as tourism and cultural heritage represent relevant and exciting new domains for IS scholars. In fact, our work offers strong support for the added value such technologies bring to the visitors’ experience.
