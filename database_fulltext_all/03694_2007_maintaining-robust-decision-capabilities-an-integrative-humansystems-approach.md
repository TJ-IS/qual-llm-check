# Maintaining robust decision capabilities: An integrative human–systems approach

Simon Véronneau <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Yan Cimon <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Logistics & Operations Management Department, HEC Montreal 3000 Cote-Ste-Catherine Montreal, Qc H3T 2A7, Canada Williams School of Business, Bishop's University 2600, College St. Sherbrooke (Lennoxville), QC, J1M 0C8, Canada

Received 16 June 2005; received in revised form 1 May 2006; accepted 9 August 2006 Available online 25 September 2006

## Abstract

The purpose of this paper is to examine how to maintain robust and effective decision capabilities for organizations involved in critical operations. Following a multidisciplinary review of the literature, we propose an integrative and cumulative view that is articulated threefold. First, we present an operations resource management, or ORM, framework. Second, we suggest an integrative approach for decision capabilities as they rest on the interaction between humans and systems. Third, we derive a decision model. We show that careful consideration must be given to the interplay among humans, systems, and the environment in which they operate. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Critical operations management; Human–system interaction (HSI); Operations resource management; Information management

## 1. Introduction

On July 1, 2002, at 21:35:32, a Tupolev 154 M collides with a Boeing 757-200 in midair near the city of Ueberlingen, Germany, killing 71 people [9]. While for most people this accident represents a single catastrophic event, the trained operations resources management (ORM) professional will see this as a consequence of a chain of errors that was not stopped in time. Operating in critical environments calls for good resources management. Piloting airplanes, controlling air traffic, navigating ships, and conducting military operations are just a few examples of critical operations. To most a 9-1-1 call center represents the most critical type of operations one will face. While to them, the operation of these facilities remains conceptually simple, in reality, it is anything but. They deal with few information channels and the decision is limited to a small range of possible responses. This is unlike the previously mentioned operations that have to deal with a wide array of information channels and numerous possible responses. The complexity of the decision process is clearly higher. Maintaining a robust decision capability within these environments is a challenge, and when there are failures, crises occur. From such situations, our research question comes to mind immediately: How is a robust decision capability maintained through critical operations?

The purpose of this paper is to shed light on this extremely important question. First, we lay the ground for an ORM framework. We examine and define what is a critical operations environment, linking it to ORM, and explain its constituent elements before discussing some challenges. Second, we move on to present an integrative view of issues related to the interaction of humans and systems in such an environment. This view is built on the interrelationship of the psychology of decision-making, issues related to decision support systems (DSS), human– system interaction (HSI), and queuing. Third, we put forward a decision model. We consider the impact of task loading on decision capabilities and suggest a decision matrix based on the criticality of events and the slack time remaining before a crisis (STRC). We conclude by pointing out to some implications for academics and practitioners.

## 2. Context of critical operations: Framing the problem

## 2.1. Definitions and environments

Critical operations are operations that have a great degree of exposure to risk, where failure leads to loss of life and/or crisis. In the literature, the names of highreliability industry [23]; time-critical/high-risk operations [66] as well as high-pressure/high-risk environment [7] have also been used to describe such and environment.

We operationalize the term “critical operations” as operations that consistently have a small STRC, having a value that could be as low as zero or even negative. We use Pauchant and Mitrof's [[51]: 15] definition of a crisis as a “disruption that physically affects a system as a whole and threatens its basic assumptions, its subjective sense of self, its existential core” and for the STRC we adapt a classic definition of “the time remaining before the due date minus the processing time remaining” [[12]: 624], where the processing time refers to the time it takes to make a decision and accordingly coordinate an appropriate response. The STRC is important information for critical operations management. In the context of the operations manager having their workstation capacity considered infinite, it is the only practical way tasks can get prioritized and queued for their criticality.

Critical operations require more than technical expertise, they require teams to work well together as a cohesive unit. The pioneer in good resources management research within critical operations was the aviation industry through cockpit resource management. This later evolved into crew resource management (CRM) that reflected the change to include the entire crew onboard the aircraft as a cohesive team. The advent of the concept of CRM had as an objective to address the issues arising in the late 1960s–early 1970s which were common problem to crew performance leading to accidents such as preoccupation with minor mechanical problems; inadequate leadership; failure to delegate tasks and assign responsibilities; failure to set priorities; inadequate monitoring; failure to utilize available data; and failure to communicate intent and plans [65].

Following the success of CRM in aviation, other industries adapted the concept to their own operational needs. Bridge resource management (BRM) first appeared in the early 1990s mainly to respond to the issue of the development of chains of errors on ships. Since maritime accidents are seldom the cause of a single catastrophic event but rather the result of a series of non-serious incidents, the concept of BRM was developed and relied upon one main principle to avoid chains of errors: situational awareness [58]. Two core objectives of the International Maritime Organization sanctioned BRM course are (1) to gain “a greater understanding and awareness of efficient bridge procedures and bridge teamwork during watch keeping and ship handling, in normal and in emergency situations [and (2) to gain] greater awareness and understanding of a good interactive communication style by building a common shared mental model of the planned passage” [31: 3–4]. Engine room resource management (ERRM) share the same principles as BRM but applied to the ship's engine control room. The ERRM and BRM principles are bridged during training to emphasize seamless integration of the two teams that have to closely work together even though they are situated in different locations aboard the ship. Yet another field that benefited from CRM-type training is offshore oil exploration. Marine centers that offer BRM courses also offer courses for offshore oil platform personnel. These courses developed specifically for the offshore oil industry are composed of four modules: decision-making, communication, assertiveness, and stress [22].

Within the critical operation environment of hospitals, team resource management (TRM) training is emphasised while there previously was a paucity of research in this field [7]. This goes to show the broad applicability of the first theoretical approaches developed in CRM. In this line of research, four critical domains were identified by Kohn et al. [35]. They contend that the key elements in improving safety in an operating theatre involve creating (1) effective teamwork; (2) a blame-free environment for reporting and investigating near misses; (3) relationships between individual and system errors; and (4) an environment designed for the safe use of equipment.

The validity of this was demonstrated in another study from Hugh [29], where TRM principles were applied to laparoscopic bile duct surgery. It was found that such principles significantly contributed to reduce injury incurred during surgery and went from 1 injury per 200 cases to no injury per 2000 surgeries. The conclusion was that these new principles contributed greatly to achieving this improvement.

Like many other traditional critical operations environments, where one person is in charge, the culture in the operating room had always been to never question the surgeon. It was found that applying the concept of CRM and allowing every team member to speak up in an open communication environment was beneficial [20]. Surgeons went as far as requiring team consensus before irreversible or critical steps during surgery, something unthinkable just a few years ago. It is also recently reported that CRM training had a positive impact on the working habits of healthcare professionals, where it was welcomed as a healthy change to their work habits with 95% of respondents agreeing that CRM training would reduce workplace errors [24]. Finally, as Andersen and Bove [2] report, TRM is also being used in other nonmedial fields such as air traffic control.

## 2.2. Operations resource management

As we can see, there is a wide array of similar and complementary approaches that are seldom regrouped under a common umbrella. To address this deficiency, we posit that an essential tool in successfully managing critical operations includes the construct of ORM. This construct emerges from the current void being filled by a few concepts that are used in applied settings. As mentioned above, the various research undertaken in specific industries are similar in nature but lack a unifying framework.

Furthermore, previous strands of the literature have a few shortcomings, as they do not integrate the degree of robustness of decision capabilities. We have seen CRM evolve to be gradually more inclusive and the term TRM to be generalized to all settings that deal with teams. Arthur et al. [3] suggest that for TRM training to be successful in the health care industry, more research and evidence on teamwork from other industries is required. We therefore suggest that the term ORM would be more representative of the broad scope of applications of these theories, providing a common ground for further research. ORM should then be applied to all organizations that operate within critical environments and build upon five strands of the literature that relate to (1) true systems integration; (2) Boyd's cycle; (3) shared mental models; (4) standard operating procedures; and (5) robust design. We review them one by one.

## 2.2.1. True systems integration

First, true systems integration arises as a solution to problems related to the management of interdependencies or chains of interdependencies [e.g., 8] among other system elements. These problems grow from mismatches in the connections between information channels and seemingly discrete events that lead to suboptimal decision-making. Early approaches to systems integration tended toward a juxtaposition of systems' information flows within a common system or display (i.e., integration built around the technical aspects of the system). While this classical operationalisation of systems integration is thought to bring about systemic rigidities [17], true systems integration focuses on the seamless mesh of data fusion, data filtering and ultimately resulting into a managed information flow that entails information-rich situational awareness for optimal decision-making (i.e., integration built around users' needs, the task environment, and system's characteristics). Without true systems integration, one is forced to question any preconception about systemic robustness since unexpected results may happen to eventually affect adversely the ability to decide and react in a timely as well as efficient manner. Therefore, true systems integration comes as an enabler of efficacious decision-making.

## 2.2.2. Boyd's cycle

Second, Boyd's decision cycle is essential to prevent the escalating effect of processing overload. Boyd contends that to defeat an adversary, one's decision cycle–consisting in observing, orienting, deciding, and acting–needs to be the fastest and most efficient [27]. Failure to manage and address events as they happen eventually results in a systemic overload and hampered decision capability, resulting in a cascading effect that ripples throughout and results in decreased situational awareness and control. A similar argument was made for the maintenance and survivability of lifeline networks [54] (i.e., infrastructures providing essential services for a population's needs such as electricity, telecommunications, transportation, etc. [[54]: 87]) and the criticality associated with managing the chain of events. It is paramount that information regarding the antecedents and consequences of a failure be disseminated in a fast and efficient manner.

## 2.2.3. Shared mental model

Third, having a shared mental model has been identified as a significant contributor to better team performance and should be incorporated in training curriculum. “Mental models are organized knowledge structures that allow individuals to interact with their environment” [[44]: 274]. Therefore, a shared mental model consists of communicating and synchronizing one's mental model among teammates. Mathieu et al. [44] as well as Weick and Roberts [64] similarly found that team performance was related to mental model sharedness, allowing team members to better anticipate and understand the needs of their teammates. When interfaced with Boyd's decision cycle theory, shared mental models ensure that cycle reduction from all team members is moving in the same direction.

## 2.2.4. Standard operating procedures

Fourth, standard operating procedures (SOP) are present in many industries and are at the core of good operational management. They serve as an important guide to employees and are usually composed of two sections for each given procedure. The first section is the standard operation that must be followed also called standard policy. The second serves as guide for employees, and illustrates best practices; it is also called the guidance section. For example, a key success factor of Japanese car manufacturer Toyota is their high job specificity which explains every procedure in minute detail [56], supporting the usefulness of such procedures in critical settings.

## 2.2.5. Robust design

Fifth, the robust design of decision systems is analogous to Taguchi's robust design of products [see [59]]. Like a product, the decision process must be capable of resisting adverse utilisation outside of its theoretically perfect utilisation. However, one core difference is that in the case of a product, Taguchi considers the signal and the noise, where the noise is the disturbance to the signal that is the product. In the case of decision processes, one may often be faced with an unstructured clutter of information where the signal becomes the noise, preventing it from functioning properly. Given this consideration, it is essential to develop a decision-making process that will not generate noise from its very operation but rather keep the signals neatly organised and prevent them from interfering with one another [59]. The robust design of the decision systems, similar to Taguchi's robust design, entails an ability to accept variability in process and field usage.

## 2.3. Challenges and dimensions of ORM

The five strands mentioned above are paramount to ORM since they lay the foundation to understanding and operating within these complex systems. These five strands also bring a fresh look to the importance of the decision cycle from an ORM perspective. Furthermore, the relevance of such considerations are better seen through the lens of a shared mental model, a key element in enabling actors to understand one another [45]. This could not take place without a robust design built into the decision systems. The failure to posses such a holistic system would prove detrimental in critical situations.

However, despite the much needed ORM framework built on the five strands discussed above, we are confronted with major challenges. Current concepts consistently fail to address how humans and systems interact either by focusing one aspect of this pair, or simply ignoring the importance of integrating the two. The solution is to deal with this issue through an integrated framework that simultaneously addresses how humans and systems relate within critical operations, since both of these dimensions remains too often ignored [63].

## 3. An integrative approach to decision capabilities

An integrative approach of human and system issues within ORM is dependent on the careful consideration of the aforementioned shortcomings. To study how humans and systems interact in an ORM framework, an in-depth analysis of the four building blocks that constitute the framework is required through a carefu examination of the literature. One must also consider the dynamics of HSI. Behind this interaction, we find a human side that commands a careful examination of the psychology aspects in decision-making. We also find a system side calling for a look at how DSS are used. Their effect on decision capabilities within an ORM framework leads to an important link between these concepts with questions of queuing and criticality of decisions. Fig. 1 enables us to visualise how these four concepts relate to each other.

## 3.1. Human–system interaction (HSI)

An increased use of technology has become necessary in managing the growing complexity of operations resource. Martinsons and Chong [43] found that the growing scope and sophistication of technologies calls for good system design and understanding of social and organizational issues to prevent adverse effects on future communication patterns. As such, factors relating to HSI are important components in ensuring the robustness of decision capabilities during a time of crisis. At its roots, research on HSI endeavours not only to improve the safety aspect of such interactions, but also its user-friendliness [e.g., [1]]. Following Agah's [1] extensive survey of the literature on human–computer interaction, the main conclusion to be drawn is that systems diversity (compu ters, robots, etc.) and use are reflected in the varied and fragmented scope of the research, which makes potential generalizations very difficult. Nonetheless, three main issues constantly arise in the field of HSI. One issue is the scope of human interaction: how much leeway shall humans possess. A second issue is the joint behaviour of man and machine, especially in an interactive setting. A third is the ergonomics of it all.

## 3.1.1. The scope of human intervention

A fundamental question in HSI is the determination of what the scope of human intervention should be given that many strenuous and repetitive tasks may now be automated or managed through the use of technology. There are cases, such as in aviation, where human action is subject to “envelope protection,” i.e., the overriding of human actions by computers should there be unusual or potentially harmful extreme variations in the commands given to the machine [34]. The question of which tasks should be devolved to humans or to machines and how they are to interact still remains open and largely dictated by the context.

## 3.1.2. Human and machine behaviours

User and computer behaviours need to be simultaneously considered as an interactive system [19]. This is an important stepping stone to the reduction of contextual interpretation ambiguities for mental models to be properly mapped so that the gap between machine behaviour and human cognition is bridged in situations requiring an enhanced awareness based on criticality issues. Currently, there are ongoing efforts to fix problems associated with the gap between expectations of information and results when querying systems [e.g., [40]].

## 3.1.3. Ergonomic considerations

As we move towards a closer behavioural fit between man and machine, there is an increasing need for establishing common standards in the field of ergonomics [e.g., [50]], some dwelling specifically into HSI [57]. Knowing that usability and the ergonomics of interfaces improve the users' comfort and productivity. For example, the adoption of common standards for Video Display Units [e.g., [57]] yields important benefits. However, in the human–machine system, there is a crucial human element that necessitates a reflection on the psychology of decision-making.

## 3.2. A human factor: The psychology of decision-making

Another building block in the robust decision framework within critical operations is the psychological dimension(s). According to Simon [55], fact and value are two fundamental elements to consider in appreciating the process of decision-making. Furthermore, human action is constrained by bounded rationality [e.g., [42]]. The main impact of the aforementioned factors is the impossibility for individuals to fully appreciate the antecedents and consequences relating to their decisions. However, there is more to psychological factors that affect decision-making, especially in a critical operations environment. One needs also to consider the influence of the social context, the effect of fatigue, the impact of stress, and the degree of risk-taking.

## 3.2.1. Social context

The human condition provides for a social context influencing decision-making based on perceptions of appropriateness, identity, and rules-based choice as decisions relate to a set of expected consequences [45]. Previous research on this matter has shown that the order that the information is presented introduces bias to an individuals' judgement, but less so if a correct anticipation of the aforementioned order existed [45]. Messick [45] adds that information labels and the rationale for decision-making also have an effect.

## 3.2.2. Stress

Apart from the social context, emotional stress, both work and non-work related, has a significant impact on the information processing abilities of individuals as exemplified by increased reaction times in situations of increased stress [36]. Furthermore, it is known to impact on decision-making as judgement does [e.g., [37]].

## 3.2.3. Risk-taking

Some posit that a precautionary principle applies to decision-making in many circumstances [e.g., [39]], thus providing some grounds for explaining risk-averse behaviours and the management of risk. However, since critical operations involve risk-taking, the human condition is found again to have a great influence. Emotions too are found to very much influence risktaking behaviour. For example, anger increases risktaking by men, while for women, disgust decreases it [see [21]].

## 3.2.4. Fatigue

Increased attention has been focused on fatigue management over the last few years. Although not yet part of official government sanctioned curriculum, fatigue and sleep management has been introduced in some BRM curriculum. Current research in aviation has shown that fatigue and sleep deprivation can be a significant although an underreported risk. This underreporting will persist as long as the system design and economic considerations strain human resources [10]. These problems are not just typical of aircraft operations but occur in every organization that operates in a critical environment where operations occur around the clock and where workers are subject to irregular or unusual working hours. Furthermore, since fatigue slows the response to stimuli [10] the practice of “doubling the watch” in the operations center, when assignable variation is detected or apprehended in the task load, will result in an increase of Boyd's decision cycle and reduce robust decision capability. As awareness of these problems is raised, and regulation on maximum working hours enforced, the risk associated with fatigue should diminish. Until then, it will remain present and one of the central problems to maintaining robust decision capabilities. The psychology of decision-making is not sufficient in itself to ensure robust decision capabilities; we need to tie in the DSS to ensure that it will be maintained.

## 3.3. Issues related to the use of decision support systems (DSS)

While DSS are used in a variety of contexts, they must undergo thorough evaluations to achieve an efficient design and solve data management and warehousing issues. Furthermore, not only are they used for the management of systems change [18], but they also need to be managed and adapted over the long run, as in the case of corporate Intranets [e.g., [38]]. The use of DSS is influenced both by the context of its usage and by a need for customization that its multiple usage entails.

## 3.3.1. Contexts of DSS usage

There are many areas witnessing an ever-increasing use of DSS to manage the inherent complexity associated with a wide range of activities. In farm planning, DSS are used for applications such as investment analysis or machine selection [53]. Within law firms, they are used for fee determination given the many methods and challenges associated with setting a fee structure [61]. In retail commerce, geographic information systems are gaining ground [47]. In wood processing, DSS assist in purchase management for budgeting and contracting by modeling the existing supply chain [e.g., [26]]. The telecommunications industry provides a revealing multilevel view. At a network level, mobile communications planning relies on DSS to address the inherent complexity of network planning and time constraints associated with the mobility of users, as the informationintensive nature of the process would not be well served by human processing power alone [11]. At an organisational level, DSS are shown to support the organisation's tasks (e.g., budget and resource allocation), cutting across business functions and hierarchy, and connecting decision makers while providing them with a common picture, as in the case of Korea Telecom [32]. While there is some commonality, there are also factors that drive customization.

## 3.3.2. Factors for DSS customization

The variety of uses constrains DSS customization in achieving for effectiveness. There is a wide range of issues to account for. Issues relating to tasks (type, level of interdependency) and case-based reasoning are central because of the limited recurrence of similar potential crises within the ORM environment. Along these lines, and since such an environment is so data intensive, data warehousing is to be considered as well as DSS evaluation to introduce feedback for future improvements.

3.3.2.1. Task-related issues. Empirically, the type of task to be performed is found to be a moderating variable to DSS effectiveness [e.g., [30]]. As such, developers need to account for task type in the design stages of DSS. Tasks are categorized by types and dimensions [30]. The former include the generation of ideas, choice and negotiation while the latter dimensions are creativity, normative influence, information richness and task interdependence [30: 3]. Tasks in an ORM framework are seldom independent. Migliarese and Paolucci [[46]: 241] present three broad task interdependence types that are very similar to Thompson's [62]: generic (i.e., interdependence through the use of common resources); sequential (i.e., a linear chain-like relation); and reciprocal (i.e., bi-directional and feedback-based influence). They contend that the use of groupware applications represent an important advance in managing both complexity and interdependencies in organisational processes as these two factors should drive the requirements of the architecture of such systems. The use of an intelligent agent is one avenue toward better DSS customisation, as in systems assisting individuals in financial decisions [see [49]].

3.3.2.2. Cased-based reasoning. One possible avenue found in the literature is the use of case-based reasoning systems. In electronic circuit diagnosis, these systems allow for the exploitation of the existing information while permitting diagnosis under incomplete knowledge thus increasing the efficiency of the processes in a manufacturing setting [4]. Such systems collect and connect data regarding cases, a great assistance in illstructured decision-making environments [25]. A drawback is limited system adaptiveness in large part because of their limited “learnability” and issues of judgmental nature. The difficulty in addressing the dynamic nature of decision-makers' preferences implies limited ranges of effectiveness. Hence, the maintenance of acceptable levels of effectiveness generates a need to store and treat massive amounts of data.

3.3.2.3. Data warehousing. Data warehousing holds two promising avenues: knowledge warehouses and web warehousing. Implementing a knowledge warehouse provides a means of retrieving, sharing and using vast amounts of codified knowledge for better decisionmaking. Future trends include the consideration of managers' mental models improvement [48]. At the crossroads of data warehousing and web technology, web warehousing makes data retrieval more intuitive and leads to important gains in efficiency [60]. Such an approach resulted in important information sharing benefits in healthcare [6] industries.

3.3.2.4. DSS evaluation. The evaluation of DSS is important as they affect the process and outcomes of decision-making [52]. Phillips-Wren et al. [52] found that through DSS use, the improvements noticed in the process of decision-making were not necessarily concomitant with an improvement in the outcomes. However, they note that overall, DSS use has had a significant and positive improvement when compared to the absence of DSS use.

## 3.3.2.5. Command and control information systems.

Command and control information systems constitute the information technology architecture that enables the flow of information to users. This provides for a better situational awareness and contributes to the provision of a better operational picture to decision-makers and their aides [see 41]. These systems free the operations manager to spend more time on crucial activities. They have a central role of conveying information within DSS and have the advantage of reducing personnel requirements for such functions as they do, for example, in the case of military exercises [16].

## 3.4. Queuing of decisions

Notwithstanding the issues mentioned above, queuing issues arise with regard to decisions within critical operations: what to prioritize and how to prioritize it is a continuing challenge. As evidenced by research on crowd control [e.g., [5]], one must be able to assess the criticality of events associated with a specific range of situations. Consistent with the literature, we posit that the potential magnitude of a crisis, all STRC being equal, should determine its criticality in turn driving the queuing process.

## 3.4.1. Consequence as criticality

Consequence should drive criticality when decisionmaking is considered in critical operations. In the case of communication networks, for example, circuit design and leasing decisions, it is suggested that queuing costs be considered [15] with an objective of cost minimization. Furthermore, traffic load and delay were shown to have significant impacts on systems design decisions, entailing a renewed importance for queuing.

## 3.4.2. Queuing for criticality

Ensuring that the most important and relevant issues are queued properly is an important challenge that involves the establishment of a higher importance to certain issues [e.g., 13], which are a way to compensate for eventual breakdowns in processing ability. In light of this, consolidating systems is thought to decrease queuing time with the same total processing power. Clustering information systems may prove an efficient solution as in the case of airline reservation systems [14]. For instance, Cheng [14] mentions that “[the] optimal arrival rate decreases as the delay cost increases since a higher delay cost will discourage the submission of low value jobs” [[14]: 29]. Understanding the importance of cascading effects [54] behind the range of critical decision processes proves essential to robust decision-making abilities.

Consistent with an ORM perspective, integration of the above elements allows for a holistic and detailed view of the constituting elements of the constructs and their interrelationship that goes beyond Fig. 1. We integrate the pieces of the puzzle in a coherent and more detailed model shown in Fig. 2. Beginning with HSI, the constructs of human and machine behaviours, the scope of human intervention and ergonomic considerations are tightly interwoven, as the interaction between information systems and human resources is a major driver for successfu system implementation [e.g., [43]]. With regards to the psychology of decision-making, the social context influences stress, that also is in a mutually influentia relationship with fatigue, similar to the relationship between the latter and risk-taking. It is widely recognized that decision abilities are affected by the social context, fatigue, stress and risk taking [see 36,39,43] A different picture looms from DSS that is similar to the specific case of groupware [e.g., [46]]. Since the context for DSS usage drives its customisation, a construct that influences issues related to the types of task involved: task-related issues, DSS evaluation, case-based reasoning, and data warehousing remain interdependent, while DSS evaluation has an impact on data warehousing choices. Consequence as criticality drives the queuing for criticality constructs similar, for example, to intensive care units [33].

## 4. Toward a decision model

A worthy decision model within critical operations needs to take into account the effects of task loading on decision capabilities. Furthermore, the interaction between systems and information has to be considered prior to considering a decision matrix since it has important influences on decision capabilities.

## 4.1. Task loading and decision capabilities

Task loading has an important effect on decision capabilities. A high level of task loading in a small amount of time significantly reduces the decision capabilities of actors. A main issue is the clutter of information. In acquiring information, operation managers have to resort to an information channel before knowing the level of criticality of the information or its STRC. While the exact nature of the information is unknown, the actual channel it comes from is. Therefore, operations managers are more likely to respond to channels with the highest likelihood of criticality as opposed to the true criticality of the information conveyed. This does not represent a problem until multiple information is received at the same time, hindering the capacity of the operations manager to get within his Boyd's decision cycle.

Therefore, the capacity to render proper decisions is altered for that moment. A typical example could be the navigation officers on cruise ships, who may have a close quarter situation alarm on his radar at night for another passing ship, while the internal 9-1-1 phone system starts ringing. At that point, the ships' officer has to worry about the safety of the entire ship and should focus solely on it, but he does not know whether the 9-1-1 phone is someone with seasickness or a heart attack. Therefore, his task loading exceeds, for that moment, his decision capability. Fig. 3 represent a stylized view of this issue.

As we can see from Fig. 3, task load has an adverse effect on decision capabilities. In situation 1, we see a sudden and major rise in task loading $( \mathrm { T } _ { 1 } )$ causing a great stress on decision capabilities $( \mathrm { D } _ { 1 } ) . \ \mathrm { A s } \ \mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ surges above a certain threshold resulting in a task overload, a decrease in decision capabilities is witnessed as represented by $\mathrm { D } _ { 1 }$ . When $\mathrm { D } _ { 1 }$ crosses a certain threshold, there is a breakdown in decision capabilities caused by a clutter of information. The reason behind these spikes is that high STRC tasks and low STRC tasks can arrive simultaneously and in a random order even though the margin for actions is different.

Thus, the goal of the operations manager is to stay in control as depicted in situation 2, without crossing a threshold that would cause a task loading overload or decision capability breakdown. With a task loading following curve $\mathrm { T } _ { 2 } ,$ the decision capability is maintained at $\mathrm { D } _ { 2 } .$ . While the area under the curves in situation 1 and situation 2 may well be equal, showing an identical challenge to operation managers, we posit better management smoothes the task loading/decision capability peaks from $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 } , \mathrm { D } _ { 1 } \mathrm { t o } \mathrm { T } _ { 2 } , \mathrm { D } _ { 2 }$

Referring back to the Ueberlingen accident mentioned at the beginning, a similar problem occurred when the air traffic controller diverted his attention from station A where two closely approaching aircraft in transit, a Boeing B757-200 carrying cargo and a Tupolev TU 154M carrying passengers, to answer the call of another aircraft at an adjacent station B, an Airbus A320 that was landing soon. The fact his attention was detracted to workstation B to answer the call from the Airbus A320 aircraft was one of the contributing factors in the collision of the Boeing B757-200 and Tupolev TU 154M, since he failed to see signs and hear communications from station A that led to the collision [9]. This clearly shows that systems and information are key elements for the maintenance of robust decision capabilities.

## 4.2. Systems and information

As we continue toward a decision model, it is important to explain how systems and information lead us to a workable model enabling operation managers to move from situation 1 to situation 2 in considering the dimensions of task criticality and STRC. Thus, on one hand, we need to consider the current shift from systems integration (i.e., the amalgamation of the channels conveying tasks) to the growing importance of intelligent systems (i.e., channels not only conveying tasks, but assisting managers in their prioritization and the management of task loading). While on the other hand, it is not possible to dismiss issues related to the acquisition, sharing and distribution of information.

## 4.2.1. From the integration of systems to intelligent systems

In a study of integrated real-time emergency management system, Hargreaves et al. [28] found that combining technologies such as communication position and imaging into a single integrated system provided the best way to generate and manage information, and render efficient the use of resources. While the operations managers' dream is to have all the routine tasks fulfilled by automated computer processes, their nightmare is also to be trapped in a highly structured environment where automated processes would take ill-advised decision on behalf of the operator. Even though there is a current focus on physically integrating machines and improving their ergonomics, the fact remains that the operations manager has to deal with numerous independent systems and monitor several communication channels. Two basic reasons are the root of the problem. First, critical operations call for redundancy of systems and independence of them from one another. This fail-safing method is the reason that on a ship, for example, radar and VHF radio are independent. As well, we can find between 2 and 4 of each, varying with ship size and function. Manufacturers are making efforts to better integrate them by minimising distances between equipment but they remain far from true integration. The second reason, apart from the redundancy and independence of systems, is that of technology improvement. As new technology becomes available it is added on the older one in the operation center but not necessarily integrated with one another. It is easy to conceive that over the life span of a ship, or of a nuclear power station, numerous technological improvements occur over the years. Hence, in order to achieve a sequenced and structured environment, there is a need for a true integration of the systems used by operations managers without losing the redundancy and independence of these systems.

A common practice is for operations managers to call for other managers to join in when task overload occurs, or when assignable variation in task load is predicted. In the best case, the overload is detected and managed before the loss of decision capability. In other cases, it is detected after which leads to near misses or crises. Having an intelligent system would allow for the equivalent of having co-workers on the backseat always on standby who would seamlessly bounce into action. It would also allow for a faster response to assignable variation and allow the manager to keep a tight decision cycle. Using such technologies as voice recognition, whose usage and availability is increasing, in various industries, would allow for quicker response. If interfaced with the enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems then all command and control issues within this system could become powerful tools for maintaining a short decision cycle. We posit this would be an important key in achieving true system integration, impacting both information acquisition and information sharing and distribution, allowing for a seamless flow of information and data.

## 4.2.2. Information acquisition, sharing, and distribution

As described by Worm et al. [66], the processing of information during critical operations consists of basic components that include information acquisition, information sharing, and information distribution. The great amount of information received by the operations managers can be daunting. The channels they monitor are numerous and often poorly interfaced. In the worst cases, they are distributed physically over the area of the room, which further increases the decision cycle time by requiring them to move around within the control room. This can lead to missing other information and alarms coming in. By introducing an integrated system, all communication channels, including alarms and operational data, could be rooted verbally in a headset. This would allow the operations manager to have a faster acquisition of information for processing. The system could also prioritise information and queue secondary information when the operations managers are already tasked loaded with more critical issues, it could also reroute this information to another manager who is available or on standby.

The current redundancy of systems and the simple split between the different communications systems used in operations leads to undue delay in the sharing of critical and routine information that increases Boyd's decision cycle. The integrated system could allow for a faster task sharing and re-direction of alarms and communication. A simple verbal confirmation to the system could task load a specialised worker to investigate or solve the situation without unduly tasking the manager with relay of too much information.

## 4.3. A decision matrix

The use of systems, integrated or intelligent, coupled with proper and efficient information acquisition, sharing and distribution, while efficient to smooth the curves presented in Fig. 3, does not automatically warrant that it is done to such an extent that the critical thresholds will not be crossed. To prevent such events leading to a breakdown of the decision capability, one needs at this stage to simultaneously consider the criticality of decisions and the STRC. This is done using the decision matrix presented in Fig. 4.

This matrix enables the prioritization of tasks according to both their criticality and STRC. The main benefit is that it constitutes an easy and efficient way of managing the task loading in order to keep the decision capability under control.

Four possible situations occur and individuals categorize events or actions to take within these four positions. In the decision matrix the two main axes represent the basic decision assumptions. The criticality (high vs. low) is measured by the magnitude and/or scope of the crises that would result from inaction or improper response while STRC (high vs. low) may be, as discussed in the definitions section above, a numerical value. As STRC increases, pressure eases off, while criticality increases, pressure increases. The most pressing case is in a situation of the outmost criticality to have a low STRC. We have numbered the different position within the decision matrix representing the priority assigned to each category of decision. Using the matrix, the first situation to be addressed (priority one) by the operations manager is undoubtedly the high criticality of an event coupled with a low STRC; while the last situation (priority three) in the case of a low criticality and a high STRC should be the last to be addressed. Problems arise in situations (priority two) of low criticality—low STRC and high criticality–high STRC. These cases are solved only through the judgement of the operations manager who determines which task is completed first according to their situational assessment on what is to be prioritized between criticality and STRC. This is why the best way to help the operations manager in his judgement call is to give him the tools he needs to keep his Boyd Cycle as short as possible.

## 5. Conclusion

We started by asking ourselves how to maintain robust decision capabilities within critical operations. The answer is threefold. First, an ORM framework is essential as it provides for a more general and applicable venue than the more specialized CRM or BRM approaches from which it takes its roots. Second, having an ORM framework at hand, it then becomes of paramount importance to understand the intricacies of decision capabilities as they stem from the interaction of humans and systems. Following an exhaustive review of the literature, we frame an integrative view where four interrelated concepts emerge: the psychology of decision-making; issues related to DSS; HSI; and queuing. Furthermore, each of these concepts contains a set of constructs making for a more complete, but also more complex, picture of how humans and systems combine for decision-making within the aforementioned ORM framework. Third, as we built on the two previous elements, the ORM framework and an integrative view of human and systems, we posit that task loading as it relates to decision capabilities needs to be managed, keeping the interplay of systems and information in mind, in order for them not to cross critical thresholds. To prevent such a situation, we present a simple decision matrix that prioritises the decisions to be taken based on the criticality of the event requiring such decisions and on the STRC. The result of this matrix is that while priority one and priority three situations are easy to separate, priority two situations require a judgment call from decision-makers.

Going back to the Ueberlingen accident we can conclude that if an ORM framework had been in place at the air traffic control center, certain key contributing elements of the chain of errors would have been averted. When the DSS system would have noticed the task load increase it could have relayed the information instantaneously to the other air traffic controller–in the adjacent room–to come to “double the watch”. This would have freed the current operator from spending important time on trying to contact his colleague and would have allowed for the splitting of the task load once his colleague would have arrived. Furthermore, even though the center was ergonomically designed, with just a few feet separating the two stations [see 9], this separation proved to be too much for one operator to maintain his situational awareness. Clearly, this task overload made the operator's Boyd cycle longer than the cycle required for successful decisions to be rendered. Having the two workstations truly integrated would have allowed for the sole air traffic controller to have a better HSI, a shorter Boyd cycle, and to maintain awareness of the situation developing at the other station, which had two fast approaching planes on a collision course.

Two key elements in the Ueberlingen accident are directly addressed within this paper: One is the fact that the task load increase was too much for one operator to handle, leading to valuable minutes being wasted trying to reach another operator to come and help. Secondly, the critical information was not integrated and available on two distinct systems. No one can assert boldly and with certainty that removing these two key elements in the chain of errors would have prevented the collision of the two aircraft in itself. We can, however, assert that these two elements were significant contributing factors to the accident [see 9], and that these key shortcomings would have been corrected by a system like the one we propose in this paper. Having an intelligent system recognising the need for more resources and automatically requesting them would have prevented the queuing from occurring, and for hasty decisions to be taken due to a paucity of resources. This integrative framework could have possibly saved the lives of these 71 persons, and it could offer great possibility for other critical operations environments in the future. We believe that applying this ORM framework to critical operations environments would certainly reduce the likelihood of loss of life and/or crises.

## 5.1. Implications for academics

The main implication for academics is that this work helps reframe the research agenda in a broader manner. Priority two situations, where judgment calls are of the essence, need to be studied further as we work to reduce significantly the possibility and scope of potential human and system errors. Another important implication relates to the importance of human socialization mechanisms in a critical operations setting. It was reported by Wiener et al. [65] that the team that engages in non-work-related discussions and socialises the most often performs better. It would be interesting to correlate this performance to a measure of sociability. We believe that an interesting avenue would be to test whether emotional quotient could be a good proxy in forecasting future team performance and simultaneously examine this factor with systems issues within the ORM framework laid out here. More research is needed.

## 5.2. Implications for practitioners

One implication for practitioners is the usability of the decision matrix laid out here. By simplifying priority one and priority three situations, one may now concentrate on priority two situations. Through a broad ORM framework, understanding the interplay between humans and systems helps making better, faster and more efficient decisions. It also reduces the scope for error by allowing a better understanding of the threshold for decision capabilities. This in turn will allow for a better training of operations managers.
