# Investigating bias in the online physician reviews published on healthcare organizations' websites

Nima Kordzadeh

Foisie School of Business, Worcester Polytechnic Institute, Worcester, MA, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online physician reviews Consumer ratings Bias Patient satisfaction Electronic word of mouth Physician rating websites

## A B S T R A C T

Making healthcare decisions is often dificult, challenging, and consequential for individuals. To facilitate and inform provider selection decisions, healthcare organizations ofer physician review services and publish the provided reviews on their websites. However, if such reviews are highly skewed, significantly inaccurate, and systematically biased, they could mislead patients. This is against healthcare organizations' responsibility of acting in the best interests of patients. To shed light on this phenomenon, this study examines two types of bias, namely between-website and within-website biases, in the online reviews of physicians posted on healthcare organizations' websites. Reviews belonging to 1921 physicians were collected from two data sources: (1) web. sites of the hospitals that the physicians were afiliated with and (2) four commercial physician rating websites including HealthGrades, RateMDs, Vitals, and Google Reviews. To test between-website biases, the ratings posted on the healthcare organizations' websites were compared against the corresponding ratings on the commercial websites. Our results suggest that the ratings provided by healthcare institutions are generally, more skewed toward scores favorable to physicians, and less dispersed, than corresponding ratings reported on commercial websites. The within-website results also supported the argument that physicians with diferent rating scores systematically receive diferent number of ratings and comments. Our findings highlight the fact that hospitals that publish physician ratings should try to identify and reduce rating biases to help patients make more informed and judicious clinical decisions, realizing the responsibility of healthcare providers in protecting patients' rights and safety.

## 1. Introduction

Electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM), realized in terms of online reviews, has revolutionized the way patients collect information about physicians and decide on which healthcare providers to choose. In the past, patients primarily relied on recommendations from family members, friends, or existing care providers when searching for a physician [1]. More recently and with the proliferation of eWOM services, people use online physician reviews to learn about positive and negative patient experiences and make informed decisions for their healthcare accordingly [2]. In a nationally representative survey conducted in the United States in 2012, nearly 60% of the respondents reported that they used online physician reviews to learn about the quality of medical services provided by primary care physicians (PCPs) [3]. Consistently, recent research has shown that people view patient reviews of physicians as important as the clinical ratings provided by the government [4] and more important than physician qualifications [5] when choosing a doctor.

While commercial physician rating websites (PRWs) such as

RateMDs, HealthGrades, and Vitals provide online reviews of healthcare professionals afiliated with diferent medical centers, numerous clinics and hospitals in the United States ofer their own rating mechanisms, platforms, and websites. The University of Pittsburgh Medical Center (UPMC), Cleveland Clinic, and Brigham and Women's Hospital located in Boston are among the healthcare organizations that ofer privately-administered physician rating services. These organizations email or text message a physician evaluation survey to patients after their appointments. Patients can then voluntarily complete the survey, provide comments, and answer questions on diferent aspects of the care they have received during their visit. The feedback received is then posted on the healthcare organization's website (HOW) and potentially used by future health consumers when making clinical decisions. The hospital can also use the collected patient feedbacks to evaluate the quality of care provided by their physicians and decide to continue or discontinue working with them accordingly.

Despite the potential value of online physician reviews, a major threat to their usefulness is that they could be biased [2,6,7]. For example, if the ratings posted on HOWs are inflated (see Fig. 1), they could lead patients to wrong perceptions of physician quality, resulting in wrong provider selection decisions [8]. These misinformed decisions could entail negative health and wellness consequences for patients and the surrounding communities. Wrong healthcare decisions can also lead to a waste of financial resources. Accordingly, providing inaccurate and misleading physician reviews is not only against healthcare providers ethical and social responsibility of protecting patients' rights and safety [9], but may severely damage a hospital's reputation and could have legal consequences for them [10.11]. Therefore, it is critical for healthcare institutions to understand the nature and extent of inaccuracy and bias in the reviews shared on their websites and to reduce that bias as much as possible.

While numerous studies have examined bias in online reviews within commercial domains, very few studies have highlighted and investigated the unique aspects of online physician reviews that could lead to the emergence of distinct forms and levels of bias in this context. The first unique aspect of online reviews in the healthcare sector is that patients generally hold a high regard for their doctors and hence, have a favorable disposition toward them as skilled professionals who heal people facing physical, mental, and emotional problems [12]. Accordingly, patients are appreciative of the care services they receive and thereby, are willing to reciprocate the favor in the form of positive reviews [6] or possibly exaggerated comments [2] for their care providers. Whereas, such levels of gratitude may not be normally produced by satisfaction with non-health products such as books or service providers such as hair stylists. Moreover, healthcare transactions (e.g., visiting a doctor or undergoing a surgery) are generally part of longterm, afect-integrated relationships between patients and physicians [13]. Therefore, patients, even if not highly satisfied with the quality of care they receive, may not be willing to share negative experiences for fear of harming the relationship. Accordingly, in most other contexts, people do not have ties with the items under review and hence, may not anticipate that writing negative reviews about a single product (e.g., book and movie) or service (e.g., hotel and transportation) could entail negative consequences for the contributors. Thus, people are likely to feel more comfortable sharing their negative feedback about non-health items than healthcare entities (i.e., physicians) in eWOM environments.

There are also diferences in the extents, sources, and consequences of bias in online physician reviews between commercial PRWs and

HOWs. For example, people may perceive a lower level of anonymity and privacy when responding to hospital-prompted surveys compared to when posting online reviews through PRWs. As a result, the reviews published on HOWs may be more conservative and hypothetically more skewed toward positive values than similar ratings on other websites. Moreover, hospitals have full control over what to publish and not to publish on their websites. Therefore, the protocols and procedures they possibly employ to select and publish patient reviews of physicians may intensify the overall level of positive skewness compared to corresponding ratings on PRWs.

In addition, the individual and societal consequences of inaccurate physician reviews can be diferent between HOWs and commercial PRWs. According to the source credibility theory [14,15], patients may believe that the reviews posted on HOWs are more credible and trust worthy than those posted on non-institutional websites. Thus, patients may rely on and adopt hospital-provided reviews more confidently than other online reviews of physicians. Accordingly, it is critical to understand bias in the physician reviews presented on HOWs. The present study addresses this phenomenon and assesses the existence of two overarching types of bias, namely within-website and between-website biases, in the online reviews within HOWs.

Within-website bias, as generally used by researchers in the context of online consumer reviews, pertains to a strong and systematic inclination of reviews (i.e., ratings and comments) within a website toward the positive or negative extreme of a rating scale or sentiment continuum [2]. Whereas, between-website bias refers to the systematic diferences between reviews (i.e., ratings and comments) associated with the same objects, persons, entities, etc. posted on two or more websites [16]. While numerous studies in the past have evaluated within-website bias [2,17], very few studies have assessed betweenwebsite bias in health or non-health contexts. This is particularly critical, and needs special attention, if the reviews posted on a category of websites, i.e., HOWs, are demonstrated to be consistently and significantly more biased than the reviews published on another category, i.e., commercial PRWs. The current study addresses this gap in the literature.

Another contribution of this research is the way we operationalize and measure bias. Extant literature in the context of online physician reviews has mostly assessed the distribution of rating scores and drawn conclusions on the existence of bias in those reviews accordingly $[ 6 , 1 7 , 1 8 ]$ . Whereas, bias could emerge in other forms such as systematic diferences between number of ratings and comments posted for doctors with diferent overall satisfaction scores. Understanding this form of bias is valuable because higher number of reviews can posi tively impact attitudes toward rated physicians [19] and their demand [20]. Therefore, if physicians with higher rating scores systematically receive higher number of reviews, people's attitudes toward those physicians could be positively influenced not only by the physicians' high rating scores, but also by their high number of reviews. Accordingly, this study extends conceptualization and operationalization of within-website bias by investigating number of reviews in terms of ratings and comments within HOWs to understand whether there is a positive association between overall rating scores and number of reviews. We also innovatively operationalize bias in terms of percentage of numeric ratings that are associated with a narrative comment. Narrative comments may present a more complete and informative picture of the total patient experience with a provider [21,22]. Comments can also be more accessible to the users who are not comfortable using numbers when making decisions [21,23]. Hence, measuring bias in this way is important and insightful in that it can capture a unique aspect of bias, namely systematic diferences in information completeness about physicians with diferent characteristics, i.e., rating scores. Accord ingly, this study addresses the following research questions:

1) How are the online physician ratings posted on HOWs systematically diferent from the corresponding ratings posted on com mercial PRWs? (i.e., between-website bias)

2) How do physicians with diferent rating scores receive diferent number of reviews? (i.e., within-website bias)

Our results extend the existing literature on socially-enabled information systems [24], eWOM in healthcare [8,25], social media-en abled patient decision-making [19], and bias in user-generated content in online environments $[ 2 , 1 6 , 2 6 ]$ . From a practical standpoint, the findings provide insights into the quality, reliability, and accuracy of the physician reviews posted on HOWs. Administrators of those websites, directors of patient-centered information systems, and healthcare quality managers in clinics and hospitals can use the results of this study to improve the processes of administering patient satisfaction surveys and publishing the results on their websites.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Bias in online consumer reviews

Prior literature has shown that online reviews of products and ser vices, in general, may not accurately and efectively reflect the actual quality of rated items because those reviews may be subject to a level of random errors and systematic bias [27,28]. Random errors may occur because people are imperfect in understanding, digesting, and remembering all the details of the use of products and services and in communicating their satisfaction, perceptions, experiences, and opinions in the form of online reviews. However, according to the central limit theorem, the mean value of the sum of random errors existing in the ratings of a specific product or service is expected to be zero in the long run [29].

As opposed to random errors, systematic biases do not occur completely randomly and can generally be attributed to systematic reasons such as those related to firms, reviewers, and reviewed items [26,27]. For example, firms could hire individuals and professional review-authoring companies to post promotional reviews about their products and services and negative reviews about competitors [30–32]. Bias may also emerge in online reviews due to the characteristics and behaviors of reviewers. Hu, Pavlou and Zhang [26] argue that the voluntary nature of online reviews leads to the emergence of two types of self selection bias including acquisition bias and underreporting bias. Acquisition bias denotes that online reviews tend to be skewed toward positive values and sentiments because consumers with a positive predisposition toward a product or service are more likely to acquire it and hence, write reviews about it [26]. As a special case of acquisition bias, the preferences and attitudes of a product's early adopters toward the product are likely to be more positive than those of followers and late-adopters [30]; consequently, the product's overall rating scores and review sentiments may systematically decrease over time [16]. The second form of self-selection bias, namely underreporting bias, postulates that people with extreme opinions about a product or service, either positive or negative, are more likely to write reviews about it [33]. In other words, consumers with moderate sentiments and opinions about products and services are generally underrepresented in online reviews [34]. Simultaneous efects of acquisition and underreporting biases lead to a positively skewed, asymmetric, J-shaped distribution of online consumer reviews [26,35].

Another potential source of bias in online reviews includes the ex isting reviews posted on a website for an item, which may influence the ratings and comments the item receives from future reviewers [36–38]. This efect, known as social influence bias [39], has also been highlighted in the previous research. Ma, Khansa, Deng and Kim [27], for example, found that individuals, in particular males, who lacked experience, geographic mobility, or social connectedness were highly likely to be influenced by prior reviews posted on online review websites such as Yelp when leaving post-consumption reviews. Dellarocas, Gao and Narayan [40] reported that existing reviews lead to the emergence of a sequential bias in that consumers preferred to rate products that had already been reviewed by many other reviewers. Consequently, items with high number of ratings received additional reviews at a faster pace than items with low number of reviews. Given that people tend to take into account ‘number of reviews’ as a sign of credibility when using reviews for making decisions $[ 2 5 , 4 1 ]$ , items that receive a higher number of reviews may increasingly draw more attentions from consumers of review information.

Other researchers argue that sequential bias in online reviews can appear in the form of a decreasing trend in the valence of reviews over time. Li and Hitt [30], for instance, reported that ratings of a book followed a declining trend. Similarly, Askalidis, Kim and Malthouse [16] found that the self-motivated reviews posted by self-selected individuals exhibited a downward trend in terms of rating stars and review length, whereas no decreasing trend appeared in the retailerprompted reviews $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ reviews provided in response to the survey invitations sent by retailers to consumers). This finding implies that exposure to existing reviews is more likely to have negative rather than positive efects on individuals' evaluations of reviewed items. This is consistent with the notion of negativity bias [42], which postulates that negative information, in general, has more value to people than positive information [43,44]. Negativity bias in eWOM suggests that negative reviews tend to be more valued and more influential than positive reviews on perceived usefulness of reviews, consumers' evaluations of products and services, and actual sales of reviewed items $[ 4 2 , 4 5 ]$ Chevalier and Mayzlin [46], for example, showed that an incremental unfavorable review was more powerful in decreasing book sales than incremental favorable reviews were in increasing sales. Bae and Lee [47] reported that negatively valenced reviews were more influential on individuals' purchase intentions than positive reviews. Finally, Sen and Lerman [48] found that unfavorable reviews for utilitarian pro ducts were perceived to be more useful than favorable reviews.

In summary, prior studies in commercial settings collectively sug gest that diferent forms of bias including self-selection bias (i.e., acquisition bias and underreporting bias) as well as social influence bias (i.e., sequential bias and negativity bias) can give an oblique direction to online reviews. Prior research has also emphasized that biased reviews can significantly and systematically impact individuals' decisions related to purchasing, adoption, and use of products and services.

## 2.2. Bias in online reviews of physicians

Bias may also emerge in online physician reviews [2]; however, the sources and nature of bias in this context are diferent from those in other contexts. For example, doctors are generally held in very high regard by the public [12,49]. Many individuals view doctors as heroes who help to heal and save people's lives. Therefore, people could have a positive predisposition toward doctors as a whole, resulting in a unique form of acquisition bias [26]. In particular, if a patient chooses, or is referred to, a doctor (e.g., a specialist) who is endorsed by another doctor who the patient trusts (e.g., PCPs), the initial positive attitude toward the newly chosen doctor could be reinforced [50]. Consistently, extant literature has shown that pre-visit trust in a physician positively impacts patient satisfaction with the visit and physician [51].

Underreporting bias may also occur in online reviews of physicians, albeit in a unique way. Underreporting bias postulates that customers with moderate sentiments are less likely to share their experiences online [26], whereas in the healthcare sector, dissatisfied patients may also avoid posting negative feedback about their doctors in online environments. A major reason for this avoidance is that patient-provider ties are mostly afect-oriented [13] and established over time; hence, these ties are deemed valuable to patients [52] and they may not want to destroy such relationships by providing negative feedback about a visit. Accordingly, dissatisfied and moderately-satisfied patients are likely to be underrepresented in online physician reviews. The results of prior studies support these notions. Lagu, Hannon, Rothberg and Lin denauer [18] reported that 88% of the physician ratings were positive, whereas only 6% were negative. Kadry, Chu, Kadry, Gammas and Macario [6] found that 61.5%, 57.74%, and 74% of the reviews on PRWs that used a 100-point, 5-point, and 4-point scale, respectively, had ratings of 75% or higher. Similarly, Gao, McCullough, Agarwal and Jha [53] found that 45.80% of the physicians rated on RateMDs received the highest scores, whereas only 12% received the lowest scores. Finally, Gao, Greenwood, Agarwal and McCullough [2] demonstrated that patients were more likely to rate and discuss physicians with higher overall scores in online environments. The findings of that study also showed that patients were likely to exaggerate their opinions when rating physicians online.

Prior studies that have examined bias in online physician reviews have mainly measured it in terms of rating distributions (e.g., Lagu et al. [18]; Kadry et al. [6]) and rarely assessed other forms of bias. Moreover, those studies have not emphasized the unique aspects of eWOM in the healthcare domain such as personal interactions and social ties between patients and healthcare providers. Moreover, very few studies have taken into account the potential role of social influence bias (i.e., existing reviews) in this context, particularly when comparing hospital-owned rating websites and commercial PRWs. This study aims to fill these gaps by extending the theoretical understanding of bias, particularly in the healthcare sector, and providing recommendations to healthcare organizations accordingly.

## 3. Hypothesis development

## 3.1. Between-website biases

The types and sources of bias in online physician reviews vary between HOWs and commercial PWRs. The reviews published on HOWs are hospital-prompted, meaning that patients receive personal invita tions from their healthcare providers to complete a satisfaction survey. As a result, patients may feel recognized, important, and appreciative of being heard, which according to the norm of reciprocity [54], can drive them to return the favor by contributing positive reviews for their physicians. Moreover, contributors to HOWs are likely to exhibit acquisition bias due to their personal interactions with the hospitals and afect-oriented relationships with their doctors [13]. Underreporting bias can also play a major role in this context, as dissatisfied patients are likely to feel their responses to hospital-prompted surveys may not be fully anonymous. Consequently, the possible fear of retaliation associated with leaving negative feedback about a doctor is expected to be high when responding to hospital-prompted surveys [3]. Fear of retaliation is a critical factor in this context because people do not normally want to be sued by their doctors [10,11] nor do they want their future care to be compromised due to negative feedback [55]. As a result, people may be reluctant to share negative care experiences through HOWs.

On the other hand, self-motivated individuals, who have not been invited, post reviews on commercial PRWs. According to the notion of underreporting bias [26], these individuals are highly likely to have extreme opinions and experiences with their care providers. In particular, dissatisfied patients are more likely to be represented in the ratings posted on PRWs than on HOWs as patients may perceive a higher level of anonymity and lower fear of retaliation when posting reviews on PRWs. Moreover, patients who use commercial PRWs to write reviews are likely to read the existing reviews of physicians before rating them. According to the theories of social influence bias [16,39] and negativity efect [43,45], existing negative reviews are significantly more likely to influence individuals' perceptions and evaluations of an entity than positive reviews. Therefore, the pre-review disposition that is highly likely to occur in commercial PRWs can negatively influence reviewers' evaluations of physicians. In the long run, this phenomenon can make reviews of physicians posted on commercial PRWs less favorable than the corresponding reviews collected by hospitals and presented on their websites. As such, we expect the physician ratings on HOWs to be more concentrated around high scores. We hypothesize:

H1. Overall rating scores of physicians are on average higher on HOWs than on commercial PRWs.

H2. Overall rating scores of physicians are on average less dispersed on HOWs than on commercial PRWs.

## 3.2. Within-website biases

Prior research has demonstrated that patient ratings of physicians on online review platforms are asymmetrically distributed and skewed toward high rating scores [6,56]. A reason for this positive skewness in ratings is likely to be an acquisition bias resulting from patients' high regard for their doctors, in particular if the doctor is endorsed by another doctor known to the patient, a family member, or a trustworthy friend [1]. Moreover, patients' emotional satisfaction with the results of a treatment or healthcare procedure can lead them to return the favor in the form of an overly positive review [57]. This is in line with the norm of reciprocity [54,58] and afect theory of social exchange that suggests emotion is a central feature of social exchange processes [59]. Dissatisfied patients, however, do not have that afective driver to post a review. In particular, as discussed earlier. if the patients' feedbacks are shared directly with the healthcare provider, information privacy concern and fear of retaliation may demotivate dissatisfied patients from posting negative reviews about their care experiences [3,60]. As such, a context-specific form of underreporting bias, with dissatisfied and moderately-satisfied patients being underrepresented, emerges in hospital-prompted reviews.

Given that high-quality physicians are more likely to have satisfied patients, those physicians are also likely to receive more reviews from their patients than their low-quality colleagues. Moreover, number of patients (or requests to be a patient) of a physician is generally associated with the quality of the physician [2]. Therefore, everything else being equal, the number of patients who can potentially leave a review for high-quality physicians is expected to be higher than that for lowquality physicians. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

H3. Physicians with higher overall rating scores on HOWs receive higher number of ratings and comments.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Sampling and data collection

Because no oficial list of hospitals that provide physician reviews on their websites exists, we used Google to find such hospitals in dif ferent regions. We realized most of the hospitals with published phy sician ratings were located in the Eastern United States. Thus, we selected three healthcare organizations from three states including Pennsylvania (University of Pittsburgh Medical Center – Mercy Hospital), New York (Northwell Health), and Massachusetts (Brigham and Women's Hospital). To diversify the sample geographically, we also randomly selected two of the 23 medical centers operating under a notfor-profit healthcare system in Utah, namely Intermountain Healthcare. The two medical centers included McKay Dee Hospital and Orem Community Hospital located in Ogden and Orem, respectively. The five sampled healthcare entities, referred to as Mercy, Northwell, Brigham, McKay, and Orem thereafter in this paper, represent a range of practice sizes, geographic locations, types of organizations (e.g., rural vs. urban, clinic vs. hospital vs. health network, afiliated vs. non-afiliated with universities, etc.), and types of medical services ofered to the community. The websites associated with the five healthcare organizations publish ratings and comments of the doctors who have a minimum number of completed surveys. Brigham, for example, posts ratings of doctors who have been rated by at least 20 patients over the past 12 months [62].

As of April 2017 (our data collection month), Mercy, Brigham, McKay, and Orem, had ratings published for 352, 500, 269, and 300 physicians, respectively, on their websites. We took all available ratings from these websites. Northwell was comprised of several medical centers and hence, more than six thousand physicians worked in the hos pitals under that healthcare system. However, Northwell's website did not clearly provide separate lists of physicians working in diferent hospitals perhaps because a high percentage of their physicians were afiliated with more than one hospital under the medical group. Therefore, we decided to collect ratings of a randomly selected 500 physicians working at any of Northwell's hospitals. By selecting 500 physicians, we made sure that Northwell would be neither underrepresented nor overrepresented in our sample. Thus, in total, we collected ratings of 1921 physicians published on the websites afiliated with the five healthcare organizations.

We then collected ratings associated with the same 1921 physicians posted on four commercial PRWs including HealthGrades, Vitals, RateMDs, and Google Reviews. These websites are among the most widely used PRWs in the United States and have been frequently used in previous studies [63–65]. However, not all the physicians had ratings on all the websites. Further, in order to improve the reliability of results and reduce random error, we excluded the physicians with less than five ratings on each website from the dataset. Accordingly, ratings associated with a total of 1111, 1216, 291, and 124 physicians were collected in April 2017 from HealthGrades, Vitals, RateMDs, and Google Reviews, respectively.

## 5. Data analysis and results

## 5.1. Characteristics of physicians and ratings

We used IBM SPSS Statistics 25 to perform all the analyses. Table 1 summarizes physician characteristics in terms of gender, specialty, and graduation year. Accordingly, 30.35% of the sampled physicians were female, which is reasonably consistent with the gender distribution of physicians in the United States as of March 2018 (34% female) [66].

Physician specialties were determined based on a five-category schema adapted from a framework provided by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services [67]. The categories included PCPs (e.g., general family practice, general internal practice, and pediatrics), medical specialties (e.g., cardiology, dermatology, and pulmonary diseases), obstetrics/gynecology, surgical specialties (e.g., orthopedic surgery, neurological surgery, ophthalmology, and urology), and other specialties (e.g., anesthesiology, occupational medicine, and preventive medicine). This categorization approach, which has also been adopted in prior studies [53], would enable us to reduce the number of specialties in our sample from > 30 to five, making our statistical analysis more powerful, parsimonious, and interpretable.

Table 2 presents the descriptive statistics of the physician ratings. Accordingly, the number of physicians with at least five reviews varies significantly across commercial websites. HealthGrades and Vitals were the two outlets that presented reviews of most of the sampled physicians, whereas RateMDs and Google Reviews presented a lower percentage of them. We also defined and calculated composite ratings representing physicians' ratings on commercial websites as a whole. To calculate the composite ratings, we only used HealthGrades. Vitals, and RateMDs because very few physicians had reviews on all the four PRWs and Google Reviews was the website with the lowest number of physicians common with the other websites. We used Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to develop a weighted average equation (Eq. 1) to cal culate composite ratings for the 218 physicians rated on HealthGrades, Vitals, and RateMDs. The PCA results showed that the ratings on the websites converged on a single component, explaining 67.41% of the variations in the ratings.

## 5.2. Hypothesis testing

In order to test the between-website biases (H1 and H2), we matched the overall ratings between the HOWs and the four commercial PRWs. Then, we conducted a series of paired t-test and Levene's test to examine diferences between mean values and dispersions of the ratings posted on those two types of websites. To test H1, we combined the ratings collected from the five HOWs and compared them with the associated ratings on HealthGrades, Vitals, RateMDs, and Google Reviews, as well as with the composite ratings. The results of the paired ttests, presented in Table 3, revealed that the ratings provided on the HOWs were collectively greater than those presented on the PRWs, supporting H1.

We performed a similar set of analysis for each hospital separately to better understand their similarities and diferences in terms of between-website bias. The results were consistent with the findings corresponding the HOWs as a whole (Table 3). The only exception was that the paired t-test performed between the ratings posted on McKay's website and Google Reviews did not show a statistically significant diference. In addition, we were not able to statistically compare the ratings posted on Brigham's website and Google Reviews because there were not enough reviews of Brigham physicians on Google for the comparison to generate a valid result.

In order to test H2, we conducted a series of Levene's test, which is a standard technique for assessing homogeneity of variance (i.e., if k samples have equal variances) [68,69]. Using Levene's test, we compared rating variations between the HOWs as a whole and the commercial websites. The results, presented in Table 4, showed that the ratings posted on the HOWs were significantly less dispersed than those published on PRWs, supporting H2. We then performed a similar analysis for each hospital. The results consistently demonstrated that the variation of overall ratings across physicians of each organization was much lower on the HOWs than PRWs. Moreover, because only one Brigham physician had more than four reviews on Google, we were not able to perform Levene's test for Brigham versus Google Reviews.

To examine the hypothesized within-website bias in terms of higher number of ratings and comments for physicians with higher overall rating scores (H3), we used negative binomial (NB) regression analysis. NB regression is a type of generalized linear regression, and an extension of Poisson regression, that is well suited for modeling over-dis persed count dependent variables [70,71] such as number of ratings and comments in the present study. This type of regression analysis has been used in management and information systems studies among others [72–74]. To test H3, we combined the data associated with the five hospitals and performed two NB regression models. Number of ratings and number of comments were dependent variables (DVs) in the first and second models, respectively. Overall rating score was included in the two models as the independent variable (IV). Following prior studies in the context of online physician reviews [2,53], we included hospital, specialty, gender, and graduation year as covariates in the models. The coefficients in the NB models were estimated using the following general equations:

$$
\begin{array}{c} L n (\mu_ {i}) = \beta_ {0} + \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {3} \beta_ {j + 1} * H o s p i t a l _ {j i} + \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {3} \beta_ {j + 5} * S p e c i a l t y _ {j i} + \beta_ {9} * G e n d e r _ {i} \\ + \beta_ {1 0} * G r a d u a t i o n \_ Y e a r _ {i} + \beta_ {1 1} * R a t i n g \_ S c o r e _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

To assess the hypothesized within-website bias in terms of higher percent of reviews with comments (PRC) for physicians with higher overall rating scores (H4), we performed an ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression model as well as a logit-linear regression model. The DV in the OLS equation was $P R C _ { i s }$ calculated for each physician by dividing their number of comments by their overall number of reviews on the website. The IV was overall rating score associated with each physician. We also included hospital, specialty, gender, and graduation year as control variables in the model. The estimated linear regression equation was as follows:

$$
P R C _ {i} = \beta_ {0} + \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {3} \beta_ {j + 1} * H o s p i t a l _ {j i} + \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {3} \beta_ {j + 5} * S p e c i a l t y _ {j i} + \beta_ {9} * G e n d e r _ {i}
$$

$$
+ \beta_ {1 0} * G r a d u a t i o n \_ Y e a r _ {i} + \beta_ {1 1} * R a t i n g \_ S c o r e _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i}\tag{4}
$$

The DV in the logit-linear model was Logit(PRC ) calculated using the following logit transformation equation:

$$
\operatorname{Logit} \left(P R C _ {i}\right) = \operatorname{Ln} \left(\frac {P R C _ {i}}{1 - P R C _ {i}}\right)\tag{5}

$$

Another interesting finding was that PCPs and surgeons were, respectively, least likely and most likely to receive comments along with ratings from their patients.

## 5.3. Regression diagnostics

During the regression analysis process, the model assumptions (e.g., normality and homoscedasticity of residuals as well as linearity of relationships in OLS models) were examined and confirmed. In addition, the observations were examined to detect potential outliers using standardized residual values and Cook's distance statistic [75]. To do so, the cutof value of 4/n was used for Cook's distance, where n is the number of observations [76]. Accordingly, five (1.7%), ten (3.7%), 17 (3.4%), nine (1.8%), and 17 (4.8%) of the physicians listed for Orem, McKay, Brigham, Northwell, and Mercy, respectively, were identified as outliers and eliminated from the regression analyses. For Brigham, we also noticed that 87 of the 500 sampled physicians did not have comment information on their pages on Brigham's website. However, it was not clear whether they had received zero comments from reviewers or it was a technical issue on that website. Thus, we decided to exclude those physicians from the analyses in which number of comments was in volved as a variable, or used to calculate a variable (e.g., PRC).

## 6. Discussion

Prior research has shown that the online physician reviews pub lished on commercial PRWs are skewed toward positive values [2]. Our findings associated with H1 and H2 suggest that the ratings posted on HOWs are more concentrated around highly favorable values than the ratings published on PRWs. Accordingly, the ratings posted on HOWs may not reliably reflect the quality of physicians and are not varied enough to clearly and significantly distinguish high-quality, mediumquality, and low-quality physicians. In other words, these ratings may not be as useful as they could be to the patients who want to rely on the physician ratings and corresponding comments posted on HOWs to decide on which doctors to choose. The considerably narrow range of rating scores reduces the amount of information they convey to reflect quality diferences between physicians. Moreover, users of HOWs who notice the consistently positive comments and high ratings of physicians may become suspicious of the honesty of website providers and credibility of reviewers. This may negatively afect users' trust in, and use of, those websites.

The results corresponding H3 demonstrated that physicians who had relatively high overall rating scores were more likely to receive a higher number of comments and ratings from patients, implying that physicians at diferent quality levels (i.e., rating scores) are subject to diferent levels of bias. This finding, coupled with the between-website biases, implies that individuals who use the reviews posted on HOWs may not find the diferences between rating scores meaningful and hence, may excessively rely on the number of ratings as a main decision criterion. This may seem to be safe for patients as higher number of ratings is demonstrated to be associated with higher overall rating scores, which means decisions based on either of those two factors would be consistent with the other one. The findings corresponding H4 suggest that reviews are in general more likely to contain comments for high quality physicians (in terms of rating scores). This finding implies that, whether patients are less likely to post comments for physicians with lower overall scores or the administrators of HOWs do not publish negative comments on their websites, those physicians' comments may not be as comprehensive as the comments of physicians with higher overall scores. Consequently, patients who do not find enough variation in the ratings (H2) to judge quality diferences among physicians may also realize that some physicians (i.e., those with overall lower scores) do not have a suficient number of comments showing their performance. Thus, patients may find these online reviews with such biases not informative enough and not helpful in making informed decisions. These propositions, however, can be examined and confirmed using observational and experimental methods in future studies.

The results associated with bias for each hospital revealed that the levels and significance of bias could vary across institutions. A plausible explanation for these diferences is that satisfaction surveys might be designed and administered diferently by diferent institutions. For example, the questions on the surveys, the population of patients to whom the surveys were sent, and the review selection mechanisms possibly used for filtering the reviews could vary from one hospital to another. These diferences and their impacts on the levels and types of biases in online physician reviews can be investigated in future research.

Another interesting finding of this study is that diferent medical specialties received significantly diferent number of ratings and comments from their patients. PCPs were shown to receive the highest number of reviews, which could be due to the fact that nearly 50% of the physician ofice visits in the U.S. are to PCPs [77]. Thus, they have a significantly larger pool of potential survey respondents. Our findings, however, showed that the fraction of reviews that contained a comment was the lowest for PCPs and the highest for surgeons. A possible ex planation for this phenomenon is that surgery is generally a more complex care process than a regular PCP visit. Therefore, patients can have more details to share about their surgery experiences than their PCP visits. Further, a successful surgery can provide patients with a higher level of satisfaction and gratitude than seeing a PCP. Conversely, a failed surgery could make patients and their caregivers more angry and dissatisfied than a PCP visit that does not go well. Therefore, both satisfied and dissatisfied patients of surgeons are more likely to be motivated to tell their care stories in their reviews than patients of PCPs.

## 6.1. Implications

From a theoretical standpoint, our results substantiated and contextualized the notions of acquisition bias and underreporting bias as well as social influence bias and negativity bias in the healthcare do main. Previous studies in commercial settings suggest that, acquisition bias generally occurs because people with a positive predisposition toward specific products and services are more likely to purchase, use, and review them [16]. Whereas, acquisition bias emerges in online physician reviews possibly because doctors are, in general, held in high regard [12,49]. This predisposition positively biases people's evaluations of doctors as a whole. Moreover, the interaction-based, generally long-term relationships between patients and physicians make patients appreciative of the care they receive from their care providers [13]. Therefore, unlike consumer reviews of commercial items such as books [46], movies [33], and hotels [78], which are mostly fact-based, patients' reviews of physicians are likely to be emotion-based [13], exaggerated [2], and thereby biased diferently.

Extant literature has also shown that underreporting bias in commercial settings is generally manifested in the form of a U-shaped rating distribution [26]. Whereas, our results indicated that negative reviews of physicians were considerably less prevalent than positive reviews, realizing a context-specific, one-tailed version of underreporting bias. The more significant skewness observed in the hospital-prompted ratings compared to the self-motivated ratings further supported the notion of underreporting bias as fear of retaliation and perceived likelihood of being identified are likely to be higher when providing reviews to hospitals than when posting reviews on PRWs. Thus, dissatisfied patients are significantly less likely to contribute reviews to HOWs. These arguments are in line with previous studies that have emphasized that leaving negative feedback about physicians may entail negative consequences for patients [10,11].

Furthermore, extant literature argues that there is a relationship between existing reviews and subsequent reviews of commercial items [27,36], which is due to the role of social influence bias [16]. Our results confirm this proposition and suggest that the more intense positive skewness observed in the invitation-based reviews compared to selfmotivated reviews observed in this study and similar studies [16] could partially be due to the combined role of social influence bias and negativity bias.

This study also introduced and distinguished between two generic categories of bias, namely between-website bias and within-website bias. Accordingly, the level of bias in online reviews could not only be diferent across contexts (e.g., healthcare vs. commercial settings) and websites (PRWs vs. HOSs), but also across items (i.e., physicians) with diferent characteristics (e.g., perceived quality in terms of rating scores, physician specialty, and graduation year). Thus, it is crucial for future studies to view bias in eWOM as a multidimensional phenomenon and examine it at diferent levels to better understand its complexity, dynamics, sources, and consequences. Finally, we introduced and examined a new measure of bias, namely percentage of reviews with comments. Our results demonstrated that this measure presents a unique aspect of bias in online reviews.

From a practical standpoint, our results demonstrated that hospitals that administer patient satisfaction surveys and publish the results on their websites should be aware of, regularly measure, and possibly reduce biases in the published reviews. In this way, healthcare providers can adhere to their social, moral, and legal responsibilities in terms of protecting patients' rights, safety, and best interests. Healthcare providers should proactively monitor their physicians' ratings posted on their websites and compare those ratings with the corresponding scores published on commercial PRWs to assess potential diferences between those ratings. find the reasons behind those differences, and reduce rating biases as much as possible. For example, providers can evaluate their review filtration and selection processes on a regular basis to ensure those processes do not significantly contribute to rating biases. Moreover, if the review collection and administration process is anonymous, it should be emphasized to survey respondents that their identity would not be recognized if they respond to survey invitation messages. This may reduce privacy concerns and fear of retaliation in the respondents, and thus make the patients' evaluations of physicians more honest and less skewed. Further, hospitals should make users of their websites aware that the ratings and comments posted on those websites are neither comprehensive nor representative of all patients' opinions about their physicians. Patients should be encouraged to use a variety of private and commercial PRWs and consider the reviews posted on those websites to be able to make a more informed and ap propriate decision.

Prior research has reported that individuals tend to choose items (e.g., physicians) that have a higher number of online reviews [25,41]. Our results suggest that higher-rated physicians receive more reviews than lower-rated physicians. Accordingly, demand for lower-rated physicians may decrease over time not only because of their relatively low rating scores, but also due to the low number of ratings and comments they receive. This phenomenon may be discouraging to lower-rated physicians because even if they improve their quality of services, their overall rating scores and number of reviews may take time to increase. To address this problem, hospitals can send more survey invitations to patients of physicians with fewer reviews. Another solution for hospitals to mitigate this issue is to publish only the N most recent reviews for each physician on their website, where N is a predetermined number $( { \bf e . g . } , N = 2 0 0 )$ . This approach could minimize the role of quantity of reviews in patients' choice of healthcare professionals, which could potentially encourage low-performing physicians to improve their quality of care and enables healthcare systems to optimize and spread demand for physicians.

Our results have practical implications for referring physicians, insurance firms, and government agencies. Previous studies have shown that physicians, in particular PCPs, increasingly rely on patient satisfaction scores published on rating websites to assess the quality of specialty physicians and make patient referral decisions accordingly [79]. Insurance companies and government payers may also use patients' ratings of physicians to make reimbursement decisions as part of value-based payment mechanisms [80]. Bias in online physician reviews likely makes such decisions and mechanisms misinformed and unfair. To mitigate this problem, a combination of online reviews and objective quality indicators such as readmission rates, patient safety indices (e.g., risk-adjusted mortality rate), and timeliness of care (e.g., waiting time and harmful care delays) should be used [56,63]. Such objective measures are typically developed, collected, and reported by hospitals or government agencies such as the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services [81] and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality [82]. These agencies can further improve the care quality assessment mechanisms by developing, promoting, and enforcing standard procedures and best practices for hospital-operated online review systems. Process standardization can ultimately enhance the usability and reliability of patient reviews of doctors.

## 6.2. Limitations and future research

The first limitation of this study is the number of medical centers that we used for data collection. Although we made sure to select clinics and hospitals from a range of organizational types, sizes, and geographic locations, the results might not be highly generalizable to all healthcare organizations in the United States. Future research can extend and strengthen the results of this study by collecting and analyzing additional data from other medical centers. Moreover, we did not have access to the reviewers' background information such as age, gender, health status, socio-economic status, and motivations behind rating physicians. Thus, we were not able to include those variables in our analyses or to compare PRWs and HOS in terms of those attributes. Future research can employ observational and experimental methods to understand the role of reviewers' characteristics in this context.

Another potential direction for future research is performing text mining and sentiment analysis on the narrative comments posted for physicians [83]. This analysis can help understand the nature and degrees of bias in the comments reported on HOWs versus those posted on PRWs. Furthermore, future research can employ qualitative (e.g., case study and interviews) and quantitative (e.g., survey-based or experi mental) methods to examine the actual physician review administration and management processes at hospitals to understand how those processes could be improved. Future studies can also examine whether/ how healthcare organizations can use the reviews collected from patients to improve the quality of care services those organizations provide to their patients. The results of such studies can shed light on whether providing private review administration systems would be necessary, given the high level of bias that hospital-prompted reviews are subject to. Finally, building on the results of this study and prio research that has addressed credibility and trust in eWOM [84,85], researchers in future studies can employ observational and experimental methods to understand: 1) how perceived bias in online reviews may influence patients' trust and distrust in the reviews and rated physicians, and accordingly, 2) how patients refer to the biased reviews for their future appointments. These studies can also examine how patients process information discrepancies between HOWs and PRWs given that some physicians may have similar reviews on the two types of websites while others may have asymmetric reviews on those websites.

## 7. Conclusion

The increasing adoption of online physician reviews by Internet users along with the growing interest of healthcare providers in ofering physician rating platforms makes it critical to understand the reliability and usefulness of the reviews posted on HOWs. Accordingly, this study examined diferent forms of bias in this context. The results demonstrated that the ratings published on HOWs were significantly more biased toward higher scores and were less dispersed than the corre sponding scores published on the commercial PRWs. The results also showed that number of ratings, number of comments, and percentage of reviews that included a narrative comment were higher for physicians with higher rating scores. Thus, Internet users should be aware of such systematic biases. Hospitals should also feel more responsible about the reviews that they collect from patients and post on their websites as patients may ultimately use that information to make critical decisions for their healthcare. Accordingly, healthcare organizations should put more efort into assuring that the reviews of physicians published on their websites are as bias-free as possible.
