# IT-Enabled Revenue Cycle Transformation in Resource-Constrained Hospitals: A Collaborative Digital Options Inquiry

RAJENDRA SINGH, VITALI MINDEL, AND LARS MATHIASSEN

RAJENDRA SINGH (rajendra.singh@moore.sc.edu; corresponding author) is a clinical associate professor in the Management Science Department at the Moore School of Business, University of South Carolina. He received his Ph.D. in computer information systems from Georgia State University. Before joining academia, he worked for over 14 years as a production engineer, business process analyst, software quality consultant, and information technology (IT) project manager in various industries. His research interests include IT-enabled transformation of health-care delivery, health-care operations, and business process innovation. He has published in MIS Quarterly, Journal of the AIS, European Journal of Information Systems, and others.

VITALI MINDEL (vmindel1@gsu.edu) is a Ph.D. candidate at the Center for Process Innovation, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University. His research interests center on health information technologies, with a focus on administrative technologies and their role in revenue management in hospitals as well as on the clinical side IT adoption. He has published in the Journal of the AIS.

LARS MATHIASSEN (lmathiassen@ceprin.org) is Georgia Research Alliance Eminent Scholar, Professor at the Computer Information Systems Department, and cofounder of the Center for Process Innovation at Georgia State University. His research focuses on development of software and information services, on IT-enabled innovation of business processes, and on management and facilitation of organizational change processes. He has published extensively in major information systems and software engineering journals and has coauthored several books. He has served as senior editor for MIS Quarterly, and serves as senior editor for Information and Organization and for the Journal of Information Technology.

ABSTRACT: Although hospitals increasingly consider solutions based on information technolgy (IT) for revenue cycle management, contextual constraints and numerous IT investment alternatives make it difficult to identify and realize appropriate solutions. Thus far, however, health IT theory has been silent on how IT can support financial and administrative purposes within hospitals, focusing instead on IT for clinical support. Against this backdrop, we report on an action research project involving IT-enabled revenue cycle transformation in a financially strapped rural hospital. Our research contributes to health IT theory by expanding the discourse to include revenue cycle transformation and by demonstrating how resource-constrained hospitals can improve their financial performance through selective investments in low-cost IT solutions across the revenue cycle. We also contribute to action research methodology by generalizing our interventions to propose digital options inquiry—a systematic approach to business process transformation that combines digital options theory with diagnostic process mapping—to help researchers and practitioners jointly understand and transform business processes.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: action research, collaborative practice research, digital options inquiry, health care, health IT, revenue cycle management.

As is widely recognized, the U.S. health-care system is incredibly complex, wasteful, and underperforming [37, 93]. Inefficiencies are common; an estimated \$107– \$389 billion is wasted annually, mostly in administrative health-care operations [15, 68]. Despite the fact that U.S. health-care spending is the highest in the world [35], hospitals face significant financial challenges as costs continue to increase and reimbursements from insurance payers continue to decline [2]. As a result, almost a quarter of U.S. hospitals persistently operate at a loss and approximately three in five hospitals operate barely above break-even margins [75]. Although hospitals have little control over the socioeconomic, market, or regulatory environment in which they operate, they can improve their financial situation by increasing the performance of their revenue cycle [68]. The revenue cycle—a complex process that includes all activities related to delivering health-care services to patients and getting reimbursed for those services [14, 48, 91]—is critical to a hospital’s business operations and must function smoothly to generate the stable cash flow needed to maintain day-to-day operations and enable ongoing investments into service improvements [85, 99]. Nonetheless, inefficiencies in revenue cycle management (RCM) are widespread, often resulting in delayed and reduced reimbursements [91].

For more than two decades, business leaders and researchers have acknowledged the need to improve IT-enabled business processes [45]. Businesses operating in dynamic competitive environments typically rely on integrating IT into their day-today operations to enhance their ability to create and capture value and to improve competitive performance [92, 96]. As a strategic differentiator [96], many organizations invest in business process transformation (BPT) [45] to improve performance and productivity [16, 51]. Following this trend, hospitals are increasingly using IT to improve revenue cycle performance; anecdotal evidence suggests that IT can lead to significant gains [24, 53]. The difficulty, however, is that for resource-constrained hospitals—such as small community hospitals in rural areas—ongoing investments in expensive, large-scale IT solutions such as electronic medical record systems (EMR), computerized physician order entry systems (CPOE), and various enterprise systems are infeasible. Further, a multitude of desirable IT options exist, thus complicating decisions about which solutions to invest in [42], and many hospital managers do not realize that options exist beyond the costly systems promoted by health IT vendors. Despite this situation, the academic literature remains silent on IT use for financial and administrative purposes within hospitals, focusing instead on IT’s impact on clinical support. We thus ask the following research question: How can a resource-constrained hospital improve its revenue cycle performance through low-cost IT investments?

To investigate the question, we applied collaborative practice research (CPR) [76], a form of action research, to transform the revenue cycle of a financially strapped rural hospital (EMC, a pseudonym) in southeast Georgia. Over a two-year period, we worked closely with key stakeholders to diagnose critical information management problems, identify information requirements for improving revenue cycle performance, and develop affordable, small-scale IT solutions ranging from better use of the existing EMR system to implementation of an open source software (OSS) solution. Our work expands health IT theory discourse by including revenue cycle transformation—an underinvestigated area in the literature and an important concern for hospital managers—as well as by demonstrating how resource-constrained hospitals can improve their financial performance through selective investments in low-cost IT solutions across the revenue cycle. Further, we contribute to action research methodology by generalizing [67] the results of our collaboration with EMC into digital options inquiry, an approach to BPT that combines digital options theory [96, 97] with diagnostic process mapping [59, 66] to help researchers and practitioners jointly transform business processes.

## Hospital Revenue Cycle Management

The hospital revenue cycle begins when a patient or physician determines the need for a medical service; it ends when the charges associated with the service have been resolved through insurance payments, contractual adjustments, write-offs, or patient payments [68, 85, 98]. While the hospital staff manages and executes the revenue cycle, the cycle itself is an intrinsic part of a larger transaction involving patients and other payers, including Medicare, Medicaid, and commercial insurers. Figure 1 provides an overview of the hospital revenue cycle.

As Table 1 shows, the RCM staff performs many information-intensive activities. If these activities do not occur in a timely, accurate manner, billings can be delayed, claims rejected, and revenue lost [68, 98]. In any business, managing a stable revenue flow is important, but nowhere is the revenue-related administrative process more complex than in health care, where it can use up to 12 percent of patient service revenue [18], resulting in an estimated total cost burden of \$471 billion to U. S. health delivery organizations in 2012 [56]. Many factors increase RCM complexity. Internally, the process involves the coordination and exchange of complex information between various departments—some directly associated with clinical decision making and patient care, and others (such as registration and billing) that support care delivery and finance. Externally, some departments interact with a variety of payers, clinical providers, laboratories, billing service providers, and collection agencies. Thus, for each patient encounter, RCM not only involves delivering clinical care, but also communicating clinical and financial information to numerous internal and external partners. This complexity is one of the reasons many hospitals struggle financially. Other reasons include federal and state regulations, the expected social responsibility of hospitals to care for patients regardless of their ability to pay, the high default rate on bills (estimated to be about 50 percent) among uninsured patients, and decreasing reimbursements [2]. Moreover, reimbursements can take months and may be substantially lower than the claimed charges, straining the hospitals’ cash flow.

To improve RCM performance, hospitals deploy various IT solutions to automate rule-based tasks (such as coding and billing) and improve coordination and information exchange within and across organizational boundaries. These IT solutions vary widely in their capabilities, and range from simple spreadsheets to complex, fully integrated enterprise systems. Today, IT’s impact can be seen in all stages of RCM. Patient scheduling systems help optimize patient flow, registration systems capture information needed for billing, EMR and CPOE facilitate the clinical documentation required for itemizing medical charges, and practice management systems enable charge entry, claim submission, and account reconciliation. The practitioner literature provides anecdotal evidence on the positive impact of IT use in revenue collection activities [24, 53]. However, the IS literature on health IT has paid little attention to RCM [85], despite computerized billing being among the first IT systems in hospitals [39, 69] and one of the most commonly used.

Not all hospitals benefit equally from IT investments and costly IT solutions can have disappointing results [85]. Moreover, hospital decision makers often find it difficult to match their diverse institutional environments [68] with appropriate IT solutions. For resource-constrained hospitals, acquiring the latest technologies is infeasible, so they often rely on legacy IT and paper-based solutions for RCM activities. Consequently, these hospitals lose revenue because their IT solutions are inadequate. The problem is exacerbated by the fact that many hospital managers find it difficult to identify appropriate IT solutions from the multitude of desirable alternatives, or may not even realize that their existing IT systems can help improve certain processes.

## Digital Options Inquiry

Considering the wide variety of software solutions available for any organizational function, decision makers can feel overwhelmed, especially because many software vendors typically overpromise and underdeliver [64]. Moreover, it is not only the numerous alternatives that make identifying and implementing appropriate IT solutions difficult, it is also the changing business environment [104]. New regulations, technological advances, new competitors, and macroeconomic changes can reduce the life cycle of existing IT, which, in turn, necessitates investment in newer IT solutions even before businesses can earn a reasonable return on earlier investments. Decision makers are also often tempted to adopt the latest technologies, which can be problematic because contextual conditions vary from one organization to another [57, 95]. Clearly, IT decisions are important; productivity and organizational performance improve when IT is aligned with the task at hand [7, 51] and the results tend to be better when IT investments target specific business processes [45, 88]. But identifying appropriate IT solutions for specific business processes remains difficult and, while choosing solutions is central to IS strategy research [11, 96, 108], there is little consensus on how best to approach IT investment decisions [12, 41, 94], especially in dynamic environments rife with uncertainty.

In such environments, digital options—that is, IT-enabled strategic options—can offer organizations an incremental choice process that lets them consider IT investment alternatives in a cost-effective way [96]. With roots in the financial industry, an option gives an organization rights to future investment in a strategically important opportunity without obligations for full investment, at least until until a substantial portion of the opportunity’s uncertainty has been resolved [1, 36]. Bowman and Hurry [20] suggest three sequential stages of financial options: a shadow option (i.e., an investment opportunity that awaits recognition), a real option (i.e., an investment opportunity for which preferential access to a future investment has been obtained), and a struck option (i.e., an option that has been activated, often through a larger investment). Similarly, in the IT domain, a digital option can exist in one of three distinct stages: (a) the available digital option is known but awaits development; (b) an actionable digital option is selected and examined; and (c) a realized digital option has been acted upon [97]. Studies examining financial investments have shown that options thinking can provide sustained performance and a competitive advantage by helping organizations manage uncertainties associated with investments in different assets [20]. Management literature has also considered the identification and management of strategic managerial options an important organizational capability [61, 62].

Drawing on financial options theory, Sambamurthy et al. [96] argue that digital options can support strategic decisions about IT investments to transform business processes. Emphasizing the role of digital options in enabling digitized work processes and knowledge systems, they proposed a capability-building and entrepreneurial action framework that can enhance organizational agility in uncertain competitive environments. Further, they suggested that—like financial options—the value of digital options increases when the organization holding the options is able to exploit the opportunity. Despite the value of Sambamurthy et al.’s [96] influential work in bringing options theory into the IT discourse, questions remained about how researchers can examine digital options in an organizational context. To address this issue, Sandberg et al. [97] propose an iterative model for examining digital options that includes four interdependent activities. The first activity —context appreciation—involves understanding the organizational context and identifying suitable business processes for IT investment. The second activity—process characterization—involves identifying and describing key tasks and their interdependencies, the nature of the information processing involved in each task, current IT capability, and performance implications. In the third activity—information requirements analysis—each task is analyzed and characterized according to its information requirements. Finally, the digital options recognition activity involves iterating between the results from process characterization and information requirements analysis to provide guidance on what needs to be addressed and which digital options would be appropriate for that purpose. Although Sandberg et al.’s [97] model helps in understanding the role of organizational context, business process characteristics, and information requirements in determining digital options, it does not explain how researchers and practitioners can systematically engage in context appreciation and process characterization activities and develop the selected digital options to realize BPT.

Hence, during our engagement at EMC, we worked closely with RCM staff to diagnose problems in the hospital’s revenue cycle, identify related information requirements, and develop various IT solutions in response to those requirements. Generalizing [67] from these collaborative problem-solving experiences, we draw on digital options theory [96, 97] to propose a model for digital options inquiry that centers on the interplay between identifying process information requirements and developing digital options for BPT. As shown in Figure 2, by combining digital options theory with diagnostic process mapping [66], the model offers researchers and practitioners a systematic approach for jointly engaging in BPT.

## Diagnostic Process Mapping

Just as physicians must diagnose a patient before considering treatment options— including specific procedures and medications—organizations must diagnose a business process before considering their digital options [97]. Such diagnostic activity must recognize the business process context by analyzing perceived problems, their root causes, and their effects. The literature identifies different process diagnosis techniques that vary in complexity and sophistication [59]. We adapt a simple yet useful method of mapping business process problems [66] that can lay the ground for context appreciation and process characterization activities [97] and for identifying information requirements. Such an approach is especially helpful in action research, where academic knowledge and experience merges with practitioners’ domain expertise to uncover the underlying causes of problems in business processes. Outside researchers bring a fresh perspective, which helps practitioners illuminate the perceived process problems. At the same time, practitioners help in understanding the specific organizational context.

Generally speaking, a map is an interpretive description of a situation that offers insight into possible actions to take in that situation or ones like it [66]. In drawing a map, one can select and attach meaning to some elements or events instead of others [66]. Diagnostic maps are based on the idea that a problem situation has a cause and effects that call for possible solutions. Accordingly, a diagnostic map of a business process relates situations perceived as problems (including anomalies and failures) to their root causes and to more general organizational or behavioral features [66]. Each map locates and describes an existing problem, as well as possible responses (as perceived by participants) following the pattern in Figure 3 [66]. This mapping technique forces participants to systematically examine a situation as a specific problem, viewing it as the cause or consequence of other problems within other maps.

## Information Requirements Identification

After uncovering business process problems in an organization, participants must identify the information requirements as the basis for developing specific digital options [27, 31, 97]. We can view an organization as a set of multiple interlocking processes [29], and many factors can disrupt these processes. Such factors may include lack of human capabilities [65], insufficient IS resources [92], and issues with cross-functional coordination and integration [60, 109]. Often, however, organizations face information management problems rooted in a misalignment between the information required and the information available [31]. For instance, an organizational unit might have diligent employees and managers, and even the resources it needs. However, if the information it uses to support its operations is incorrect or incomplete in key respects [31], it is likely to produce suboptimal output. Moreover, because processes and tasks interlock in modern organizations [29, 70], suboptimal outputs caused by information deficiencies are likely to ripple throughout the organization, causing complications elsewhere. Researchers have identified multiple information requirements, including equivocality, ambiguity, quality, uncertainty, timeliness, and shared understanding [3, 13, 27, 28, 84]. Here, we focus on requirements as requisite conditions for effective information capture and exchange across a business process. Researchers have considered two broad categories of such information requirements: reach and richness [26, 38, 96].

Figure 3. Diagnostic Process Mapping

Information reach refers to the “number of people who participate in the sharing of information” [38, p. 23]. Sambamurthy et al. [96] define reach as the extent to which a firm deploys common, integrated, and connected IT-enabled processes. Hence, we find high reach in processes that “tie activity and information flows across departmental units, functional units, geographical regions, and value network partners (including suppliers, customers, and vendors)” [96, p. 247]. Generally, information reach refers to the flow efficiency of messages between (a) tasks within a process, (b) a task within one process and a task within another process inside the same organization, and (c) a task within a process and a task within another process in a different organization. Information flow problems include those related to the rate and timeliness of message exchange among intended recipients within and across organizational boundaries.

Information richness is also important for supporting management and organization design [27], as it can be futile and even damaging if information is inaccurate, incomprehensible, confusing, or incomplete. Generally, information richness denotes the degree of the information’s accuracy. Specifically, it refers to the quality of information from the user’s perspective [38, p. 23]. Accordingly, users might define information richness in terms of its bandwidth (the amount of information that can be transmitted), currency (speed of information transmission), customization, interactivity, reliability, and security [38, p. 25]. Sambamurthy et al. define information richness as the “quality of information collected about transactions in the process, transparency of that information to other processes and systems that are linked to it, and the ability to use the information to adapt or reengineer the process” [96, p. 247].

Together, information reach and richness provide an accurate profile of a business process. To achieve BPT, however, reach and richness must complement each other. Any imbalance—or trade-off—between reach and richness can generate asymmetries that limit information exchange within and across organizational boundaries [38, p. 28]. By allowing easy information sharing, digital networks make it possible for people to exchange rich information, thereby bridging information asymmetry and eliminating the reach–richness trade-off [38, 96].

## Digital Options Development

Next, participants evaluate a portfolio of digital options and choose specific ones to develop, engaging in exploration (searching for new assets and capabilities) and exploitation (operating with and refining existing assets and capabilities) [73]. Each organization has specific internal and external circumstances; to arrive at an appropriate balance between exploration and exploitation [73], participants must continuously identify and develop digital options, finding alternative options for those that are not realized.

Navigating the three stages of digital options—available, actionable, and realized [97]— can be difficult. The process requires that participants understand multiple alternatives, skillfully evaluate each alternative, and effectively implement the selected alternative. As the available options increase, it may not be feasible to develop all of them. Moreover, the consideration of available digital options must take into account the organizational context [89], including level of competition, financial constraints, or cultural idiosyncrasies. For example, considering a centralized IT solution holds limited value if the organization cannot afford it or key employees are likely to resist it due to internal politics [87]. Insufficient attention to organizational context will likely result in failure to move a digital option from actionable to realized.

## Business Process Transformation

BPT—variously referred to as business process reengineering [29, 46], business process management [58], collaborative business engineering [34], and process innovation [47]— is an organization-wide approach that “requires redesigns in which IT, business processes, and other organizational elements are aligned and jointly optimized” [45, p. 8]. Compared to other management approaches, BPT focuses on intra- and interorganizational business processes and requires a complex alignment of processes, information, and systems as work practices change to accommodate emerging information needs during IT adoption and implementation [87]. While prior research [97] has suggested a useful processual approach to recognizing an organization’s digital options, our digital options inquiry model includes explicit consideration of a digital option’s impact on BPT. Thus, diagnostic mapping, analyzing information requirements, and developing select digital options can lead to the realization of digital options that successfully transform the target business process.

## Research Design

## Collaborative Practice Research

Because the driver of this research was to improve RCM performance enabled by IT in a hospital context, we selected action research [9, 33, 90, 103] as our research method. Many studies have successfully adopted and applied action research to implement IT-enabled organizational change and studied the outcomes of such change [8, 50, 55, 74]. Action research has also proved useful in investigating complex issues related to health care, and specifically in studying the consequences of IT implementations [21, 22, 30, 63].

Combining theory generation with researcher intervention to solve immediate organizational problems [10], action research seeks to “contribute both to the practical concerns of people in an immediate problematic situation and to the goals of social science by joint collaboration within a mutually acceptable ethical framework” [90, p. 499]. Susman and Evered [103] have suggested several characteristics that make action research a desirable method in a study such as ours. Most important, action research let us participate in both the research and problem-solving aspects of EMC’s situation in a way that links theory with practice and thinking with doing [102]. Further, as a particular form of engaged scholarship [106], action research helped us understand the different perspectives of key EMC stakeholders and thereby to study complex problems related to RCM transformation.

Despite its utility, action research presents specific challenges, including the need for researchers to have an in-depth understanding of the local context and to adapt the research design and specific interventions to accommodate changes in that context [25, p. 153]. To address these challenges, we adopted a particular type of action research—collaborative practice research [76]—in which researchers work closely with key stakeholders involved in a specific business practice to gain sufficient understanding of the local context and to participate actively in the problem-solving process. Hence, choosing CPR helped us reduce the substantial RCM knowledge gap that existed at the start of our engagement.

CPR strikes a useful balance between rigor and relevance and has been successfully applied in other information systems (IS) studies [19, 40, 54, 55]. Iversen et al. [55] suggest several characteristics that distinguish CPR from other action research approaches. For this study, our CPR has focused on understanding, developing support for, and improving specific professional practice, and has supported close collaboration between researchers and practitioners to develop and implement the agreed-upon interventions. With this focus on interventions into a specific business practice, CPR affords unique opportunities to develop action research methodology dedicated to that practice. Hence, similar to the way that CPR was applied to reflective systems development [77, 78, 79] and contributed to action research into IS development, this study has applied CPR to develop digital options inquiry as a contribution to action research into BPT.

## Data Collection and Analysis

The first and third authors were involved throughout the collaboration with EMC; the second author joined later with an interest in RCM and in adding independent analytical capability to the research team. Data collection started in March 2008, when we held an initial workshop with EMC’s revenue cycle stakeholders. As a workshop follow-up, EMC’s chief financial officer (CFO) invited us to visit the hospital in April 2008. During that visit, we interviewed the CFO, the business office director, the director of coding and documentation, the billing supervisor, a nurse manager, and the IT manager. These early interactions resulted in a two-year collaboration with EMC. Our goal was to identify and develop affordable, smallscale IT solutions that would improve EMC’s revenue cycle performance. We visited EMC once each month for a full-day session in which we reviewed the progress of various interventions with the steering committee (the CFO, the business office director, the director of coding and documentation, and the two researchers), interviewed RCM staff, and planned for subsequent interventions. In addition, the first author visited EMC every other week to collect data, facilitate and manage interventions, provide training to revenue cycle staff, and coordinate technical issues with EMC’s IT team. We made a final follow-up visit in June 2011.

We collected evidence from multiple primary and secondary sources (see Table 2). In all, we conducted more than 125 semistructured in-person interviews at EMC, covering more than 40 employees across key managerial functions and different RCM stages. We prepared a protocol to structure the interviews and to collect appropriate information, and tailored it for specific interviewees. For example, the protocol for the IT specialist included information about EMC’s IT infrastructure, an overview of IT solutions supporting RCM, current and planned projects, security risks, and other technical challenges. Interviews typically lasted 30 to 120 minutes, with the two researchers taking separate notes. We recorded all interviews, except those that discussed specific patient cases or when an interviewee requested that the interview not be recorded. We transcribed all the interviews and workshops relevant for diagnostic mapping, requirements identification, and analysis of possible solutions to various RCM problems. We conducted direct observations of how different RCM staff members conducted their day-to-day work, which technologies they used and how they used them, what problems they faced, and which opportunities for improvement they saw. Apart from face-to-face interviews and direct observations, we also interacted through e-mail and phone to clarify issues and collect additional information. In particular, we requested and received weekly data updates for key interventions. We also reviewed secondary data sources, including technical specifications of the EMR system, internal presentations, minutes of monthly staff meetings, and e-mails.

We generalized [67] the digital options inquiry model (Figure 2) based on our experiences at EMC and used it to organize and analyze the rich data, as well as to present a comprehensive account of how we collaborated with EMC stakeholders to explore and exploit digital options to address the hospital’s identified RCM information requirements. ## Analyses and Results

## Diagnostic Process Mapping

Following the diagnostic mapping technique and based on both discussions with RCM staff and field observations during the initial EMC visits, we created a map for each revenue cycle stage. These maps (summarized in the Online Appendix) identify relevant internal and external conditions that led to problems in the hospital’s RCM, reveal the immediate impact of those problems, and point to possible interventions to address them. We improved the maps’ validity through discussions with key stakeholders, which helped us establish both shared awareness of RCM problems and a joint commitment to action. Overall, the maps illustrated the need to address several information reach and richness requirements to improve EMC’s RCM performance, as summarized in Table 3 and detailed in the following.

## Information Requirements Identification

Most problems reported during EMC’s billing and revenue recovery stages were rooted in information reach and richness deficiencies in the scheduling and registration stages. We found that the registration stage related deficiencies in information richness were often a result of inadequate training, time pressures, and unavailable or outdated insurance information (Table 3, row A). For a medical claim to be accepted and successfully processed by a third-party payer, it must be complete and accurate, containing all details of patient demographics and the subscriber’s insurance coverage. Missing information caused delays in claim submission; if claims were delayed more than 60 days after a patient visit, some payers refused reimbursement. We observed that the hospital submitted 60–70 claims every month with missing or inaccurate information, which led to processing slowdowns and subsequent denials. In addition, deficiencies in information richness during scheduling and registration often caused inefficiencies in later RCM stages, requiring billing specialists to spend considerable time tracking down information they should have already received (Table 3, row B).

Another information richness requirement related to patient creditworthiness (Table 3, row C). Approximately one in six patients were self-pay patients—that is, they were uninsured and thus responsible for paying the bills out of pocket. Further, among the insured, a large proportion did not meet their insurance deductibles and were responsible for paying a share of the medical bill. Without knowing a patient’s financial situation, EMC registration staff members could not know which patients they should ask to pay upfront (for nonemergency care) and could not estimate the likelihood of getting paid back. Not knowing the creditworthiness of patients also hurt the revenue recovery stage as the business office staff did not possess all the relevant information needed to negotiate payments on delinquent accounts. We also detected information richness deficiencies during the clinical encounter stage that impacted the documentation, coding, and billing stages. In many cases, nurses and physicians neglected to document some procedures, medical equipment, lab tests, or medications provided to patients (Table 3, row D). As a result, the claims submitted to thirdparty payers and patients did not include all charges. This would lead to the hospital providing care without getting reimbursed for it. We uncovered further deficiency in information richness in the billing and payment posting stages (Table 3, row E). After receiving reimbursements from third-party payers, the billing and payment posting departments did not have the information on whether the payments received were in accordance with the contractual agreement. As is typical in the health-care industry, EMC had negotiated rates for reimbursements for different codes with each insurance company, and the payment posting department was not certain whether it received correct payments.

The diagnostic mapping also helped uncover information reach issues within RCM-related departments and other hospital functional units (such as finance) and externally with third-party payers. Within RCM, we observed deficiencies in information flows between the registration and the billing departments. Often, information that was supposed to be sent by registration staff to the billing department was delayed—and in some cases, not sent at all—causing breakdowns in coordination (Table 3, row B). In addition, we found information reach deficiencies in the revenue recovery stage, when the business office staff attempted to reclaim overdue payments from patients (Table 3, row F). The staff often failed to initiate timely follow-ups with self-pay patients. As a result, unpaid balances were often sent to collection agencies or had to be written off. We also found deficiency in information reach between the hospital and thirdparty payers (Table 3, row G). To authorize nonemergency care, the scheduling staff had to send the information it obtained from patients and their physicians to insurance companies to get preauthorization. Without that preauthorization, the hospital could provide care, but would not get reimbursed. In many cases, the information transfer from the scheduling staff to the payers took too long; in other cases it did not happen at all. Both scenarios led to lost revenue.

## Digital Options Development

Based on the information requirements derived from the diagnostic maps, we worked with EMC stakeholders to identify and develop digital options to improve RCM performance. Some of these options were based on exploration efforts (i.e., they were new assets or capabilities that EMC acquired), while others were based on exploitation efforts (i.e., they built on EMC’s existing assets and capabilities).

To address the information richness deficiencies during registration, we exploited a feature in the hospital’s EMR system (Table 3, row A). We discovered that the EMR contained an unused registration quality report that could help registration staff review and address missing and inaccurate information by identifying errors based on nine preconfigured parameters (including patient address, demographic information, and subscriber insurance information). After securing staff commitment and ongoing training, we made the option actionable in a pilot run and subsequently realized it as an integral part of the registration stage. The hospital later complemented this initiative by having the EMR vendor configure pop-up messages for all common registration errors to remind registration staff to correct any missing or incomplete patient information during registration (Table 3, row A). To address information richness requirements related to patient creditworthiness, we explored available IT solutions on the market (Table 3, row C). EMC made this option actionable by acquiring an online credit-checking software, then quickly realized the option by integrating the software into the registration and revenue recovery stages.

We also addressed information richness deficiencies in the clinical-encounter stage by exploiting an existing unused CPOE module in the hospital’s EMR to capture patient encounter-related information (Table 3, row D). Although the option was readily available and actionable, it was not easily realized due to resistance from the clinical staff. The physicians and nurses expressed concerns that using the CPOE might increase their workload and disrupt their routine. Therefore, the option was put on hold. Instead, we worked with the director of coding and documentation to design paper-based charge checklists that the nurses agreed to use. Over time, as the nurses got used to documenting charge-related information based on checklists, their resistance to CPOE subsided; by June 2011 (nearly two years after initial implementation), 80 percent of nurses used CPOE. In contrast, the physicians refused to use the paper-based checklists, and by summer 2011, the physicians used CPOE for only 27 percent of orders. Thus, the CPOE exploitation took much longer than anticipated, and the option was realized only partially by the clinical staff. Finally, we addressed information richness deficiencies in the billing stage by exploring new digital options (Table 3, row E). We created an Excel-based system that compared received payments for charges with contractually agreed-upon rates for each diagnosis code for each payer. To make it actionable, we tested the option in a pilot study, where it proved sufficient. It was subsequently realized as an integral part of the billing stage.

To address information reach problems resulting from ineffective information flow across RCM stages, as well as information richness problems due to incomplete or inaccurate information in various RCM activities, we explored software solutions that could help to effectively report and manage the problems. We considered several alternatives on the market, but eventually decided to go with an option that had proved useful in another EMC process: an open-source issue-tracking system that the IT department used to manage its work orders. This option was readily available to help address information reach and richness problems, but we had to reconfigure the system substantially to make it actionable as a tool for coordinating work across the RCM stages (Table 3, row B). The problem-tracking system let billing staff track problems originating in the registration stage and share timely information about correcting them (see the screenshot of the system interface in Figure 4). Once the system was realized, it played a vital role in coordinating work and effectively addressing problems in various RCM stages.

To address the information reach deficiencies in revenue recovery, we exploited an underutilized capability in the hospital EMR system (Table 3, row F). We identified a custom report that listed accounts that were more than 90 days overdue and brought those accounts to billing personnel’s attention before the default deadline (typically 120 days after the date of service). Using this new report, the billing department was able to contact patients sooner about outstanding payments. Again, to make it actionable, we tested the option in a pilot run and subsequently realized it as an integral part of the revenue recovery stage. We also addressed the information reach deficiencies related to missing preauthorization of care from third-party payers and insurance eligibility by developing a digital option that combined exploitation and exploration of IT and organizational capabilities (Table 3, row G). We exploited existing capabilities by creating a new patient access coordinator position to manage all preauthorization requests. Then, as we explored available IT solutions to complement the new position, we worked with the registration supervisor to identify payers’ web-based portals for submitting preauthorization requests and tested it to determine its potential as an actionable digital option. In addition, we worked with the CFO and the business office director to extend the existing laboratory services scheduling system to all patients, thereby centralizing admissions and insurance verifications. Thus, we realized a combination of existing capabilities (the new patient access coordinator position and the scheduling system) and new IT capabilities (portals) as a comprehensive digital option that improved performance during the scheduling and registration stages.

## Business Process Transformation

Developing this portfolio of digital options helped improve EMC’s RCM performance. Based on the registration quality report, the registration staff could easily identify and correct missing or incomplete patient information daily (Table 3, row A). As Figure 5 shows, the number of unresolved problems at the end of each month dropped significantly after this option was realized in November 2008 (from an average of nearly 70 per month to less than 10 per month). The minor upward blip in May 2009 resulted from a training issue: some new staff members had joined the team, but once they became familiar with the registration process and started using the registration quality report, the number of problems subsided again.

Using the problem-tracking system improved the flow and accuracy of information exchange across the revenue cycle (Table 3, row B). As a result, more RCM-related problems were identified—and resolved effectively—by the RCM staff before they could lead to delays or claim denials. When the problem-tracking system was first implemented, only 20 or so problems were initially identified, but the number of identified problems increased significantly as more RCM staff recognized its value and began using the system (see Figure 6).

Further, although EMC’s gross revenue fell by 18 percent (from \$62.07 million to \$50.78 million) between 2008 and 2010—understandably because of the worsening local economy due to the U.S. recession—its total profit margin increased from –3.76 percent to +3.91 percent and its cash flow margin increased from 3.73 percent to 12.74 percent, suggesting that the BPT efforts helped improve the hospital’s financial situation. Based on our discussions with the CFO, we sensed a greater commitment to EMC’s financial future among RCM staff and an increased level of confidence among the administrative leaders in their ability to steer the hospital through a particularly difficult time by developing various digital options. These were perhaps the most encouraging outcomes of our CPR efforts.

## Discussion

This study reports on an action research engagement with EMC, a rural hospital that was struggling financially. Over a two-year period, we worked closely with EMC stakeholders to diagnose and address various RCM-related problems. We identified important information requirements related to the revenue cycle and developed various digital options [96, 97], many of which were successfully realized. We have documented in detail how developing this portfolio of digital options significantly contributed to improving EMC’s RCM performance. Based on our experiences with BPT at EMC, we offer two contributions to knowledge. First, we expand the health IT discourse to include revenue cycle transformation and document how resource-constrained hospitals can improve their financial performance by identifying and realizing a portfolio of heterogeneous, low-cost digital options across their revenue cycle. Second, as a contribution to action research methodology, we generalize our interventions at EMC to propose digital options inquiry as a systematic approach to BPT based on collaboration between researchers and practitioners. We now discuss these contributions in detail.

## IT-Enabled RCM Transformation in Hospitals

In recent years, almost all hospitals have invested in various IT solutions—such as EMR, CPOE, laboratory and pharmacy automation, patient portals, and medical imaging—to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of health-care delivery [4, 5, 6, 82]. These solutions have led to improved management of patient care and clinical outcomes [17, 80]. Improving patient outcomes is no doubt important, but it is also necessary to focus on the financial health of hospitals as it directly impacts their ability to provide quality health care [99]. Given that many hospitals struggle to maintain stable cash flow, it is important to understand how IT can be leveraged to improve not only their clinical operations but also their administrative activities [82]. While the health IT literature has paid considerable attention to clinical support, few studies have examined how hospitals can use IT to improve their business operations. Some researchers have, however, recognized the need for empirical research that examines IT’s role in improving business performance in health care; they have also considered how clinical and administrative IT differ in their application, adoption, and return-on-investment (ROI) schedule and how they might complement each other to deliver better and more affordable patient services [82, 85, 98]. In this study, we examined various RCM functions at EMC and experienced firsthand how complex information was exchanged within the hospital and with its payers. We developed various digital options that could improve EMC’s financial performance by supporting these information exchanges. Based on this experience, we contribute new insights that are relevant to both health IT researchers and practitioners.

First, creative use of existing platforms and low-cost, simple software solutions (and even open-source software, or OSS) can be valid alternatives—at least in the short term—to complex enterprise systems. Often, hospital executives are tempted to follow industry trends and acquire costly RCM systems [42, 53]. While such acquisitions may be a reasonable strategic move for large, financially robust hospitals, it is not likely to be the case for resource-constrained hospitals. As past research on the financial impact of enterprise systems shows, it can take several years before ROI is fully achieved and contextual factors can interfere with IT adoption and implementation [52, 100]. Regardless of the long-term value proposition of enterprise systems, the reality is that resource-constrained hospitals—which typically struggle to maintain a stable cash flow—can rarely afford them and thus must find other ways to leverage IT. Our study illustrates that exploiting existing IT systems (such as EMR and CPOE) and acquiring low-cost software solutions can yield significant benefits. In fact, the most effective digital option realized in our study was a simple OSS solution (the problem-tracking system). Studies have shown that OSS can have a positive impact on organizational performance [49], but they also show that managers tend to be wary of OSS and often reject it [44]. Our study demonstrates how an appropriately selected and configured OSS can be a viable solution to improve RCM performance.

Second, before considering appropriate digital options, researchers and practitioners seeking to improve hospital RCM need to determine information requirements in various RCM stages in terms of information quality (richness) and flow (reach). Our experiences show that targeting information reach and richness requirements in individual RCM stages had a positive impact on related RCM processes and overall RCM performance. RCM practitioners have recognized that transformation of front-end processes (such as registration) can significantly improve RCM performance [32]. While it is not feasible to control the dynamic external environment under which RCM operates (significant reimbursement-related policy changes would be needed to accomplish that), smart use of IT can help RCM staff quickly identify and correct problems during patient–hospital interactions. The key is to capture the problems caused by deficiencies in information reach and richness as early in the revenue cycle as possible—such as during registration and scheduling— before the problems become difficult to detect and costly to correct. Understandably, not all information deficiencies can be detected early on; some will inevitably be identified during later revenue cycle stages. However, as we demonstrated, a portfolio of heterogeneous and affordable IT solutions can help RCM staff prevent many front-end problems and detect other problems so they can be tracked and addressed before they adversely affect overall RCM performance.

Third, health-care practitioners sometimes perceive IT as a panacea for organizational problems [86]. However, in many cases, IT is most effective when it targets specific processes [45, 88]. Practitioners may discuss the need for radical RCM transformation, but starting from a clean slate may be unrealistic and is likely counterproductive, as it might destroy valuable existing know-how and even trigger resistance from staff. The debate on the trade-off between incremental and radical BPT is well covered in literature [58, 101, 105] and is also relevant to RCM transformation. A radical approach (such as implementing a new practice management system) might be tempting given a software vendor’s promise of speedy implementation. However, it can also be risky and lead to severe disruptions in day-to-day operations. In line with existing recommendations regarding EMR implementation in both practitioner journals [81] and IS literature [105], an incremental approach that targets information requirements in specific processes can be effective and yield quick results at a lower risk.

Fourth, our study reinforces the impact of organizational context on RCM transformation. While we were successful in realizing digital options aimed at improving administrative processes, we were less successful with digital options seeking to improve the important interactions related to clinical encounters with patients (Table 2, row D). Getting physicians to collaborate with management is a key factor impacting hospitals’ financial performance [43]. Previous research also finds that physicians and nurses often resist new IT [110]. Indeed, our attempt to get the clinical staff to use CPOE to provide timely information about the patient encounter met with resistance. Several factors may explain the clinical staff’s resistance to CPOE. Generally, clinicians are focused on patient care and pay less attention to the business side of operations [71]. Further, overburdened by the demands of patient care, physicians and nurses are often reluctant to use any new IT that may affect their routine or productivity.

## Digital Options Inquiry into Business Process Transformation

The increasing pressure to improve organizational performance provides opportunities for researchers to engage in BPT through action research. However, such engagements can create challenges for researchers, who must acquire in-depth understanding of business processes and adapt their research design and interventions to accommodate specific organizational contexts [25]. In our study, we had to make sense of information exchanges and technology usage related to the extremely complex RCM process in a hospital. Often, the first and third researchers—lacking any prior knowledge of RCM—felt like explorers who had arrived on an island inhabited by natives without a common language in which to properly communicate. Using CPR methodology [76] was therefore particularly helpful as it let us work closely with stakeholders in a specific business practice (RCM) at EMC and to participate directly in the problem-solving process. Drawing on Pettigrew’s contextualist inquiry [89], CPR provided the opportunity to become immersed into RCM’s content and context as a prerequisite for our engagement at the hospital.

CPR methodology has previously been used to advance reflective systems development as a basis for action research into IS development [77, 78, 79]. In this study, we applied CPR to the practice of IT-enabled BPT [45] and drew on our problem-solving experiences at EMC to develop a model for digital options inquiry (Figure 2). As business processes have become more information-intensive and require advanced use of IT to support coordination and management, IS researchers have paid increasing attention to BPT [23, 59, 72, 107]. Such efforts require matching technological solutions with problems caused by breakages in information transfer and processing. As we have illustrated here, digital options inquiry makes these efforts readily available for action research engagements by IS researchers.

The digital options inquiry model uses diagnostic mapping [66] as a first step to diagnose a problem situation; it thus extends Sandberg et al.’s [97] model by providing a structured approach to context appreciation and process characterization activities. Building on Sambamurthy et al. [96] and Sandberg et al. [97], the digital options inquiry model identifies specific information requirements related to both quality (richness) and flow (reach). The participating researchers and practitioners then work together to identify available digital options that may either be readily present (and thus can be exploited) or made available (and thus require exploration). An available digital option may become actionable through further development and collaborative weighing of its merits in relation to its risks, and it may eventually be realized when it is implemented as an integral part of the business process. By emphasizing the actual development of select digital options to realize BPT, the proposed model further extends Sandberg et al.’s [97] model, which ends with digital options recognition.

As such, the digital options inquiry model (Figure 2) offers a practical, theoretically informed tool that researchers and practitioners can use to identify, select, and implement appropriate IT investments for BPT. As a reflection of an incremental approach to strategic decision making [20], options thinking lets researchers and practitioners consider multiple technological alternatives without having to first commit to and invest in them. Hence, with its focus on revealing information requirements [96, 97] and both exploring new investments and exploiting existing ones [73], the proposed model invites researchers and practitioners to engage in strategic thinking about IT usage—even in the face of resource constraints that leave little room for managerial maneuvering.

## Conclusion

As with any research, it is important to emphasize limitations in how we approached the action research project at EMC. First, our interventions evolved over a two-year period and used a specific set of IT interventions. EMC also had important path dependencies in play, including the availability of the OSS for implementing the problem-tracking system and the preconceived notions about EMR usage among physicians and, to a lesser extent, nurses. In addition, our findings rely on the development of specific IT interventions in a small, resource-constrained rural hospital. As such, further studies could focus on other aspects of RCM and develop other digital options; find ways to more effectively transcend the boundaries between the administrative and clinical sides of health-care delivery; and engage in IT-enabled RCM transformation in less resource-constrained hospital settings.

Despite these limitations, our research documented how realizing a portfolio of digital options helped transform EMC’s revenue cycle and significantly improve the hospital’s financial performance. Although the practitioner literature offers anecdotal evidence of the benefits of investing in various IT solutions for RCM, to the best of our knowledge, no other empirical research has, in similar detail, examined how IT can improve the financial and administrative side of health-care delivery. In addition to our practical contribution to EMC’s RCM transformation and performance, our research makes two important contributions to knowledge. First, our empirical investigation of RCM—a critical but underinvestigated area in the health IT literature and an important concern for hospital managers expands the agenda for health IT research. By providing a detailed account of how a hospital was able to explore and exploit digital options to improve its RCM, we provide important insights that can inform further research into IT-enabled RCM and can help hospital managers struggling with similar challenges. Second, we contribute to action research methodology by offering digital options inquiry as a practical model for collaboration between IS researchers and practitioners. Although our digital options inquiry model was developed within a rural hospital, we suggest it is generalizable to non-healthcare contexts. We hope our study will inspire IS researchers and practitioners to further advance BPT theory through action research.
