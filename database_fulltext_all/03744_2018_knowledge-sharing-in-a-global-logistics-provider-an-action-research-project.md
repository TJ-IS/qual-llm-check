# Knowledge sharing in a global logistics provider: An action research project

Louie H.M. Wong, Robert M. Davison

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Action research Knowledge sharing China Work systems theory Punctuated equilibrium theory

## A B S T R A C T

We report on an Action Research investigation into knowledge-sharing practices in Velox, a global logistics organisation and in particular its operations in Guangzhou, China. Our study is premised on Work Systems Theory and Punctuated Equilibrium Theory. Following a description of background and context, we describe two linked Action Research cycles, following the principles and criteria for Canonical Action Research. Our interventions were successful, with both managers and employees at Velox expressing satisfaction with the outcomes, which included radical changes to operational procedures. We discuss the implications and contributions for theory, practice and method.

## 1. Introduction

In the hypercompetitive business world, where customer satisfac tion is central to long-term success, it is critical that organisational employees are able to access the right knowledge at the right time so that they can create services and solutions to meet customer needs [1]. Given the potential for knowledge to act as a driver of competitive advantage [28], it is not surprising that organisations have demonstrated considerable interest in how IT can facilitate ‘collaboration among di<sup>f</sup>erent units and individuals unconstrained by the boundaries of geography and time’ [2]. Knowledge may be located in an explicit form in formally structured repositories, sometimes known as knowledge management systems (KMS), which can be searched e<sup>fi</sup>ciently and e<sup>f</sup>ectively [29]. Alternatively, knowledge may be understood to reside in a tacit form in the heads of individuals and so may only be accessible through interpersonal communication. In this paper, we examine how knowledge is shared by the employees of Velox as they create knowledge-centric services and solutions for their customers. Velox is a global logistics provider in the business of global courier, express and parcel delivery (CEP). We focus in particular on Velox’s customs clearance operations in Guangzhou, China. Velox operates in a highly uncertain environment due to a dynamic legal environment as well as intense competition with both local and global organisations. In such an environment, work-related knowledge about e<sup>f</sup>ective procedures and best practices must be both updated frequently and made available to all who need to know.

We made initial contact with Velox through the Human Resources (HR) Managing Director (China) at an executive education workshop in Hong Kong where the second author was a speaker on Knowledge

Management (KM). This initial contact led to a series of meetings with senior Velox executives in Hong Kong and Guangzhou where we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a shared interest in leveraging knowledge sharing (KS) by employees in order to solve signi<sup>fi</sup>cant problems in the customs clearance process. This led to our proposing a practice-focused but theorydriven action research-based investigation in which we would work with Velox both to identify and to overcome the key barriers to e<sup>f</sup>ective knowledge sharing. Our guiding research question in this endeavour was ‘How can e<sup>f</sup>ective knowledge sharing among employees be en abled and sustained?’.

Following this introduction, we introduce the relevant literature on knowledge sharing in the Chinese context. We also explore the two theories that we adopted in the research. We present a more detailed account of the research context and method, before describing how we engaged in two Action Research (AR) cycles over 18 months, following the principles established by Davison et al. [3,4] for Canonical Action Research (CAR), from project initiation to project completion. We discuss our contributions to practice, research and method, and then conclude the paper.

## 2. Literature review

Three areas of literature are relevant to this investigation. We <sup>fi</sup>rst concisely synthesise the relevant literature on knowledge sharing, especially in the Chinese context, in order to set the scene for the research. We then examine the core principles of Work Systems Theory, which we applied as a diagnostic tool to facilitate our in-depth understanding of how work is performed in Velox, and the barriers to knowledge sharing that occur. Finally, we delineate Punctuated

Equilibrium Theory (PET), which we adopt as a focal theory to guide our intervention in this investigation, seeking to identify ways to overcome the aforementioned barriers.

## 2.1. Knowledge sharing in the chinese context

Knowledge Management is a discipline in its own right with a number of dedicated journals and conferences such as Journal of Knowledge Management, Journal of Intellectual Capital, Knowledge Management Research & Practice, International Journal of Knowledge Management and The Learning Organization. While KM has a long history [5–7], in the Chinese context, the study of KM is a more recent phenomenon, with studies of both formal KM systems and the less formal knowledge-sharing arrangements that are salient for the current study (e.g. [8,9,1]. Martinsons et al. [31] provide a comprehensive review of the KM literature in China, locating over 250 articles, though, as they remark, many are of poor quality. Informal KS in China is often pre mised on ‘knowing who knows’ [10], but in addition it is important for the knowledge seeker to have a strong relationship with the person who knows [11]Davison et al., forthcoming). While it is commonly the case that KS is mediated by technology [9,11]), the mere presence of tech nology cannot guarantee that employees will engage in KS activities in the Chinese context [2], since ‘positive interpersonal relationships (ibid.) are essential to knowledge-sharing practice. Recent research into KS practices in China (e.g. [9,12,13,1,14,31,11]) has revealed a remarkable inclination for employees at all levels to rely on social media applications for interpersonal communication activities, including knowledge sharing. This preference for social media exists irrespective of the physical distance between interlocutors who may equally be situated across a table, a country or the world [15].

## 2.2. Work systems theory

The diagnostic tool or instrumental theory [4] that we use to document and analyse KS practices in Velox is Work Systems Theory [16] (see Fig. 1). As Alter [16], p.75) remarks, ‘A work system is a system in which human participants and/or machines perform work (processes and activities) using information, technology, and other resources to produce speci<sup>fi</sup>c products/services for speci<sup>fi</sup>c internal and/ or external customers’. All work takes place under the aegis of an organisational environment, is governed by organisational strategies and is facilitated by infrastructural components, which include legal issues prevailing in the social context. The two-headed arrows indicate that the various items of the model are engaged in two-way relations. Thus, while participants undertake work, the nature of that work may also in<sup>fl</sup>uence future behaviour. Work itself involves the application of information resources, which may be online or o<sup>fl</sup>ine. These resources are also updated as a consequence of the work done. Likewise, a range of technologies may support the work that is undertaken. Knowledge about how these technologies create value is maintained up to date by the participants who make use of them so that these local best practices can be adopted in future. Meanwhile, the work done by participants leads to the creation of products and services for customers, who may later provide feedback that is re<sup>fl</sup>ected on by the participants and may in<sup>fl</sup>uence future work assignments.

## 2.3. Punctuated equilibrium theory

Punctuated Equilibrium Theory [17,18] was developed in the discipline of palaeobiology as an alternative to phyletic gradualism. In essence, PET is premised on revolutionary changes that interrupt or punctuate a more stable state or a period of evolutionary change. PET has been applied in both management and information systems. For instance, in an example that is germane to our own situation, Gersick [18] suggests that punctuating stable but dysfunctional behaviours in groups with radical changes can lead to dramatic enhancements in a group’s productivity. Newman and Robey [19] draw on PET to account for the way the users and analysts/developers of information systems engage episodically in more stable or evolutionary relationships except when interrupted by revolutionary encounters that may modify the stable state and so push the systems development project in a new direction. Meanwhile, Sabherwal et al. [20] found that strategic alignment practices varied over time, alternating between periods of evolutionary and revolutionary change, corresponding to PET. A central argument in PET is that a change agent may cause a dramatic or a revolutionary shift in an internally stable state in an organisational process. For an organisation that wishes to see radical change, the action potential of a change agent can be a powerful one, since a stable yet stagnant state can be transformed rather quickly. However, this approach is not without risks, since it is entirely possible that the revolutionary shift may create signi<sup>fi</sup>cant internal disruption and lead to unexpected side e<sup>f</sup>ects that negatively a<sup>f</sup>ect the organisation’s in ternal operations.

## 3. Research context

In this section, we provide a concise background of the research context. Velox, founded in the 1970s, is one of the leading players in the global courier, express and parcel delivery industry with more than 165,000 employees worldwide. Velox provides priority and economy courier and delivery services to over 220 countries and territories using its own <sup>fl</sup>eet of aircraft. Velox entered the China market in the early 1980s with inbound and outbound courier and delivery services. It was one of the pioneers granted rights to operate international all-cargo <sup>fl</sup>ights into and out of China. Velox operates over 200 inbound and outbound <sup>fl</sup>ights every week through its Asia Paci<sup>fi</sup>c hub at Guangzhou Baiyun International Airport in southern China.

Typical CEP services (also known as ‘express services’) involve transport logistics of a shipment from a sender’s location to a receiver’s location with guaranteed delivery time and shipment status tracking. Velox o<sup>f</sup>ers express services to and from major cities around the world with guaranteed delivery times ranging from one to three days. In the case of cross-border delivery services, an important value proposition to customers is the provision of transparent shipment tracking systems and e<sup>fi</sup>cient customs clearance. However, customs clearance is a complicated and sometimes bureaucratic operation, especially in China. It is critical that Velox develops and applies practical knowledge, estab lished relationships and best practices and processes as it strives to handle customs clearance issues e<sup>fi</sup>ciently and thereby ensure cus tomer satisfaction.

The primary context of our research was Velox’s Guangzhou gateway and the team of employees there. The gateway is located at Guangzhou airport, which is under customs and airport security surveillance. This gateway is Velox’s Asia Paci<sup>fi</sup>c hub and one of the pri mary domestic gateways in southern China providing customs clearance services for inbound and outbound shipments. The gateway team has to strive to achieve a balance between regulatory compliance, quality of service and e<sup>fi</sup>ciency. In recent years, Velox has continuously implemented performance improvement initiatives in order to optimise its processes and operations. Though these programmes help enhance both e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness and e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, they have also exposed critical weaknesses in knowledge-sharing practices that require concentrated attention.

## 4. Methodology

Action Research is a methodology that focuses on the solving of organisational problems, even as there is a deliberate attempt to contribute to scholarly knowledge Avison et al., 1991. AR enhances the relevance of academic research by linking research and practice. However, the visibility of AR in leading IS research publications is in a critical condition [21]. For example, [21] could only identify four AR papers [22–25] in Information & Management among the 1792 articles published from 1986 to 2016. We hope that this paper can constitute a small step in the process of increasing the visibility of AR.

Davison et al. [3,4] have developed a set of principles and criteria to guide action researchers, in addition considering the role of two forms of theory, in what is termed Canonical Action Research. CAR is by nature collaborative, with the researcher and organisational client working together. It is important that the client agrees with all diagnoses, planned changes and evaluations, because the client is the principal bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary of the project. CAR is appropriate to the current study because we embarked on a project that involved not only working with a variety of stakeholders at Velox and solving their organisational problems involving knowledge sharing but also contributing to the academic literature.

The initial stage is the diagnosis, where the researcher in dependently diagnoses the nature of the organisational problem situation. This process is facilitated with one or more instrumental theories that act as analytical tools to help ensure accurate problem diagnosis [4]. Action planning follows, where the researcher aims to identify a suitable course of action to address the problem situation, as experi enced by organisational stakeholders. It is important to point out that these stakeholders should not be limited to senior managers but must also include frontline employees. A failure to address a problem situation as experienced by frontline employees is likely to lead to a rejection of any solution proposed later [9]. A focal theory, often a predictive cause and e<sup>f</sup>ect theory [26], is also deployed here to ensure the intellectual basis for the organisational change process. In the third stage, the researcher intervenes according to the action plan so as to e<sup>f</sup>ect change. This is followed by the evaluation stage, where the researcher considers if the intervention was successful, according to prespeci<sup>fi</sup>ed measures. This assessment requires both comparison with the pre-intervention state and an analysis of the role of theory: did it predict change accurately? Finally, the researcher must re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the entire CAR process: was it successful, did the change occur as expected, did unexpected outcomes emerge and what other lessons can be learned? If the signs are all positive and the problem situation is successfully resolved, the researcher can exit the organisation; alternatively, there may be a need for a subsequent cycle. The entire cycle is founded on an agreement between the researcher and the client, which includes provision for access to data, measurements, reporting requirements and a non-disclosure agreement (NDA).

## 4.1. Data collection and analysis

Our data collection was informed by WST [16], which serves as an instrumental theory in this project. We collected research data through meetings, corporate documentation, observations, interviews and surveys. Meetings were held with senior managers and operations-level employees in order to gather information about the research context, communicate and validate <sup>fi</sup>ndings, solicit feedback and brainstorm action plans. Meeting notes were kept and some meetings were audiorecorded. In addition, data were collected through relevant corporate documentation such as organisation charts, operations <sup>fl</sup>owcharts and business overview slides. We also visited Velox’s operational locations at Guangzhou airport to observe work practices and interview frontline personnel. Semi-structured interviews were used to collect data from operations employees. Guided by WST, we developed interview questions on <sup>fi</sup>ve key themes: 1. Knowledge acquisition/sharing; 2. Information and work <sup>fl</sup>ow; 3. Technology for knowledge sharing and communication; 4. Policy, relationship and workarounds; and 5. Work culture. Sample interview questions are included in Appendix A. All interviews with frontline personnel were audio-recorded and later transcribed. The collected data were then analysed and structured into a work system snapshot (see Fig. 3). Follow-up communication meetings with frontline employees were conducted and surveys were sent to all employees to validate <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 5.1.1. Entrance

Through the o<sup>fi</sup>ces of Belle, the Velox China Human Resources Managing Director, the researchers met with key members of the China team in November 2014 (See Table 1). Guided by the principle of the Researcher–Client Agreement (RCA) for CAR projects [3], we <sup>fi</sup>rst explained the CAR approach and established mutual understanding for on-going commitments such as time and resources, implementation of changes in the organisation and a formal agreement to allow the researchers to access both employees and relevant internal corporate information. Subsequently, a research proposal, which detailed the research objectives, scope, approach and estimated budget, was sent to Velox for a formal review. The budget only related to costs with respect to travel to and accommodation in Guangzhou. No fees were charged for researchers’ time. This proposal received a positive feedback from Velox’s management team and Velox’s legal department prepared a formal non-disclosure agreement for the project. Meanwhile, a project team was formed with members from various functional departments including operations (two senior managers), marketing (one manager) and human resources (one senior manager and one manager). The project was also sponsored by Albert and Belle, Velox’s senior man agement team in China.

## 5.1.2. Diagnosis

The diagnosis phase in the <sup>fi</sup>rst cycle mainly leveraged the preliminary discussions. To ensure an independent analysis of the critical KS issue, we separately met with core project team members to discuss the background and current situation. During these meetings, we discussed the plausible causes of the KS issues in the gateway team from Velox’s management perspective. Most of the Velox managers agreed that there was a lack of awareness about key KM and KS concepts in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Since most of the operations knowledge is embedded into process work<sup>fl</sup>ows and technologies, such as their global package tracking system, it was commonly assumed that knowledge is also embedded

and codi<sup>fi</sup>ed.

Some senior managers, particularly those from HR, attributed ine<sup>f</sup>ective training as the root cause of the knowledge-sharing issue. Elaine remarked, ‘We need to provide more up-to-date customs clearance knowledge and package them into our training materials. We should consider reviewing our training methods and course materials’.

Another identi<sup>fi</sup>ed problem was management’s failure to fully ap preciate the situation in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. For example, when the management executives were asked whether their frontline employees in the gateway would use WeChat (a popular mobile social media app in China) to share knowledge and communicate job-related information, they immediately denied it. Belle said: ‘all of our gateway staf only use company-provided systems to perform their job. Everyone has to follow the policy without exception’. However, there are numerous reports in the literature suggesting that the use of social media apps such as WeChat is inevitable in Chinese organisations (cf. [9,12]. This demonstrated the need for us to speak directly with the frontline employees to gain a more complete understanding of the situation from their perspective.

## 5.1.3. Action planning

We planned with Velox to o<sup>f</sup>er an introductory seminar to the <sup>fi</sup>eld employees at the Guangzhou gateway (airport). The purpose of this seminar was two-fold: to learn about <sup>fi</sup>eld employees’ experiences with KS and to inform these employees about current KS thinking. Although this seminar would not immediately result in changes to KS processes, we judged that it was an essential <sup>fi</sup>rst step since before the frontline employees could accept a change initiative, they would need to believe in it. The educational function of the seminar would help us to achieve this preliminary objective. In the seminar, we planned to highlight key issues connected with KS and its bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, as well as the role of technology in KS. We aimed to create awareness and stimulate on-going conversations on KS practices among employees. Ideally, our seminar would trigger an epidemic of interest that would spread contagiously among employees. Besides the Guangzhou gateway employees, selected members of the Guangzhou call centre team were also invited to join the seminar since the call centre team is also a key customer facing unit in customs clearance operations. The inclusion of the call centre team provided the researchers with a more complete view from key internal stakeholders of the customs clearance operations.

We were also concerned that both a corporate bias in favour of how better training could resolve the KS issue and the lack of frontline employees’ voices might hinder our investigation into the underlying problems. Therefore, we also designed the seminar to stimulate discussion and interactive feedback with the frontline employees, raising their awareness of KS techniques while also collecting information from them on the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of training and their current KS practices.

## 5.1.4. Intervention: the knowledge-focused seminar

We conducted three (60–120-min) sessions of the seminar in Velox’s Guangzhou facilities to a total of 60 employees and managers. The <sup>fi</sup>rst two sessions were conducted at the Guangzhou airport facilities, while the third was conducted in Velox’s call centre located in downtown Guangzhou. Debby, the client AR project leader, introduced the researchers at the beginning of each seminar and highlighted that Velox’s senior management was committed to making changes for better KS among employees. The seminar participants were reminded that they could freely provide feedback to the researchers since an NDA was in place and anonymity was also ensured. Bringing in third parties from an academic institution signalled to the employees that Velox took the whole issue very seriously.

## 5.1.5. Evaluation

Based on the feedback and discussion in the seminars, we concluded that while Velox encourages the free sharing of knowledge, which takes place extensively, a supportive KS infrastructure was absent, so all such sharing was based on individual initiatives. One employee mentioned in the seminar that ‘We have a very good open-door policy and employees are helpful. You can ask questions openly to any colleagues but there are no formal knowledge sharing initiatives. Individual team leaders irregularly use communication meetings to facilitate knowledge sharing among team members .

We assessed Velox’s technology regime as being relaxed and supportive, but observed how employees made extensive (but cautious) use of non-corporate systems, especially social media, for knowledge sharing. For instance, some participants reported ‘We have created WeChat groups to share work related matters with our own teams’. During the seminars, some participants denied this non-conforming behaviour at <sup>fi</sup>rst but later admitted their usage when we told them that we had heard about the use of WeChat from other participants.

We also learned that the legal environment changed frequently, with new customs rules and regulations being imposed on an irregular basis. More problematically, the interpretation of these rules and reg ulations varied among customs o<sup>fi</sup>cials, complicating the long-term value of knowledge. For example, one seminar participant joked: ‘Sometimes, it really depends on the mood of the customs oficials’. However, when the researchers asked further clari<sup>fi</sup>cation from this participant, he declined to discuss the issue further. A further complication is that the customs o<sup>fi</sup>cials themselves were frequently rotated to other locations in order to minimise the change that they would develop corrupt relationships with employees from companies like Velox.

In general, we found that employees saw no value in training as a solution to the KS problems. One seminar participant said: ‘I resonate with the concept of sense-making in knowledge sharing. I feel many of the training activities are a waste of our time, since participants always find that not all of the information is useful to them. In other words, they cannot make sense of the training materials’.

Overall, the outcome of the seminars was positive, with many participants sharing their views about KS. The researchers also received much positive feedback from managers after the seminars in informal settings such as lunches and dinners. Some said that the seminars had encouraged them to think outside the box and alerted them to the importance of conversation and interaction for KS. Some commented that the seminar should be delivered to all employees. The epidemic of interest that we had hoped to generate through the seminar had a long way to go, given that the seminar was delivered to a relatively small number of people, but we judged from the feedback that the interest was there.

## 5.1.6. Reflection

The two researchers experienced a steep learning curve in this <sup>fi</sup>rst cycle since neither was familiar with the details of the customs clearance operations and the numerous jargons and abbreviations used in Velox’s daily business conversations. Velox provided some brie<sup>fi</sup>ng information, which proved to be helpful for the subsequent research activities, particularly as we strove to enhance communication quality and trust between ourselves and the employees at di<sup>f</sup>erent levels. Though the initial feedback from managers and employees on the seminars was positive and supportive, we felt that we had just peeled the <sup>fi</sup>rst layer of an onion. However, the researchers recognised that the mode of intervention in this cycle, a public seminar, may have hindered the participants’ willingness to discuss some of the details of the problem situation. Nonetheless, we had gleaned a preliminary appreciation of the contextual details and sparked interest among employees as to how the situation could be improved, but further investigation was required if we were to leverage the momentum of our positive engagement and intervene positively. Therefore, we initiated a new cycle, with a focus on ameliorating the problem situation.

## 5.2. Cycle 2: introducing changes to knowledge-sharing practices

The second AR cycle lasted from July 2015 to April 2016. Fig. 3 shows the timeline with highlights of key activities. This cycle focused on theory-driven change in Velox.

## 5.2.1. Diagnosis

Our diagnosis took the form of multiple interviews with employee at di<sup>f</sup>erent levels and in di<sup>f</sup>erent locations. We conducted semi structured interviews with 74 employees of Velox’s Guangzhou operations. The interviews were conducted in four to <sup>fi</sup>ve person groups. A total of 15 group interview sessions were scheduled, each lasting about 45 min.

In all these interviews, we sought to identify the features of the operating environment that a<sup>f</sup>ected KS, the types of knowledge involved and the way it could be represented, the attitudes towards KS among employees, the resources available to stimulate, encourage, reward or facilitate KS, and the technology available, or not available yet preferred, for KS.

The diagnosis recon<sup>fi</sup>rmed our earlier <sup>fi</sup>ndings that a supportive KS infrastructure was absent. All KS activities were planned as individual initiatives. One plausible root cause of the ad hoc nature of KS practices was the absence of a single formal leader empowered to drive the development of a KS culture in the gateway team.

We validated the interview <sup>fi</sup>ndings and our analysis through meetings with Velox’s management and frontline employees. We continued to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the Work Systems Snapshot (WSS), which we had started work on in the <sup>fi</sup>rst cycle, in order to ensure that we had a complete and rich understanding of the role of KS in various operational activities (see Fig. 4). As illustrated in the WSS, an abundance of technologies was provided to Velox’s frontline employees. However, employees informed us that these company-provided technologies were minimally used for the intended purposes. For instance, Microsoft SharePoint was intended to facilitate KS, yet employees commented that: ‘We use SharePoint as a cloud drive. We can download standard forms and operating procedures’ and ‘We rarely access SharePoint to share or seek knowledge’.

We also discovered that personal social media, such as WeChat, was commonly used for work purposes among the frontline employees in the Velox gateway team, despite this contravening corporate policy. WeChat’s attractiveness was its availability as a mobile app, since none of the technologies approved for use operated on mobile platforms. One interviewee told us that ‘Lync [Microsoft’s enterprise social media appli cation] is used by many people, but it’s only for the desktop. WeChat is more efective as many colleagues in our team are not in the ofice. [Lync] is not used widely. There is no policy to force employees to use it. It's up to the individual employee’. Another interviewee explained: ‘On-site colleague in the customs use WeChat to update the status of our submitted reports. It’s a bit late if we wait for their feedback after they return to our ofice. They send all these status reports to us over WeChat once they completed their work in the customs so that we can inform our customers promptly. They post multiple messages on WeChat with airway-bill numbers/status, etc., most often in the afternoon’.

## 5.2.2. Action planning

The Work System Snapshot unexpectedly functioned as an essential medium through which the researchers built trust with Velox at different levels. Firstly, the completed WSS (see Fig. 3) demonstrated that the researchers had grasped a thorough practical understanding of Velox's customs clearance operations. The frontline employees felt con<sup>fi</sup>dent that the researchers ‘know what they are talking about’. Secondly, the senior management executives recognised the rigorou and systematic nature of the analysis. At one of the validation meetings, the management executives even praised how the researchers' knowledge was superior to most managers’. Albert commented, ‘The work system snapshot coherently details the important activities, processes and participants of our customs clearance service. You [the researchers] are the experts now. We can hire you to work for us immediately’.

At this juncture, we decided to introduce Punctuated Equilibrium Theory as a focal theory that would guide the change process in this latter stage of the CAR project. We deliberately planned actions that would induce punctuations to the status quo. PET was selected because it is suitable for stagnant situations where prior change e<sup>f</sup>orts have failed. PET’s core principle focuses on the creation of a series of revolutionary changes on multiple dimensions in a short period of time. Since CAR is collaborative, we planned our changes in conjunction with both frontline employees and managers, all of whom needed to agree explicitly to any changes. Three brainstorming meetings were conducted with (a) management executives, (b) frontline employees and (c) a combined group of (a) and (b), including Albert, the Regional VP of China operations. These meetings were designed to encourage discussion and solicit feedback about the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed problems, focusing on revolutionary or radical changes to the status quo.

On the organisational structure dimension, we proposed to create a new position, which we called the Knowledge O<sup>fi</sup>cer (KO). We proposed that the KO should lead and enforce the ongoing KS initiative, driving changes in Velox. Ideally, the KO should supervise a team of two to three employees, all of whom should possess a similar level of customs clearance knowledge and experience, be able to work independently and be able to support each other with respect to KS work. It was clear that the KO and team would require full support from Velox’s management through empowerment, job function positioning and respect from others. The KO should be highly visible, act as a single clearance knowledge contact window to customer contacting teams, that is, the call centre and sales teams. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four key roles for the KO team: Knowledge Gatekeeper, Communication Ambassador, Team Integrator and Technology Explorer.

On the knowledge culture dimension, by persuading Velox’s senior management to consider creating the KO position, we expected to induce a mind-set change in Velox’s senior management that KS is a journey rather than a one-time project. Moreover, although IT can lubricate KS practices by employees, a supportive organisational culture is critical to the success of the ongoing KS initiative.

On the technology infrastructure dimension, a revamping of the old KS platform using Microsoft SharePoint was proposed. The new platform should have a new design and incorporate interactive features. The new design would be context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c for customs clearance work and would provide a much easier way for employees to visualise and locate relevant knowledge. The proposed interactive features included an online forum for interactive discussions, designed to encourage conversations as a primary form of KS.

In addition, we planned to measure a number of relevant indicators, including the weekly average number of enquiries from the customer service team to the gateway team, the weekly average SharePoint hit count and the weekly average number of visitors to SharePoint, in order to evaluate the outcomes of our intervention. We expected the number of enquiries to decrease if KS practices improved, whilst the number of SharePoint visitors should increase if more employees started to seek and share knowledge on the platform. Prior to the intervention, the baselines of all three measurements were recorded in January 2016.

## 5.2.3. Intervention

Velox accepted all our recommendations and shortly afterwards secured permission from regional corporate HQ for the initial hire of one KO in the inbound customs clearance team. The KO was hired in January 2016 and implementation of the proposed actions occurred during February to April 2016.

## 5.2.5. Reflection

We recognised that revolutionary change is a key to sustainable KS practice. Guided by PET, we suggest that a KO should be a perpetual change agent to fuel the necessary punctuations that will drive the creation of a new and better equilibrium in the organisation. The KO has a key responsibility to continuously and dynamically monitor and drive initiatives, simultaneously supporting employees and managers. In addition, the simple provision of information systems (e.g. SharePoint) to employees does not guarantee proper practice. Such systems must be at least adequate to the task. The mere adoption of SharePoint does not signify good KS practice. It was ironic that Velox employees only used SharePoint for <sup>fi</sup>le sharing and cloud storage purposes. Regular re-assessment of SharePoint use that critically examines on-going work practices is essential; further interventions may still be needed in future.

From a theoretical perspective, the key advantages associated with both the Work Systems Theory, which we deployed as a diagnostic tool, and Punctuated Equilibrium Theory, which provided the intellectual and practical basis for our change-oriented interventions, were noted. While neither of these theories is new, equally neither has been extensively applied in the IS discipline and so our validation of the applicability of the theories is important.

## 6. Exit – and the aftermath

With the completion of the second cycle, and in particular with the hiring of a KO who would be responsible for the ongoing success of the KS project, we reasoned that the practical and scholarly objectives of the project had been met and thus that the project had been successful. With the agreement of Velox’s senior management, we exited the project at this time.

A year after we completed work at Velox Guangzhou, Albert and Debby approached us to indicate that they would like us to repeat the KS project at their Shanghai Pudong Airport operational site. While this work is in progress at the time of writing, and lies beyond the scope of the current paper, it is worth mentioning because for us, this re-invitation constitutes a critical success factor: our earlier work in Velox Guangzhou has proven very successful, to the extent that Velox now plans similar ventures across China and the Asia Paci<sup>fi</sup>c region. The Shanghai project is but the <sup>fi</sup>rst of these.

## 7. Discussion

Getting the right knowledge to the right people at the right time is a perennial problem for organisations. In Velox, the situation is compli cated by the fact that environmental knowledge changes dynamically and unexpectedly: the legal environment changes often while the customs o<sup>fi</sup>cers who apply the laws and procedures are frequently rotated to other locations. In such a di<sup>fi</sup>cult climate, knowledge management initiatives need to be nimble if they are to be successful. In our CAR based investigation of KS practices in Velox, we were able to spur new interest from both employees and their managers in a centralised KS initiative, with changed mind-sets and reinvigorated organisational policies. Drawing on Work Systems Theory, we analysed the key com ponents of work at Velox and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed opportunities for improvement. We intervened to enact these improvements through a number of recommendations premised on Punctuated Equilibrium Theory and the need for revolutionary change to overcome a stagnant situation. Qualitative and quantitative assessments after intervention indicated positive outcomes, a situation still current a year later.

## 7.1. Implications for practice

The success of this research endeavour can be attributed in large measure to the deep involvement of the <sup>fi</sup>eld employees throughout the project. Their active participation and interaction during the di<sup>f</sup>erent stages of the CAR cycles induced a sense of ownership in the ultimate KS-related changes. Therefore, organisations should consider encouraging active participation of employees during all stages of a CAR pro ject. This not only reduces employees’ potential reluctance to accept change but also subtly changes employees’ mind-sets. As we recalled from the cycle 2 action-planning meetings, one of the employees suggested an idea which we had originally proposed during informal dis cussions over lunches and dinners a few weeks previously. We were amazed and delighted that the employee had internalised our idea and suggested it as her own. The process of internalisation enhanced the idea through the employee’s own interpretation. An idea from oneself is always more convincing than the same idea from senior management or from an external party such as a researcher. However, the realisation of active employee participation requires the right organisational culture (cf. [9] as well as the project team’s/research collaborator’s commitment to extended engagement with and focus on the <sup>fi</sup>eld employees.

Organisations that operate in di<sup>f</sup>erent industries certainly have di<sup>f</sup>erent KS contexts and challenges. However, the proven successful application of the CAR process model in this study implies that other organisations can consider applying the same CAR process model as a generic model to drive their KS initiatives. The CAR approach adopted in this study made no prior assumptions of the organisations’ KS context and issues. Through CAR’s systemic stage-by-stage approach, the underlining KS issues were e<sup>f</sup>ectively diagnosed and resolved.

## 7.2. Contributions to action research method

A key hurdle that we experienced in this project related to entry to the organisational context. Although the physical aspect of entry was unencumbered, the sociological and linguistic aspects were more complex [27]. Davison et al.’s [3,4] guidelines for CAR do not describe project entry in much detail, and there is no mention of any speci<sup>fi</sup>c preparations that researchers may need to make. In our experience, it was essential for us to engage deeply with Velox in an extended prediagnosis phase in order to develop su<sup>fi</sup>cient understanding of the various nuances of the context to persuade all stakeholders to accept our involvement. These nuances covered both linguistic issues, such as the technical vocabulary used by employees, and sociological issues, such as employee manager relations. We suggest that current models for the practice of CAR need to be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed with a stronger pre-diag nostic entrance phase (see Fig. 5) where researchers get to grips with the research context in su<sup>fi</sup>cient detail so as to be readily accepted by organisational insiders.

We also highly recommend that action researchers should focus the <sup>fi</sup>rst cycle of a CAR project on an ‘onion peeling’ process. Researchers can leverage the various <sup>fi</sup>eld interaction opportunities in the <sup>fi</sup>rst CAR cycle, both formal and informal, to build trust and establish rapport with the di<sup>f</sup>erent stakeholders in the collaborating organisation. During this cycle, it is important to interact slowly and deeply with the organisation, probing beneath the surface issues to engage with the organisational culture and thereby to gain a better appreciation of the problem situation. With the necessary trust established, the research participants would feel much more comfortable in sharing their experiences, which will increase the likelihood that the researchers can dig down to the core of the onion and discover the underlying causes of the problem situation.

## 7.3. Contributions to theory

The identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of theory is never an easy task in AR projects [3,4] Although [4] document the critical roles of instrumental and focal theory, the precise selection of these theories requires extended re <sup>fl</sup>ection. Our selection of WST proved fortuitous as it pushed us to probe deeply into aspects of Velox’s operational procedures that we might have otherwise ignored. The WSS that we created enabled us to compile a detailed account of how customs clearance work was undertaken, including the speci<sup>fi</sup>c technologies that were used for knowledgesharing activities. Meticulous documentation of these processes focused our attention on imperfections in the sequence of work activities and enabled us to pick out speci<sup>fi</sup>c problems that needed attention. We were also able to identify situations where employee behaviour deviated from supposed institutional norms, for instance when they relied on WeChat as a communication channel. Meanwhile, PET’s focus on revolutionary changes that could punctuate the stagnant situation we encountered inspired us to seek a radical solution. We sought to revise the organisational structure and culture, precipitating changes that proved to be acceptable to employees and their managers, and successful in practice. PET is a neglected theory in IS. IS researchers have occasionally applied PET as a descriptive (Type I) theory to explain phenomena [20,30], but rarely in other guises. In this study, we apply PET in the role of a Type IV theory (termed a focal theory by [4] to drive the action-planning process and predict the outcomes of the change. We demonstrate that PET can be an e<sup>f</sup>ective theory for both explaining the change rationale and predicting the outcomes. We suggest that IS researchers should consider adopting PET as a Type IV (predictive) theory, instead of limiting its use to a Type I (descriptive) theory [26], particularly in CAR-based research projects.

The changes that we proposed transcended a mere technological solution. We sought to blend technological elements, such as a revamped SharePoint, with human resource innovations, notably the hiring of a knowledge o<sup>fi</sup>cer, and cognitive change among senior managers, leading them to accept the value proposition o<sup>f</sup>ered by mobile social media. Collectively, these actions contributed toward addressing our research question, viz.: ‘How can e<sup>f</sup>ective knowledge sharing among employees be enabled and sustained?’ We suggest that the answer to the question is that employees need a cultural and technological environment that structures, rewards and facilitates knowledge sharing, with explicit support from senior management. The cultural and technological environment is not something that should be imposed on employees, but rather it should be developed in conjunction with them. A joint development process, which recognises the legiti macy of both employee needs and managerial controls, is more likely to lead to the creation of an environment that will be accepted by both parties and consequently to ensure that the KS activities of employees are enabled and sustained.

## 8. Conclusion

Action Research has the potential to bring about signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive change in organisational problem situations, ameliorating circumstances for managers and employees alike. Our successful inter vention in Velox owes much to the open-mindedness towards change exhibited by all the managers and employees we worked with. We suggest that organisations may apply the same CAR model as a generic model to drive their KS initiatives and encourage employees’ active participation throughout the process. Meanwhile, a combination of WST and PET ensured thorough problem diagnosis and radical recommendations for improvement. Action researchers need to pay careful attention to organisational circumstances, familiarising themselves with salient details of the context, including jargon and local cultural nuances, if they are to gain the respect of clients and so have the authority to e<sup>f</sup>ect persistent change. The basis for this change should not be limited to technological possibilities. As we demonstrate in this report, technological solutions must be blended with locally appropriate human resource-related measures and a willingness to try new ways of working. All of these components must co-exist in a mutually supportive framework that is underpinned by an e<sup>f</sup>ective set of

governance procedures. Ultimately, the success of any project is likely to depend on the good will and sheer hard work of a number of very di<sup>f</sup>erent people, who all have to learn to work together. Action researchers, as organisational outsiders, may play a formative role here, if they are able to command the respect and trust of employees at all levels of the organisation. Our study demonstrates how to employ the <sup>fi</sup>rst CAR cycle as an ‘onion peeling’ process and contributes to the Action Research method in terms of enhancing the CAR process model with an interpolated pre-diagnosis stage. Furthermore, we show the predictive power of PET as a focal theory in a CAR-based research project. As a method, Action Research has much to o<sup>f</sup>er the IS researcher who is keen to investigate and address organisational problem situations in a rigorous fashion while simultaneously contributing relevant scholarly knowledge.
