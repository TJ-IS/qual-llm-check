# Implications for strategic IS research of the resource-based theory of the firm: A reflection

## a b s t r a c t

This paper reviews key concepts from the resource-based theory (RBT) of the firm, including evidence of ‘‘empirical support’’ for RBT. However, the paper then turns the conventional logic of empirical testing of RBT on its head, and argues that all that empirical testing does is to show researchers’ success in identifying valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable (VRIN) resources. Examining the IS literature from this perspective, the paper identifies a number of resources that really do seem to have been sources of competitive advantage. It concludes with recommendations on how RBT should be used in future strategic IS research

## Introduction

In their twentieth-year ‘‘retrospective’’ on the development of the resource-based theory of the firm, Barney et al. (2011) argue that ‘‘resource-based theory has evolved from a nascent, upstart perspective to one of the most prominent and powerful theories for understanding organizations’’. Supporting this claim, Kraaijenbrink et al. (2010, p. 350) say:

‘‘The resource-based view (RBV) has become one of the most influential and cited theories in the history of management theorizing. It aspires to explain the internal sources of a firm’s sustained competitive advantage (SCA). Its central proposition is that if a firm is to achieve a state of SCA, it must acquire and control valuable, rare, inimitable, and nonsubstitutable (VRIN) resources and capabilities, plus have the organization (O) in place that can absorb and apply them (Barney, 1991a, 1994, 2002). This proposition is shared by several related analyses: core competences (Hamel and Prahalad, 1994), dynamic capabilities (Helfat and Peteraf, 2003; Teece et al., 1997), and the knowledge-based view Grant (1996b).’

Resource-based theory (RBT) provides an alternative explanation of the sources of a firm’s competitive advantage that complements Porter’s (1979, 1996) ‘‘strategy as positioning’’ perspective (Rivard et al., 2006). Its focus on the rent-generating capability of key scarce resources, and its rapid rise to prominence in the strategic-management literature, has led many IS researchers to use RBT as the underpinning theory for their studies of a broad range of issues related to:

(a) the benefits of ICT use in organizations, including research into the impact on organizational performance of IT generally (Bharadwaj, 2000), IT-supported business processes (Melville et al., 2004), knowledge-management systems (Alavi and Leidner, 2001; Meso and Smith, 2000), enterprise systems (Beard and Sumner, 2004), and mobile devices (Sheng et al., 2005), and

(b) IT-management practices, such as the pursuit of complementarity between IT assets, organizational IT capabilities, and business strategy (Ross et al., 1996; Aral and Weill, 2007; Nevo and Wade, 2010), enterprise architecture (Bradley and Byrd, 2007), and project management (Tarafdar and Gordon 2007).

However, questions continue to be raised about whether ICT resources and/or capabilities can be a source of competitive advantage (Carr, 2003), and if they are, whether any such competitive advantage is sustainable. These are tough questions to answer. With these thoughts in mind, the research question addressed in this paper is as follows:

What are the implications for strategic IS research of the Resource-based theory (RBT) of the firm?

To answer this question, the paper first reviews some key concepts from RBT, then explores empirical evidence of support for RBT as reported in the strategic-management literature. Support is found to be quite strong, though by no means unequivocal. However, a key contribution of the paper is that after presenting the summary outlined above, we then ask what it really means to say that empirical support for the RBT is ‘‘quite strong’’. Our answer, which is seemingly at variance with literature-review papers such as Newbert (2007) and Crook et al. (2008) from the strategic-management literature, is that for resources such as an oil field in Brunei, RBT is incontrovertibly true! Any firm or country that controls a valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable (VRIN) resource such as an oil field clearly has a sustainable competitive advantage over the marginal producer (e.g., of shale oil or biofuel today). In short, as posited by the resource-based theory of the firm, there is no doubt that control of truly VRIN resources is a source of sustainable competitive advantage. Framed from this perspective, the ‘‘tests’’ of RBT reported by Newbert (2007) and Crook et al. (2008) are really tests of whether different types of resources that the researchers thought might be VRIN, were actually VRIN.

The second half of this paper examines from this perspective the IS literature on RBT and the use of ICT as a source of competitive advantage. The consensus view that emerges here is that it is not ICT resources, per se, that provide competitive advantage, but rather, that where such advantage exists it is the way that they are used or integrated (Nevo and Wade, 2010)—in combination with other resources in the organization—that provides such advantage. Further, as a way of summarizing key IS research on RBT, a table of ICT-enabled resources that IS researchers have found to contribute to competitive advantage is presented (Table 3).

The implications for strategic IS research of the perspective of RBT advocated in this paper are three, First, strategic IS researchers can contribute to the world’s stock of knowledge about RBT by attempting to assess the extent to which various types of ICT and ICT-enabled resources in different contexts actually produce competitive advantage. For example, it seems likely that digital-business capabilities (Kohli and Grover, 2008) including the capacity to envision and build them, are fruitful areas to study. Second, RBT may be used to justify claims that resources similar to those shown in past studies to be VRIN (e.g., see Table 3) are also likely to be sources of competitive advantage. In this second type of study, the evidential requirements for VRIN-ness, competitive advantage, and causality, are less onerous than for the first, because reliance can be placed, in part, on evidence from the prior studies. Third, in the least-demanding type of use of RBT, IS researchers may simply cite ICT-RBT studies (e.g., those in Table 3) to support claims that ICT resources sometimes contribute to organizational value.

## The Resource-based theory (RBT) of the firm

As Barney et al. (2011), Kraaijenbrink et al. (2010), Priem and Butler (2001), Rumelt (2003), Teece (2009), Wade and Hulland (2004), and many others have pointed out, definitions of concepts such as resources, capabilities, and competitive advantage in the RBT have evolved over the years, and are still subject to many questions. Thus it is important to define the terms used in this paper. For the purposes of this paper, a reasonably up-to-date and consistent set of definitions is presented in Table 1.

The biggest decision in choosing the definitions in Table 1 was whether to treat resources and capabilities as separate things, or to treat capabilities as a type of resource. The RBT community is divided on this question. For example, Amit and Schoemaker (1993) use the former approach when they say:

‘‘We thus define the firm’s Strategic Assets as the set of difficult to trade and imitate, scarce, appropriable and specialized Resources and Capabilities that bestow the firm’s competitive advantage.’

[Amit and Schoemaker, 1993, p. 36]

Here, resources and capabilities are treated as separate things, and the overarching concept is ‘‘asset’’. By contrast, Barney’s definition, in our Table 1, uses the alternative approach. In Barney’s definition, assets and capabilities are the separate things, and the overarching concept is ‘‘resource’’. Although we are aware of Kraaijenbrink et al.’s (2010, p. 358) conclusion that Barney’s (1991) definition of ‘‘resource’’ is ‘‘clearly overly inclusive’’, we nonetheless chose to use Barney’s definition in this paper because it is the meaning implicit in the term ‘‘resource-based’’ view or theory of the firm.

Please note that Table 1 contains just the set of definitions used in this paper. It is not intended to provide ‘‘the’’ definitive set of answers to the many definitional questions about RBT. Further, it contains some inconsistencies. For instance, in defining the ‘‘O’’ in VRIO (see the last row in Table 1), Barney (1995) contradicts his own 1991 definition of resource, by talking of ‘‘resources and capabilities’’ as if they are separate things. As a second example, in his 2010 paper on business models, Teece (2010) argues, convincingly, that a firm’s business model is an important determinant of its profitability. So, by our definitions in rows 2 and 3a of Table 1, business models are both resources and assets. However, the latter interpretation is inconsistent with the accounting definition of asset, also in our Table 1 (as row 3b). Accountants do not normally regard business models as assets.

Note also that our definitions of resource, asset, and capability in Table 1 are recursive. This is because aggregations of resources are also higher-level, or compound, resources, e.g., a factory is an aggregation of many resources, and a firm itself may be viewed as a bundle of resources. Thus, Helfat and Peteraf’s (2003) definition of ‘‘Organizational Capability’’ in row 5 of Table 1 is recursive because it talks in terms of ‘‘utilizing organizational resources’’. This definition only makes sense if the resources referenced are not the same things as the higher-level resource of which the organizational capability is a part.

Finally, note that of the many possible definitions of ‘‘competitive advantage’’ available (e.g., see Rumelt (2003)), we have chosen Peteraf and Barney’s (2003) definition for this paper. Their definition of ‘‘economic value’’ is largely consistent with Ghemawat and Rivkin’s (2006) ‘‘wedge’’ in the latter’s definition of competitive advantage: ‘‘A firm is said to have a competitive advantage over its rivals if it has driven a wide wedge between the willingness to pay it generates among buyers and the costs it incurs’’ (p. 3). However, Ghemawat and Rivkin’s (2006) definition lacks the concept of comparison to the marginal competitor in the market of interest, a very important concept in our chosen definition.

## The core argument in resource-based theory

The core argument of the resource-based theory of the firm (RBT) is that due to their control of scarce resources some firms are more profitable than others. Because such firms have lower costs, they make superior returns compared to other firms in the same market. These lower-cost firms are said to have a competitive advantage. Their returns over and above the marginal producer are called rent (Ricardo, 1817), defined in Table 1. Rent and VRIN-ness are the two key concepts in RBT. When used appropriately, VRIN resources produce rent. Rent flows to the low-cost firm because its costs are lower than those of the marginal producer

If it is possible for other firms to obtain control of similar scarce resources to those of the low-cost firm, and so reduce their costs too, the low-cost firm’s competitive advantage will be lost (its rent will drop to zero). In this case, the low-cost firm’s original competitive advantage is said to have been temporary or not sustained. Such competitive advantage is temporary because the resources in question are not Inimitable or Non-substitutable, the ‘‘I’’ and ‘‘N’’ components of being VRIN. (For more discussion of what it means for a resource to be VRIN or VRIO, please see Barney (1991, 1995, 1997).)

Ricardo (1817) illustrated his concept of rent with an example based on benefits to the owner of a particularly fertile and well-watered field for the growing of wheat compared to owner of a marginal plot of land. Note that merely owning the fertile field is not enough to produce rent. Rather, the land must be worked (i.e., tilled, planted, harvested) and choices must be made about which type of crop to grow, and when to harvest, if the rent-producing capability of the field is to be realized. Ricardo assumed that the farming and management capabilities required to produce a good crop of wheat from fertile land were in ready supply. (This is the $\because 0 ^ { \dag }$ in VRIO.) Thus he viewed the scarce (VRIN) resource, the land, as the source of competitive advantage. A similar argument lies behind today’s literature on the RBT: although many other assets and capabilities must be employed if a firm is to profit from its rent-capable resources, the focus of RBT is on (a) the scarce resources that are the source of the competitive advantage, and (b) the firm’s relative advantage compared to the marginal producer in the relevant product market.

The Achilles-heel of RBT thinking is the need to identify the resource (or resources) that is the cause of the rent. For example, Ricardo attributed rent from wheat growing to the fertility of the land. However, lower cost could be due to a host of other factors. For example, for today’s farmers, such low cost could be due to: use of tractors for cultivation, use of fertilizer, choice of grain to plant (e.g., planting a particularly productive and disease-resistant hybrid), distance of the land from key markets, cost of labor, and choice of times for planting and harvesting (a knowledge resource). In an RBT-based explanation of the low-cost farmer’s competitive advantage, one has to argue that these other factors are widely available to others in the marketplace, i.e., not VRIN, and therefore, that the competitive advantage may be attributed to the fertility of the land

The preceding sort of argument becomes even more difficult to justify when the resources in question are complex (e.g., synergistic combinations of complementary resources), dynamic capabilities (Teece 2009; Eisenhardt and Martin, 2000), contribute to product differentiation, or where the various resources that contribute to the firm’s profitability are only somewhat or partially VRIN. For example, implementation of an ERP system involves a complex series of steps: process blueprinting, software configuration, interface building, data conversion, testing, organizational-process adaptation, and user training and support. The resultant system is certainly unique to the implementing organization. Does this mean that the resultant system is VRIN and therefore likely to be a source of competitive advantage, or should it be argued that ERP systems are not VRIN because many other firms have gone through similar implementation processes and have similar systems? In cases such as this, the position advocated in this paper is that RBT predicts that the resource in question will only be a source of sustainable competitive advantage if the resource is VRIN. Therefore, we argue, if researchers want to adopt RBT as their theoretical foundation, it is up to them to convince their readers that the resources in question are VRIN (or at least V and R, for temporary competitive advantage). The best way to do that is probably empirically (although it can also be done by argument).

## Empirical evidence of ‘‘Support’’ for RBT

Rather than arguing, as we have in the Introduction, that RBT is obviously true if the resource in question is clearly VRIN, some researchers in the strategic-management literature have conducted studies to test the level of empirical support for RBT. For example, Newbert’s (2007) literature review of 55 empirical papers from leading research journals (including three papers from MISQ and one from ISR) concludes that empirical support for RBT is only moderate:

‘‘Table 2 shows that the 55 articles in the sample contain 549 individual tests of the RBV as discussed above, 292 (53%) of which were supported.’

[p. 127]

In a similar vein, Crook et al.’s (2008) meta-analysis of ‘‘125 usable studies with 127 samples, over half of which appear in either the Strategic Management Journal (42 studies) or the Academy of Management Journal (28 studies)’’ reports that:

‘‘based on data from over 29,000 firms contained within 125 studies, our results show that, on average, resources identified by researchers as strategic are related to performance at $\Gamma _ { \mathrm { c } } = 0 . 2 2 .$

[p. 1150]

‘‘The results for our second hypothesis indicate that resource measures that meet RBT’s criteria are more strongly related to performance than measures that do not meet the criteria. When criteria are met, we estimate that the overall effect of strategic resources on performance is $\Gamma _ { \mathrm { C } } = 0 . 2 6$ . This effect is 117 percent larger than for resources that do not meet RBT’s criteria $\mathit { \left( \Gamma _ { \mathrm { r } } = 0 . 1 2 \right) } .$

[p. 1151]

In the last two quotations, ‘‘r ’’ measures the correlation between control of some type of scarce resource and some other indicator of competitive advantage. In the context of their study (which has a sample of 125 studies), a correlation of 0.22 (or 0.26 for resources that passed more stringent tests of VRIN-ness) suggests strong, but not overwhelming, support for RBT.

What does it actually mean to say that a study provided ‘‘empirical support’’ for the RBT? The following example shows one way this question has been answered. In her highly-cited paper in MIS Quarterly, Bharadwaj (2000) used reports of ITleader firms in the US in the trade magazine, Information Week, during the four years 1991–1994, to identify 56 firms with superior IT capabilities. The financial performance of these firms was compared to that of 56 firms matched on industry and size. Data on financial performance came from Computstat. By comparing financial performance ratios for matched pairs of firms, Bharadwaj found that the IT leaders outperformed their pairs for return on assets, return on sales, operating income/ assets, and operating income/sales, though not for operating expense/sales. This, she argued, was evidence that firms with superior IT capabilities achieved better financial outcomes than firms with lesser IT capabilities:

‘‘Viewed from a resource-based perspective, the empirical findings indicate that IT capability is [a] rent generating resource that is not easily imitated or substituted. Isolating mechanisms such as time compression diseconomies, connectedness of resources and social complexity allow firms with high IT capability to achieve and sustain superior performance. The analysis suggests that IT-resources not only take time to acquire and build, but also highlights the difficulties raised by complementary resources and resource-embeddedness.’’

[Bharadwaj, 2000, p. 186]

Santhanam and Hartono (2003) later replicated the Bharadwaj (2000) study, but in their study they compared the 56 firms to average ratio for their industry, not to arbitrarily matched firms. Their results, however, were similar.

Studies such as these are the basis for the claims of empirical support for the RBT in Newbert (2007) and Crook et al. (2008). In reality, however, we argue that RBT is true, and the many studies they reviewed provide varying levels of support for the researchers’ claims that the resources they chose to study were VRIN. (In other words, because of our conclusion that it is ‘‘obvious’’ that RBT is true, our interpretation of Newbert’s (2007) and Crook et al.’s (2008) work is the opposite of those authors!) Our position is consistent with that of Bharadwaj (2000) when, in the quotation above, she says ‘‘the empirical findings indicate that IT capability is [a] rent generating resource’’ (she does not claim that her study supports RBT). Further, if the role of empirical studies is to identify rent-generating (VRIN) resources, it is not clear that it makes sense to combine studies of different types of resources and report average r<sub>c</sub>s as Crook et al. (2008) have done. For example, from our perspective it is no surprise that Crook et al. (2008) found that average r s were higher for resources that, in Crook et al.’s judgement, were more clearly VRIN (their hypothesis 2, with evidence in their Table 2, p. 1150). Indeed, given that RBT is true, it would have been most surprising if more VRIN-like resources had not yielded higher returns.

## The RBT in published information systems research

Having surveved the strategic-management literature on RBT—summarized above—we then turned to the IS literature on RBT-related phenomena. To understand the way RBT has been used by IS researchers, we conducted a two-step literature review. First, we reviewed many highly cited papers from the IS literature that have focused on RBT-related topics, including those on competitive advantage from use of IT. Nine were selected for detailed discussion in this paper, and twelve resourcetypes from Step-1 are summarized in Table 3 (below). Second, we also examined a random sample of 50 papers from the eight top-quality journals on the AIS ‘‘Senior Scholars’’ list (AIS, 2011), to see how a broad cross-section of IS researchers have used RBT in their research. The reason for Step-2 was to be able to make some sound, general, evidence-based observations about the way that RBT has been used in IS research (Seddon and Scheepers 2012).

## Step 1: Review of selected RBT-related papers in the IS literature

In this section, we summarize insights from nine RBT-related studies from the IS literature. Each paper was selected for discussion because it provides insight into some aspect of the strengths, limitations, and/or findings of RBT-based research in IS. This discussion has been arranged in chronological order to parallel the increasing maturity of RBT-based research in the strategic-management literature over the past twenty years.

First, in a paper that predates Barney (1991) (and therefore does not mention RBV or RBT), Copeland and McKenney (1988) describe the development of two airline-reservation systems, called SABRE and Apollo, that provided real competitive advantage to their owners, American Airlines and United, respectively, for some years. The fact that in 1983 the US Civil Aeronautics Board felt obliged to regulate the use of these airline-reservation systems (Copeland and McKenney, 1988, p. 363) is clear evidence of their capacity to provide competitive advantage. Copeland and McKenney’s (1988) paper provides what appears to us to be the clearest evidence of an ICT-related resource providing competitive advantage of any paper discussed in this section. For this reason, we have placed it at the top of our ‘‘league table’’ of VRIN ICT-related resources reported by IS researchers. This league table is presented as Table 3 at the end of this section.

Second, in an early attempt to understand the implications of RBT for IS research, Mata et al. (1995, p. 492) asked: ‘‘under what conditions will a firm’s heterogeneously distributed resources and capabilities be a source of sustained competitive advantage?" Their answer was to list and evaluate five sources of competitive advantage that had been discussed in the IS literature. Based on analytical argument they concluded that of the five, only IT managerial skills (e.g., IT managers’ capacity to understand the needs of business managers, develop appropriate applications, coordinate IT activities, and anticipate future IT needs of the business) was likely to be a source of sustained competitive advantage. Examples of such advantage they cite include the development of SABRE (Copeland and McKenney, 1988) and Walmart’s logistics system (Mata et al., 1995, p. 499). Note that the causal pathway they present is this: (1) management insight into ways that IT could be used to create competitive advantage, leads to (2) IT-system development, which leads to (3) the observed competitive advantage. In this sequence, the VRIN resource they identify is ‘‘(1) managerial insight’’, not the resultant system or systems, e.g., the airline reservation system that we argued was the VRIN resource in the previous paragraph.

The seemingly contradictory claims in the two previous paragraphs may be reconciled as follows. Copeland and McKenney’s (1988) airline reservation systems were a source of temporary powerful competitive advantage. Today, however, airline-reservation systems are no longer VRIN and do not convey competitive advantage to those who control them. By contrast, the capacity of the managers who could sense the competitive possibilities of building such systems are longer lived. Their capacities, and the capacities of those to whom they have passed on their knowledge (including those who have learnt from their predecessors’ insights), live on, and may be applied today in the search for ways of using IT as a source of competitive advantage. Thus both resources highlighted in the two paragraphs above are sources of competitive advantage, but IT managerial skills are likely to provide competitive advantage for longer.

Third, in their literature review of 24 IS studies with an RBT perspective, Wade and Hulland (2004, p. 109) classified 54 ‘‘information systems resources from previous studies’’ under eight headings. Five of those eight headings, together with 27 (of the 54) examples from the IS literature, are shown in Table 2. Note that most of the resources in Table 2 are what we have defined (in Table 1) as capabilities, not IT assets. For example, ‘‘Business systems thinking’’ and ‘‘Capacity to manage IT change’’ in row 1 of Table 2 are capabilities not assets. Note also that resources such as those in Table 2 are much more specific than the broad-brush general capability tested by Bharadwaj (2000) and Santhanam and Harjono (2003) discussed earlier. Finally note that many of the resources in Table 2 are very similar to Piccoli and Ives’ (2005, p. 753) list of barriers to erosion of sustained competitive advantage. This similarity seems to reflect leading IS thinking on RBT at the time.

The position taken in this paper is that it is fine to speculate that resources such as those in Table 2 might contribute to a firm’s competitive advantage, but unless they can be shown empirically to do so, or be shown to be VRIN, RBT is not a helpful lens for understanding their contribution to organizational well-being. Yes, resources such as those in Table 2 may lead to cost savings and improved organizational outcomes, but one does not need to invoke RBT—a theory about Ricardian rent and sources of (sustained) competitive advantage—to explain how they do so.

Fourth, Weill and Ross (2004) claim that IT governance, defined as ‘‘specifying the decision rights and accountability framework to encourage desirable behavior in using IT’’ (p. 2), is a source of competitive advantage. At the risk of oversimplification, they make three claims. First, they say that ‘‘effective IT governance is the single most important predictor of the value an organization generates from IT’’ (pp. 3–4). Second, based on a study of 256 firms globally, they report that: ‘‘firms with above-average IT governance following a specific strategy (for example, customer intimacy) had more than 20 percent higher profits that firms with poor governance following the same strategy’’ (p. 122). Third, they say: ‘‘We are not saying governance performance caused superior financial performance. However, we can say superior financial performers have high governance performance’’ (p. 123). Reading between the lines, Weill and Ross (2004) are essentially claiming that effective IT governance (defined on pp. 239–240) is a VRIN resource that provides a source of competitive advantage. Further, they provide quite strong empirical evidence (20% higher profits) to support this claim. We therefore classified the strength of evidence that IT governance is a VRIN resource as ‘‘Strong’’ in our summary table below (Table 3).

Fifth, Ray et al. (2005) explored RBT-justified hypotheses between various IT-related variables and the relative performance of customer-service processes in 104 firms in the life-and-health-insurance industry in 1999. Shared knowledge between line and IT staff about the business operations and strategies of the customer service unit was shown to have a strong positive association with the performance of customer-service processes. Ray et al. argued that this shared knowledge was VRIN because ‘‘the development of shared knowledge is often a path dependent and socially complex process’’ (p. 630), and thus, that the observed association was consistent with RBT. Note that this paper uses a construct quite a long way ‘‘upstream’’ from Competitive Advantage, namely, Customer Service, as the dependent variable in a RBT-based study. (The assumption, of course, is that firms offering better customer service make higher returns.)

Sixth, Davenport and Harris (2007), in their book ‘‘Competing on Analytics’’, argue that some firms use business analytics (BA) so effectively that they gain competitive advantage from BA. Based on RBT logic, one can infer that Davenport and Harris (2007) believe that the BA resources in the 32 firms that they studied must be VRIN. Note that this does not mean that all BA resources are VRIN. As Davenport et al. (2010) point out in their later book, in many firms BA resources are merely regarded as useful, and not a source of competitive advantage. Because Davenport and Harris (2007) make a very strong claim about the contribution of Business Analytics to competitive advantage, and in addition, provide evidence from 32 organizations to support that claim, we classified the strength of their evidence (that BA resources may sometimes be VRIN) as ‘‘strong’’ in our summary table below (Table 3).

Seventh, in their conceptual paper, Nevo and Wade (2010) argue that ‘‘IT assets can play a strategic role when they are combined with organizational resources to create IT-enabled resources’’ (p. 163). Further, they argue that management efforts to integrate various ‘‘compatible’’ ICT and non-ICT resources result in new resources with ‘‘emergent’’ properties that are VRIN. Finally, they argue (as depicted in their Table 3, p. 171) that the greater the integration effort, the more likely the emergent ICT-enabled resource(s) is to be VRIN. It is through this pathway, they say, that ICT-management efforts (mainly ICT-related decision making and ICT implementation projects) may contribute to sustainable competitive advantage. This proposition was tested empirically by Nevo and Wade (2011). In the latter study, Nevo and Wade used statistical analysis (PLS) and data from 168 survey respondents to test hypotheses that IT integration efforts in respondent firms’ customer-service departments led to synergistic resources that were VRI (not VRIN) and helped the firm reduce cost, increase revenue, and obtain competitive advantage. Their Fig. 2, p. 412, shows that most hypotheses were supported. By measuring both VRIN-ness and competitive advantage explicitly, this paper provides a very nice RBT-informed test of the idea that synergy between various IT and other organizational resources is an important resource that contributes to a firm’s competitive advantage.

Eighth, a well-evidenced study of the impact of ICT on firm performance is that of Aral and Weill (2007). The first half of their paper provides an RBT-based theoretical framework for understanding how IT resources contribute to firm value. The second half uses OLS regression analysis of survey data from 147 senior IT executives in large US firms to explore the association between IT resources (assets and capabilities) and various indicators of firm performance, e.g., return on assets, gross profit margin, cost of sales, and new product development. According to Aral and Weill, their various tables of regression results show that (a) different types of IT asset contribute differently to firm performance, and (b) ‘‘firms derive greater value per IT dollar by having stronger organizational IT capabilities’’ (p. 777). With respect to (b), although evidence of an interaction effect in Aral and Weill’s Table 8 (p. 775) is not very strong, e.g., no interaction terms are significant for return on assets, a number of significant interaction effects are reported. Thus Aral and Weill’s findings are more-or-less consistent with Nevo and Wade’s (2010) arguments that synergistic combinations of ICT and non-ICT resources are important sources of competitive advantage from ICT. Overall, because many regression coefficients were not significant, we rated the strength of evidence of the VRIN-ness of the resources studied by Aral and Weill (2007) as weak.

Finally, in an argument highly compatible with both Alter (2002) and Nevo and Wade (2010, 2011), Kohli and Grover (2008) argue that rather than regarding investments in IT, per se, as the drivers of business value, it is more helpful to view IT as being embedded in business processes, thereby creating digital business capabilities (e.g., digital logistics, digital customer service, and digital analytics) that provide value to organizations:

‘‘In our examination of the relationship between IT and some form of value, we have also accepted the complementarity argument. IT, as simply hardware and software tools, does not create value in isolation, but must be a part of a business value creating process with ‘‘other’’ IS and organizational factors operating in a synergistic manner (Melville et al., 2004; Wade and Hulland, 2004).’’

[p. 26]

Kohli and Grover’s (2008) article provides useful guidance on the types of capabilities that researchers might wish to study from an RBT perspective. In particular, they highlight the importance of the information assets that emerge almost as a by-product of the digitization of business processes, that can be analysed to provide important business insights (Davenport and Harris 2007), e.g., to understand trends in customer purchasing patterns or to identify patterns in customer complaints, and so enable a firm to compete more effectively.

## Step 2: Examination of a broad cross-section of IS research mentioning RBT

For Step 2 of this survey of the way that RBT has been used in the IS literature, we examined a random sample of 50 papers in eight top IS journals that contained the term ‘‘resource based view’’. Specifically, we used Publish or Perish (http://www.harzing.com/pop.htm), which in turn uses Google Scholar (http://scholar.google.com.au/) to search for all articles that used the term ‘‘resource based view’’ (which, in a Google search, includes ‘‘resource-based view’’ (hyphenated)) in each of the eight journals in the Senior Scholars list (AIS, 2011). The number of papers returned by Google Scholar in each journal is summarized in Table 4. A total of 322 papers were identified.

A sample of 50 papers was then randomly selected for analysis from these 322 papers. (After 50 papers had been analyzed the pattern was reasonably clear, so analysis stopped at 50.) Each paper was read and classified into one of four categories based on the paper’s level of reliance on RBT to inform its hypothesis formulation and/or general discussion. Results of that analysis are shown in Table 5.

In this sample of 50 papers:

\- As shown in row 1 of Table 5, about 50% of papers made only passing reference to RBT (and in about half of those papers the strings ‘‘resource based’’ or ‘‘resource-based’’ appeared in the title of a cited paper and were not actually mentioned in the body of the paper). As an example of a paper that made a passing reference to RBT, McKinney and Yoos (2010) report the title of Wade and Hulland’s (2004) paper ‘‘The Resource-Based View and Information Systems Research’’, in their Appendix B (p. A5). This is the only place where the string ‘‘resource-based’’ appears in their paper.

\- As shown in row 2 of Table 5, about 30% of papers were classified as having a Low reliance on RBT. Low-reliance-on-RBT papers typically mentioned RBT by name, and suggested that RBT provided grounds for believing that the resource in question was valuable, but made no attempt to discuss the VRIN-ness of resources studied or to assess the extent of competitive advantage that might flow from ownership or control of such resources.

## Additional insights from the ICT-RBT literature review (from Steps 1 and 2)

Based on the IS studies reviewed in both Steps 1 and 2 there appears to be an emerging consensus that—consistent with Carr (2003)—ICT-assets are commodities that convey little competitive advantage, per se, and therefore, that it is the way that such assets are deployed and used within an organization, in combination with other resources (the terms ‘‘synergy’’, ‘‘co-specialized assets’’ and ‘‘resource complementarity’’ are often used), that has the potential to provide a source of competitive advantage (Zhu, 2004; Piccoli and Ives, 2005; Kohli and Grover, 2008; Nevo and Wade, 2010; Asadi Someh and Shanks, 2013). This idea is nicely summarized by Kohli and Grover’s (2008) suggestion that organizations can use IT to build digital-business capabilities that enable them to provide better products or services for their customers.

A second insight from our literature review is that because different firms have different histories of investment in resources, their options at any time are likely to be constrained, at least in part, by past investment decisions (Murer, 2010). Thus ICT investments and the resultant digital-business capabilities that emerge are path dependent (Copeland and McKenney, 1988; Dierickx and Cool, 1989; Mata et al., 1995, p. 492; Teece et al., 1997), which, in addition to causal ambiguity (Barney, 1991; Peteraf, 1993), restricts the ability of competitors to exactly match a successful firm’s source(s) of competitive advantage.

Finally, it is apparent from the analysis of the 50 papers summarized in Table 5 that although many IS research papers contain the strings ‘‘resource based view’’ or ‘‘resource based theory’’ only about one quarter of them (24% of the sample) actually relied on RBT as the primary basis for their theorizing, and only half of those papers (12% of the sample) provided strong evidence of the VRIN-ness of the resources they studied. In the 30% of the sample classified as having ‘‘low reliance on RBT’’ (row 2 in Table 5), RBT contributed to the study simply by helping the authors argue that ICT-enabled resources could or might contribute to organizational benefits.

## How should RBT be used in future strategic IS research?

The question that this paper set out to answer is: What are the implications for strategic IS research of the Resource-based Theory (RBT) of the firm? Based on the above reviews of both the strategic-management literature and the IS literature, the answer offered here is that RBT may be used in future IS research in three main ways: (a) additional studies that seek to identify VRIN ICT-based resources that result in competitive advantage in various contexts, (b) studies that use evidence of resource VRIN-ness from prior studies to justify claims that the resources studied are likely to be important contributors to organizational benefits, and (c) studies that merely cite studies such as those in Table 3 to support arguments that ICT resources may contribute to organizational value. Studies of type (c) are not strategic, but they informed by strategic IS research. Each of these three types of study is now discussed in turn.

## (a) Studies that seek to identify VRIN ICT-based resources in various contexts

IS researchers may contribute to the world’s stock of knowledge about RBT by seeking to identify VRIN ICT-based resources that yield competitive advantage in various contexts. The goal in such studies would be to explore the extent to which ICT and ICT-enabled resources actually help a firm achieve competitive advantage. For this type of study, the literature discussed in the previous section suggests that ICT resources rarely provide competitive advantage directly, but rather, that it is the way that they are used (or integrated) in combination with other resources that provides such advantage. Further, since the dependent variable in RBT studies is normally competitive advantage (or perhaps some upstream construct such as the process-improvement construct in Ray et al. (2005)), the resources selected for study need to have a high enough impact on the dependent variable for their influence to be plausibly causal. For example, one would not expect to find any significant empirical association between, say, a firm’s ownership or control of most of the resources in Table 2 and competitive advantage. Therefore, for RBT-based tests of the power of IT-related resources for producing competitive advantage, there seem to be two approaches worth pursuing:

(i) Focusing on some part of the business. For example, Nevo and Wade (2011) focused on just the customer-service department, and explored the effect of IT-based resources on that department’s contribution to competitive advantage. Ray et al.’s (2005) approach was similarly departmentally focused.

(ii) Study high-impact resources such as IT management (Bharadwaj, 2000; Santhanam and Hartono, 2003) or IT-related resources in firms or industries where the causal link between the resource bundle and competitive advantage is reasonably clear and the effect size is likely to be measurable, e.g., the airline-reservation systems at American Airlines and United Airlines in the 1980s (Copeland and McKenney, 1988; Hopper, 1990). In particular, case studies of highly successful eBusiness firms like Google, Amazon. Facebook, and eBay would be expected to reveal VRIN IT-related resources—possibly including their business models—that drive their competitive advantage. Also, case studies of highly successful firms that produce IT products, e.g., Microsoft, Apple, Cisco, Oracle, and SAP, may reveal VRIN ITrelated product-development resources—again possibly including their business models—that drive their competitive advantage. The contribution of such studies would be to show, more clearly than is understood at present, which types of IT-related resources contributed to the competitive advantage, and how they did so. Another fruitful direction for a type (a) study might be to examine Kohli and Grover’s (2008) digital-business capabilities, possibly using a research instrument such as that developed by Nevo and Wade (2011)

In both types of study (a(i) or a(ii)), the goal is to convince the reader that (1) there was a competitive advantage, (2) the ICT-related resource in question was VRIN (or at least VR, i.e., Valuable and Rare), and (3) that the resource in question was the primary source (or cause) of the observed competitive advantage. Since context is also likely to be a major factor (Priem and Butler, 2001, p. 32), it is also important that the context in which the resource is used is made clear.

(b) Studies that use evidence of resource VRIN-ness from prior studies to justify claims that the resources studied are important contributors to organizational benefits

As an alternative to testing whether ICT-based resources are a source of competitive advantage, an IS researcher may argue that (i) RBT plus (ii) evidence of resource VRIN-ness from prior studies (studies such as those in Table 3) justifies hypotheses to the effect that the use of very similar resources in the current study will contribute to competitive advantage. This is just a simple generalization argument (Tsang and Williams, 2012). Much depends on whether the contexts of the two studies are similar enough to justify the claim that findings from the prior study apply in the new setting(s). The evidentiary requirements in this second type of study are less onerous than for studies of type (a). The goal in type-(b) studies is to convince the reader that either (1) the resource in question was VRIN, or (2) the resource in question was the source of the observed organizational benefits (not both).

An example of such an approach might be a study that argued that Davenport and Harris (2007) have already demonstrated that business analytics (BA) was a source of competitive advantage in the 32 firms they studied, and therefore, that it might be expected that the BA capabilities in organizations in the researchers' proposed study might also be a source of competitive advantage. However, since Davenport et al. (2010) have also argued that in many firms BA is not a source of competitive advantage, it would be essential that the authors of the proposed study convinced the reader that the resources in their study were VRIN. For example, consider a study like that of Cosic et al. (2012) which hypothesizes in its Fig. 1 that BA Capability Maturity will lead to Value and Sustainable Competitive Advantage. If Cosic et al. (2012) were to adopt this type-(b)- study logic:

\- It would be necessary to explain that their hypothesis was justified by a combination of RBT and a generalization from, say, Davenport and Harris’ (2007) claim that BA was a source of competitive advantage in the 32 firms they studied.

\- In addition, in the future empirical work foreshadowed by Cosic et al. (2012), it would be necessary to convince the reader that either (1) the BA Capability Maturity in the firms studied was, indeed, VRIN (e.g., by asking questions like those in Nevo and Wade (2011, p. 416) about their VRIN-ness), or (2) the BA Capability Maturity in the firms studied was indeed the driver for the observed competitive advantage.

The second dot point is necessary because RBT is a theory about competitive advantage from rent from VRIN resources, so for RBT to be applicable, either VRIN-ness or competitive advantage flowing from control of the resource must be established. This same two-step logic would be useful in many future studies of type (b).

(c) Studies that merely cite studies such as those in Table 3 to support arguments that ICT resources may contribute to organizational value

The final way that RBT may be used in future IS research is simply to support claims that ICT resources sometimes contribute to organizational value. Strictly, such studies—which are similar to those in row 2 of Table 5—do not depend on RBT at all for their theorizing, and are unlikely to be concerned with strategic applications of IT. However, they benefit from strategic ICT-RBT research because it provides helpful grounds for arguing that ICT-enabled resources are potential sources of organizational benefits. Because such studies do not themselves depend on RBT, they have no need to show VRIN-ness of ICT-enabled resources studied or that such resources actually do create competitive advantage.

## Conclusion

As documented in the Introduction, the resource-based theory (RBT) of the firm has emerged as one of the dominant theories in the strategic-management literature for explaining why some firms are more profitable than others. The RBT has also been of keen interest to IS researchers since—contrary to Carr’s (2003) argument that ‘‘IT doesn’t matter’’—it provides strong theoretical grounds for arguing that investment in information technology is a potential source of competitive advantage.

Due to this interest in the RBT among IS researchers, the purpose of this paper has been to answer the question: What are the implications for strategic IS research of the Resource-based Theory (RBT) of the firm? The answer offered above is that (a) strategic IS researchers may contribute usefully to the world’s stock of knowledge by conducting further tests of the VRIN-ness, or otherwise, of ICT-related resources in various contexts; (b) evidence of the VRIN-ness of resources such as those in Table 3 may be used as the theoretical basis for hypothesizing associations between use of similar ICT-related resources or resource bundles and competitive advantage; and (c) future IS studies may simply cite strategic IS studies such as those in Table 3 to support arguments that ICT resources may contribute to organizational value without using RBT in their own theory development. In studies of type (a), researchers have to both (i) show that some VRIN resource is associated with competitive advantage, and (ii) provide evidence of causality. Studies of this type would be of interest to both the IS research community and the broader community of Strategic Management researchers, e.g., would be publishable in the Strategic Management Journal. In studies of type (b), the justification of the hypothesis is a combination of RBT and the evidence from some earlier study, but the researchers still have to show that the resources of interest are VRIN, or were the cause of competitive advantage. In studies of type (c), RBT is not part of the theoretical foundation of the study.

In studies of type (a) or (b), there will be many tough questions to be answered. Key questions include:

\- How should resources be conceptualized? (e.g., should IT-management capability or the results of their labors (the implemented systems), or both, be treated as the resource? Are synergy and/or business models resources?);

\- What does VRIN-ness mean and how it should be identified? (e.g., all ERP systems are clearly different, but seen from afar, they all do much the same thing. So are ERP systems VRIN or not?); and

\- What dependent variable should be used? (e.g., apart from competitive advantage, may one use organizational performance, or some upstream measure (e.g., improved process performance) as the dependent variable for whichever organizational unit of analysis is chosen?).

Also, since IT management is very much concerned with a firm’s capabilities for building and deploying new capabilities, i.e., with a firm’s dynamic capabilities (Teece, 2009; Eisenhardt and Martin, 2000), Kraaijenbrink et al.’s (2010) recent critique of RBT suggests an important direction for future RBT-informed strategic IS research

‘‘We have suggested the clarity and explanatory power of the RBV should improve when it clearly distinguishes among the building, acquisition, and possession of capacity (resources and capabilities) and the processes of deploying that capacity in the firm’s actions.’’

[Kraaijenbrink et al., 2010, p. 365]

Moreover, Kraaijenbrink et al. (2010) also warn that researchers must distinguish between potential sources of competitive advantage and realized competitive advantage. In other words, once new IT systems deployed, many other factors beyond the VRIN-ness of those new ICT-enabled resources will affect realized return on ICT investments. Controlling for these factors is important. In short, there are many finely nuanced issues to consider in any future IS research that claims to build on the resource-based theory of the firm.

Finally, as a way of summarizing this paper, we list its five key contributions: (1) the compact summary of RBT in the first half of the paper, with its focus on Ricardian rent as a key defining feature of RBT; (2) the argument that RBT is obviously true in some circumstances, which means that so-called ‘‘empirical tests’’ of RBT in the strategic-management literature are really only tests of researchers’ suspicions that certain resources might be VRIN-like in certain circumstances; (3) the discussion of key RBT-related studies from the IS literature, including compilation of the list of ICT-related apparently-VRIN resources in Table 3 for which empirical evidence of the contribution to profitability of ICT-related resources actually exists; (4) evidence of the way that RBT is being used in the IS literature, based on a review of a random sample of 50 high-quality papers from the IS literature that have mentioned ’’resource based view’’; and (5) the recommendations concerning three classes of RBTrelated research that would be valuable for future strategic IS research. With respect to (5), a key argument is that the place to look for evidence of rent-generating IT-related resources is in very high-impact contexts such as those listed in point (a)(ii) above, where the causal link between the resource bundle and competitive advantage is reasonably clear and the effect size is likely to be measurable.
