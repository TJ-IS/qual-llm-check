# Perception and expectation gaps of information systems provider skills: the impact on user satisfaction

Debbie Tesch,\* Ruth Miller,<sup>†</sup> James J Jiang<sup>‡</sup> & Gary Klein<sup>§</sup>

\*Williams College of Business, Xavier University, Cincinnati, OH 45207-3236, USA, email: tesch@xavier.edu, <sup>†</sup>College of Business Administration, University of Louisiana at Monroe, Monroe, LA 71209, USA, email: miller@ulm.edu, <sup>‡</sup>Department of Management Information Systems, University of Central Florida, Orlando, FL 32816-1400, USA, email: jjiang@bus.ucf.edu, and <sup>§</sup>College of Business and Administration, University of Colorado at Colorado Springs, 1420 Austin Bluffs Parkway, Colorado Springs, CO 80933-7150, USA, email: gklein@uccs.edu

Abstract. Past research indicates that expectations play a crucial role in the final satisfaction of users of information systems (IS). This includes expectations regarding the skill levels exhibited by the providers of the IS services and products. Typically, the expectations are examined as gaps from perceived performance or as gaps from realistic expectations. The interaction of these gaps has not been thoroughly explored in past research, although recent theories anticipate both gaps are crucial in meeting the desires of the users. A matched pair sample of IS users and IS providers is used to collect data on expected skill levels and perceived delivery of the skills demanded. Gaps, both between these two stakeholder groups regarding expectations and within the user group regarding perceived delivery. are found to impact user satisfaction in a two-way analysis. The results indicate that expectations should be managed to higher levels and commonly understood across the user and provider groups.

Keywords: consonance, discrepancy, IS skills, user satisfaction

## INTRODUCTION

It is well recognized that the skill level of information systems (IS) providers has a great deal to do with the final success of an IS (Bruce & Pederson. 1982: Charette. 1989). It is for this reason that skills research continues to be of prime interest as indicated by decades of publications on IS personnel skills (Cheney & Lyons, 1980; Lee et al., 1995; Byrd & Turner, 2001; Wade & Parent, 2001; Tesch et al., 2003). Part of the reason for this continuation of work is the rapid evolution of the IS field. Another aspect is the breadth of knowledge required about not just technology, but the domain of the application and the interpersonal skills needed to determine requirements. The shift from a pure product development standpoint to one of being a service provider is but one of several changes in the field that have changed perspectives on skills. It is these changes and the importance of various skills to the success of both the organization and the system that prompt further studies of skill requirements.

Beyond the identification of skills, however, researchers attempt to find links between the presence of certain skills and the success of the IS function from various stakeholder views (Charette, 1989; Deutsch, 1991). In doing so, researchers have found certain skill links to be strong but others to be weak or even non-existent (Byrd & Turner, 2001). Few argue that the lack of a proven link indicates the lack of need for skills, instead opting for the argument that certain factors have been omitted from their models or that perception measures of skills (i.e. skill proficiency) alone may not be adequate to provide the evidence required (Green, 1989; Martinsons & Chong, 1999; Byrd & Turner, 2001). Following this line, Wade & Parent (2001) found that the IS providers’ ‘balance’ of skills is associated with higher levels of job performance than either ‘having too much’ (i.e. skill proficiency > skill importance) or ‘having too little (i.e. skill proficiency < skill importance). The most intriguing finding of these recent studies is the establishment of an empirical link between the importance of skill, proficiency of that skill, and outcome variables (Tesch et al., 2003). Skill importance or skill proficiency alone is unable to predict the outcomes – only how they relate together. However, the empirical results of these recent studies imply that IS management should manage user expectations of IS skill importance and IS providers’ skill proficiency.

One unique study in the IS skill literature examined the differences in expectations of skills between the user community and the IS providers (Green, 1989). Since Green's paper, researchers have examined gaps in expectations in other arenas (Linberg, 1999; Jiang et al., 2000). These studies examined system development success as being a partial result of the match between expectations among various stakeholders. In fact, IS researchers have concluded that these gaps in expectations can present problems durino system development (Klein and Jiang, 2001). IS personnel must fully understand users expectations to avoid the 'misfit' between users' needs and the delivered IS products/service. It can be expected that the impact of IS skills on system success is the result of not only matching the expectation to proficiency, but also matching expectations between users and IS service providers. This overall matching, called consonance, is a comprehensive view of multiple stakeholders, and multiple gaps to expectations and proficiency (Klein and Jiang, 2001; Klein et al., 2002a; Jiang et al., 2003). The argument for consonance is that various gaps (e.g. expectation gap or expectation–proficiency gap) can serve as indicators of eventual success. Unfortunately, no study in the IS skill literature has incorporated both the expectation gap between users and providers along with the expectation–perception gap within the user group to examine the gap impact on final outcomes for technology management and business function skills.

The purpose of this study is to examine the impact on user satisfaction, one common measure of system success, by the interaction of the expectation gap between the users and providers of IS and the expectation–proficiency gap. This comprehensive view, based on consonance between stakeholders and within users. models success as a confirmation of expectations across two stakeholders and a confirmation of expectations within the user population. The interaction of these two gaps will provide preliminary insight into the complex relationship between and within stakeholder groups in terms of expectations and perceived delivery. Lowered satisfaction with increased gaps will support the argument that efforts to achieve an alignment of criteria across multiple stakeholders can improve success of a system as viewed by the stakeholder of interest (Locke & Latham, 1990; Vancouver & Schmitt, 1991).

## BACKGROUND

Information system providers face a basic challenge in assimilating the increasing amount of new technologies and searching for more cost-effective ways to apply IS technologies to solve business problems. This implies a need for strong technological and business skills (Lee et al., 1995). It is no wonder that IS providers indicate the quality of the people working on an IS project is a much more important factor in the determination of project success than the tools or methods that they use (Charette, 1989; Schmidt et al., 2001). This skill quality implies that stakeholders hold a measurable set of expectations for each skill category as well as a perception of how well that skill quality is being met. Gaps from expectations to perceptions of delivery have been shown to be detrimental for various stakeholders’ satisfaction (Wade & Parent, 2001; Tesch et al., 2003).

Matching skills to specific tasks has also been examined. Researchers have found that project failure is more likely to occur when IS generalists (such as system analysts) are used for technical tasks such as quality assurance. testing. and estimating (Jones. 1995). Interpersonal skills effect on system success is a function of the perceived level of the IS staff’s skill proficiency as well as the understood expectations of skill requirements (Jiang et al., 2003). In fact, to increase the chance of project success, project team members should have diverse skills to be certain all bases are covered (Klein et a/.. 2002b). But gaps other than skill to task gaps are present. IS providers rate the importance of organizational skills at different levels of importance from users, some skills rated higher and some lower (Green, 1989; Nelson, 1991). These differences may be especially prominent under certain conditions, such as rapidly changing IS staffing patterns (Lee et al., 1995).

In short, research has delineated many of the differences between IS users and IS providers. This is referred to as the ‘user–designer communications gap’ (Laudon & Laudon, 2006). A social perception model explains many of these differences (Schiffmann, 1990; Baron & Byrne, 1991). Individuals develop their own schema for understanding the external world. Different working environments (e.g. IS unit vs. Accounting unit) and/or individual differences (e.g. education, knowledge) often influence one’s schema development and, thus, users and IS providers may perceive things differently. In other words, users’ and IS providers' skill expectations and perceived skill proficiencies are likely to differ. The question becomes not whether there is a difference, but what is the impact of the differences?

## HYPOTHESIS

Satisfaction — or lack thereof — resulting from an internal discrepancy was first applied to the job context (Locke, 1976). Here, satisfaction is an emotional response reflecting a dual value judgement: the discrepancy between what the individual expects and what he/she perceives he/she is getting. This was extended to the consumption context as the summary psychological state resulting when the emotion surrounding disconfirmed expectations is coupled with the consumer’s prior feelings (Oliver, 1993). The psychological state results from a cognitive appraisal of the expectation and performance discrepancy (confirmation). Lower expectation and/or higher performance lead to greater confirmation, which in turn positively influence customer satisfaction. The reverse causes disconfirmation and dissatisfaction

Oliver’s expectation–confirmation theory (ECT) is widely accepted in the consumer behaviour literature to study consumer satisfaction and service marketing in general (Anderson & Sullivan, 1993). The predictive ability of this theory has been demonstrated over a wide range of product and service contexts (Oliver, 1993; Patterson et al., 1997; Dabolkar et al., 2000). In addition, recent empirical studies have stressed the importance of user perception of performance in IS. Adopting ECT, Bhattacherjee (2001) found that continuance intention is determined by satisfaction with IS use and user satisfaction is influenced by confirmation of expectation. Web-customer satisfaction is influenced by the confirmation of consumer expectations of information quality and perceived performance (McKinney et al., 2002). In the IS skill literature, Tesch et al. (2003) found that users perceiving a positive IS skill gap, i.e. perception of skill proficiency exceeds skill proficiency expectation, will exhibit a higher level of user satisfaction than those users perceiving a negative gap. Wade & Parent (2001) found that a skill balance between the individual’s level of proficiency in the skill and its usefulness to the job leads to higher perceived job performance. As a user is a consumer of the IS services and products (Cash et al., 1988), consumers with high expectations of a delivered service or product are more likely to be dissatisfied than those with low expectations under constant levels of deliverables.

Meanwhile, other researchers have observed that levels of expectation play a critical role in user perceptions of success (Ginzberg, 1981; Doll & Ahmed, 1983). In a test of expectancy theory. DeSanctis (1983) found that higher levels of user expectation were related to higher levels of system use. User expectations exhibit a strong positive association with user satisfaction (Rushinek & Rushinek, 1986). Ginzberg (1981) conducted a field study where the realism of user expectations was defined as the difference between user expectations and IS analyst’s expectations. He found that the realism of user expectations was significantly correlated to user attitudes. In an extension applying cognitive dissonance theory (CDT), Szajna & Scamell (1993) found that subiects with high expectations will have higher user satisfaction scores than subjects with low expectations under similar performance. In short, this literature suggests that users with a higher level of expectation are more likely to experience a higher level of satisfaction when their expectations are met.

Recently, Klein & Jiang (2001) proposed a consonance model for IS success, suggesting that matching expectations of system specifications and success measures, i.e. system metrics, between users and IS service providers at the start of the IS development process supports a project moving towards the mutually understood objectives. Stakeholders must agree on a set of metrics to apply in the feedback process. Conflicts or variances should be resolved when discovered. Discrepancy among stakeholders will occur and should be expected both prior to the start of the project (i.e. expectation gap) and during the delivery (i.e. expectation– confirmation gap) process. Both gaps are important in the determination of final satisfaction in the directions predicted by ECT and CDT. Based on the IS consonance model, we expect:

User satisfaction will be highest when users place greater importance on skill levels than do the providers and perceived skill levels exceed skill expectations.

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

## Sample

Meaningful consideration of perceptions (skill proficiency) and expectations (skill importance) from IS providers' and users' points of view reguires paired comparisons of IS providers and IS users. Therefore, for each observation, a member of the IS staff and an IS user who worked on an IS project together were asked to complete the survey instruments. A list of potential participating organizations was built from the business college advisory boards of two doctoralgranting institutions. Projects were selected by the organizations based on recent completion. Initial contact with participants was made in one of two ways: (1) by contacting IS department directors or (2) by contacting an IS staff member. When initial contact was with the IS department director, the purpose of the study and the survey instruments were described to the IS director. IS directors who agreed to participate were asked to distribute survey instruments to an IS user and an IS provider who worked on the same project. When initial contact was made with an IS provider, the initial participant was asked to distribute the survey instruments to one IS user participating in the same project.

All subjects in this convenience sample were asked to provide information about skill levels and expectations. In addition, IS users were also asked to complete a survey regarding their satisfaction with the product or service provided. All the respondents were assured that their responses would be kept confidential. Self-addressed return envelopes for each participant were provided to the subjects. A total of 346 completed surveys were returned. Eighteen surveys were discarded because of missing observations of either IS provider or IS user. A total of 156 responses were received from users and 172 from IS personnel. Of these, 108 matched pairs existed on single projects. All valid responses were used in scale validation; only the 108 matched observations were used in other analysis. The users were primarily 40–50 years old, with a median work experience of 15 years, about 45% female, with more than 60% holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. The IS providers mostly had less than 10 years of experience, and were almost 10 years younger on the average than the users, with about 75% being male, and 85% holding a bachelor’s degree or above. Projects varied in duration from a few months to over 3 years. All were major software development projects.

## Constructs

## IS skill expectation/perception

The measurement of IS skills presents a challenge because of the changing nature of the field. Nelson (1991) proposed a skill measure based on prior work (Benbasat, 1980; Nelson & Cheney, 1987). The measure was developed further to include items for three categories of skills: technology – the specifics of the hardware and software; management – the understanding to lead and organize projects; and functional – knowledge about the processes and market of the organization (Trauth et al., 1993; Lee et al., 1995). An independent content analysis of the job market arrived at a similar categorization to include: technical knowledge of hardware and software; business knowledge of management, functionality, and social interaction; and system knowledge to include problem-solving and development methodology (Todd et al., 1995).

Table 1 identifies the items on the instrument employed from the categories derived by Lee et al. (1995). The instrument asked respondents to indicate their perception of the importance of each identified skill as well as their satisfaction with the indicated skill level. Each scale was scored using a five-point scale ranging from 1 (unimportant/unsatisfied) to 5 (very important/ very satisfied). All items were presented such that the greater the score, the greater the importance/satisfaction of/with the skill/knowledge. Although not a direct measure of expectations, importance has been used in previous studies involving expectation gaps and in drawing conclusions regarding expectations (Trauth et al., 1993; Tesch et al., 2003).

To examine the reliability and validity of the skill measure. we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) on each variation of the metric. Goodness of fit indices considered includes (1) Root Mean Square Residual (RMR); (2) Bentler’s Comparative Fit Index (CFI); (3) Bollen’s Non-normed Fit Index (NNFI); and (4) an Adjusted Goodness of Fit (AGFI) (Bollen, 1989; Bentler, 1990). Table 2 shows the results for user and provider expectations and user perceptions. The CFA for the IS user expectations measure resulted in an RMR of 0.05 (≤0.10 recommended), a CFI of 0.94 (≥0.90 recommended), an NNFI of 0.94 (≥0.90 recommended), and an AGFI of 0.80 (≥0.80 recommended). The fit indices indicate an acceptable fit for the measurement model.

Convergent validity is assessed by reviewing the t-tests for the factor loadings. The t-tests for each indicator loading demonstrate a high convergent validity, as shown in Table 1. Discriminant validity is empirically demonstrated by a confidence interval test of an interval of plus or minus two standard errors around the correlation between factors (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988). If the interval does not include 1.0 (or −1.0) discriminant validity is demonstrated. For example, in this case the correlation between technology management and business functions is 0.32 with a standard error of 0.06. The interval is then from 0.20 to 0.44, which does not include the value of 1.0. Therefore, discriminant validity is demonstrated for these two factors. Likewise, the Cronbach alpha value for the technology management and business functions constructs all exceeded the recommended level of 0.70. as indicated in Table 1.

## User satisfaction

User satisfaction is employed as the measure of IS success in this study because the skill expectations and perceptions are being elicited from users and the measure is a common surrogate for success in IS studies (DeLone & McLean, 1992). User satisfaction was measured by a 13-item scale shown in Table 3 as developed by Baroudi & Orlikowski (1988). This construct contains three dimensions – user involvement, IS provider service, and information product quality. Respondents were asked to indicate their agreement or disagreement with each item on a five-point Likert-type scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

A CFA was conducted to examine the validity and reliability of the use of this construct in this study. Because of an insignificant loading (e.g. <0.50), one item was dropped from the model. The CFA results for the final IS career satisfaction measure indicate a good fit with the data. ## DISCUSSION

Previous research on IS provider skills has been extended by incorporating a consonance model into the examination of deviations from expectations for technological and business skills. This new view greatly extends our understanding of the gaps in skill expectations that users have of their IS providers. The gaps in skill expectations found in the data for this study indicate a joint relationship exists that is rooted in the acquisition of consonance, or agreement among more than one stakeholder group (Klein & Jiang, 2001; Klein et al., 2002a).

An examination of the one-dimensional results from Table 4 on its own would lead to false conclusions. Table 4 shows that failure to meet expectation results in lower satisfaction. It may lead one to lower the levels of user expectations to reduce the potential gap between what is delivered and what is expected or to increase the possibility of meeting the expectation. On the other hand, Table 5 indicates that, as often observed in the IS literature, allowing users to hold high expectations has the effect of raising satisfaction in one dimension. In other words. although only weakly supported, the results in Table 5 would indicate that it would be desirable to allow users to hold expectations that are higher than those held by the IS provider. It is the two-dimensional analysis shown in Table 6, however, that presents the fullest picture. Table 6 shows the highest satisfaction to be in the cell where expectations are high, but so is performance. The lowest level of satisfaction occurs where expectations are low and perceived performance is even lower. Essentially, this calls for setting high standards. but not so high that the standards cannot be attained. To fail to meet expectations causes more of a drop in satisfaction than to exceed low expectations.

Practitioners must consider the setting of expectations in the light of all results. The joint results and other studies on consonance indicate that a realistic set of expectations will result in the best target for satisfaction and other measures of success. What is critical is a common understanding and alignment of achievable criteria across multiple stakeholders (Locke & Latham, 1990; Vancouver & Schmitt, 1991). In practice, a good start is accomplished through goal-setting, partnering, and careful selection of project team members (Locke & Latham, 1990; Cowan et al., 1992; Klein et al., 2002b).

Extending the recently explored ‘importance–proficiency’ gap effect, the results of this study are significant to researchers because it is not just user expectations that are important, it is the realism of user expectations that is potentially a factor in IS success or failure. Studies have empirically demonstrated this for a number of years. Ginzberg (1981) found that the realism of user expectations was significantly correlated to user attitudes of IS providers’ delivery of systems. Ginzberg’s main emphasis is not on the direction of the unrealistic expectations, but on their presence. Some researchers asserted that user involvement in the design of an IS brings about realistic expectations of system capabilities (Gibson, 1977). The key is that the interaction of users and IS providers during the development process generates realistic user expectations (Doll & Ahmed, 1983).

Limitations are present in the study through the collection of data over only two stakeholders in the organizational implementation of systems. There are also managers and owners to consider, and these groups may have very different views. The choice of the user as the second stakeholder also dictated the use of user satisfaction as the measure of success, although others may be as appropriate. The sample size is sufficient for the analysis conducted, but more advanced models would require a larger sample and a broadening of the region to further generalize. While other dimensions of success are overlooked by this approach, user satisfaction is commonly applied in IS studies as a measure of success (DeLone & McLean, 1992). The narrowing of the scope does allow for examination of the issues, but diminishes the ability to generalize. To this end, in extensions of this research, the concepts should be examined over more stakeholders, different measures of success, and expectations regarding development factors other than IS providers’ skills. In addition, future studies should expand the context to include additional organization types and projects of varying scope.
