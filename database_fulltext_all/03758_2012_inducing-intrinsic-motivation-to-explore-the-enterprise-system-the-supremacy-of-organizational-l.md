Abst ract : The adoption of an organization-wide system, such as an enterprise system (ES), has often been mandated by organizational management, which may not necessarily motivate users to proactively explore the system’s features and subsequently apply pertinent features that best support their job tasks. Anchoring on self-determination theory, this research investigates the antecedents and consequences of users’ intrinsic motivation to explore ES features. We propose two organizational levers (i.e., autonomous job design and socialization tactics) that the management could exercise to trigger intrinsic motivation, thereby leading to improved ES feature exploration. Intrinsic motivation is manifested by hedonic motivation and normative motivation, whereas ES feature exploration is conceptualized as a dual-dimensional outcome reflected by cognitive behavior (exploratory usage) and positive affect (exploration satisfaction). Through a two-stage survey of 127 organizational users in China, we find general support for our research model. We further observe significant moderating effects of prevention focus on the association between organizational levers and intrinsic motivations. Beyond demonstrating how organizational users respond to different organizational levers, this research examines a broader, enduring challenge, which is to determine how organizational users can be induced to be intrinsically inspired to innovatively harness implemented information systems.

Key w ords and phras es : enterprise system, exploration usage, intrinsic motivation, organizational levers.

In t he ongoing bid t o disc over how organizat ions can turn their enterprise systems (ES) investment into actual performance, scholars have paid increasing attention to the role of organizational users [28, 31, 49, 50, 60, 66]. Proponents of innovation theories have argued that the innovative use and application of technology is necessary for an organization to materialize the benefits of ES; moreover, these proponents have pointed out that such innovative ideas are generated by the users rather than the management or information technology (IT) staff [20, 54, 63]. In particular, with the complexity and configurability of ES, users’ proactive exploration of system features, subsequent adaptation to the system by altering their work processes, and selective appropriation of features would determine the success of system adoption [21, 31, 33, 35, 56, 60].

However, the adoption of ES is typically mandated by the organizational management, which may not necessarily motivate users to proactively explore the system [56, 64]. Hence, an investigation into the methods for motivating users to behave in line with organizational goals after the system’s formal organizational adoption, as well as the ways in which user motivation would lead to innovative system usage, is of great interest and significance [25, 48].

Previous research has primarily assumed the rather passive role of users and focused on user readiness to accept the system while largely ignoring the importance of having users proactively adapt the system to realize ES benefits [29, 42, 54]. According to creativity theory, complex, innovative, and extra-role tasks such as ES feature exploration require users to be intrinsically motivated [6, 18]. In line with this expectation, user intrinsic motivation should be a central construct for research on how to spark user motivation to explore the system [31, 56]. However, few prior studies have explicitly conceptualized such intrinsic motivation in their research models, with the exception of Li et al. [42] who investigate the effects of motivation on users’ routine and extended use of a system. Intrinsic motivation is a complex multidimensional construct [18, 25, 44, 55] that plays a critical role in ES implementation. Thus, a clear conceptualization, more nuanced approach, and inclusion of this construct in the research model would shed new light on our understanding of its causes and effects.

In addition, prior research has primarily focused on investigating the effects of technology-centric individual perceptions such as perceived usefulness and ease of use on user acceptance of the system [8, 10, 29, 43, 65] while largely ignoring the effects of the various aspects of organizational context on user adaptation to the system, especially that of organizational mechanisms [51]. Given that the alignment of users’ individual interest and organizational goals is a critical challenge for ES implementation, understanding how management can induce the necessary motivation and exert influence from that perspective is imperative, thus allowing users to be self-motivated to perform tasks that would allow the organization to reap the purported system benefits [14, 64]. Fundamental questions that need to be addressed are related to the identification of (1) the components of users’ intrinsic motivation, (2) specific organizational levers that the management can apply to align users’ individual interests with organizational goals of ES adoption, (3) conditions under which the organizational levers can effectively enhance users’ intrinsic motivation to explore the system, and (4) the cognitive and affective outcomes of users’ intrinsic motivation.

The current study is an effort to address these issues. Drawing on self-determination theory (SDT), we examine how users could be intrinsically motivated to proactively engage in the exploration of ES features through the proposed organizational levers. In our research model, we conceptualize intrinsic motivation as consisting of two components: intrinsic hedonic and normative motivation. We posit that job autonomy and organizational socialization tactics are the two key organizational levers that have positive effects on users’ intrinsic motivation to explore ES features [23, 25, 55]. Moreover, following SDT, we contend that intrinsic motivation energizes users to expend effort on the exploration of the system and thus affects the extent of users exploratory usage and satisfaction with the exploration process [6, 18].

The research model is tested using data collected from 127 ES users in Chinese organizations. To explicitly capture the context in which the data were collected, we consider the unique characteristics of Asian people (in particular, Chinese), who tend to have a prevention regulatory focus, which is a behavioral orientation formed through societal and cultural norms [40]. According to the regulatory focus theory, people with prevention focus orientation strive to fulfill general needs for safety and security through the pursuit of “oughts” (i.e., duties, obligations, and responsibilities) [26]. The essence of this focus is captured succinctly in the age-old Chinese proverb: “bu qiu you gong, dan qiu wu guo” (regardless of whether an individual seeks achievements, he should nonetheless avoid committing mistakes). This focus is particularly pertinent as we conducted the data collection in China, a country that is characterized by a collectivist philosophy in which the people subscribe predominantly to the prevention focus orientation [1]. We explicitly incorporate regulatory focus theory into the theoretical development of SDT to examine the moderating effect of prevention focus on the association between two organizational levers (i.e., job autonomy and socialization tactics) and intrinsic motivation. Our thesis is that when the organizational tactics match the user’s regulatory focus, the user is more likely to perceive, encode, recall, and assimilate the information conveyed by the organizational lever [27], thereby strengthening the organizational lever’s effects on intrinsic motivation.

This research has four major theoretical contributions. First, building on the notion that users need to proactively explore and innovatively appropriate ES features, this research deepens the investigation of intrinsic motivation by theorizing it to be reflected in two dimensions: the normative and the hedonic. Second, we build on and extend the system usage conceptualization as a dual-dimensional outcome reflected by cognitive behavior (exploratory usage behavior) and affective assessment (exploration satisfaction). The concurrent consideration of both aspects of behavioral outcome answers the enduring need to address the user’s affective response to system implementation [53]. Third, this study goes beyond existing research by explicitly investigating specific measures that can be instituted by organizations to raise the intrinsic motivation of employees to explore system features. In other words, it illuminates how intrinsic motivation can be raised through organizational levers. Fourth, the study of the prevention focus construct explicitly incorporates the research context into our model, as the data were collected in China. This research is also a response to the increasing recognition among scholars of the need to take the cultural context into consideration in information systems (IS) studies. In addition to the theoretical contributions, this research also suggests how organizations could effectively harness the organizational levers to cultivate intrinsic motivation among users, ultimately elevating the odds of success in complex IS implementations such as ES.

## Research Background

## User Intrinsic Motivation for Enterprise Systems Feature Exploration

Mot ivat ion m obilizes and energizes an individual’s efforts and persistence in conducting a relevant task [6]. Previous IS research has endorsed the importance of motivation in IS implementation [11]. There are two categories of motivation: extrinsic motivation and intrinsic motivation. Extrinsic motivation refers to the impetus to act on acquiring externally administered rewards, including career advancement, prestige, and positive evaluations from others [18]. Arising from the desire to obtain outcomes that are distant from work, extrinsic motivation is often short-lived and could cease once the mandates vanish [46]. Intrinsic motivation is a psychological force arising from personal enjoyment or the internalized value of work [18]. Different from extrinsic motivation, intrinsic motivation exists within an individual and is associated with a behavior that is internally driven and is likely to be self-sustainable [18].

This research focuses on user intrinsic motivation in the context of ES implementation for two major reasons. First, for the organization to reap benefits from ES, it requires users to proactively explore the system and discover innovative ways of applying the system to best support their daily job tasks [56]. Passive acceptance of the system, which differs from the intent of the software design, would jeopardize the organization’s return on investment [54]. Second, as a complex system, ES provides a wide spectrum of features or feature sets for users to explore and appropriate, which imposes significant challenges on organizational users [60]. Users are required to expend significant cognitive efforts to overcome the knowledge barriers to adaptive system use [58]. Adding to this challenge, ES exploration is usually not clearly specified in job descriptions and depends largely on the initiative of individual users [34]. Thus, the motivation for proactively exploring ES features would differ from that required for a decision to accept the system [31].

According to SDT and creativity theory, mobilizing individuals to conduct complex, innovative, and extra-role tasks such as ES feature exploration requires intrinsic rather than extrinsic motivation [31, 39]. Arising from within, intrinsic motivation drives individuals to expend intensive effort on performing the related task and to be persistent when faced with difficulties, even when there is no explicit expectation governing the behavior. Thus, intrinsic motivation has often been observed to yield a positive effect on the performance of complex, innovative, and extra-role tasks [6, 55]. Empirically, there are scattered studies that lend support to the effects of intrinsic motivation on user learning of large-scale systems [39, 41].

Prior IS research on intrinsic motivation has focused on its hedonic dimension [39, 41], which arises from the satisfaction of the user’s need for competence and autonomy afforded by tackling tasks [23]. The need for competence represents an individual’s desire to achieve success through one’s personal efforts and to take personal responsibility for the outcomes. The need for autonomy refers to the desire to be self-determining or self-initiating [18]. According to SDT, human beings possess a third basic need—the need for relatedness, which is defined as the need to be connected to others and to be effective in the social world [18]. Satisfaction of this need will arouse an employee’s normative motivation toward conducting tasks that would benefit the organization [25].

Thus, we consider intrinsic motivation as a construct of two components [44]: (1) a task-related, enjoyment-based hedonic component (i.e., intrinsic hedonic motivation) and (2) a social, values-based normative component (i.e., intrinsic normative motivation). Intrinsic hedonic motivation is propelled by the goal of being engaged in selfdetermining and competence-enhancing behavior [18], whereas intrinsic normative motivation is driven by the goal of engaging in behaviors that are compliant with norms and values [7]. Ignoring either would be an impediment to understanding how the management could intrinsically motivate employees [23].

## Organizational Levers and User Intrinsic Motivation

A significant portion of extant IS literature investigates the management’s influence on user technology acceptance [14]. A common research tendency is to treat the management’s participation or support as a black box (i.e., a single overarching construct) and ignore the specific levers that management personnel can apply to influence postadoptive behaviors in general and motivation to explore the system in particular [58, 64]. Such levers are critical for the successful implementation of large packaged system such as ES. Consequently, an investigation of how organizational levers could be applied to intrinsically motivate users to explore ES features is essential, but has been largely neglected.

According to SDT, the management can facilitate employees’ intrinsic motivation by introducing appropriate organizational levers that support psychological needs [23, 55]. Specifically, an appropriate job task design enables the organization to support employee needs for competence and autonomy, thereby facilitating the development of hedonic motivation. Job autonomy, which refers to the freedom and authority of employees to determine the extent to which they are engaged in a task and how they conduct the task, is regarded as the most important job characteristic that can intrinsically motivate employees to engage in innovative job tasks [6, 49].

In addition, the organization can fulfill the employee need for relatedness by applying socialization tactics [18, 23]. Socialization tactics refer to organization-wide activities—formal or informal social events, such as meetings, training workshops, and user sharing sessions—that instill organizational goals and norms. The information shared at such events can enhance employee identification with the organization and internalization of its norms and values [23]. Moreover, the information serves to guide the evaluation of job tasks and enables employees to recognize their possible impact on the organization, and thus facilitates the development of normative motivation for conducting tasks that benefit the organization [37].

Hence, our study focuses on the two aforementioned organizational levers: job autonomy and socialization tactics. These two levers were first conceptually proposed by Gottschalg and Zollo [25], but without empirical validation, as pivotal factors affecting employees’ intrinsic motivation within the context of the alignment of employee interest with organizational goals for gaining competitive advantage. With significant adaptation to the ES exploration context and empirical assessment, we are able to provide a more refined and empirically validated understanding of the two levers. Notably, our intention is not to examine an exhaustive list of organization levers but, rather, to focus on the two pertinent levers that would affect hedonic and normative motivations.

## Prevention Focus and User Intrinsic Motivation

Adding to the theoretical lens of SDT in our setting, we argue that the effectiveness of organizational levers in arousing users’ intrinsic motivation is contingent upon an individual’s motivational system [57]. According to the regulatory focus theory, there are two distinct motivational systems: promotion focus and prevention focus [26]. Individuals with a promotion focus endeavor to fulfill general needs for nurturance, achievement, and gain through the pursuit of ideals. Individuals imbued with this focus are primarily concerned with the absence and presence of positive outcomes. In contrast, individuals with a prevention focus strive to fulfill general needs for safety and security through the pursuit of duties, obligations, and responsibilities. As a personal trait, the regulatory focus affects the salience of information available in the work environment, and thus moderates the relationship between the expectancy of goal attainment and motivation [27]. Specifically, a prevention focus would make individuals alert to information about the organization’s norms and values, whereas a promotion focus would lead to high sensitivity to information regarding individual achievements. Consequently, employees with different regulatory foci could react to organizational levers differently [57].

An individual’s regulatory focus is determined by one’s self-view, which is induced by the culture in which this person lives [1, 40]. A culture with a high degree of individualism nurtures an independent self-view, which leads individuals to manifest a promotion focus. In contrast, a culture with a high degree of collectivism such as the culture in China, nurtures an interdependent self-view and thus makes individuals exhibit a prevention focus [1]. Given that this study was conducted in China, we focus on examining the effects of prevention focus as it has been found to be a common characteristic among Chinese workers.

We used the prevention focus concept instead of employing cultural values for two main reasons. First, the use of cultural values would be more appropriate when there is a cross-cultural comparison across different cultural dimensions such as individualism versus collectivism, high versus low power distance, and high versus low uncertainty avoidance. However, the context of the current research involves only one culture. As the goal of this research is not cultural dimension comparison, we decided not to use a cultural values perspective. Second, by modeling prevention focus, we are responding to the increasing call from scholars to incorporate features unique to the specific cultural context under investigation, hence bringing more unique insights.

## Antecedents of Motivation for the Exploration of Enterprise Systems Features

When a user has a high level of autonomy, he or she can self-identify not only the ES features to explore but also how to explore them. Knowing best about his or her own interest, the user will sense a better control over the expected outcomes and therefore adopt an active rather than passive orientation toward system exploration. In addition, because of the highly interdependent nature of the system, the application of a new feature often engenders the need to change how a job is performed [60]. With a high perceived level of job autonomy, the user would have positive expectancy and valence about the consequences of feature exploration and therefore derive a sense of selfdetermination from such behavior [49]. This would reduce the obligation aspect and enhance the enjoyment aspect of feature exploration [2, 4]. Hence, we propose:

## Hypothesis 1a: A user’s perceived job autonomy is positively related to his or her intrinsic hedonic motivation to explore ES features.

Job autonomy serves as an individual-level organizational lever, whereas socialization tactics focus on individual-to-individual interactions, which could foster a sense of belonging and create opportunities for the sharing of experiences and organizational norms. Through socialization, the employee would recognize the value of job tasks that conform to organizational values and norms, which in turn would arouse intrinsic normative motivation [62]. These notions can be applied to the context of ES feature exploration. Specifically, socialization tactics provide users with opportunities to communicate with colleagues concerning ES-related issues and thus address their need for relatedness. For instance, users would share their experiences and beliefs pertaining to the benefits of feature exploration [60]. The management would also be able to convey a clear message that ES feature exploration is a desirable behavior for the organization. Thus, we posit:

Hypothesis 1b: A user’s perceived extent of organizational socialization is positively related to his or her intrinsic normative motivation to explore ES features.

## Moderating Effect of Prevention Focus

An individual with prevention focus typically assumes responsibility by following rules and guidelines [26]. Users with a high level of prevention focus would be alert to information from the work environment regarding their job expectations, to avoid the anxiety they would experience if they fall short of the “oughts.” With job autonomy, the management does not provide detailed information regarding what tasks an employee should perform and how to perform them. This lack of clear guidelines would cause a user with a high prevention focus to experience more negative feelings and thus appreciate less the given autonomy, compared to a user with low prevention focus. Accordingly, we propose:

Hypothesis 2a: The prevention focus of a user’s motivational system weakens the relationship between perceived job autonomy and the user’s intrinsic hedonic motivation.

In addition, a user with a prevention focus is more sensitive to information regarding the organizational values and norms [26, 27]. At formal or informal social events, users with a high level of prevention focus are inclined to pick up the message that ES feature exploration would benefit the organization and to regard such behavior as part of their job responsibilities. In other words, a prevention focus facilitates the management to align user motivation with organizational goals. Therefore, given the same socialization tactics, a user with a higher level of prevention focus would tend to have a higher intrinsic normative motivation to explore ES features. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2b: The prevention focus of a user’s motivational system strengthens the relationship between perceived organizational socialization and the user’s intrinsic normative motivation.

## Consequences of Motivation to Explore Enterprise Systems Features

Our research interest lies in two consequences of intrinsic motivation to explore ES features, that is, exploratory usage and satisfaction with the exploration process. According to SDT, motivation would energize individuals to expend effort and thus have a positive effect on the behavior outcomes [18, 23]. The effort exerted can be manifested in two dimensions: intensiveness and persistence. Specifically, with an intrinsic hedonic motivation, users’ curiosity and interest in learning ES features would drive them to engage in exploring ES features, be open to challenges, and be willing to take risks [23]. They would expend intensive effort in acquiring information and trying out innovative ES feature applications, which would subsequently lead to a greater extent and scope of exploratory usage. An intrinsic normative motivation also enables users to expand their scope of attention to innovative ES applications that would benefit the organization [25, 45]. Given the complex and highly interdependent nature of ES, a feature application may affect the business processes involving multiple users and possibly groups [56]. A normative motivation drives the user to exert intensive effort in thinking systematically to consolidate and align different perspectives. Thus, the user would explore a wide range of ES features and evaluate how different features or feature sets benefit individuals, groups, and the organization.

In addition, SDT contends that intrinsic motivation affects behavior outcomes by encouraging persistence [18, 55]. With the aroused intrinsic hedonic motivation, users’ desires to explore their interests and engage their curiosity would make them psychologically absorbed in the process of performing ES feature exploration [2]. Such psychological engagement provides energy for a sustained effort, and users persist with the complex tasks. Intrinsic normative motivation also allows users to integrate the behavior of ES feature exploration with their personal values system. According to SDT, individuals will persistently engage in behaviors that are endorsed by their own personal values system [18]. Therefore, we expect that a high level of intrinsic motivation, whether hedonic or normative, will allow users to exert intensive and persistent efforts on ES feature exploration, thus creating a positive effect on the exploratory system usage. Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 3a: A user’s intrinsic motivation is positively related to his or her exploratory usage. Specifically, (i) a user’s intrinsic hedonic motivation is positively related to his or her exploratory usage of the system’s features, and (ii) a user’s intrinsic normative motivation is positively related to his or her exploratory usage of the system’s features.

In addition to the cognitive outcomes, intrinsic motivation could influence affective outcomes. There are two fundamental perspectives on the psychological value of work, that is, work has both an instrumental and a terminal value [15]. The instrumental value view regards work primarily as a means to an end. In contrast, the terminal perspective views work as a positive activity that produces a feeling of dignity and self-respect [15]. Given that intrinsic motivation drives individuals to enjoy the task per se, effortful engagement at work is energized, which in turn leads to affective consequences such as satisfaction [18, 23]. Indeed, there is considerable empirical support for the positive effects of intrinsic motivation on an individual’s satisfaction with the work process [12].

Intrinsic hedonic motivation causes a user to become psychologically absorbed in the exploration process and experience a kind of psychological “flow,” a sense of merging with the ES feature exploration activities [2]. As such, intrinsic hedonic motivation allows the individual to derive a sense of enjoyment from the ES exploration process. In contrast, intrinsic normative motivation leads a user to perceive value in the feature exploration process, and ultimately take pride in creating effects that benefit the organization. Hence, we expect that a user who is intrinsically motivated would derive satisfaction with the work process as posited below:

Hypothesis 3b: A user’s intrinsic motivation is positively related to his or her satisfaction with ES feature exploration. Specifically, (i) a user’s intrinsic hedonic motivation is positively related to his or her satisfaction with ES feature exploration, and (ii) a user’s intrinsic normative motivation is positively related to his or her satisfaction with ES feature exploration.

In addition, complex, innovative cognitive behavior affects satisfaction and mediates the effects of antecedent variables on satisfaction. According to SDT, by enacting the behavior that is of interest and consistent with personal values, an individual would attain the desired goal and thus derive satisfaction from the process [18, 55]. Extant literature consistently provides strong empirical support for the positive effects of innovative cognitive behavior on satisfaction [47]. Extending this notion to the current research context, we expect that a higher level of exploratory usage of ES features rooted in cognitive behavior would lead to a greater level of satisfaction with the exploration process, as stated below:

Hypothesis 4: A users’ exploratory usage of system features is positively related to his or her satisfaction with the exploration process.

## Control Variables

To account for the differences among individual users and the potential effects of other factors, we consider control variables that are pertinent to individual characteristics for intrinsic motivations, exploratory usage, and satisfaction with the exploration process. Specifically, we include personal IT innovativeness as the control variable for intrinsic hedonic and normative motivations. Six control variables for exploratory usage and satisfaction are also incorporated. These variables include the user’s ability to explore ES features, user age, work experience within the organization, experience with the system, computer experience, and computer self-efficacy. We select these variables because past research has indicated their possible impact on users’ exploratory usage and satisfaction with the exploration process. For instance, with a higher level of perceived ability, there is a possibility that users may be more optimistic about the outcomes of their respective exploration of ES features and therefore would more proactively explore the target system’s features. Thus, perceived ability has a positive effect on user performance. In addition, we include users’ perceived customization level of the system as a control variable for exploratory usage. Perceived customization reflects whether it is easy for users to explore the system and thus may play a key role in explaining the variation in the exploratory usage of ES.

## Research Methodology

Survey m et hodology w as adopt ed t o t est t he m odel as it offers the basis for establishing generalization and the opportunity for replicating the investigation. To test our research model, the entire survey was conducted in two stages. In the first stage of the survey, we collected responses pertaining to antecedents and motivations. Three months later, we conducted the second stage of the survey. The questionnaires distributed in the second stage were focused on the assessment of participants’ ES exploration behavior and outcomes (i.e., exploratory usage and exploration satisfaction). By collecting survey data at two points in time, we addressed several problems inherent in a single response survey, such as respondents’ correlating the outcomes with precursor factors (i.e., common method bias). Two of the plausible reasons for not conducting a two-stage survey in prior studies include the additional effort required to collect survey forms twice rather than once and the constraints imposed by the participating organization regarding the survey. This study is one of the few studies that collect survey data at two points in time.

## Measurement

The measurement items in our questionnaire were adapted from existing validated and well-tested scales in the extant literature, which were proven to be of sound validity and reliability. In the questionnaire, all the items were measured with five-point Likert scales ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” The instruments for job autonomy and socialization tactics (a second-order construct with the dimensions of collective and serial tactics) were adapted from Ahuja and Thatcher [4] and Jansen et al. [30], respectively. The measurement scales for intrinsic motivation (with cognitive and affective components) were adapted from Amabile et al. [6], whereas the items for normative motivation (with the components of identification and compatible values) were adapted from Cooper and Jayatilaka [17] and Karahanna et al. [34]. Both types of intrinsic motivation were measured as formative constructs.

In addition, we followed the two-step method suggested by Burton-Jones and Straub [13], and operationalized exploratory usage as a second-order formative construct with the dimensions of cognitive absorption and new feature exploration. Cognitive absorption captures user employment of the system and represents the extent of user absorption when using the system [2, 13]. We adopted items for cognitive absorption that were first developed by Agarwal and Karahanna [2]. New feature exploration captures the innovative use of the system in a task and represents the extent of innovative use of the features related to the core aspects. The scale of new feature exploration was developed by following the approach proposed by Burton-Jones and Straub [13]. To construct a task-centered scale of new feature exploration, we consulted an independent domain expert (in this case, an SAP consultant) and identified core activities that were supported by the system, namely, data input, information retrieval (including report generating), and data analysis. These items were adapted for the task domain and set at mid-range task specificity to explore the class of features explored rather than specific features [13]. This approach would balance the need for broad applicability with the need for focused questions [31]. The adapted items were further revised based on the comments of the independent expert.

Furthermore, we adapted items for satisfaction with the exploration process from Brown and Peterson [12]. User ability was measured with instruments adapted from Jones [32]. Prevention focus items were adapted from Shah and Higgins [57]. In addition, we adapted items for computer self-efficacy from Compeau and Higgins [16], the scale for personal IT innovativeness from Agarwal and Prasad [3], and the scale for system customization from Klein and Rai [38]. ## Field Survey

We surveyed ES users from three manufacturing companies in China that had implemented ES from SAP AG for about one year. We first approached a large consulting company that provides firms with solutions on SAP system implementation. This company provided us with a list of 10 firms that had implemented SAP for about a year and had adopted the same set of modules (i.e., financial, operations, and customer relationship management). We sent out an invitation, together with an endorsement letter issued by the consulting company, to each of the companies on the list. Three companies in the electronics manufacturing industry agreed to participate in our study. We randomly selected 600 respondents from their employee lists. Although the survey respondents were anonymous, we required the respondents to generate their own participation codes through a coding system to enable our linking of their responses for the two stages of the survey.

In the first stage of the survey, we sent each respondent the first questionnaire, together with a cover letter explaining our research objectives. The respondents were assured that their responses would be used for research purposes only and that only aggregated statistical data would be reported. In the first part of the survey, the respondents were required to provide information on their demographics, perceived job autonomy, and the socialization tactics applied by their firms, as well as their motivation for exploring ES features. We received a total of 228 returned questionnaires, representing a 38 percent response rate.

Three months later, we sent the participants the questionnaire for the second stage of the survey to collect data on their exploratory usage and satisfaction with the exploration process, together with a thank-you letter for their participation and an explanation of that stage of the survey. We received 196 returned questionnaires, for a response rate of 32.7 percent. Among the returned questionnaires from the two stages of the survey, we received 181 pairs of matched questionnaires. Four pairs of matched question naires were incomplete and thus discarded. In addition, 50 matched responses were from employees who belonged to the IT and research and development departments. Their responses were also discarded because they were not the direct users of the ES. Thus, we collected 127 pairs of usable questionnaires. A majority of the respondents are ages 26 to 35 (65.35 percent); have an associate, bachelor’s degree, or above educational levels (84.25 percent); and are from various departments, including logistics (22.83 percent), production (16.54 percent), procurement (18.11 percent), accounting and finance (18.11 percent), quality control (10.24 percent), and sales and marketing (14.17 percent). We tested for nonresponse bias by comparing the chi-squares of the responses to demographic questions from the first 25 percent of the respondents to those of the final 25 percent. Any significant difference would indicate the presence of a nonresponse bias, which was absent from our results.

## Data Analysis

## Common Method Bias and Multicollinearity Test

Alt hough our s urvey dat a c ollect ion w as c onduct ed in tw o st ages , there remained a concern that all the data were perceptual and collected from a single source [52]. Consequently, we examined the possibility of common method bias with Harman’s one-factor test by establishing whether a general construct accounts for the majority of the covariance in the measurement scores. Before conducting Harman’s one-factor test, we checked the adequacy of sample size. It is suggested that an effective factor analysis requires a minimum sample size of 300 [61]. Given that we had only 127 data points, we assessed the adequacy of the sample size using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure, which stipulates that a KMO value of 0.70 and above indicates that factor analysis would be effective, regardless of sample size. The results showed that the KMO value of our data was 0.80. Accordingly, we proceeded to conduct the exploratory factor analysis. The resulting principal component factor analysis yielded 15 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 and accounted for 79.952 percent of the variances. The first of these 15 factors accounted for 20.005 percent of the variances. Thus, common method bias was unlikely to be a serious concern in our study.

In addition, we conducted a multicollinearity test by assessing the variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance values of the constructs. Generally, the common rule for the presence of multicollinearity is that VIFs should be higher than 10, or tolerance values should be less than 0.1. Our results indicated that the highest VIF was 1.346, which was much lower than the benchmark value of 10, and the lowest tolerance value was 0.740, which was higher than the benchmark value of 0.1. As such, multicollinearity did not appear to be a significant problem with our research.

## Assessing the Structural Model

Following the confirmation of good psychometric properties in the measurement model, we examined the structural model to assess the explanatory power of the constructs and the significance of the posited paths. The partial least squares approach was applied to analyze the structural model. Seven control variables, namely, personal innovativeness, ability, age, work experience within the organization, experience with the system, computer experience, and computer self-efficacy, were considered in the analysis of the structural model. We assessed the predictive validity by examining the $R ^ { 2 }$ values and the structural paths (see Tables 4a and 4b). The control model was run with only six control variables (i.e., excluding personal innovativeness, which is a motivation factor). We observed that $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 1 4 6$ on exploratory usage and $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 3 0 9$ on exploration satisfaction. Following this step, we ran the theoretical model with only intrinsic motivations (i.e., intrinsic hedonic and intrinsic normative motivations) and excluded the control variables (exploratory usage $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 1 4 5$ ; exploration satisfaction $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 4 2 2 )$ . Finally, we ran the theoretical model by including all the control variables (exploratory usage $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 1 8 ;$ exploration satisfaction $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 4 7 0 )$ . To assess the relative importance of intrinsic motivations (i.e., intrinsic hedonic and intrinsic normative motivations) versus the control variables of individual differences, we computed the effect size $( f ^ { 2 } )$ , which refers to the magnitude of change in $R ^ { 2 }$ . Effect sizes of 0.02, 0.15, and 0.35 indicated small, moderate, and strong effects, respectively. The results indicate that intrinsic motivations account for 7.2 percent $( f ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 9 2 )$ and 16.1 percent $( f ^ { 2 } = 0 . 3 0 4 )$ of the variance in exploratory usage and exploration satisfaction, respectively.

The results of the structural model are presented in Figure 1. Among the seven control variables, only Ability has a significant positive effect on feature exploration satisfaction. In general, the research model is largely supported. Specifically, the positive effects of organizational levers on intrinsic motivations are significant. Thus, H1a and H 1b are supported. The positive effects of intrinsic hedonic motivation on exploratory usage, those of intrinsic hedonic motivation on exploration satisfaction, and those of intrinsic normative motivation on exploration satisfaction are found to be significant. However, the relationship between intrinsic normative motivation and exploratory usage is insignificant. Therefore, H3a(i), H3b(i), and H3b(ii) are supported, whereas H3a(ii) is not supported. In addition, the results offer statistical support for the relationship between exploratory usage and exploration satisfaction. Thus, H4 is also supported. Generally, the structured model explains 21.8 percent and 47.0 percent of the variances in exploratory usage and exploration satisfaction, respectively.

## Discussion and Implications

## Discussion of Findings

Our int erest in invest igat ing t he ant ec edents and c ons equenc es of user intrinsic motivation in the exploration of ES features is triggered by the important active role played by organizational users in integrating IS feature applications into business processes [13, 21, 31]. The dearth of prior research on how users can be energized to proactively explore system features, which is a precursor to such integration, renders further relevance to this investigation. Overall, the results show support for our proposed research model and yield several interesting observations.

First, job autonomy and socialization tactics have positive effects on intrinsic hedonic motivation and intrinsic normative motivation, respectively. This finding is consistent with the empirical results of Ahuja and Thatcher [4] and Li and Hsieh [41], thus confirming that organizational factors play a critical role in enhancing users’ intrinsic motivation for ES feature exploration [31, 49].

Second, prevention focus weakens the relationship between job autonomy and intrinsic hedonic motivation, which is in accord with the suggestion by regulatory focus theory [26, 27].

Third, intrinsic hedonic motivation has positive effects on exploratory usage and satisfaction with the exploration process. This finding is consistent with the notion of SDT and creativity theory [6, 18], which contends that intrinsic motivation drives individuals to conduct complex, innovative, and extra-role tasks. The finding also confirms the results of previous empirical IS research [39, 41].

Fourth, exploratory usage has a significant positive impact on satisfaction. This finding implies that a motivation such as intrinsic hedonic motivation may indirectly affect satisfaction through exploratory usage, in addition to its direct effect on satisfaction.

Contrary to what we hypothesized, the linkage between intrinsic normative motivation and exploratory usage is insignificant. The different effects of hedonic and normative motivation on exploratory usage may be because these two types of motivation would lead to users’ different predispositions. Specifically, hedonic motivation aims to improve an individual’s feelings derived from the related task [45]. Hedonic motivation energizes users to focus on the sensation of exploring innovative ways of applying ES features. Thus, users with hedonic motivation tend to explore more ES features. In contrast, normative motivation gives users the desire to act appropriately and leads users to consciously exercise discretion in their explorations due to considerations of interrelated perspectives [45]. Consequently, users who are normatively motivated may not necessarily explore more ES features as revealed by our post hoc interviews with ten respondents. Users with normative motivation cared about the new feature application’s potential impact on other individuals, groups, and the organization. For a complex, highly interdependent system such as ES, these users would spend time on coordinating and communicating with coworkers, and analyzing the pros and cons of a specific innovative ES feature application, which might constrain the scope of features explored. Thus, normative motivation does not have the same significant effect on exploratory usage as hedonic motivation.

In addition, the results show that prevention focus has an insignificant moderating effect on the relationship between socialization tactics and users’ normative motivation. A post hoc data analysis (see Table 4a) reveals that rather than a moderating effect, prevention focus has a direct effect on normative motivation $( \beta = 0 . 1 9 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ One potential explanation for this result is that according to regulatory focus theory, prevention focus makes individuals sensitive to role expectations, which are translated into “ought” goals that they feel obliged to fulfill [26]. These individuals believe that their high-quality relationship with the organization requires them not to fall short in their duties. If they do, they are likely to experience guilt and anxiety due to threats of social embarrassment and rejection [27]. The desire for avoiding such anxiety will be translated into motivation to perform tasks that are consistent with job expectations. Thus, in the current research context, users’ prevention focus has a direct effect on their normative motivation for ES feature exploration.

## Limitations and Future Research

It is important to evaluate our current study’s results and contributions in the light of its limitations, which point to future research efforts. First, there are other organizational levers that may affect the user’s intrinsic motivation to explore ES features. Given the concerns about creating a parsimonious model, we did not intend to include an exhaustive list of organizational levers; rather, we focused on the two most pertinent ones. However, future research exploring the effects of other organizational levers (e.g., transformative leadership) on motivation to explore ES features could shed new light on the possible ways that an organization can leverage to elevate user motivation to explore innovative ES feature applications.

Second, although we collected data at two points in time, which enabled us to explain the causal relationships between different types of intrinsic motivations and exploration outcomes, our data were obtained from a single source only. In addition, all of the major constructs were measured by respondent perceptions, which were subjective. Objective data on individuals’ exploration performance, such as managers actual evaluation of users’ job performance, would provide insights into how intrinsic motivations affect cognitive outcomes of innovative tasks. Such multiple-source data would alleviate the concern of common method bias. Data analysis indicates that the common method bias is not a serious concern for the current study. Nevertheless, we urge future researchers to undertake the compilation not only of objective data but data from multiple sources as well.

Third, this study collected and analyzed survey responses from three manufacturing companies, which could potentially differ in the degrees of SAP implementation. To address this issue and considering that different users might have access to different SAP functionalities, we included a construct—system customization—as a technologyspecific control variable. After controlling for the system customization, we did not observe any significant deviation from the original findings, thus further demonstrating the robustness of the findings on the use of organizational levers in encouraging intrinsic motivations, which lead to exploration usage. However, we encourage studies to further contextualize at the company level when examining organizational users responses to systems such as ES.

Fourth, we focused on users’ exploratory usage of ES with the presumption that discovering innovative, value-adding use of system features would improve the functional fit and subsequently enhance organizational benefits [31, 56, 59]. However, ES involves multiple users, thus requiring users to communicate with and transfer the new knowledge about innovative use of ES features to others [39, 60]. Moreover, institutionalizing innovative ways of applying the system may be met with resistance [10, 36]. Overcoming organizational inertia plays a critical role in turning the results of users’ ES feature exploration into organizational benefits [56]. Undoubtedly, an important direction for future research is the investigation of how motivations of different stakeholders affect the institutionalization of newly discovered ways of using the system to enhance the integration and access of information, thereby reaping organizational benefits [56].

Looking beyond, future research might transcend user exploration of ES features, and investigate how exploratory usage is interrelated with other usage behaviors such as exploitative or deep structure usage [13]. According to Jasperson et al. [31], postadoptive behaviors are classified into three types: individual feature adoption decision, individual feature use, and individual feature extension. Routine individual feature use enables users to gain in-depth knowledge of individual features, which may trigger feature exploration, a requisite part of feature extension. By the same token, exploration behavior leads to a more helpful routine use of system features [31]. A balance between exploration and exploitation is critical for long-term performance [13]; hence, determining how the exploratory and exploitative usage of system features affects each other and jointly support users’ job tasks represents an interesting topic for future research.

## Theoretical Implications

Our study has four key theoretical contributions. First, unlike other studies on user motivation to explore large-scale system features, such as the work by Ko et al. [39] and Li and Hsieh [41], we examined the nuances of intrinsic motivation. Previous studies have conceptualized intrinsic motivation as a single component construct (i.e., as a hedonic motivation). Consequently, these studies have overlooked how different types of intrinsic motivation differentially affect users’ system exploration [25]. Our classification of intrinsic motivation into task-related and social components (i.e., intrinsic normative and hedonic motivation) enriches the understanding of the construct of intrinsic motivation for system exploration. In particular, the current study extends the literature by highlighting the possibility that the management can spark users intrinsic motivation, aside from making system exploration more enjoyable.

Second, although user feature exploration is the precursor to achieving an organization’s strategic goal of ES adoption [31], the extant IS literature seldom goes beyond studying the factors that affect user intention to explore a system, to actual exploratory usage [4, 41]. As such, factors that may enhance the motivation of employees to proactively explore and identify system features that best support their job tasks are often overlooked [8, 13]. The creativity literature suggests that without intrinsic motivation, capability cannot be transferred into the performance of innovative activities [6]. By theorizing and empirically examining the antecedents and consequences of users intrinsic motivation for ES feature exploration, the present study extends the current understanding of the critical factor for ES implementation success. To this end, we explore how intrinsic motivation would cause both cognitive behavior and affective outcomes of ES feature exploration. The postadoptive stage is a long period in which ES values accrue; thus, it is critical that users achieve positive affective feelings toward ES feature exploration to enable them to remain engaged and continuously learn about the better use of the system. However, research is generally lacking on the affective consequences of system diffusion, particularly on large-scale system feature exploration, but is nevertheless important as suggested by the current study [53]. Such an explicit consideration of affective response is enduringly emphasized in other disciplines, such as management and psychology [19]. As indicated by our results, a high level of intrinsic normative motivation would not lead to exploratory usage. However, such motivation enhances an individual’s satisfaction with the exploration process. Thus, if we ignore the affective outcomes of system exploration, we might erroneously conclude that intrinsic normative motivation would not affect an individual’s feature exploration. Hence, this study not only provides a more in-depth understanding of the interrelationships between intrinsic motivations and IT innovative use but also contributes to our understanding of how intrinsic motivation and cognitive behavior are related to user satisfaction in the process of system diffusion.

Third, this research extends our understanding of the possible organizational interventions that can enhance a user’s intrinsic motivation to proactively explore ES features. Previous studies on technology diffusion have mainly focused on the effects of technology-centric perceptions [43, 65]. Their research findings provide limited guidelines for ES diffusion since technology-centric perceptions such as perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use cannot drive users to proactively explore ES features. The present study investigates possible organizational levers that the management can apply to enhance users’ intrinsic motivation to explore ES features, thus distinguishing the current research from other studies on users’ motivations to innovate with IT [41]. By incorporating organizational factors into the research model, our research enriches the IT diffusion literature and provides new insights into how the management can enhance employees’ intrinsic motivation to explore system features and thus align their interest with the organization’s strategic goals of adopting ES.

Fourth, this study introduces the concept of regulatory focus (prevention focus in particular) to IS research, thus showing that individual differences in motivational systems may lead to variations in the effectiveness of organizational levers to motivate individuals. In so doing, we provide a more precise and complete view of the ES implementation situation. Many organizational and IS adoption theories were developed in the West and have been used in their entirety without much adaption in studies in an Asian context. The examination of prevention focus in this research offers a more nuanced view of how organizational interventions could affect intrinsic motivation, and demonstrates the need for adaption of such theories when applied in an eastern (in this case, China) context.

## Practical Implications

Our study has two practical implications. First, our findings suggest that ES-adopting organizations could enhance users’ intrinsic motivation to explore ES features through organizational interventions. Organizations could spark users’ intrinsic hedonic and normative motivation to explore ES features by allowing job autonomy and applying socialization tactics, respectively. Our study reveals that intrinsic hedonic and normative motivations have differential positive effects on users’ exploration behavior outcomes. Intrinsic hedonic motivation affects both exploratory usage and satisfaction, whereas normative motivation only influences users’ satisfaction. As such, by increasing job autonomy, organizations may enhance users’ exploratory system usage and satisfaction with the exploration process. In addition, by elevating normative motivation, socialization tactics can be applied to enhance user satisfaction, which would ultimately render users’ ES exploration process sustainable.

Second, when applying organizational levers, organizations need to be aware of the negative effect that prevention focus has on the effects of job autonomy on intrinsic hedonic motivation. In particular, to enhance users’ intrinsic motivation to explore ES features, the organization benefits by providing users with appropriate training that would temporarily discourage the initiation of prevention focus, which would subsequently alter employee attitudes toward the task of ES feature exploration. For instance, the management in a high collectivism culture might attempt to offset the effects of prevention focus by reducing employee anxieties about making mistakes through promoting a more open and risk-tolerant organizational culture or sharing stories with the employees that motivate personal achievements [24]. Consequently, the management would deemphasize prevention focus, and employees would exhibit a stronger inclination to enjoy greater autonomy in exploring system features.

## Conclusion

This st udy exam ined how job aut onom y and s oc ializat ion t act ics enhance individuals intrinsic hedonic and normative motivations, respectively, and how intrinsic motivation affects their ES feature exploration outcomes in terms of exploratory usage and satisfaction with the exploration process. Furthermore, we discovered how an individual’s prevention focus moderates the effects of organizational levers on intrinsic motivations. Beyond demonstrating how organizational users respond to different organizational levers, this research addresses a broader, enduring challenge of how to induce employees to be intrinsically inspired to harness complex IS systems such as ES for organizational purposes.
