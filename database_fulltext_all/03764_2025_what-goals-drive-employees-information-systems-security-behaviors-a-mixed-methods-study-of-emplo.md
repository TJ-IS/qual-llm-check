# What Goals Drive Employees’ Information Systems Security Behaviors? A Mixed Methods Study of Employees’ Goals in the Workplace

Sebastian W. Schuetz,<sup>1</sup> Heiko Gewald,<sup>2</sup> Allen C. Johnston,<sup>3</sup> Jason Bennett Thatcher<sup>4</sup>

## Abstract

IT security remains high on the agenda of CIOs, with employees’ adoption of security behaviors— behaviors that employees adopt to protect organizational IT assets—being a top concern. To explain when employees adopt security behaviors, the information systems security (ISec) literature has mainly employed deductive studies that draw on theory-based assumptions about goals—mostly from behavioral theories used in criminology and public health (e.g., avoiding sanctions, avoiding harm from threats, avoiding disapproval and blame). However, as these theories typically do not theorize about employees’ goals specific to the workplace, they offer limited insights into the goals that employees pursue at work. Subsequently, not much is known about the goals that motivate employees’ security behaviors at work. Against this backdrop, this research provides a complementary, inductive-first inquiry into the work-related goals that drive employees’ security behaviors. Using a qualitative-quantitative mixed methods research design, we identify four goals (Study 1) and evaluate their importance for predicting employee security behaviors (Study 2). Overall, we find evidence that employees’ work performance and blame avoidance goals are the most salient predictors of security behaviors; as a result, our findings suggest that employees engage in security behaviors primarily because they believe it will help them meet supervisors’ expectations—a key goal that has been largely ignored in the previous ISec literature.

Keywords: Security Behaviors, Goal Systems Theory (GST), Work Performance, Blame Avoidance, Organizational Citizenship, Career Advancement

David Gefen was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on May 28, 2023, and underwent two revisions.

## 1 Introduction

IT security is a major concern for organizations around the world. According to the Society for Information Management’s annual survey of C-suite executives, IT security has ranked as the most important IT management issue for the past five years in a row (Kappelman et al., 2022). The perceived dangers associated with IT security are driven by real losses estimated to be now in the tens of trillions (Morgan, 2022). Accordingly, insurers have been warning not only big corporations but also small and medium-sized businesses to pay attention to the risks posed by IT security incidents (e.g., Hiscox, 2022). Thus, IT security risks now attract attention from executives at all types and sizes of businesses around the world.

The weakest link in the corporate chain of security defense is often the human factor, as employees’ negligent behaviors related to the secure handling of IT systems and assets are a common first point of entry for cyberattacks (Balozian & Leidner, 2017; Chen et al.,

2012; Siponen & Vance, 2010). Therefore, explaining when employees adopt security behaviors, behaviors that protect organizational IT assets, is a key interest in information systems security (ISec) research (e.g., Feng et al., 2019; Mou et al., 2022; Schuetz et al., 2021; Vedadi & Warkentin, 2020).<sup>1</sup>

To explain employees’ security behavior at work, prior ISec research has contextualized a rich set of non-workspecific reference theories. Among the most frequently adopted theories are deterrence theory, protective motivation theory, and neutralization theory (cf. Cram et al., 2017). What these and other reference theories share is the assumption that human behavior is goal directed (Austin & Vancouver, 1996), with each theory proposing a different goal. For example, deterrence theory proposes an avoidance goal by suggesting that criminals are motivated to avoid punishment and sanctions (Gibbs, 1975). Similarly, protection motivation theory suggests that individuals want to avoid harms to their health (Maddux & Rogers, 1983; Rogers, 1975), and neutralization theory suggests that delinquents wish to avoid social disapproval and blame (Sykes & Matza, 1957). Cumulatively, these theories provide considerable insight into the goals that drive employees’ security behaviors (e.g., sanction avoidance, harm avoidance, disapproval and blame avoidance).

These important insights notwithstanding, there has interestingly been little examination of work-related goals, that is, the goals that employees strive to achieve at work. This research gap is largely due to the literature’s predominant reliance on theories that theorize about non-work behaviors (such as criminal actions, health-relevant activities, and juvenile delinquency) and the goals that drive them. But given that these theories originate from non-work contexts, they may not capture the contextual goals that employees pursue at work. Thus, despite prior work that considers the influence of role-related expectations (e.g., Moody et al., 2018), little is known about the goals that employees pursue at work and how these goals relate to employees’ security behaviors. However, given that the security behaviors of interest all take place at work, examining the work goals that employees pursue when they perform security behaviors is an important yet understudied research opportunity.

To shed fresh light on the work-related goals that motivate security behaviors, we complement the predominantly deductive, theory-first literature with an inductive-first inquiry into the goals that drive employees’ security behaviors at work. In doing so, we were able to identify work-related goals and estimate their importance for predicting security behaviors. To connect work-related goals and security behaviors, we developed a framework based on goals systems theory (GST) (Kruglanski et al., 2002) and evaluated the framework using a sequential mixed methods design involving two studies (Venkatesh et al., 2013).

In Study 1 (qualitative), we interviewed employees to identify work-related goals that drive security behaviors. This allowed us to identify four common goals that were shared by the majority of participants: career advancement goals (getting promoted), work performance subgoals (meeting supervisor performance expectations), organizational citizenship subgoals (being seen as a good employee and colleague), and blame avoidance subgoals (avoiding being blamed for a security incident). Participants also identified security culture as an important, relevant factor influencing the extent to which security behaviors and these goals matter to them. Building on these findings, in Study 2 (quantitative), we evaluated the power of the alignment between the subgoals and security behaviors to predict employee security behaviors using a highly controlled survey of 1,250 employees. Our findings suggest that work performance subgoal alignment is the most salient predictor of employee security behavior and that blame avoidance subgoal alignment is of secondary importance. In a post hoc analysis, we further demonstrate that security culture, in particular, regulates the extent to which security behaviors are aligned with the pursuit of these subgoals.

These insights complement the predominantly deductive ISec literature by identifying new goals (i.e., work performance, career advancement), validating one existing goal that has been derived from reference theories (i.e., blame avoidance), introducing the concept of goal alignment (rooted in GST) as a new lens to understand employees’ security behaviors, and revealing goal alignment to be a product of security culture, mediating its effects. These findings offer specific new implications for existing theory: We find that (1) future work should consider employees’ career goals to understand their ultimate motivations to perform security behaviors; (2) there is a need to integrate career and work performance constructs in our models of security behavior (e.g., Cram et al., 2019; Moody et al., 2018), as such constructs are currently missing but are highly predictive of behavior; and (3) the concept of goal alignment captures a critical mechanism through which security culture shapes employees security behaviors.

## 2 Background

The ISec literature uses several theories to explain security behaviors, including the theory of reasoned action (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975), the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen, 1985, 1991), protection motivation theory (Maddux & Rogers, 1983; Rogers, 1975), general deterrence theory (Gibbs, 1975), and rational choice theory (Simon, 1955). Cram et al. (2017) and Moody et al. (2018) provide comprehensive summaries of the various reference theories used in the ISec literature (Table 1 provides an exemplary overview). These theories share the assumption that behavior is goal driven. This common assumption also underpins some of the most widely used theories of behavior in broader IS research, including rational choice theory (Simon, 1955) and other derivative theories such as the theory of trying (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005).

Consequently, as shown in Table 1, the theories often used in ISec research make assumptions about behavioral goals. We have already examined general deterrence theory, protection motivation theory, and neutralization theory as examples. Other examples, as mentioned, include rational choice theory, which assumes that the most lucrative behaviors are chosen based on the goal of maximizing payoffs (Simon, 1955), and organizational commitment theory, which assumes that employee behavior is driven by the goal of staying with one’s organization (Meyer & Allen, 1997). Many ISec studies that apply these theories have shown that these goals are useful to make predictions about employees’ security behaviors.

Notwithstanding the insights gained from these theories, the full range of goals that employees pursue at work that may influence security behaviors remain understudied. The reason for this is that existent theories are too generic (i.e., they formulate generic goals—e.g., behaviors are performed to attain desirable outcomes) or too specific to a problem domain (e.g., avoid threats to one’s health) to fully capture the context-specific goals that influence employees’ security behaviors at work. Consequently, despite the multitude of studies examining employees’ security behaviors in workplace settings (e.g., Balozian et al., 2023; Schuetz et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023), little is known about the goals that employees pursue when they perform security behaviors at work.

## 3 Mixed Methods Research Design

To identify relevant workplace goals and estimate their relationship to security behaviors, we employed a sequential mixed methods design (Creswell & Clark, 2017; Creswell & Creswell, 2017). Accordingly, we first used a qualitative method (semi-structured interviews) to identify goals that are relevant to employees’ security behaviors in the workplace. Our approach follows prior approaches in the reference disciplines to identify goals (e.g., Bagozzi & Dholakia, 1999). Then, we estimated the importance of each goal using a quantitative method (survey). While a qualitative study might have been sufficient to identify goals and make inferences about their influences, our objective was also to quantify the relative importance of each goal in predicting employees’ security behaviors. Thus, we conducted a quantitative study to formalize our propositions, test them, and validate their generalizability through a large, randomly drawn sample (Kelle, 2006). Overall, our mixed methods approach allowed us to confirm and corroborate findings across studies (Venkatesh et al., 2013, 2016).

## 3.1 A General Theory of Behavior: Goal Systems Theory

To understand the role of goals in determining security behaviors, we built on GST as a guiding framework. In GST, goals refer to internal representations of desired states, which can be broadly construed as outcomes (Austin & Vancouver, 1996). In particular, GST theorizes about goal systems and thus supports a comprehensive explanation of behavior based on the simultaneous pursuit of multiple goals. Specifically, according to GST, people consciously or unconsciously pursue multiple goals at any given time, and these goals are interrelated in a system that determines their behavior (Kopetz et al., 2012; Kruglanski et al., 2002). GST predicts that people take action to achieve goals, and that the specific action taken is determined by the number of goals that can be achieved with that action, the desirability of the goals, and the feasibility of achieving them (Kopetz et al., 2012; Kruglanski et al., 2002). Thus, we chose GST, as it provides a sound theoretical framework for understanding the link between goals and behavior.

In GST, goal systems consist of goals and goal means. Table 2 provides definitions and examples of the entities and their properties of interest. As noted above, goals refer to internal representations of desired states, which can be broadly construed as outcomes (Austin & Vancouver, 1996), such as an employee’s desired state of meeting a supervisor’s expectations. Goal means refer to behaviors that achieve goals (Kruglanski et al., 2002). For example, completing security training could serve as a goal means for meeting a supervisor’s expectations.

Understanding how a goal system shapes behavior requires understanding two essential characteristics. First, the motivational characteristic, referred to as goal commitment, captures an individual’s determination to pursue goals within a goal system (Kruglanski et al., 2002)—for example, the extent to which one is determined to meet supervisor expectations. Second, structural characteristics, also referred to as associations, capture cognitive links between goals and between goals and means (Kruglanski et al., 2002)—for example, the extent to which completing security training is seen as linked to meeting supervisor expectations. In Study 1, we identify the goals for which security behaviors serve as goal means.

In goal systems, goals exist within a hierarchy. At the highest level are higher-order goals, which provide purpose or meaning to lower-level subgoals and determine their level of pursuit (Austin & Vancouver, 1996; Kruglanski et al., 2002). Higher-order goals cannot be achieved through a single action but instead require the achievement of multiple subgoals (Kopetz et al., 2012; Kruglanski et al., 2002). For example, meeting supervisors’ expectations might be a subgoal of a higher-order goal such as receiving a promotion. Likewise, receiving a promotion could be a subgoal of another higher-order life goal, e.g., being able to buy a house by earning more money. Because the level of the pursuit of a higher-order goal transfers to and thereby reinforces the goal pursuit of associated subgoals (Shah & Kruglanski, 2002), someone who wants to buy a house would likely be driven to earn a promotion and would therefore work harder to meet supervisors expectations and might be more motivated to complete security training to do so. Thus, higher-order goals are broad, general, and stable goals that are common across individuals and provide answers to “why” questions (Austin & Vancouver, 1996; Bagozzi & Dholakia, 1999; Barrick et al., 2013; DeShon & Gillespie, 2005).

Research on goal systems typically focuses on a particular level of goals, selecting the one most relevant to the research question (Austin & Vancouver, 1996). At the lowest levels, goals are highly situational and idiosyncratic (Kruglanski et al., 2002) and are typically related to projects or day-to-day activities (Unsworth et al., 2014). As such, they do not lend themselves to efforts to develop a generalizable account of employees’ goals. Therefore, we focus on goals that are common to all employees and thus focus on higher levels within the work context.

Associations between goals can be positive (facilitative) or negative (inhibitory). When associations are positive, they facilitate the transfer of commitment between goals, such that a commitment to one goal is reinforced by a commitment to a facilitative associated goal (i.e., a higher-order goal or another goal at the same level). For example, if an employee is trying to meet their manager’s expectations and is also trying to learn about cybersecurity, then completing security training would satisfy both goals; the commitment to the two goals would reinforce each other and increase the motivation to perform the goal means (i.e., complete the training). However, this transfer of commitment depends on the strength of the association (Kopetz et al., 2012; Kruglanski et al., 2002).

In contrast, when associations are negative, goal conflict arises. Goals in inhibitory associations compete for cognitive resources (Kruglanski et al., 2002; Shah et al., 2002; Shah & Kruglanski, 2002). For example, suppose an employee is motivated to complete a security training session at the end of the workday to meet their supervisor’s expectations, but also wants to leave work early to spend time with their family. In this case, conflict arises when the achievement of one goal is seen as incompatible with the achievement of another (Kruglanski et al., 2002). As a result, goal commitments compete, and the employee will perform the goal means associated with the goal to which they are more strongly committed. Understanding employees’ security behaviors from a goal systems perspective thus necessitates identifying the goals for which security behaviors serve as a goal means and their associations.

## 4 Study 1: A Qualitative Inquiry into the Goals that Drive Employees’ Security Behaviors

To first identify the work-related goals that drive employees’ security behaviors, we adopted a phenomenological research approach (Creswell & Creswell, 2017; Marshall & Rossman, 1989) and focused on distilling the essence (i.e., common goals) shared across individual experiences (Langbridge, 2007). Thus, our analysis “proceeds from the central assumption that there is an essence to an experience<sup>2</sup> that is shared with others who have also had that experience” (Marshall & Rossman, 1989, p. 12). Because this essence is, by definition, common and thus shared by many individuals, identifying it typically requires few participants.<sup>3</sup> As noted by Creswell and Creswell (2017), identifying commonalities in individuals’ experiences typically requires interviewing between 3 and 10 relevant participants to reach theoretical saturation, i.e., the point at which new data collections no longer reveal new insights.

## 4.1 Research Design: Participants and Procedure

We recruited 14 participants for semi-structured interviews through a network of firms affiliated with the authors’ universities. Participants were purposively selected to share the common experience of working in jobs that require using computers and handling organizational information. Fourteen participants were recruited from eight different organizations in two different countries, including a major retailer, a major food manufacturer, a prominent fashion retailer, two major professional services firms, a midsized software company, and a university hospital. Four of these organizations were Fortune 500 companies. Participants’ tenure at their respective organizations ranged from 1 to 22 years, with a mean (median) of approximately 6.5 (3.5) years. Participants’ mean (median) age was approximately 39 (38), and about 43% were male. Participants’ positions included two CIOs, a managing director, a database administrator, a sourcing analyst, a user experience designer, a legal counsel, a data analyst, a marketing manager, a director of accounting and controls, an academic alliance manager, a certified public accountant, a chief nursing officer, and a charge nurse. The participants represented a wide range of work experience. No compensation was provided.

We used a script of predefined questions that allowed for follow-up questions to guide our interviews (see Appendix A). All participants agreed to the stated privacy policy and confirmed that their interviews could be recorded and transcribed. The transcriptions were anonymized, and individual transcripts were shared with their respective participant upon request. All but one participant agreed to be quoted. All interviews were conducted by two researchers, either face-to-face or online. Our script included broad, open-ended questions designed to elicit rich descriptions (Moustakas, 1994; Smith & Osborn, 2003). GST informed the development of our script, resulting in questions designed to elicit participants’ higher-order work goals, supporting and conflicting subgoals, and ancillary factors that might influence the associations between these goals and security behaviors. These questions served as a guide to ensure that we covered all the bases. Interviews lasted approximately 45 minutes. They were conducted sequentially, meaning that we recorded, transcribed, and analyzed each interview before moving on to the next. The questions were refined iteratively after each interview. No new goals or subgoals emerged after the ninth interview. We conducted additional interviews to confirm theoretical saturation (Strauss & Corbin, 1994), resulting in 14 interviews.

## 4.2 Analysis

Our analysis of the interview data identified goals relevant to security behaviors common among participants. We analyzed the data using in vivo coding (Miles & Huberman, 1994). In this process, data (words and phrases) from the participant’s own language were annotated with codes, i.e., labels, that were assigned symbolic meanings. Our GST framework provided general codes (e.g., higher-order goal, subgoal). We sought to identify goals specific to the workplace context and thus looked for common themes among the codes in this respect (Moustakas, 1994). This allowed for the emergence of subcodes that further specified these codes (e.g., higher-order goal—career advancement) (Creswell & Creswell, 2017). These subcodes thus represented the core themes of participants’ experiences (Moustakas, 1994), structured through our theoretical lens. The principal investigator conducted the first round of coding. When new subcodes emerged, earlier transcripts were reviewed in light of the new theme. After all the interviews were coded, a second researcher independently coded the interviews using the final codebook. This allowed us to assess interrater reliability using Cohen’s kappa, which was 0.64. Values between 0.61 and 0.80 indicate substantial agreement (Fleiss et al., 2003).

To identify the common goals shared among most participants, we used counting. Counting in qualitative research identifies what is important and recurrent and protects against bias (Miles & Huberman, 1994). Thus, we counted the number of participants that mentioned each goal to identify the most common goals. Table 3 shows the goals shared by the majority of participants.

## 4.3 Results

Higher-order goals: We started our interviews by discussing the individual higher-order goals of our participants. The most frequently mentioned goal was career advancement, which refers to participants’ pursuit of career objectives, such as promotion and higher management positions. For example, Participant 2 (P2) explained that in his company, there is “an 18-month cycle for promotions. And if the leadership feels that a person is qualified, they do give their due promotions. So, my goal is to achieve that promotion.” P9, who worked in an accounting firm, suggested that her ultimate goal was to “be a partner one day.” P13, who already had managerial responsibility for a business division, stated that “[his] career goal is to attain an exciting operational responsibility.” Thus, the most common answer to what participants wanted to achieve at work was career advancement. Hence, we conceptualize career advancement as a higher-order goal because it relates to employees’ long-term objectives.

We note that many of our participants reported not viewing security as particularly relevant for achieving their goal of career advancement. Of the 11 participants who commented on career advancement, only five stated that security was important, whereas the others stated that security was either irrelevant to their career advancement or not a significant factor. For example, P3 stated: “It doesn’t have a direct impact on my promotion.” This sentiment was shared by other participants as well. P1 explained that “[security] is not something that people really look at” when it comes to making promotion decisions. Regarding the relevance of security, P4 stated: “to the extent that what my goal is, is to practice legal work, not so much [relevant].” P6 concluded that security “is not very important and … has really no impact on what I do.” Likewise, P14 responded that she could not imagine a scenario in which security would positively or negatively affect her career. P12 agreed:

It’s like personal hygiene: nobody notices when you apply deodorant—only if you don’t. There is nothing to gain for doing security well. But if there [is an incident], then there are consequences. No one gets rewards from the CIO for not causing an incident. It’s the expectation.

Therefore, security behaviors did not seem to be directly linked to career advancement. Accordingly, we conceptualized it as a higher-order goal and sought to further identify potential subgoals that participants pursued that would create an indirect link between security behaviors and the higher-order goal of career advancement.

Subgoals: Participants mentioned various subgoals linked to career advancements and to security behaviors. The most frequently mentioned subgoal in our interviews concerned work performance, referring to participants’ ability to meet their supervisor’s performance expectations. Participants saw a clear connection between performing well at work and advancing in their careers:

Mostly, my promotion is evaluated based on my performance. Mostly, it works like, you know, performance on the work itself as well as my relationship with the clients, like have I ever received any complaints? Did I meet the deadlines? Did I develop [a] good relationship with my team? Did I contribute something to the firm? (P9)

As P5 explained, good work performance helps employees attract management’s attention, and “individual workers want to stand out the most as this is how a person will get promoted.” Likewise, P9 stated that security behaviors fall within the expected behaviors at their firm:

It’s part of the job; you know that this is what they expect from you, and everyone has a role in protecting the data they handle. I mean, if, for example, information is leaked because of me, it would affect my performance as well.

We henceforth refer to this objective as work performance and conceptualize it as a subgoal for achieving career advancement. We were not surprised to see a work performance goal emerge, as previous literature has already pointed to potential conflicts between performing security behaviors and achieving work objectives (Karjalainen et al., 2019; Puhakainen & Siponen, 2010). However, we were surprised that this was the most frequently mentioned goal, as work performance has remained a peripheral topic in the ISec literature.

Consistent with prior work, seven participants expressed that performing security behaviors sometimes conflicted with achieving work performance goals. P6 stated that he only performs preventative security behaviors “if it doesn’t impact my workflow for mandatory everyday things.” P7 explained that “[violating policy] is a lesser risk than not fulfilling our team goals that ultimately determine our bonus.” P3 agreed and stated that “[security] slows down the process, and time is money.” P10, a CIO, suggested that people in his organization frequently excuse not following security protocols by claiming they are too busy with other things. Taken together, these findings confirm that meeting work performance goals can conflict with performing security behaviors.

However, a sizeable number of participants suggested the opposite, meaning that they felt that performing security behaviors helped them achieve work performance objectives. This surprised us, as we had assumed otherwise, given that prior studies have mainly pointed to the conflict between security and work (e.g., Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Karjalainen et al., 2019). P4 explained that strong security guidelines “make it easier. All I need to know is what do you need me to do so that the whole network stays secure.” P5 agreed, stating, “I think [preventative security behaviors] are very beneficial. So even though we think, ‘Okay. It’s taking time and everything,’ but it’s better for the future and the future of the company and the individual.” Similarly, when asked whether security ever conflicted with her work as an auditor, P9 replied: “It’s not a lot and I mean after a while you just get used to it, so I don’t think it interferes with my work.” The notion that many security behaviors have already been habituated was expressed by P8, who concluded that she did not feel any conflict between meeting security expectations and performing her work. In summary, the evidence suggests that some employees view security behaviors as having an inhibitory relationship with work performance goals, while others view this relationship as facilitative.

The second most frequently mentioned subgoal associated with career objectives concerns organizational citizenship, which refers to participants’ desires to be seen as good employees and colleagues by members of their organization. As P4 explained, “for a long tenure at [Fortune 100 company], it helps being a good employee, getting promotions, and basically just being seen as a good colleague.” This notion closely aligns with organizational citizenship, which reflects “what a good employee ought to do” (Smith et al., 1983, p. 67) and includes employees’ obedience and respect for rules, instructions, and the stewardship of organizational resources (Podsakoff et al., 2000). We henceforth refer to this concept as organizational citizenship.

Participants consistently mentioned seeing a clear connection between organizational citizenship and security behaviors. P2 explained, “I think a good employee would always be very mindful of security policies.” P1 agreed that being a good employee requires preventing security threats, stating, “I think it is part of my job to protect the data of my organization.” Likewise, P5 stated: “I mean, as a person, as an employee, I think it’s important. It helps the company as well as the individual.” P12 also shared this sentiment: “Most people just want to do a good job, aren’t querulous, and do the best they can [in terms of security] in the scope of their abilities.” Thus, our findings reveal that organizational citizenship is a subgoal that is facilitatively associated with security behaviors.

The third most common subgoal that emerged was blame avoidance, i.e., the desire to avoid being blamed by peers and superiors for security incidents, which was mentioned by eight participants. As P3 explained, performing security behaviors is generally not seen as related to career advancement, but being blamed for a security incident would undoubtedly have a negative impact on his career:

I would say [security] doesn’t have a direct impact [on promotion]. If you make a security mistake, it is going to impact you. But otherwise, I wouldn’t say that it was seen as one of the things that has to be known to go up.

P6 shared that sentiment:

[Security] is not something that people really pay much attention to. I mean, I handle a lot of [customer] email addresses. If anything goes wrong, then, yeah, people will look at me. But I don’t see it being a problem as of today.

P7 added that this would only be an issue if something could be attributed to her:

[Only] if it’s actually super clear that it can be traced back to me, I think, yes. So, if it really affects the whole company and everyone gets aware of that, it would be pretty severe. However, for those smaller incidents …, no one really cares. As long as it’s not affecting the business or customer satisfaction and things that we measure, then [management] doesn’t really see it.

As P4 put it, no one wants to be seen “as the weakest link” responsible for a severe data breach, as that would likely affect their goal of career advancement. Importantly, none of the participants was worried about specific sanctions but rather about a generic negative impact on their careers (Straub & Nance, 1990). Since participants reported that they were concerned about being blamed for a security incident, which was a critical step in achieving their higher-level career advancement goal, we refer to this goal as blame avoidance.

Participants characterized performing security behaviors as having a facilitative association with blame avoidance. As P5 explained, “it is better to be safe and comply with the rules. If you don’t follow the guidelines, you might become responsible for a security incident, and if you do that, you will face the backlash.” Similarly, P4 shared the following anecdote to illustrate the importance of following security protocols to avoid unwanted consequences:

Noncompliance can cost me the opportunity to work here. When I first came to work here, there was a lawyer on staff who had circumvented the infrastructure and had exposed confidential information outside of the organization. She was walked out once this was discovered.

We, therefore, identify security behaviors as being facilitative to blame avoidance.

Across the participants’ answers, security culture was a frequently cited explanation for why security was not seen as particularly relevant. For example, P1 explained that security is not taken very seriously: “colleagues writing down password on notes, etc., is a very normal culture here. I’ve seen it a lot and I’ve done it myself too.” P7 agreed, explaining that:

We have a team that is trying to establish a security culture. However, aside [from] them, I don’t think that my boss and most of the people in my company would care too much about it whether I follow security policies or not.

Conceptually, security culture refers to the norms, values, and beliefs about security shared by the individuals in an organization (Chen et al., 2015). Therefore, participants’ beliefs about the importance of security behaviors for the accomplishment of various goals may reflect the security culture at a workplace. In other words, the extent to which security is seen as relevant to these goals captures not just individual beliefs about security but also the security culture at work.

## 5 Study 2: Quantitative Investigation

To confirm the results of our qualitative investigation and estimate the power of each goal in predicting security behaviors, we conducted a large-scale survey of prequalified respondents. This allowed us to formalize our findings from Study 1, quantitatively assess their validity, and achieve greater generalizability with more control using a large, randomized sample of participants (Kelle, 2006).

## 5.1 Model Development

To understand how the identified goals can predict employees’ security behaviors, we again drew on GST. To recap, GST suggests that there are two properties of a goal system that determine which goal means is enacted: (1) goal commitments, which refer to the extent to which one is determined to pursue a goal (Kruglanski et al., 2002), and (2) associations, which capture the strength of linkage between goals and goal means (Kopetz et al., 2012; Kruglanski et al., 2002). Figure 1 depicts a conceptual map of an employee’s goal system, which we used to make our predictions. Our focus is on the associations (labeled alignment) because they link security behaviors to the identified subgoals. Thus, commitment to these goals served as a basic premise of our predictions. In other words, employees must be committed (determined) to pursuing career advancement goals and, subsequently, work performance, organizational citizenship, and blame avoidance goals for these goals to play a role in their behavior. However, how these goals influence behavior depends on their alignment.

Consequently, in our research model, we focused on alignment and statistically controlled for commitment to these goals in our subsequent analysis. As depicted in Figure 2, our model thus suggests that perceived alignments between security behaviors and these subgoals can predict security behaviors. Because the associations underlying alignments are highly contextual, learned, and influenced by the environment (Kruglanski et al., 2002), we also controlled for the influence of organizational factors related to security and the work environment in which employees work (hereafter referred to as workplace environment factors). Finally, to fully isolate the effects of subgoal alignment, we further controlled for individual demographics, work characteristics, and firm characteristics.

## 5.2 Alignment Between Subgoals and Security Behaviors

Assuming that employees are motivated to achieve the subgoals of work performance, organizational citizenship, and blame avoidance, whether they perform security behaviors will depend on the associations between these subgoals and the security behaviors. Since the strength of the association determines whether goal commitment leads to the performance of security behaviors (Kruglanski et al., 2002; Shah et al., 2002), the association strength between security behaviors and these subgoals (hereafter referred to as alignment) is critical in determining whether security behaviors will be performed. For example, if the alignment between security behaviors and work performance goals is high, employees will view security behaviors as instrumental to achieving their work performance goals. Under such circumstances, commitment to the work performance subgoal will be transferred and motivate the performance of security behaviors. In contrast, when alignment is low, the commitment to a goal—one’s motivation to pursue it—will not transfer to the misaligned goal means. In consequence, the misalignment between security behaviors and goals leads to low motivation to perform these security behaviors.

Notably, associations are additive, such that the alignment with more subgoals will increase the motivation to perform security behaviors because achieving more goals increases the perceived value of performing security behaviors (Kopetz et al., 2012; Kruglanski et al., 2002). More goal associations and stronger goal associations lead to more commitment to a given mean, which makes it easier for individuals to self-regulate and enact that mean over others (Bandura, 1991). Therefore, we expect that the alignment between security behaviors and each of the three subgoals will predict security behaviors.

H1: Employees will perform security behaviors when they perceive that performing these behaviors will help them achieve work performance goals.

H2: Employees will perform security behaviors when they perceive that performing these behaviors will help them achieve organizational citizenship goals.

H3: Employees will perform security behaviors when they perceive that performing these behaviors will help them achieve blame avoidance goals.

## 5.3 Controls Related to the Workplace Environment

Several other factors related to the workplace environment can influence the alignment and the resulting performance of security behaviors. Based on our interviews and the ISec literature, we identified six influential factors that affect the alignment between employee goals and security behaviors. We refer to these as workplace environmental factors because they are beyond the control of employees. Because our focus is on alignment, we do not provide formal hypotheses for these factors. However, consistent with statistical control guidelines (Becker et al., 2016; Carlson & Wu, 2012), we define them below and justify their inclusion in our analysis.

The results of the first phase highlighted security culture as a critical factor. Participants frequently mentioned security culture as a reason why security behaviors were relevant to their goals. Furthermore, in organizations with a strong security culture, engaging in security behaviors can be an important way to be seen as a good citizen and meet supervisors’ expectations. Along these lines, prior work has shown that security culture can influence security behaviors (e.g., Bulgurcu et al., 2010;

Mikuletič et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). Therefore, it is important to include the influence of security culture in our model.

Next, an organization’s past data breaches—i.e., security incidents in which consumers’ personally identifiable information, such as their social security number, driver’s license number, name, and birthday, is exposed to unauthorized third parties (Romanosky et al., 2011)—may influence how serious employees take security and security behaviors. In response to data breaches, organizations ramp up security measures to learn from past failures and prevent future incidents (Kwon & Johnson, 2014). Thus, the occurrence of a data breach can trigger several managerial interventions, such as increased training, tightening of information security policies, resetting of managerial expectations regarding employee security behaviors, and perhaps increased monitoring or sanctions. Hence, we anticipate that the occurrence of a recent data breach will influence the alignment between employees’ work-related goals and security behaviors.

Organizations’ use of information security policies (ISPs), i.e., a “set of formalized procedures, guidelines, roles and responsibilities to which insiders are required to adhere to safeguard and use properly the information and technology resources of their organizations” (Lowry & Moody, 2015, p. 434), may influence goal associations. If ISPs are used within the organization and communicated to employees, employees may associate security behaviors with their work performance and blame avoidance subgoals because they define the formal expectations about employees behavior and the sanctions that will be imposed in response to policy violations (D’Arcy et al., 2009). They also define what a “good employee” should do. Therefore, we predict that ISPs will strengthen the link between security behaviors and organizational citizenship goals.

Also, organizations’ practice of imposing sanctions, referring to the use of disincentives (i.e., punishments) to enforce compliance with organizational information security policies (Straub, 1990), will likely influence the strength of the association between security behaviors and blame avoidance because being made responsible for a security incident is a prerequisite for sanctioning. Thus, the practice of sanctioning would be expected to increase one’s motivation to avoid being blamed for security incidents.

Finally, an organization’s security training, awareness, and education (SETA) programs likely influence associations, because they (1) disseminate knowledge about security threats, (2) explain countermeasures and remedies, (3) increase awareness of employees’ roles and responsibilities, and (4) detail possible sanctions for ISP violations (Posey et al., 2015). As such, SETA programs can be used as a tool for changing users’ behavior (Dey et al., 2021). We expect two aspects of SETA programs to be environmental factors and anticipate that the frequency of training and the frequency of awareness communications will influence the extent to which security behaviors align with employees’ subgoals.

## 5.4.1 Participants

We partnered with a professional market research firm that maintains an extensive, representative panel of prequalified German citizens to survey 1,250 participants. To sample the average worker, all participants met the following criteria: full-time employment, daily use of IT at work, having no or limited responsibility for managing employees themselves (less than five reports), being employed by firms with 50 or more employees, and having more than two years of work experience. ## 5.4.2 Measurement & Procedure

Goal commitment and association strength were measured through an adapted measure from Shah and Kruglanski (2002) and Hofmann et al. (2012). Security culture was measured using a scale from Knapp et al. (2007), and we adapted Posey et al.’s (2015) security behavior measure. We preferred a general behavioral measure over a specific behavioral measure due to the missingness of an agreed-upon measure of individual security behaviors that is reflective of the overall phenomenon. Therefore, in the absence of a validated measure, measuring a handpicked, specific behavior or set of behaviors could lead to an incomplete reflection of the phenomenon, which is broader than the specific behaviors (Hanisch et al., 1998; Hui & Triandis, 1985; Posey et al., 2013). Thus, we drew on the existing, validated measure of Posey et al. (2015) and translated the items into German. Other German scholars reviewed the translations and then back-translated them to ensure equivalence with the original items (Brislin, 1970). Appendix B reports our complete instrument.

## 5.5 Main Analysis

We tested our hypotheses regarding the effect of our subgoals on security behaviors using a hierarchical linear regression framework. Hierarchical linear regression frameworks help explain whether the primary research variables (alignment with the three subgoals) explain a statistically significant amount of variance after controlling for demographics, controls, and alternative factors.

Entering very strongly correlated variables into a regression model (a common rule of thumb is $r \geq 0 . 8 0 )$ can lead to less reliable coefficient estimates of affected variables (i.e., large standard errors) because too much variance between such variables may overlap (Mansfield & Helms, 1982). The subsequent risk of inflated confidence intervals may increase the chance of Type II errors (failure to reject the null hypothesis). To address this issue, we examined the variable’s variance-inflation factors (VIFs) and applied a conservative cutoff of 5.0 (a more common cutoff is 10.00, see Hair et al., 2006; O’Brien, 2007). Only age and work experience showed inflated variances (VIFs > 5.0); therefore, we dropped age from our subsequent analysis in favor of work experience and reported the maximum VIF values for each analysis, indicating no risk of collinearity.

## 5.6 Results

Table 5 shows our hierarchical linear regression results. Model 1 includes demographic variables only and explains the slight variance. Model 2 adds commitment to higher-order goals and subgoals. Model 3 adds environmental factors, and Model 4 adds subgoal alignments. Comparing Model 3 and Model 4, adding subgoal alignments explained significantly more variance than adding environmental factors, indicating the importance of these subgoals in explaining security behaviors. Comparing Model 2 and Model 4 reveals that subgoal alignments were better predictors than subgoal commitment, whose relationships with blame avoidance commitment and organizational citizenship commitment became insignificant in Model 4. In contrast, the relationship with work performance commitment was revealed as significant only after controlling for work performance alignment.

Model 5 adds environmental factors and subgoal alignments, rendering one environmental factor (sanctioning frequency) insignificant. Model 5 also explains the most variance. Based on Model 5, we present the following findings: First, work performance alignment had the largest, positive, significant coefficient, supporting H1. Second, organizational citizenship alignment had no significant coefficient, thus rejecting H2. Third, blame avoidance alignment had the second largest, positive, significant coefficient, supporting H3. Comparing the coefficients between subgoal alignments and environmental factors, we observed subgoal alignments to be the most important predictors of security behaviors.

## 5.7 Post Hoc Analysis

Our main analysis isolated the effects of subgoal alignment on security behavior. However, an alternative specification would examine workplace environmental factors, such as security culture, ISP, training frequency, etc., as antecedents to subgoal alignment. Such a specification can illuminate the extent to which these factors drive subgoal alignment and whether the concept of goal alignment, as a mechanism, explains the effect of these factors on security behaviors. Therefore, we tested this alternative specification in a post hoc analysis using bootstrapping mediation analysis (Zhao et al., 2010). In doing so, we specified a structural model in which each environmental factor had one direct and three indirect paths on security behaviors. The indirect paths were the paths mediated by the three subgoal alignment constructs. All other variables were otherwise modeled as direct effects on security behaviors, identically to our main analysis.

## 6 Discussion

The purpose of our study was to provide a different perspective on employee behavior by empirically arriving at the goals that drive employee security behaviors at work, rather than taking the approach common in the ISec literature of starting with theorybased assumptions about goals. To this end, we conducted interviews (Study 1) that identified career advancement as a higher-order goal indirectly related to security behaviors through work performance, organizational citizenship, and blame avoidance as subgoals. Our follow-up survey (Study 2) estimated the influence of these goals and found that work performance and blame avoidance were the most salient predictors of security behaviors. Next, we bridge these two sets of findings to draw metainferences (Creswell & Creswell, 2017; Venkatesh et al., 2013, 2016). Table 7 provides a summary of the resulting meta-inferences.

## 6.1 Meta-Inferences and Theoretical Implications

## 6.1.1 Meta-Inference 1: Career Advancement Goals Lend Purpose to Security Behaviors

Our qualitative findings suggest that many employees do not see a direct link between performing security behaviors and achieving career advancement goals. This is not surprising, as goal pursuit can often be unconscious, especially for higher-order and implicit goals (Austin & Vancouver, 1996; Bargh et al., 2001; Kruglanski et al., 2002). However, our findings indicate that the higher-order goal of career advancement plays an important role in motivating the various subgoals we identified and, through them, security behaviors. This was evident in the interviews in that participants stated that pursuing their subgoals could help their careers. Indeed, in our quantitative study, we observed moderate zero-order correlations between commitment to career advancement and commitment to two subgoals (organizational citizenship and work performance), supporting the role of career advancement as a plausible overarching goal. Similarly, while we observed a significant direct relationship between career advancement goals and security behaviors, the estimate was small compared to the role played by alignment with subgoals. Thus, we conclude that career advancement is likely a higher-order goal that motivates the pursuit of the subgoals of blame avoidance and work performance, thereby providing purpose and meaning to employees’ security behaviors.

## 6.1.2 Meta-Inference 2: Work Performance is a Key Determinant of Security Behaviors

Our findings are consistent regarding the relationship between work performance and security behaviors, as both qualitative and quantitative results suggest an important role for the relationship between security behaviors and work performance. Our qualitative results indicate that some employees view security behaviors as having an inhibitory relationship with work performance goals, while others view this relationship as facilitative. Indeed, our quantitative results show that high commitment to work performance is negatively associated with security behaviors. However, these effects were counteracted by alignment. Of all the estimates, the effect of alignment between work performance and security behaviors was the largest. Therefore, we conclude that work performance alignment is a critical determinant of security behaviors.

## 6.1.3 Meta-Inference 3: Organizational Citizenship Does Not Drive Security Behaviors

Our qualitative findings suggest that security behaviors are essential to participants’ organizational citizenship goals. In this sense, we also found a moderate zero-order correlation between organizational citizenship alignment and security behaviors. However, when controlling for other factors in our regression analysis, the role of organizational citizenship alignment was insignificant.

One likely reason for this statistical result is that organizational citizenship is highly correlated with alignment with other goals, particularly work performance. However, in a regression analysis, organizational citizenship alignment did not explain any additional variance in the dependent variable beyond that already explained by work performance. There is a theoretical justification for this finding: Associations between goals and means occur not only top-down (i.e., an employee has a goal and finds a means to achieve it) but also bottom-up (i.e., an employee performs a means and finds a goal to justify it) (Austin & Vancouver, 1996). Thus, it is plausible that when employees are asked why they engage in a security behavior, they may retrospectively cite organizational citizenship as a justification—perhaps because of its social desirability—even if the original behaviors were performed for other reasons. Here, our analysis shows that work performance and blame avoidance are better predictors of security behavior than expressions of commitment to the organization.

## 6.1.4 Meta-Inference 4: Employees Don’t Want to Receive Blame for Security Incidents

Our qualitative results identify blame avoidance as a key reason why employees engage in security behaviors. Our quantitative results support this finding, with blame avoidance alignment having a positive effect on security behaviors. Of all the estimates, the effect of blame avoidance alignment on security behaviors is the second largest. Therefore, we conclude that blame avoidance is another important determinant of security behavior.

## 6.1.5 Meta-Inference 5: Security Culture Aligns Security Behaviors to Subgoals

Participants in our qualitative study cited security culture as one of the reasons why security did or did not matter to them at work. Our quantitative results confirmed that security culture has a significant positive influence on their performance of security behaviors. Additionally, a post hoc analysis revealed that the impact of security culture is mediated by the alignment of work performance and blame avoidance. This suggests that the effect of security culture materializes by aligning security behaviors as a means to achieve these two subgoals. Therefore, we conclude that the concept of goal alignment is a mechanism through which security culture influences security behaviors.

## 6.2 Theoretical Contributions and Implications

Our main contributions are to the literature on security behaviors, which has adopted a host of goal-oriented theories to explain these behaviors (e.g., Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Johnston et al., 2015; Siponen & Vance, 2010). This prior work often adapts the theoretical assumptions about goals as drivers of security behaviors without considering whether these goals are relevant in the work context or to security behaviors. Departing from this approach, our study focused on identifying the goals that drive employees’ security behaviors at work. One significant contribution of our research is the identification of these goals. Additionally, we introduced and validated the construct of goal alignment, rooted in GST, to capture the relationship between these goals and security behaviors. Our findings demonstrate that goal alignment, i.e., the extent to which a security behavior is seen as instrumental for obtaining a subgoal, significantly predicts the security behaviors, surpassing all other factors in our model. This validation of goal alignment as a key explanatory mechanism represents another major contribution of our study.

Our specific findings also hold important implications for the theory development in this discourse. First, we identified career advancement as a higher-order goal in the workplace setting. While a few studies in the ISec literature have acknowledged the role of employees’ organizational commitment, which relates to employees’ desire to stay with an organization (Herath & Rao, 2009; Posey et al., 2015), the importance of career advancement has not yet been recognized. Here, our work suggests that this is a critical construct. It not only provides meaning to other subgoals that directly motivate security behavior but might also be a critical boundary condition for our goal-oriented theories: As career advancement answers “why” employees want to pursue subgoals, such as avoiding sanctions, a lack of career advancement goals might explain why some of our theories fail to explain the occurrence of certain behaviors in some instances, i.e., the failure of sanctions to deter computer abuse. Therefore, this finding lays the groundwork for future theory development on what motives drive employees’ security behaviors at work. In doing so, we close the gap with theory in the management discipline, which often considers career goals to better understand employee behaviors at work (Barrick et al., 2013).

A second implication follows from our identification of work performance goals. While the ISec literature suggests that security requirements often conflict with work goals (Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Karjalainen et al., 2019; Puhakainen & Siponen, 2010), our study further highlights the importance of the fit between work performance goals and security. Because employees are naturally driven to meet the expectations of their supervisors, our results suggest that they are most likely to engage in security behaviors when doing so is instrumental to achieving that goal. While perhaps intuitive, given that work is what employees are hired to do, paying more attention to the alignment of work performance goals and security behaviors may be overdue in IS research, as little work has examined this factor, and constructs related to work performance are missing from current unifying models (Cram et al., 2019; Moody et al., 2018). Our results show that this may be a significant blind spot in our literature, as work performance alignment was the strongest predictor of security behaviors in our study. Therefore, this finding calls for more research that examines the alignment between security behaviors and work performance.

The third implication relates to the identified blame avoidance goal. The literature has already suggested that security behaviors can be avoidance motivated because employees neither want to be blamed nor punished for causing security incidents. Our findings confirm that it is primarily the avoidance of blame, rather than the fear of specific punishments, that motivates security behaviors. Indeed, while many participants stated that they did not want to be seen as responsible for security incidents, they were less concerned about specific formal sanctions than they were about a general negative impact on their careers. Therefore, avoidance motivation for security behaviors may be related more to the blame avoidance goal posited by neutralization theory (Sykes & Matza, 1957) than to the sanction avoidance goal prevalent in deterrence theory (Gibbs, 1975). In other words, security behaviors might be driven more by the desire to avoid being seen as responsible for a security breach and subsequently facing disapproval by important others than by the desire to avoid formal sanctions or punishment. Therefore, our finding regarding this goal confirms the focus of the literature on the study of neutralization techniques (e.g., Vance et al., 2015) and the appropriateness of applying relevant theories. Thus, our contribution to this stream of literature lies in revealing which avoidance motivation is more common and validating blame avoidance as a critical driver of security behavior.

Another implication also relates to what we did not find. Contrary to expectations and findings from previous literature, our examination of common goals associated with security behaviors does not suggest that employees engage in security behaviors to avoid harm from security threats or specific formal sanctions, as assumed by protection motivation theory and deterrence theory (which, outside the IS world, focuses mainly on formal punishments; see Paternoster, 2010). However, given that these goals are assumed in many ISec studies that draw on protection motivation theory and deterrence theory, this is an important observation and underscores the existing criticism that these theories may not be well suited to explain organizational employees’ security behaviors (e.g., Johnston et al., 2015; Warkentin et al., 2016; Willison et al., 2018). A theoretical explanation for this finding may lie in the contextual dependence of goals (Austin & Vancouver, 1996). Because these goals may be activated primarily in situations in which employees process cues about security threats or sanctions—i.e., when reading a fear appeal, an ISP, or a statement about sanctions—they may not be active in employees’ general, everyday workplace situations and thus did not appear in our empirical investigation.

Finally, our work also contributes to the literature on security culture, which is widely recognized as an important factor in securing organizational IT assets (e.g., Chen et al., 2015; da Veiga et al., 2020; Hassandoust & Johnston, 2023). Despite this recognition, prior work has mainly focused on defining what security culture is (e.g., da Veiga et al., 2020; e.g., Hassandoust & Johnston, 2023) and how it can be cultivated, rather than on exploring the mechanisms by which culture influences employees’ security behaviors. Studies examining the influence of security culture often conceptualize it as norms and model its direct effects (e.g., Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Mikuletič et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). Our work, grounded in GST, contributes a new perspective to this literature. We do so by conceptualizing a new mechanism, goal alignment, through which security culture exerts its effects, and by identifying the specific subgoals whose alignment with security behaviors is crucial.

Furthermore, our post hoc analysis revealed that security culture, alongside other workplace environmental factors, serves as a foundational element influencing goal alignment, thereby demonstrating the extent to which these subgoals are pursued in organizational contexts. Our measure of security culture correlates with workplace environment factors such as communication frequency, ISPs, sanctioning frequency, and training frequency, suggesting that these factors might be relevant components or consequences of a functional security culture. Therefore, our study provides new insights into the impact of security culture by showing the foundational role it serves and elucidating the mechanisms through which it works. However, the concept itself remains somewhat ambiguous, warranting future research that builds on our findings and develops a more comprehensive framework for conceptualizing and examining security culture.

## 6.3 Practical Implications

Our findings provide two levers for practitioners seeking to promote security behaviors among employees. The first lever lies in enhancing career opportunities to give employees a reason to stay with their organization, care about their job performance, and subsequently engage in security behaviors. We found that career advancement goals are the ultimate reason that employees engage in security behaviors, as long as such behaviors are seen as instrumental to achieving work performance or blame avoidance subgoals. If employees remain unmotivated and disengaged (i.e., feel they are in a “dead-end” job), they may become “walking vulnerabilities” because they view security behaviors as irrelevant. Therefore, we encourage CIOs and CISOs to recognize the importance of career goals and to work with human resource officers to develop programs that can increase employees’ pursuit of career advancement goals.

The second lever is to align the performance of security behaviors with job performance and blame avoidance goals. A first takeaway from this finding is that regular direct-report supervisors should make it clear that their expectations include employees’ performance of security behaviors—and that anything that may lead to a security incident is directly at odds with meeting supervisors’ expectations. Furthermore, our findings suggest that ISPs, security culture, and training can reinforce such alignment to systematically promote such expectations throughout an organization. Thus, we recommend that CIOs and CISOs raise awareness of expectations through ISP, training, and by fostering a strong security culture in their organization. If these levers are combined in such a way that employees are committed to their careers and see security behaviors as critical to their career advancement goals, they should be more likely to engage in security behaviors.

## 6.4 Limitations and Future Work

One limitation of our general approach is that it does not link goals to specific sets of behaviors, such as extra-role or in-role behaviors (e.g., Hsu et al., 2015), or even particular specific behaviors, such as password creation (e.g., Khern-am-nuai et al., 2023) or security knowledge sharing (e.g., Yoo et al., 2020). This was intentional to gain a high-level understanding of the goals generally related to security behaviors. While the examination of specific behaviors is often seen as desirable (Aurigemma & Mattson, 2019; Cram & D’Arcy, 2023; Siponen & Vance, 2014), it runs the risk of poorly representing the general phenomenon (Hanisch et al., 1998; Hui & Triandis, 1985; Ironson et al., 1989; Posey et al., 2013). For example, Ironson et al. (1989, p. 199) suggested that “the specificity of the measure should match the specificity of the criterion.” Given that we used general measures of subgoal alignment, a general measure of behavior seemed most appropriate. That said, the importance of the identified goals may vary for different behaviors (e.g., organizational citizenship goals might be more critical for extra-role behaviors). Therefore, extensive future research is needed to develop a goaldriven understanding of specific, critical security behaviors that also incorporates specific goals, and our research is only a stepping stone in this direction.

Future replications in different occupational contexts would allow for further identification of relevant contextual goals. For example, future research could create contextualized models of employees’ securityspecific goal systems in specific contexts (such as nuclear power or the military) and occupations (such as medicine or accounting) to provide additional insight into how goals may vary across contexts and occupations. Similarly, to gain insights relevant to a specific organization, researchers could also develop an organization-specific model of employee goals to understand what drives employee security behaviors within that organization and to validate the role of identified goals.

In addition, other known higher-order goals, such as morality and the pursuit of freedom, as well as subgoals such as avoiding sanctions (e.g., D’Arcy et al., 2014; Herath & Rao, 2009; Lowry & Moody, 2015; Myyry et al., 2009), could be identified through a literature review and integrated to build a comprehensive model. Thus, our general model represents a first step in building a richer understanding of security-related goals. That said, more work is needed to link goals to the design of effective security interventions. For example, fear appeals that tap into career advancement goals and subgoals may be found to strengthen the association between compliance and goal attainment.

Finally, our methods, being observational in nature, limit our ability to draw strong causal inferences. While our theoretical framework suggests that goal pursuit and alignment drive behavioral outcomes, our empirical approach lacks the conditions required to definitively establish causality. <sup>4</sup> Future research is needed to validate our causal logic with causal evidence.

## 7 Conclusion

Scholars have primarily taken a deductive approach to predicting employee security behaviors at work using reference theories from other fields. However, as these theories make no assumptions about the goals that employees pursue at work, little is known about how security behaviors help or hinder employees in achieving workplace goals. Thus, to complement the literature, we undertook an inductive-first approach aimed at identifying the most common work goals that employees pursue when enacting security behaviors. Our findings show that work performance goals play the most important role, and that career goals give meaning and motivation to the pursuit of such goals and security behaviors. Strikingly, the role of career and work performance goals has been largely unaddressed in the literature on security behaviors; however, our study clearly reveals their primacy. Therefore, our work suggests a new avenue for powerful theorizing by incorporating career and work performance goals into models of security behaviors.

## Appendix A: Semi-Structured Interview Guidelines

## Briefing:

Data will be stored anonymously and will not be reported to your organization. Please answer honestly.

Please consent to these interviews being recorded and transcribed for academic purposes.

None of this will get back to your organization.

## Background:

1. How long have you worked for your organization?

2. What’s your current role? How long have you been in your current role?

3. Have you had security training at your current organization? (If yes, how often?)

4. How aware are you of the information security policies of your organization?

5. What requests do you usually get from the security department?

6. What are the most common threats your IT department warns you about?

7. How do you typically deal with these threats?

8. What other behaviors do you normally do to secure organizational information assets?

## Questions on higher-order goals:

1. What ultimate goals do you pursue in your organizations?

2. Why do you pursue them?

3. What do you do to pursue them?

4. Please explain to what extent preventative security behaviors are relevant to the achievement of these goals.

## Questions on facilitative subgoals:

1. From your experience, when do you find security behaviors helpful? Please give examples and explain the situation.

2. In these situations, what are the goals or objectives that performing security behaviors help you achieve?

3. To what extent do you believe that performing security behaviors is important to obtaining these goals? Please explain why.

## Questions on conflicting subgoals:

1. From your experience, when do you find security behaviors conflicting or interfering? Please give examples and explain the situation.

2. In these situations, what are the goals or objectives with which security behaviors are conflicting?

3. To what extent do you believe that performing security behaviors may conflict with the accomplishment of these goals? Please explain why.

## About the Authors

Sebastian W. Schuetz is an assistant professor of information systems at the Leeds School of Business at the University of Colorado Boulder. He received his PhD in information systems from the City University of Hong Kong. His research interests relate to information security management. His work has appeared in Journal of the Association for Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Management Science, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Applied Psychology, Journal of Information Technology, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Decision Support Systems, Information Technology & People, Internet Research, Computers & Security, International Journal of Information Management, and in the proceedings of several international conferences.

Heiko Gewald is a research professor of information management at Neu-Ulm University of Applied Sciences in Germany and the director of the Institute for Digital Innovation (IDI). He holds a master’s degree in business administration from the University of Bamberg, Germany, a European master’s of business science from Heriot-Watt University Edinburgh, UK, and a PhD in information systems from Goethe University Frankfurt, Germany. His research focuses on the use of digital resources by the aging generation, HealthIT, and IT management. He is a frequent speaker at conferences contributing to these matters. His work has been published in European Journal of Information Systems, Information & Organization, Information & Management, Journal of Economic Commerce Research, Health Systems, Communications of the ACM, and many other leading journals. His research has been funded by various institutions, including the DfG (German Research Foundation), BMBF (Federal Ministry of Education and Research), and the European Union, as well as private organizations. Dr. Gewald is a member of the AIS College of Senior Scholars.

Allen C. Johnston is the Hewson Professor of Cybersecurity and a professor of management information systems at the Culverhouse College of Business at the University of Alabama. The primary focus of his research is in the areas of behavioral information security, privacy, data loss prevention, collective security, and innovation. His research can be found in such outlets as MIS Quarterly, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Information & Management, Journal of Information Technology, and Decision Sciences, among others. He currently serves as an associate editor for MIS Quarterly, a senior editor for European Journal of Information Systems, and a member of the editorial review board for Journal of the Association for Information Systems. He is also a founding member and former chair and vice-chair of the IFIP Working Group on Information Systems Security Research (WG8.11/11.13).

Jason Bennett Thatcher holds the Tandean Rustandy Esteemed Endowed Chair at the Leeds School of Business at the University of Colorado. He received a BA in history and a BS in political science from the University of Utah and an MPA and PhD in Business Administration from Florida State University. Dr. Thatcher’s research examines the influence of individual beliefs and characteristics on technology use, decision-making, and cybersecurity, as well as strategic issues tied to information technology use in organizations. He serves as a senior editor at MIS Quarterly and has served as a senior editor of Information Systems Research. His work appears in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Applied Psychology, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, and Journal of Management Information Systems. Jason has published approximately one FT50 paper per year, each year since completing his PhD in 2002. When not working, he can be found eating street food, singing karaoke, and exploring odd corners of the world
