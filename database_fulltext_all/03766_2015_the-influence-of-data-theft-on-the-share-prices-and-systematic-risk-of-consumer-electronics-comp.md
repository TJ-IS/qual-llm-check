# The influence of data theft on the share prices and systematic risk of consumer electronics companies

Oliver Hinz <sup>b,1</sup>, Michael Nofer <sup>b,</sup>\*, Dirk Schiereck <sup>a,2</sup>, Julian Trillig <sup>a,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> TU Darmstadt, Department of Economics and Law, Chair of Corporate Finance, Hochschulstraße 1, 64289 Darmstadt, Germany <sup>b</sup> TU Darmstadt, Department of Economics and Law, Chair of Information Systems|Electronic Markets, Hochschulstraße 1, 64289 Darmstadt, Germany

Keywords: Cost of capital Data theft Event study Information security Systematic risk

## A B S T R A C T

Based on recent hacker attacks targeting consumer electronics companies, this article investigates the direct financial consequences for the owners of victim companies. To quantify this effect, we analyze whether the companies’ stock returns react to announcements of data theft. In addition, we also analyze the effects of such thefts on systematic risk. The results indicate that the share prices of both directly affected and similar companies decrease. However, market players do not change their evaluations of systematic risk, and hence, companies need not expect a higher cost of capital in the following years. - 2014 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

A series of hacker attacks has demonstrated that data theft is a potential threat to a company’s business success [10,43]. Even governments (e.g., the USA) and international organizations (e.g., the United Nations, International Olympic Committee) are affected by security incidents [6]. The substantial influence that security has on consumers’ trust [26] and the damage that may be caused by security incidents raise questions regarding the form and amount of investments that companies should make in their IT infrastructure. This research area has been the subject of controversy [19,65]. Since the beginning of the computer age, scholars have stressed how dangerous the use of information technology might be for companies with respect to the storage of sensitive data [55,73]. Frequently, the necessary amount of IT investments is derived from the costs of security incidents. The reaction of the capital market is particularly relevant from the perspective of listed companies and their investors. A number of studies have therefore addressed the impact of privacy and security incidents on the share prices of companies [17,20,60].

While past studies at the beginning of the Internet age observed stronger negative effects of security breaches on the share prices of affected companies, more recent studies indicate that these influences have diminished over time [35,79]. Gordon et al. [35] reported that the influence of security breaches on stock returns was stronger before 9/11/2001 than that in subsequent years. Yayla and Hu [79] studied the periods between 1994 and 2000 and between 2001 and 2006, showing that announcements of security breaches in the second period exert a less significant influence on share prices relative to those in the first. The number of hacker attacks reported in the press increased dramatically in recent years. While in 2005, DataLossDB.org reported 157 incidents, this number increased to 1651 in 2012.<sup>3</sup> It is therefore unclear whether investors remain sensitive to security breaches or these events are already incorporated into market prices. Using a novel dataset covering the period between 2011 and 2012, our study will contribute to an improved understanding of capital market reactions to security breaches following the numerous hacker attacks that have occurred in recent years.

In addition to share price reactions of affected companies, we also seek to study the effects of information transfer in this article. This occurs when an event at one company affects the share prices of its competitors that are not directly affected by the event but operate in the same industry.

The third and central research question concerns the influence of security breaches on systematic risk, which has broad implications for the optimal amount of investments in IT security. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that analyzes the extent of losses by considering changes in systematic risk for an international sample of companies. Systematic risk, represented by the beta factor in the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM, see [54,59,71,78]), is a crucial parameter for the calculation of a company’s cost of equity. The cost of equity reflects the return requirements of equity investors. The present study provides companies with information concerning both short-term share price developments following data thefts and investors’ risk assessments, and thus the long-term funding conditions. This has direct implications for the financial effort necessary to avoid such incidents.

Thus, our study makes three major contributions to the IS domain. First, we investigate whether information transfer and the influence of security incidents on share prices persist in contemporary financial markets. Previous studies generally collected data from the dot-com era [17,20,27,32,46]. We use a dataset that covers the years 2011 and 2012. Second, we extend previous findings on share price reactions and information transfer to the specific industry of consumer electronics. Third, our main contribution is the consideration of systematic risk when studying the influence of security incidents on international companies’ business success.

The paper is structured as follows: First, we will discuss the importance of IT for companies and introduce the consumer electronics industry. In Section 3, we develop our hypotheses before describing the database and the method employed in the empirical analysis. The results are presented in Section 5. The article concludes with a discussion of our findings, implications for companies and suggestions for future research.

## 2. Domain background

## 2.1. Importance of information technology for companies

The importance of information technology and its value to a company’s success is a permanent subject of debate in both academic and practical circles. Schryen [68] and Kohli and Grover [49] provide extensive literature reviews on studies focusing on the business value of IT. There is mixed evidence concerning the importance of IT in companies. In the context of this article, the stock market performance of companies is particularly interesting [25,42]. Other measures of company performance include accounting measures, such as ‘‘Return on Investment’’ or ‘‘Return on Equity’’ [72], knowledge [40,49] and productivity [e.g.,15,44,53].

In the 1990s, the public debate was inspired by the productivity paradox, which postulates that there is no positive correlation between IT investments and productivity [14,56]. Since then, numerous authors have critically examined the monetary investment that companies should make in their IT infrastructure. However, more recent studies focusing on the company level also reported positive correlations between IT investments and productivity [15,53]. Studying the period between 1981 and 1987, Dos Santos et al. [25] demonstrate that the influence of innovations on share prices depends on the degree of innovativeness involved. While there is no impact of non-innovative investments, a positive stock market reaction was observed following the announcement of innovative investments.

From a strategic perspective, proponents of large IT investments emphasize that technical innovations can lead to competitive advantages over competitors [8,28,65]. However, as a prominent critic of such a strategy, Carr [19] argues that all companies now possess the same IT resources, which is why IT is merely a necessity but not a potential means of strategic differentiation. Following this approach, companies should primarily focus on ensuring the functionality of information technology. In contrast, Bhatt and Grover [9] contend that leveraging IT assets can be a powerful tool to differentiate and gain competitive advantage for a firm.

Thus, a differentiated approach is required to assess the importance of information technology at the company level. In recent years, the security of computer systems has been placed at risk by hacker attacks intended to steal data [6,10]. Other serious threats are denial-of-service attacks and the defacement of web pages [32]. Recent developments in this regard have rendered the question of the monetary effort required to protect customer and employee information increasingly important.

## 2.2. Consumer electronics companies

We take the consumer electronics industry as an example because there has been a series of hacker attacks in recent years. Electronic Arts, Activision Blizzard, Nintendo, Sega, Sony and Ubisoft have recently been affected by data thefts. These incidents emphasize that consumer electronics companies are particularly vulnerable to data theft.

Their customers register for online networks and store sensitive information, such as passwords, email addresses and credit card data. These data are particularly vulnerable to hacker attacks. Furthermore, Galbreth and Shor [31] argue that data theft is more attractive when software products provide numerous computers to attack. This is typically the case in the consumer electronics industry.

In addition, the market for consumer electronics is very transparent. Information on products and companies is widely accessible, and hence customers can easily compare prices or services [41]. In the event of a security incident, customers might have the opportunity to switch to another company very quickly. Studying share price reactions to announcements of data theft is therefore particularly interesting for this industry.

The focus on one industry (i.e., consumer electronics) is also motivated by the second research question, which addresses the industry-specific information transfer. Spillover effects can be analyzed by studying the share price reactions of competitors that operate in the same industry but are not directly affected by a security breach [27,80].

## 3. Theoretical background and hypothesis development

In the following sections, we derive our hypotheses from the previous literature on the relationship among security breaches, share prices and the systematic risk of the affected company. Section 3.1 refers to the influence of security incidents; this is followed by descriptions of information transfer (Section 3.2) and systematic risk (Section 3.3).

## 3.1. Influence of security incidents on share prices

The financial loss for companies and the decline in shareholder value can be determined by examining the reaction of the capital market. A number of studies have demonstrated the negative impact of security incidents on a company’s share price [17,20]. Thereby, one can distinguish both between different types<sup>4</sup> and severities of incidents. Cavusoglu et al. [20] note that the stock prices of companies that have reported a security incident show an average abnormal return of -1.23% on the day following the announcement. Kannan et al. [46] also report negative but not significant effects. Other studies noted different effects of security incidents, depending on whether confidential information (e.g., credit card numbers) was accessed: While these events cause a negative stock market reaction, the effects are either not significant [17] or of lower magnitude when confidential information is not affected [32]. Yayla and Hu [79] studied different types of security breaches, confirming a negative stock market reaction following such announcements. In addition, the authors found that the magnitude of the share price decline depends on the business type, breach type and the year of the incident. In an earlier study, Hovav and d’Arcy [38] also found that the business type plays an important role. According to the authors, the market values of ‘‘Internet specific’’ companies are more affected than are other companies. Garg et al. [32] studied 22 security incidents between 1996 and 2002, observing an average share price decline of 4.5% within three days following the announcement. Telang and Wattal [77] studied vulnerability announcements of software vendors between 1999 and 2004, observing an average share price decline of 0.6%.

In addition to security issues, studies have also investigated the impact of privacy incidents on share prices. Muntermann and Roßnagel [60] observe a negative abnormal return the day after the announcement of a privacy incident, although this result does not hold over a longer period. Their study confirms the results of Acquisti et al. [1], who identify negative abnormal returns on the day of an announcement. However, these effects diminish over the following days. Unlike these negative announcements, Gordon et al. [34] suggest that voluntary disclosures concerning information security are positive related to a firm’s market value.

A potential reason for such negative stock market reactions is the close relationship among privacy, security, trust and the willingness of customers to purchase products from a company. For example, Suh and Han [75] have demonstrated that consumers’ perceptions of data integrity influence trust in ecommerce companies. In turn, the purchase decision strongly depends on consumers’ trust in the company [47]. Seock and Chen-Yu [69] found that privacy/security is the most important website attribute for online shoppers. In a recent laboratory experiment, Nofer et al. [62] reported a direct consumer reaction to security incidents.

Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1 (:).

The announcement of data theft has a negative effect on the share price of the affected company.

## 3.2. Information transfer

Data protection incidents might not exclusively influence the firm value of the directly affected company. Rather, there is empirical evidence that the share prices of competitors in the same industry can also decrease.

Studying the influence of management forecasts [5] or communications of company figures [22], previous research indicates the possibility of information transfers. Freeman and Tse [29] propose a model for predicting quarterly figures based on the earnings announcements of previously reporting companies in the same industry.

While information transfer has been observed in finance and economics [30,76], evidence for the IS domain – and the IS security domain in particular – is scarce. We were only able to identify two studies focusing on information transfer with respect to security breaches.

Ettredge and Richardson [27] consider information transfer using the example of the Internet industry during the dot-com era.

The study addresses Denial of Service (DoS) attacks, which were directed against certain well-known Internet companies (e.g., Amazon, eBay, Yahoo). These incidents occurred within a brief period in 2000. The results indicate that the share prices of companies that operate in the same industry but were not directly affected by data protection incidents also declined. Zafar et al. [80] studied a broad range of incidents (i.e., Denial of Service, website defacement, data theft, and data corruption) between 1997 and 2007 and found that the announcement of a security breach influences not only the share price of the affected company but also that of competitors in the same industry.

While information transfer is understudied in the IS domain, there is evidence from other areas suggesting that information might spread among market participants. For example, information cascades have been observed in laboratory experiments. Following this approach, Anderson and Holt [3] report that individuals use the information of others and follow previously established patterns. Convergent behavior and herding was also observed in financial markets: Investors decide how to trade stocks based on the beliefs of other market participants, even ignoring private information in many cases (see [37] for a review). According to [81], investors tend to herd more if they sell rather than buy stocks. In the case of data theft, these effects should therefore be more pronounced.

Previous research on information transfer and information cascades suggests that a company’s announcement of data theft will influence the behavior of shareholders of other companies in the same industry.

Therefore, we hypothesize:

H2 (:).

The announcement of data theft has a significant effect on nonaffected companies in the same industry.

## 3.3. Systematic risk

In addition to the effects on share prices, data theft also has a relevant effect on systematic stock return risk for companies and their investors. Regarding financial investment decisions, systematic risk is an important basis for decision-making. Capital allocation and the assessment or reduction of risk is becoming increasingly important in the contemporary competitive market environment [16].

In essence, risk can be divided into two types: systematic and unsystematic risk [7]. Unsystematic risk concerns the microeconomic risks of companies that investors can eliminate through a diversified stock portfolio. In contrast, systematic risk is based on market-specific changes (e.g., exogenous shocks, interest rate changes, natural disasters). Thus, systematic risk cannot be avoided through diversification (efficient portfolio composition). Systematic estimates the proportion of the total investment risk for which an investor requires compensation because he is unable to eliminate this risk. If systematic risk increases, a company’s cost of equity also increases. In this case, investors demand a higher return for undertaking the risk [71].

In the present study, we examine whether data thefts affect systematic risk. Companies that prove vulnerable to data theft may be regarded as systematically more prone to further attacks, and investors might demand higher compensations for this disclosed risk. We can expect two reactions from investors in this regard: Either the risk of data theft has already been priced such that the risk assessment remains unchanged, or investors make a new assessment of systematic risk. In the latter case, additional IT investments should decrease the level of risk. From the investor’s perspective, however, such investments are not justified if the risk assessment remains unchanged.

Although research on systematic risk is scarce, the general possibility of changes in risk evaluations has been observed in the literature. Aggarwal et al. [2] observe a change in risk in the field of setting information technology standards. According to the authors, market risk decreases for companies that establish technology standards with other companies. Furthermore, there is evidence from research on privacy that systematic risk can be influenced by privacy breaches. Nicholas-Donald et al. [61] observed a 4% increase in systematic risk when a privacy breach occurred.

We therefore hypothesize:

## H3 (:).

The announcement of data theft leads to an increase in systematic risk for the affected company.

The aim of the following empirical analysis is to investigate the relationship between security breaches and share prices as well as systematic risk.

## 4. Data and methodology

## 4.1. Data

The events we analyze are related to announcements of data thefts. We define data theft as illegally acquired access to personal information concerning a company’s customers. The scope of the stolen data varies and in simple cases includes names, addresses, or dates of birth and in serious situations includes credit card information. We use the websites datalossdb.org and attrition.org as databases to select our events. These databases have proven suitable in comparable research areas [60]. Both web pages provide detailed information on press releases concerning data thefts. The community-style of the platforms fosters the creation of a comprehensive, worldwide database.

The website data comprise information from January 2007 to August 2012. We focus on data thefts related to consumer electronics companies, which allows us to test for information transfer. These analyzed events are crosschecked and verified in a subsequent step using reports from other press databases (www. pressdisplay.com). In so doing, we ensure that we identify the earliest possible release date. As we find that in all cases the announcement by the affected company is the earliest date on which the news is publicly available, the databases we consider can be regarded as a credible and accurate resource.

Reports on data theft are included in our sample if the announcing company is listed on a stock exchange and has publicly traded shares. In addition, companies are excluded from the data set, if only a few large shareholders control the outstanding shares. We thereby ensure that the shares have sufficient stock trading liquidity and thus can reflect possible market reactions. Company data and the market indices are retrieved from Thomson Reuters Datastream utilizing total returns (RI).

Table 1 reports a chronological list of the retrieved announcements regarding data thefts. To control for confounding events (which would ultimately dilute our findings), we browse the press release database of the respective company. However, we do not observe a confounding event for any of the announcements below.

The sample used in this study comprises six developers of software and hardware components from the consumer electronics industry. Two of the six companies (Nintendo, Sony) develop and produce game consoles and game software. Sega ceased console development and focuses – along with Electronic Arts, Ubisoft, and Activision Blizzard – on the development of game software. In addition, all companies offer online networks where their customers can register using personal data. Registration allows customers to gain access to online games, retrieve new games, including through cashless payments, and information is transmitted. Our evidence moreover indicates that the analyzed companies suffered from hacker attacks on their networks during the first half of 2011 or the middle of 2012.

To verify for information transfer and validate the results, we also consider two different samples. First, we browse the Thomson One Banker database for companies with the same primary SIC Codes as the companies in Table 2 have. In the next step, we select all companies including ‘‘game’’ in their company description. In so doing, we obtain a sample of 62 companies, which we refer to as ‘‘reference companies’’. Second, we include only the companies that are directly affected (victim companies) by data theft at any point in time, i.e., the companies listed in Table 2. This sample is referred to as ‘‘affected reference companies’’.

## 4.2. Event study

We conduct an event study [57] to measure the impact of data theft on the stock price performance of companies in the consumer electronics industry. This is the state-of-the-art methodology for this purpose and has been extensively applied in the areas of accounting and finance [24,51,58,70]. The influence of unexpected major catastrophes, such as natural disasters [48] or terrorism [67], on the stock returns of affected companies is also analyzed using an event study approach. In the IS domain, researchers have applied this method to study the effects of data thefts on affected companies [17,20,46]. Furthermore, effects following IT investments [24] and e-commerce announcements [74] have also been examined. Thus, this methodology is recognized as an internationally accepted standard instrument in evaluations focusing on the capital market.

In general, the aim of an event study is to investigate the effect of an event (in our study: a security breach) on the respective company’s share price. We first calculate the return that would have been observed in the absence of the event. The event study approach therefore compares a company’s stock return with market returns, wherein the market is represented by a general market index. We estimate the market model [11] using a linear regression (OLS) over a period of 200 days<sup>5</sup> prior to the event date (Formula (1)). We therefore follow previous studies such as Dos Santos et al. [25] or Im et al. [42] that also used an estimation period of 200 days.

$$
R _ {i, t} = a _ {i} + \beta_ {i} * R _ {m, t} + e _ {i, t}\tag{1}
$$

where $R _ { i , t }$ is the return of company i’s shares on day t. $R _ { m , t }$ determines the market return on day t. a and $\beta _ { i }$ are the intercept and slope parameters for company i.

We use the S&P Global 1200 index return as the market return $R _ { m , t } .$ . This index represents the benchmark for all companies in our sample. Following Chevalier [21], Geyskens et al. [33], Hood [39], Koch and Fenili [50] and Peterson [64], we insert a 30- day window between the estimation period and the event to exclude the potential bias of the event on the estimation period (Fig. 1).

In the next step, we calculate the abnormal stock return (AR = abnormal return), which is an indicator of the capital market’s reaction. This return determines the difference between the normal or expected return, which would have been observed absent the event, and the actual stock return that was realized after the event occurred (Formula 2):

$$
A R _ {i, t} = R _ {i, t} - (\widehat {\alpha} _ {i} + \widehat {\beta} _ {i} R _ {m, t})\tag{2}
$$

In the following step, the abnormal returns for each day in the event window are estimated and cumulated over the event window. The cumulative abnormal returns (CAR) represent the effect of a data breach on a company:

$$
C A R _ {i} = \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} A R _ {i, t}\tag{3}
$$

To assess the statistical significance of the results, we employ a standard two-tailed t-test, the modified t-test proposed by Boehmer et al. [12] and the modified t-test proposed by Johnson [45]. All have the joint null hypothesis that the mean and median cumulated abnormal returns in every observed period are equal to zero.

## 4.3. Risk modeling

To determine the equity value of a company, we transform (uncertain) future cash flows into a current value. Risk could be considered either using an interest rate surcharge on a risk-free interest rate or a so-called risk deduction on the expected payments. The surcharge method is widely used in practice and theory and is also applied in this study by using the beta factor [e.g., 4,13]. The amount (cost) of the data theft caused by changes in risk can be divided, in a simplified manner, into the debt and equity share of the company. Financial institutions and rating agencies calculate the costs of debt by applying appropriate assessment procedures that are generally easily accessible. The cost of equity – the expected return for equity holders – and thus the determination of the cost of systematic risk are determined solely by considering capital market data. The basic concept is a capital market model, introduced in the 1960s by Lintner [55], Mossin [59], Sharpe [71] and Treynor [78] as the Capital Asset Pricing

Model (CAPM). Eq. (4) reports the cost of equity while considering systematic equity risk:

$$
R _ {i} = r _ {f} + \beta_ {i} (R _ {M} - r _ {f})\tag{4}
$$

Here, $r _ { f }$ denotes the risk-free rate, $R _ { M }$ the return of the market portfolio, and $\beta _ { i }$ a company’s specific amount of systematic risk. The beta factor does not refer to the component of this risk that can be reduced to zero through diversification (unsystematic risk) but to the systematic risk of the company. A risk-related change in the required return for equity holders is represented by a change in the beta factor. This change describes the extent to which a company’s systematic risk changes if a change in the market return or the riskfree rate occurs. In Eq. (5), the beta factor denotes as the ratio of the covariance (cov ()) of the return of the individual stock with the return of the market portfolio and the variance (var ()) of the return on the market:

$$
\beta_ {i} = \frac {\operatorname{conv} \left(R _ {i} , R _ {M}\right)}{\operatorname{var} \left(R _ {M}\right)}\tag{5}
$$

Using Eq. (5), a rolling beta factor is calculated for the observation period. The covariance of market returns with stock returns and the variance of the market returns are held constant over a period of 250 trading days (1-year betas). The beta factor and analyzing the change in this factor in particular has been applied in various research domains and can be regarded as an established method for modeling systematic risk. Al Refai [4] and Brooks et al. [13] analyze the effect of wars or warlike conflicts on the risk of the capital markets of affected countries by considering this beta factor. Havenner et al. [36] and Paleari and Redondi [63] report a shift in the beta factors of companies that are affected by regulatory changes. Changes in systematic risk resulting from seasoned equity offerings are also analyzed through this method [52,66].

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Results of the event study

Table 3 reports the cumulative abnormal stock returns following the announcement of data theft. The numbers in columns two (mean) and three (median) represent the average abnormal changes in stock returns. The average abnormal returns are cumulated corresponding to the length of the event windows (column 1). Our evidence suggests that various event windows exhibit constant negative returns for both means and medians. All test statistics applied yield nearly identical values and the same level of statistical significance. This confirms that despite our small database, our results are robust. The event window prior to the actual event contains only insignificant CAR. However, for the event day ([0,+0]) and the event window comprising one to three days after the event ([0,+1], [0,+2] and [0,+3]), we observe significantly negative abnormal returns. CAR on the event day can be quantified as -1.16% and as -4.06% when the two subsequent days are included. This result supports hypothesis 1 that announcements of data theft have a negative effect on the market value of the affected company.

The results are in accordance with those of similar studies. Campbell et al. [17] analyze disclosures of security incidents affecting U.S. companies and report an average CAR of -5.4% ([-1,+1]). In a similar study, Cavusoglu et al. [20] report an average CAR of 2.1% ([0,+1]) for all companies that are traded on a stock exchange in the United States. A negative CAR of -5.19% is reported by Ettredge and Richardson [27], who analyze the effects on Internet companies of being involved in a series of ‘‘denial of service’’ attacks in February 2000.

Table 4 reports the AR and the CAR of the affected companies. We observe negative AR for all companies on the event day itself. Moreover, five of the six companies show negative AR in the event windows comprising the following days. For Sony, Nintendo, and Ubisoft, our analysis yields a negative cumulative abnormal return of 8.2%, 17.6%, and 5.0% for a 20 day window [0,+20]. For EA, a minor change of +0.4% is observed while Sega and Blizzard exhibit an abnormal excess return of 5.1% and 4.6%, respectively. We interpret the highly divergent behavior of Sega’s stock returns as indicating that only a minor or insignificant data loss occurred. This conclusion becomes more persuasive when one considers that Sega’s announcement on the data theft indicated that no payment information was affected. This result moreover confirms the findings of Campbell et al. [17], who report significant negative stock returns for security breaches in which confidential information was lost, whereas no significant market reactions could be observed for security breaches affecting only non-confidential customer information.

The second research question concerns the effects on nonaffected companies in the same industry when an affected company reports a data theft (i.e., the former are referred to as reference companies). We classify firms as belonging to the same industry according to SIC Codes. All public companies with the same SIC Code as the sample companies are retrieved from Thompson One Banker. Additionally, the key word ‘‘game’’ must be in the company’s business description and the average market capitalization in 2011 must be higher than \$ 100m. The matching sample (‘‘reference companies’’) comprises 62 firms.

## 5.3. Robustness checks

Given the small number of companies considered in the analysis and the economic developments following the tsunami and nuclear disaster at Fukushima in March 2011, further robustness checks of the results are in order.

We obtain similar results when the covariance between market and stock returns is held constant over a shorter period. By reducing the rolling period (to 90 or 120 days), the beta factors clearly become more volatile, as individual changes have a greater influence. Our expectation is then that smaller changes are examined that cannot be observed in the 250-day analysis. We also confirm the results when we consider short rolling periods.

In addition to the systematic risk captured by the beta coefficients, we analyze whether the announcements cause a change in the variance (the sum of systematic and unsystematic risk) of the stock returns of affected firms. We assess the conditional variance of the market using a GARCH (1, 1) model. The changes in the variance are insignificant when analyzing both the dummy variables and variables in levels.

Furthermore, we calculate the ‘‘unlevered’’ beta factors. We identify the changes in the beta factor due to a change in the debt/ equity ratio (‘‘financial risk’’) and crosschecked whether the results in Table 5 exclusively reflect a change in ‘‘business risk’’. Although no changes in financial risk following data breaches are expected, we control for this potential bias; again, our findings remain qualitative unchanged. The results are thus confirmed when using ‘‘unlevered’’ betas.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Summary

This study examined the reactions of the capital market to data thefts at consumer electronics companies, which have implications for the economically optimal level of investment in IT security. We analyzed the impact of data theft on share prices and systematic risk. The results illustrate that the disclosure of a data theft leads to a significant decline in the affected company’s share price. Negative returns can be observed not only on the day following the announcement but also over a 10-day window. This result confirms the results of previous studies reporting a negative stock market reaction [17,20,32]. In addition, our results indicate that the share prices of other companies in the same industry also exhibit negative abnormal returns. Similar to prior studies of other industries [27], we observe information transfer among consumer electronics companies.

In capital markets, where individuals exhibit economically rational behavior, one can expect investors to correctly assess risk such that the capital market is in equilibrium. In our study, risk measures exhibit heterogeneous reactions to data thefts. The investors’ average risk assessment remains unchanged. This contradicts prior results obtained by Cardenas et al. [18], who studied the influence of security breaches on the systematic risk of companies traded in the United States. The authors reported a 22% increase in systematic risk. A potential explanation for this difference is their focus on companies traded in the United States, while we use an international sample. Furthermore, Cardenas et al. [18] studied an older period between 2002 and 2008. Numerous privacy and security breaches have been reported in the press since that time

## 6.2. Implications and outlook

The results of this study provide practical and theoretical contributions. The unchanged systematic risk suggests that investors generally anticipate the occurrence of data theft and that such thefts have already been priced into the beta factors as an accepted source of a company’s operational risk. Contemporary investors might regard security breaches as an inevitable occurrence. According to Gordon et al. [35], other possible reasons for the diminishing effects of security breaches include effective recovery strategies or a more persistent willingness of customers to maintain business relationships with the affected companies. Thus, customers consider security breaches to be less severe due to the cumulative occurrence of these incidents in recent years. In contrast to the beginning of the Internet age in the last decade, where hacker attacks represented a new and dangerous phenomenon, customers might now be more willing to purchase products from breached companies due to the relatively common occurrence of such breaches.

For practitioners, the short-term loss in value suffered by the affected companies and their competitors can be interpreted as the monetary damage caused by the respective data theft. This refers to the costs of responding to the data theft (e.g., clarification, compensation payments, additional information provision to customers, promotional activities). The differences between short-term and long-term effects provide important information for companies as part of the discussion concerning the level of IT security investment that is justified from an economic perspective. Although negative equity returns can be expected following data thefts, our results indicate that the long-term financing conditions are not affected. Equity investors do not demand a higher return after security incidents because the perceived risk of the investment remains unchanged. Based on this evaluation, the relevance of data theft must be corrected to the extent that there are no increasing capital costs for the affected companies. Despite serious consequences (loss of reputation, compensation for clients, decreasing share price), the investors’ return requirements are not affected by data thefts. Therefore, unrestrained spending to ensure the security of IT systems is not sensible. Even if a company’s data security could be completely guaranteed, the company would be unable to avoid a short-term share price decline in the event of a data theft at one of its competitors. Thus, the monetary effort to avoid such incidents should be kept within limits.

To prevent negative impacts on short-term corporate development, companies need a clear privacy and security policy, which informs employees and stakeholders of important rules with respect to the treatment of sensitive data. Although security should not be purchased without considering the costs, ensuring an adequate level of security is important. Companies should assign tasks to specific employees and clearly define who is responsible for security protection.

However, critically, the present study may be influenced by aspects that cannot be considered in our analysis. Three of the companies investigated are from Japan. Despite applying a correction factor for these companies, we cannot exclude the possibility that our observations of both economic activity and capital market perceptions are distorted. Another critical note is the small sample size. Despite the robustness of the results, individual events may nevertheless distort the overall result. Furthermore, this study exclusively focuses on the economic consequences of data theft. However, policy makers could consider the data per se worthy of protection and thus enact laws that require even greater efforts in IT security.

Companies will likely continue to face substantial challenges concerning data protection issues and data theft. An interesting question for future research would be to study the impact of data theft on the systematic risk in other industries. The companies examined in this study belong to the consumer electronics industry and are therefore very active on the Internet. Thus, it is possible that investors generally regard Internet companies as more prone to data theft. Future research could therefore compare our results with those from other more traditional industries.
