# The interplay between evidence and judgment in the IT project prioritization process

## a b s t r a c t

Strategic decisions concerning IT investments are based on a project prioritization process designed to accommodate evidence-based management and rational decision-making. Our empirical study of IT project prioritization in a financial institution shows how managers reach decisions under norms of evidence-based management. We use a rich dataset derived from a longitudinal study. We analyze managers’ decision-making practices and identify four tactics of supplementing, substituting, interpreting, and reframing the available evidence. The choice of tactic depends on how decision makers perceive the available evidence. All tactics involve the use of judgment. We also identify a number of devices employed to enable such judgments. Our study shows that although evidence is not playing its designated role in evidence-based management, it nevertheless plays a central role in the IT prioritization process.

## Introduction

The proliferation of information technology in organizations, combined with the restructuring of work into projects, has brought IT project portfolio management to the attention of both academics and practitioners. More and more organizations have adopted IT portfolio management practices to manage effectively their limited financial and human resources for IT investments. Within such constraints, strategic decisions are made to choose proposals for IT projects and build a project portfolio (Archer and Ghasemzadeh, 1999). Popular IT project prioritization methods include the use of financial tools that claim to offer evidence-based comparisons for prioritization decisions (Dickinson et al., 2001; Kester et al., 2011). Respected scholars in the field have repeatedly called for information systems (IS) to provide data that can facilitate rational decision-making (Davenport, 2010; McAfee et al., 2012). Common to these studies is the understanding that managers are empowered when they have accurate information, in turn leading to less biased decisions and enhanced organizational performance. The financial tools and methods used for IS project prioritization are characterized by a rational, objective, and scientific approach based on the assumption that IT projects contribute to ‘‘specific ends, ends that can be articulated, are shared and are objective’’ (Howcroft and McDonald, 2007, p. 4).

Branded as evidence-based management'. such ideas have gained prominence both in academia and among practitioners. Researchers describe evidence-based practice as ‘‘a paradigm for making decisions that integrates the best available research evidence with decision-maker expertise and client/customer preferences to guide practice toward more desirable results’’ (Rousseau, 2006a, p. 258). A growing number of researchers is calling for additional quantitative data to improve decision-making based on evidence (e.g. Tingling and Brydon, 2010) as well as empirical studies to demonstrate how evidence can ‘‘both advance management knowledge and address pressing practical questions’’ (George et al., 2014, p. 324). Despite the numerous conceptual arguments for evidence-based management, there are very few studies on the actua use of evidence in management practice.

Furthermore, research has shown that organizations exclusively basing prioritization decisions on financial methods may perform worse than organizations using a variety of methods (Dickinson et al., 2001). This is especially true for IT projects because a large proportion of their costs are hidden and many of their benefits are intangible (Irani and Love, 2002) It is thus claimed that additional information to quantitative data is required to capture the full-range of benefits and costs since financial methods have only a limited ability to include the non-quantifiable intangible benefits and non-measurable hidden or indirect costs of IT projects (Farbey et al., 1999). According to Bannister and Remenyi (2000), the limitations of existing evaluation methods force decision makers to rely on ‘gut feeling’ in IT-related matters. Tallon and Kraemer (2007) claim that subjective assessments such as perceived IT impact, intuition, and insights may de facto yield more accurate and even richer assessments of IT projects than do the financial methods. Moreover, Frisk et al. (2014) propose an interpretive collaborative approach to IT investment decision-making. However, our knowledge of the actual use of financial methods and alternative assessment practices in IT prioritization processes is limited due to a lack of empirical studies.

In this paper, we use the concept of evidence to capture the use of financial methods in the IT project prioritization process. Evidence comes in the form of scientifically proven financial models, the use of which produces data such as measures of costs and benefits. Data on costs and benefits is thus expected to be the product of rigorous analysis derived from state-of-the-art information processing systems and hence to count as evidence. Addressing the calls from both IS and management scholars to study the role of evidence in strategic decisions and management, we set out to investigate these issues in the case of the IT project prioritization process. We study how decision makers use evidence alongside other sources of information in practice. We exploit a rich dataset derived from a longitudinal case study of the IT project prioritization process in a division of a large Scandinavian financial institution. Over a period of eight months, we observed the prioritization process with the following research question in mind:

## - How do decision-makers use evidence and other sources of information in the IT project prioritization process?

Our findings show that, decision makers are by no means limited to the use of evidence during the prioritization process. We identify a set of tactics of substituting, supplementing, interpreting, and reframing evidence through the use of various judgment devices. According to Karpik (2010), such devices are used to make informed judgments of value in situations in which established evaluative standards are unavailable. In our case, the judgment devices enable decision makers to reach a prioritization decision when evidence is not enough. Decision makers deploy different tactics depending on how evidence is perceived (for instance, as poor or ambiguous).

Our study contributes to both research and practice. In the domain of IT project prioritization, we explain the adaptation of evidence-based management practices to reflect IT projects’ characteristics (Smithson and Hirschheim, 1998; Howcroft and McDonald, 2007) and how this adaptation facilitates decision-making. Decision makers adopt tactics appropriate to addressing the unstructured and complicated task of IT project prioritization. Our study shows that although evidence is not playing its designated role in evidence-based management, it nevertheless plays a central role in the IT prioritization process. In the domain of evidence-based management, we undertake an in-depth discussion of the relationships between evidence and other sources of information during the decision-making process, a topic highlighted by other researchers in the area (e.g. Rousseau, 2006b; Briner et al., 2009). Finally, for practitioners, we offer a process-oriented toolbox for IT project portfolio management.

The paper is structured as follows. The next section presents the theoretical background, covering IT project prioritization and evidence-based management as well as our research strategy. We then present the research method, followed by a description of the empirical setting. After this, we present our findings and discuss our contributions. The paper concludes by highlighting future research directions.

## IT project prioritization and evidence-based management

## Prior research on IT project prioritization

Since the 1960s, management science has produced extensive research on project prioritization. Numerous methods of project prioritization have been developed, but few have been successfully adopted by organizations (Martino, 1995). These studies are mainly prescriptive, with financial models dominating as the project prioritization tools (Cook and Green, 2000; Bardhan et al., 2004). The literature is predominantly characterized by a normative discourse that promotes a range of techniques and methods for providing solid information to decision makers. The downside to this discourse is a lack of emphasis on applying the proposed models to the complexities of a real-life context. These prescriptive studies

presume a cycle of investigation, analysis, and review, with systematic evidence injected at all stages (Archer and Ghasemzadeh, 1999).

Project selection and prioritization problems are generally addressed within a broad portfolio management framework (Engwall and Jerbrant, 2003). The IT project prioritization literature has inherited many of its methods from the product development and project portfolio management domains, with a focus on ensuring an optimal portfolio of projects (Balasubramanian et al., 2000; Ghasemzadeh and Archer, 2000; Kim et al., 2000; Stamelos and Angelis, 2001; Loch and Kavadias, 2002; Bardhan et al., 2004). McFarlan (1981) was among the first to advocate adopting the portfolio management approach to IT projects, using the calculated risk of a project as a selection criterion. At the same time, organizations have adopted these methods in an attempt to justify IT investments (Farbey et al., 1999), using analytical mechanisms when making prioritization decisions. Jeffery and Leliveld (2004), for instance, reveal the impacts a synchronized IT portfolio management process on the return-on-asset (ROA).

These methods, however, have been subject to extended criticism due to their narrow perspective on the nature and impacts of IT (Ballantine et al., 1999; Smithson and Hirschheim, 1998; Howcroft and McDonald, 2007). A number of researchers argue that the IT evaluation process is subjective and contextual (Smithson and Hirschheim, 1998; Farbey et al., 1999; Howcroft and McDonald, 2007) as well as political (Wilson and Howcroft, 2005). Intangible and non-financial benefits comprise a large proportion of IT projects’ returns, yet it is nearly impossible to calculate hidden and indirect costs prior to development and implementation (Muralidhar et al., 1990). According to Bannister and Remenyi (2000), these limitations to existing evaluation methods force decision makers to make IT-related decisions based on other grounds, such as ‘gut feeling’.

More recent IS research has broadened the focus by considering how organizations coordinate selection and prioritization decisions (Fonstad and Robertson, 2006; Weill and Aral, 2006; Weill and Ross, 2009). Fonstad and Robertson (2006) call for additional empirical studies into the role of organizational actors in IT project selection and prioritization. The current literature, however, still advises organizations to manage and select their portfolio of IT projects based on measures of ROI and cost-benefit analysis, which are methods claimed to provide the maximum benefits to the organization (Jeffery and Leliveld, 2004). By numerically representing the IT projects, this evidence-based management approach makes them easily comparable and therefore easy to rank and prioritize.

## Evidence-based management in organizational decision-making

Notions of rationality continue to dominate organizational decision-making despite knowledge of its many limitations (Simon, 1947; March, 1994). According to the rational approach, organizational choices should be made by selecting the alternative with the highest expected value (March, 1994). However, critics of the rational approach have showed that the reality of making strategic decisions is far from what the theory prescribes (Simon, 1947; Cyert and March, 1963; March, 1994; Langley et al., 1995). Organizational characteristics pose further limitations to the rational model by conditioning how decisions are made (Shapira, 1997). For example, the ambiguity of available information affects decision makers’ interpretations and evaluations (March, 1994). If evidence is the foundation for rational decision-making, the use of evidence by decision makers is far from trivial.

Rationality is commonly understood in a procedural sense (March, 1994). A rational procedure implies rigorous and systematic information gathering, analysis, and calculation, which are steps akin to the scientific method (Potworowski and Green, 2012). Pfeffer and Sutton (2006, p. 12) state that evidence-based management means ‘‘subjecting business practices and decisions to the same scientific rigor used for technical or medical issues.’’ Evidence-based management thus constitutes a scientific process, and its conclusions are taken as a scientific output, i.e. as evidence (Hornung, 2012; Yates and Potworowski, 2012).

The concept of evidence-based management is inspired by evidence-based medicine, an approach that guides clinicians to make decisions based on ‘‘the best available evidence’’ (Sackett et al., 1996). ‘The best available evidence’ refers to the consensus opinion of the scientific community on the basis of rigorous methods for establishing what does and does not work, allowing the identification of cause-and-effect relationships to replace ‘‘formal authority, reputation, and intuition’’ (Pfeffer and Sutton, 2006, p. 79); idiosyncratic beliefs; and conventional wisdom. In medicine, other sources of information are utilized when evidence is mobilized to enhance professional practice, complementing individual expertise and making intuition more reliable (Sackett et al., 1996). In evidence-based management, however, the aim is to replace current practice (Morrell, 2008) since evidence makes us ‘‘skeptical about experience, wisdom, or personal credentials as a basis for asserting what works’’ (Rousseau, 2006a, p. 268). Managerial subjectivity as a result of learning from limited personal experience and ambiguous feedback leads to poor decisions and misunderstandings

By its nature, evidence-based management presumes it is possible to achieve a higher degree of managerial rationality in decision-making processes. Evidence-based management posits that decision makers possess the required cognitive capabilities and intellectual skills to interpret, understand, and determine the validity of scientific evidence relevant to strategic decisions (Yates and Potworowski, 2012). The concept of ‘evidence’ implies demonstrable, proven, and generalized facts. These may be inconsistent with local experience and circumstances, and they may neglect the interests of stakeholders and individual organizations. Despite often being local, difficult to demonstrate, and largely subjective, such specific conditions influence the impact that evidence has on decision outcomes. Besides, not all evidence is taken at face value. Rousseau (2006b) distinguishes between different types of evidence in accordance with the rules of scientific inference (Reay et al.,

2009). This distinction is especially important for management since scholars still argue about what constitutes legitimate evidence and have failed to identify generalizable cause-and-effect relationships on which managerial best practices should rest (Learmonth, 2006; Cascio, 2007).

Briner et al. (2009) suggest that evidence-based management should not focus narrowly on the use of external evidence but should also take into account individual expertise and judgment, local context, and stakeholders’ interests. Such elements play a legitimate role in specific decision-making processes. These scholars thus acknowledge that information besides externally validated evidence and scientific results may be valuable in determining the right decisions. Taking this as our point of departure, we now investigate the role that evidence plays in a specific IT project prioritization process.

## Evidence-based management in IT project prioritization

Having reviewed the main research findings on IT project prioritization and evidence-based management, this managerial approach at first glance appears well suited for explaining and supporting the use of theory-driven, prescriptive methods and tools in IT project prioritization. Indeed, the extensive use of methods such as ROI and cost-benefit analysis suggests a reliance on theoretical models and scientific evidence in IT project selection and prioritization. However, a more thorough reading of the literature in both research areas reveals a number of calls for additional sources of information in making strategic decisions about IT project prioritization. These additional sources are expected to help better tailor the decision to the actual context and circumstances in question.

In the IT project prioritization domain, a number of researchers have argued for the importance of the intangible characteristics of a project and its associated assets, which are not captured by methods such as cost-benefit analysis (Farbey et al., 1999; Howcroft and McDonald, 2007). For example, in a typical IT project, many benefits are intangible, making their financial values difficult to calculate. In this case, expert judgment might supplement financial calculations in assessing the overall value of the project to the organization. However, when such ‘soft’ measures of value enter into the equation, projects become incommensurable to a certain extent. The prioritization decision thus becomes more than just a ranking by financia value. The decision cannot be made without a taking into consideration managerial judgment.

Managerial judgment, which is typically considered a source of bias and subjectivity (Pfeffer and Sutton, 2006), is thus an inevitable aspect of evidence-based management. Complementary reliance on expert judgment may in fact lead to better decisions by providing adequate information and accurate representations of the specific context to which the prioritization decision applies (Briner et al., 2009; Briner and Rousseau, 2011).

Recognizing these observations in the literature (the inevitability of intangible benefits and managerial judgment on one hand and the ambiguity of such judgment as a potential source of both bias and insight on the other hand), we set out to empirically investigate the role of evidence and judgment in IT project prioritization. We took an inductive approach and observed managers’ use of evidence in actual decision-making contexts. We also looked for other sources of information that these managers deployed to make the evidence useful.

Our empirical context is a financial institution in which ROI and cost-benefit analyses constitute canonical practices. We expected that the role of managerial judgment in considering intangible benefits in IT project prioritization processes would be problematic and subject to challenge. To explore these issues, we conducted an in-depth case study of a complete IT project prioritization process. The next section provides a description of our research method.

## Research method

We conducted a longitudinal case study to investigate how managers practice evidence-based management during the IT project prioritization process in a large Scandinavian financial institution.

Empirical data was collected over a period of approximately 18 months and focused on the organization’s IT project prioritization for 2011, before and after the final portfolio was decided. Several data collection techniques were applied to understand the IT project prioritization process (Walsham, 1995; Myers and Avison, 1997): real-time observation, recording of meetings, semi-structured interviews, workshops, and collection of written documents produced by the organization (see Table 1 for a detailed account of the collected data and its use).

Real-time observations took place in two departments of the IT unit where one researcher observed managers in their natural setting. These observations primarily served to establish an understanding of the activities of the two departments and the context in which IT project prioritization took place. The richest sources of empirical data about the prioritization process are the 43 transcribed semi-structured interviews with the managers (approximately 730 pages) and eight transcribed recorded meetings (approximately 170 pages). We followed an evolutionary, iterative approach in which the data collection activities supported each other throughout the process. For example, meeting observations were used as inputs to follow-up interviews, which were conducted at a later stage.

We initially conducted semi-structured interviews (April – June 2010) with members of the prioritization groups (PG) from different levels and units of the organization. The interviews provided personal experiences with and views on the current prioritization process, particularly its circumstances and history in the organization. Two main stakeholders were identified from these interviews. The IT unit is a primary stakeholder, hosting the project prioritization process and implementing the prioritized projects. Nineteen interviews were conducted in the IT unit. The various business units are also stakeholders. The analysis evolved into an iterative process, inspired by Miles and Huberman (1994), in which data was cyclically compared with emerging themes

As a first step in data analysis, we developed a timeline of the IT project prioritization process and its phases. In the next step, we read all documents and meeting transcripts to identify components of raw data across the different sources, paying special attention to passages that covered ideas and themes such as ‘costs‘, ‘benefits’, ‘intangible benefits’, and prioritization criteria. Inspired by Pratt (2009) as well as Vendelo and Rerup (2011), we use the timeline of the process as well as the observed phenomena to present and discuss our findings.

## The empirical setting

The study was conducted at the IT unit of an international financial institution with its headquarters in Scandinavia and with offices in many different countries. We chose this setting to investigate IT project prioritization because it has a strong tradition of using financial models and objective measures such as costs and benefits in forecasting activities. The IT unit is in charge of standardizing and automating processes and developing information systems to enhance organizational efficiency. Each year, the IT unit allocates resources to a number of new projects based on an elaborate prioritization process involving all of its 21 departments.

First, the departments prepare proposals for new projects to be entered into the prioritization process. Each department has its own prioritization group (PG), with members representing the IT units, the senior management of the financial institution, and the various business units. Each PG is tasked with producing a prioritized list of IT projects for the coming year. In order to achieve this, all project proposals are entered into a spreadsheet requiring information on the project’s net present value, costs, number of man hours, benefits, time plan, sub-deliveries, project phases, releases, business drivers, and strategic focus areas.

The portfolio management office collects, consolidates, and analyzes the 21 prioritization lists it receives from the PGs. A draft portfolio of IT projects is submitted to the IT committee two weeks before it meets for final approval. The chairman of each PG presents the prioritization list to the committee and submits information on each project, including its name, scope, net two- and five-year present value, total costs and benefits, priority number on the list, and strategic focus area. The IT committee then decides the overall portfolio of IT projects for the coming year (see Fig. 1 for the detailed process).

The number of project ideas and needs for IT solutions exceeds available resources, and a sequential prioritization process is established to reduce the final project portfolio so that it corresponds with the planned budget. The available resources and their distribution across departments within the IT unit are not fixed. To a certain extent, such budgetary constraints are an outcome of the prioritization process, in view of the quality and value of the proposed projects. The process is somewhat conservative in the sense that each PG will submit a prioritization list that has a ‘price tag’ in the same order as that in the previous year’s budget. The IT committee may move project proposals up or down on the prioritization list and exercises some discretion in placing the budgetary cut-off line. However, it is assumed that the total budget and its distribution across departments will not change radically in the process. The choice of the two departments of the IT unit for this study enabled us to investigate how differences in IT projects’ characteristics influence the use of evidence in the IT prioritization process.

## The use of evidence and judgment in IT project prioritization

In this section, we describe how decisions were made in each phase of the IT project prioritization process described above (see Fig. 1). The first two phases (the generation of project proposals and the competition of project proposals for a slot in the prioritization list) are distinct for each department. In the last phase, both departmental lists are consolidated along with the remaining departments’ lists, prior to the final approval of the IT project portfolio. We begin by analyzing the similarities and differences between the two departments in terms of their use of evidence in the first two phases. Then we analyze use of evidence in the consolidation processes of creating the overall IT project portfolio.

Phase one: Developing a project proposal – A yes or no decision

The departments prepared proposals based on new project ideas. Typically, the preparation of a project proposal included three main activities: generation of a new project idea, maturing the project proposal, and informal discussions concerning the project proposal. In this phase, a project proposal was accepted or rejected on its individual merits with reference to its feasibility and importance to the department.

## Generation of a new project idea

Project ideas were initiated either by the business units or by the IT departments. The proposals evolved through discussions between department managers, business representatives, IT project managers, and IT developers. When a project idea began to mature, a manager was assigned the task of preparing a memo describing the problem or opportunity that the new project would address, sketching out a cost-benefit analysis or at least a qualitative description of the project’s anticipated benefits. There was no official or formalized process for generating project ideas, and practices varied between the two departments.

\- Department A followed a centralized process. Input was provided by the IT and business managers based on the department’s roadmap:

‘‘As the secretary of the PG, I make the draft, but I gathered the information mainly from the department members . . . We have the high-level roadmap that is revisited every time, and this is actually the overall guideline for the development plan [new project proposals]’’ (Department A Manager).

\- In Department B, the development manager made an informal call:

‘‘We start by compiling data on the projects that are running . . . Then we involve the whole department . . . to come up with ideas, new ideas, or maybe old ideas that haven’t been realized yet. So we [the managers] made what you could consider a call for proposals to all our stakeholders. . . So we got many proposals, then we made a gross list of new projects that could be taken into consideration’’ (Department B Manager).

Idea generation was rather structured in Department A, where new requirements were derived from the roadmap. In Department B, the idea-generation process was more exploratory, soliciting ideas from a wide range of stakeholders and interested parties. In both cases, however, the process produced a list of ideas that needed to mature and eventually be structured into a prioritization list.

## Maturing the project proposal

Development managers took over responsibility for developing the proposal memos into full project proposals. This entailed producing more detailed cost estimates for each new project and contacting IT developers and IT project managers to investigate technical feasibility. The preliminary project proposal might also be reviewed internally by a business analyst from the IT unit. Once the review had been conducted, the development manager submitted the project proposal to the chairman of the corresponding PG (see Appendix B). During this phase, the project proposals were enhanced, and more detailed cost-benefit analyses were made following the formal procedure in the organization. The department manager also had bilateral meetings with the business units. These meetings focused on the roadmap.

The central topic of these discussions was the departmental roadmap and the extent to which the new project aligned with it. In Department B, we observe the department manager engaging intensively with his personal network to solicit sponsors and make the projects more attractive to a broader audience.

After the informal meetings, the development managers composed a preliminary project list for presentation at the PG meeting. In the first phase of the prioritization process, we observe that a project proposal included an estimation of costs and benefits. Equally important, however, were other sources of information indicating the general attitude toward and support for the project in informal networks of colleagues or by powerful people in the organization. Without positive attitudes and general support, a project proposal would not be presented to the PG for inclusion in the prioritization list. A project proposal could be eliminated at any time during this phase if any relevant party disputed its importance, its feasibility, or the validity of the cost-benefit analysis.

## Phase two: Developing the department’s portfolio – A competitive decision

The PG was in charge of producing the proposed portfolio, a prioritized list of project proposals. Each of the two PGs that we studied held meetings in the summer and autumn and decided on the list of prioritized projects that would constitute their respective department’s portfolio proposal for the following year. Each PG meeting lasted one to two hours, and the fate of each project proposal was decided on the basis of a comparison with other projects on the list. The development managers were responsible for managing the process and served as the PG secretaries, preparing a presentation with the agenda and the list of preliminary project proposals. Each project was presented in spreadsheets, containing standard information such as net present value, cost estimates, full-time employees (FTE), benefits, time schedule, sub-deliveries, and releases. The lists distinguished between (1) ongoing projects, (2) compliance projects undertaken in order to ensure the fulfillment of current legal requirements, and (3) new business projects. Projects were prioritized according to importance, profitability, and any other criteria perceived as significant by the prioritization groups. Projects competed for the scarce resources of each depart ment and ‘battled’ across a plethora of dimensions.

The cost-benefit ratio served as a starting point for discussing new projects, followed by project-specific arguments. Project facts were presented by a PG member and could be challenged by other members who did not understand the business rationale behind the project or did not trust the claimed benefits or cost savings.

In subsequent interviews, the IT managers highlighted the lack of transparency in the prioritization process: ‘‘From the moment I gave [the project proposal] to [the development manager] until I got the feedback [from the PG], I don’t know what happened. . . It’s rather a black box’’ (IT Manager).

By implication, it was unclear which information was used to determine which new projects were included in the portfolio. Strong project interdependencies made the full picture difficult to comprehend. Moreover, such interdependencies reduced the flexibility of implementation over the course of the year, for example when market developments and competitors’ activities called for re-prioritization. ‘‘There are so many interdependencies between areas and simultaneous effects, it is highly uncertain’’ (Senior IT Director).

Such interdependencies made it difficult to produce adequate information and supply it to decision makers. This was also true due to the technical complexity of the individual projects: ‘‘With the complexity of a project of that kind, we actually risk spending double as much money, and we really don’t know at this stage how complex it is’’ (Senior IT Director).

IT managers thus faced serious problems in using financial data as prioritization criteria because of the inherent inaccuracy of cost estimates and the conditionality of claimed benefits. In the interviews, IT managers expressed disbelief and a sense of irony concerning the estimates of costs and especially benefits. The lack of a formal approach toward assessing and discussing benefits only increased the managers’ disbelief. They viewed the IT project prioritization as a ‘‘Turkish bazaar’’ in which the person with the strongest voice prevailed. Whatever reservations individual managers might have had concerning the validity of the evidence for each project proposal, they also acknowledged that the collective prioritization process built and depended on such ‘facts’.

The fundamental dilemma faced in the IT project prioritization process was thus that, without undercutting the premise of evidence-based and rational decision-making (a premise on which the prioritization process was designed and implemented) the final prioritization decisions needed to be informed by other dimensions of the project, such as intangible aspects, complexity, and interdependencies. From our data, we extracted four generic tactics for loosening dependence of the outcome of the prioritization process on the submitted evidence and permitting consideration of other project dimensions.

## Substituting evidence

Managers substituted financial data with various information sources, such as their experiences, sponsors, or personal networks. They overruled the financial data and used their judgment as an argument for prioritizing a project when their judgment contradicted the evidence.

\- During a meeting in Department A, a development manager started challenging the inclusion of a project in the list, arguing from personal experience that this would have a negative impact on the department’s roadmap:

‘‘If we’re going for this proposal, it would have a tremendous effect on the consolidation project. That would not only incur risk, it would also incur cost, it would prolong the freeze period, and it would also delay the 2012 plan moving forward. It would be like going in and just adding another four months to everything’’ (Department A Development Manager).

At the same meeting, the senior business manager proposed that a project with a very strong cost-benefit analysis be included in the list. The department manager rejected the request and proposed an alternative solution in which he emphasized that the main requirement was the smooth continuance of the consolidation project:

‘‘Right now, we’re exploring a potential solution to fulfill the need without having any effect on the consolidation project. It won’t fulfill the requirement [since losses would be incurred], but it would fulfill the need without affecting the consolidation project’’ (Department A Senior Business Manager).

In Department A, the concerns were less about costs and benefits than about the new projects’ impact on the roadmap involving the consolidation project, i.e. the common loan platform. In order to build a common platform, the department could not select product solutions that would impact negatively on the consolidation project, no matter these solutions’ costs and benefits.

\- In Department B, the PG secretary presented a project of strategic importance that had a low cost-benefit ratio. Based on his expertise, he focused on the strategic importance of a project addressing a future need of the organization:

‘‘This [project] addresses the need to get ready, because, one day, we’ll have to get out of a very technical old solution that we have at the moment . . . [What] we’re doing here is actually building up competences and starting up a process that can handle the conversion of our old system. So we’ll use all of the acquired skills when we get the technical pressure of migrating the final things. Then we will be ready to do it [the migration to the new system]’’ (Department B PG Secretary).

After the meeting, the business manager explained a project based on his expertise: ‘‘I can only write it down and tell them that this is good for the customers, this is good for the adviser. I believe that, in the long run, it will be good for the business, but I can’t show the money’’ (Department B Business Manager).

One of the business representatives reports that, in the PG meetings, projects with a negative cost-benefit ratio often make the prioritized list:

‘‘There will be a negative cost-benefit, but they will actually take the decision anyway, so it will be based on knowledge and description, but it’s not been put into a formal weighting. So sometimes there is something there that’s actually not there (Department B Business Representative)

In Department B, calculations of costs and benefits enabled decision makers to define boundaries, i.e. what does and does not count. However, such boundaries invited arguments beyond the immediately countable. Factors that made a project relevant involved the efficiency and competitiveness of a business unit. How such competitiveness would translate to increased profitability is a matter of belief rather than calculations

## Supplementing evidence

Managers supplemented the financial data with other sources of information to support the data and make it more credible. They did so either by providing qualitative information or by asking for additional information about a project.

• During a meeting in Department A. a senior IT director required additional information on a project's costs: “I think it would be nice to unwrap [Project Y] at one of the future meetings to see what we actually spend . . . so we have an understanding of the quality, content, and more details and also the actual investments.’’

In the follow-up interview, the development manager uses personal expertise to designate the nature of the projects and compensate for a poor cost-benefits case:

‘‘For instance the digital banking loan, if you took that project as just one project and evaluated it on its own merits, it would be much farther down [the list]. But due to the fact that this is one link in the chain, in this huge program, it moves up due to prioritization and the benefit of the whole program. In Department A, when a project was considered critical for the roadmap and the available evidence was not satisfactory, other information sources were mobilized supplementing the cost-benefit ratio in the prioritization decision.

\- In Department B, a senior business representative explained the importance of intangible benefits for the prioritization decision:

‘‘I think you should do the best you can, within reason, to get a cost-benefit analysis in numerical terms and then say ‘This is one part of the equation, but then there are other parts of the equation which are the intangibles’’’ (Department B Senior Business Representative).

During a meeting, the prioritization of a project of minor financial benefit was challenged. However, a development director, who was the domain expert, argued:

‘‘There are a lot of other types of benefits that are not quantified, for instance customer focus, customer experience, and so on, which actually could pull up a project with a lower benefit. . . So I really must say that although it might seem as though the benefit is small, this is a foundational project for future work’’ (Department B Development Director).

Intangible benefits and labels (e.g. foundational project) assigning specific importance to a project were important in Department B. If a label was accepted, its relevance would be used to support the narrow calculations of costs and benefits. The inclusion of a project with minor benefits was thus not considered a loss but instead an investment in sustaining performance.

## Interpreting evidence

Managers used qualitative information such as their experience and personal networks to judge the accuracy of cost estimates and the reliability of benefits. This allowed them to interpret the figures and reduce ambiguity.

\- In Department A, a senior IT director explained that managers do not take facts at face value during the meetings: ‘‘This means that we are actually interpreting ‘what does this mean’, and based on our knowledge and understanding of it, we make provisions for it in the planning’’ (Department A Senior IT Director).

\- In Department B, managers interpreted financial data based on their experience, as the department manager explained: ‘‘We are doing the initial filtering because we can then see from experience; we can judge that this is not going to fly. If it’s being presented like this it’s not gonna fly, so we might as well take it off’’ (Department B Manager).

These practices rely on individual or collective judgments. Such judgments are not made haphazardly. As the interviewees mention, they are founded on experience, knowledge, and understanding. It is clear, however, that such experience, knowledge, and understanding need not belong to the decision makers themselves. A network of experts and important or powerful people in the organization may enable the decision maker to adequately judge and interpret the presented evidence.

## Reframing evidence

Managers recalculated financial data and other project parameters on the basis of their judgment. Their experience with prioritization processes and prior knowledge of ‘acceptable facts’ allowed them to recalculate the projects’ financial parameters.

\- During a meeting at Department A, the department manager presented three alternative means of developing a new project requested by the business unit. The first two alternatives would delay the consolidation project for four months while the third alternative meant that the project would be delivered after the completion of the consolidation project. In an attempt to accommodate the new project within the consolidation project time plan, the managers reframed the tim dimension of the product:

‘‘What if we just sell it [the product] as a three months’ loan, and then we take the loss. You make a variant of a loan that’s still based on one month stibor [interbank interest rate], but we sell it price-wise, and we market it as a three months’ loan, and then we have a little loss just like we have now, for three months’ longer period until we have the right product. Will that be doable?’’ (Department A Manager).

In Department A, managers redefined the scope of a project to align it with and avoid any negative impact on the consolidation project.

\- During a meeting in Department B, the CIO said: ‘‘Maybe, the right thing would be to show the benefit of the first step but also perhaps indicate the full potential, because the full potential is much bigger’’ (Department B CIO). Another Business Director added:

‘‘I think it could be nice to be sure when we have our presentation for the IT committee that we’ll be able, in one slide, to show them where we see the benefits, because it’s a little tricky to come up and say. ‘Well, we have this project here, and we don’t get anything out of it’’’ (Department B Business Director).

When reframing, Department B managers attempted to clarify other sources of information about the project benefits considered at the meeting, especially in relation to intangible benefits. The practice of reframing stemmed from the managers’ perception that evidence was mandatory, i.e. that the cost-benefit analysis was the primary criterion upon which the final decision would be made. As a result, they felt the need to match their judgments with what was perceived as a ‘good’ cost-benefit analysis from their superiors.

## Comparisons across departments

All four tactics were used in both departments, but the interplay between the evidence and other information sources differed between them.

\- In Department A, the IT projects primarily involved incremental changes to the consolidation project, the loan platform. New projects related to platform improvements were easy to place on the roadmap. However, new projects related to new products on the platform were not easily accepted as they influenced the platform’s cost and time schedule. Discussions focused mainly on cost reduction, tangible benefits, and the time plan, and just a few business units were involved. We observed managers largely using information derived from internal expertise of the IT unit to align any new project with the consolidation project. They strengthened the projects’ trustworthiness by providing arguments concerning maintaining the loan platform’s performance

\- In Department B, IT projects involved new products or services to serve specific business and market needs. There were many business units involved and many different types of project proposals. The discussions revolved around indirect costs, intangible benefits, and strategic directions. Managers in Department B mainly relied on various sources of qualitative information to better describe the projects’ intangible assets. The various information sources mobilized by managers in Department B were used primarily for making the projects seem more desirable or more ‘commercial’.

During IT project prioritization at the PG level, evidence (such as cost-benefit calculations) played a central role as a legitimate starting point for discussions concerning a project. At the same time, because of cost-benefit analysis’ limitations when making the actual prioritization decisions, we observed that the prevalence of tactics prevented participants from being caught out and held to account for not using cost-benefit analysis alone. Evidence was more important as a topic of discussion than as a criterion for prioritization decisions. Eventually, the PGs agreed on a prioritization list of IT projects for the coming year. These lists were submitted to the IT Project Portfolio Management Office for further consolidation and analysis.

## Phase three: Developing the IT unit portfolio – Nested decisions

The final phase of the annual prioritization process involved the establishment of the IT portfolio across the various prioritization lists submitted by the 21 PGs. Once the PGs had submitted the project lists for the coming year, these lists were forwarded to the Project Portfolio Management Office, which was responsible for consolidating the lists and preparing the information to be used in the final step of the prioritization process, the IT Committee meeting

## Consolidation and analysis of the PGs’ prioritization lists

The Project Portfolio Management Office was responsible for creating the ‘Master Plan’, a spreadsheet providing detailed information on all projects in the 21 lists. The project portfolio managers then analyzed and consolidated this information. The analysis included the composition of the project portfolio in terms of business drivers and projects’ costs and benefits as well as per prioritization group. The project portfolio managers also prepared a report with information on all projects suggested by each prioritization group for the IT committee members. Information for each project included its priority rank, strategic focus area, name, releases, short project description, total cost, and net present value within a two- and five-year horizon. This analysis was later forwarded to the IT committee members, to be used during their meeting.

## Presentation of the prioritized projects list

The IT committee was responsible for deciding on the overall IT portfolio. The six IT committee members (the CIO, COO, CFO, senior representative of the business units, and two members of the executive board (see Appendix B)) met for this

During the IT Committee meeting, the chairman of each PG (usually a Business Director) gave a half-hour presentation of the prioritized projects list. The PG chairman presented the projects approved in the PG meetings and outlined their strategic importance to the financial institution. The development director, who was an expert in the IT issues of the department in question, was present as well. Following the presentation, the chairman and the director were challenged by the committee members on the number and priority of the projects and the rationale behind the group’s overall prioritization. The members asked many questions in order to investigate interdependencies between projects and the consequences of cutting projects from the list. The chairman and the IT director were invited to argue and explain why the IT committee should approve the projects they proposed.

The members of the IT committee made no secret of their reliance on the expertise of the IT managers who had constructed the various prioritization lists. They had little ground for doubting the previous prioritization decisions. However, they acknowledged the difficulties in comparing benefits between projects, especially between projects on different prioritization lists: ‘‘[How] can you compare the financial benefit of a better work force compared to the indirect benefits of people using mobile banking and thinking we’re an innovative bank? It is impossible’’ (Interview with the COO after the IT Committee’s final prioritization meeting).

Managers also emphasized the lack of justification for the claimed benefits and the absence of accountability for such estimates. In fact, they expressed a distrust of cost-benefit calculations in general, especially when the numbers looked unrealistic or when they were framed in an unusual way: “If they had focused too much on the benefits. I would have reacted because I don’t believe our benefit calculations’’ (CFO).

Senior managers underlined the importance of trusting the presenter from the PG since benefit estimations are not always accurate. In this context, the presenter’s confidence and expertise were important in countering skepticism: ‘‘I would trust that the PG would give me a prioritization where they have looked into the benefits not by calculation but by their knowledge’’ (CFO).

Committee members were likewise concerned about their ability to acquire an overview and holistic perspective of the portfolio. Project interdependencies complicated the IT prioritization process and budget allocation for each area. Budget cuts in one area might unintentionally exert a negative impact on projects in other areas. These project interdependencies could only be discovered through deep involvement in and investigation of project details. Given the impossibility of going into the details to such an extent, IT committee members were reliant on the experience and domain expertise of IT managers for understanding the project portfolio and the inherent interdependencies that should influence resource allocation. A recurring question asked of IT directors during the IT committee meeting was how removing one project from the list would affect other projects.

Informal and spontaneous communications were very important within the IT committee. The members had to gain understanding of the IT unit’s needs and of the estimations provided for each project: ‘‘You can’t read it from the paper. You need to understand them and . . . just talk to people’’ (Senior Representative of the Business Units).

In these informal debates and interactions at the meeting, PG representatives were dependent on convincing at least one committee member of the importance of the respective projects. This turned the committee members into sponsors for their specific prioritization list at the IT committee meeting. The more powerful the sponsorship, the fewer questions were asked about specific projects and thus the easier it was for the project prioritization list to be accepted: ‘‘During the meeting, we saw that because one of the committee members was strongly advocating this, then it wasn’t questioned any more by the other committee members’’ (CFO).

## Approval of the prioritization lists

After the PG lists were presented, a private meeting took place at which the IT committee members decided the final prioritization, taking into account the group list and budget of each PG. During this phase, IT committee members focused on the overall strategy and decided which PG budgets they should increase or decrease in the coming year.

The IT committee members described how they used evidence such as financial data and other sources of information in the meeting. During the meeting, they used the tactic of supplementing financial data with information based on the input of IT managers’ experiences and domain expertise. For example, when the PG director was presenting at the meeting, his opinions were generally trusted because of his strong expertise. Such solicited judgments and opinions complemented the basic fact sheet (the Master Plan) with important contextual information.

In general, the IT committee members had little reason for or possibility of distrusting the information provided by specific domain experts or their personal networks along with the financial data about the projects: ‘‘You need to be able to have some hard evidence, but I am really confident in this as [being] the right decision when I hear the soft parts surrounding it’ (Senior Representative of the Business Units).

Sometimes, they sought information in order to enrich the data provided for each project: ‘‘I went through every single project with one of my employees who is involved with most prioritization groups and asked ‘What does that mean?’’’ (COO).

The CIO, who was participating in the meeting, although not a member, emphasized the importance of sponsorship for granting legitimacy to projects with intangible benefits: ‘‘It will be great if five or six people for example said, ’We all believe this would have a considerable benefit’’’.

Sponsorship from other committee members or from various domain experts and IT managers did not prevent committee members from exercising their own judgment. In the absence of information and expertise, however, such judgments were based on heuristics, such as the convincingness of the presenter’s performance. Being unable to judge the financial data of a project, they could at least judge the presenters’ performances. A good performance was taken as indicative that a good project was being presented. Since these variations in the presentations were shared by all members of the IT committee, these heuristics offered more uniform judgments than provided by the reading of complex and somewhat incomprehensible IT project descriptions. Presenters’ performances created a common reference point for committee members’ judgments and decisions: ‘‘During the presentations, you get the impression that, okay, this person has his shop under control and what he says is probably something I should trust as a manager’’ (COO).

While practices of substituting and supplementing evidence were common in the prioritization process on the IT Committee, the other two tactics (interpreting and reframing the facts by making judgments) were employed less frequently. Furthermore, the individual projects were considered in the context of the prioritization lists in which they were submitted. Mostly, the decision covered the lists as a whole. Strong interdependencies and complexities discouraged the committee from removing individual project proposals and changing too many of the priorities across the PG prioritization lists. The decision-making process had the character of ensuring a rationale for the overall distribution of resources, not the rationale for each particular project. As in most other departments, the two departments we studied had their prioritization lists approved without change. The IT Committee found no reason to question the rationality and legitimacy of the priority decisions made at the level of the department PG.

## Discussion of the findings and contributions

In this study, we investigated an organization that has formally structured its IT project prioritization process by identifying and constructing a set of objective criteria or technologies of rationality (March, 2006) and by institutionalizing the collection of evidence (i.e. financial data) for these criteria. While these elements are obligatory, the support they offered and the degree to which they characterized the prioritization task varied from case to case, depending on the nature of the IT project and the phase of the prioritization process. Our analysis revealed that evidence is not enough for reaching an IT project prioritization decision. In particular, decision makers mobilized private information, expertise, and interpersonal trust for use alongside evidence in the decision-making. We identified four tactics that enabled managers to reach a decision, depending on the available evidence and other information sources.

IT project prioritization is a longitudinal process in which a proposed project must go through three different phases before it can be approved. A project proposal will succeed in being financed if a number of elements are in place, which underline its value at the individual, group, and organizational levels. The first phase revolves around the individual project proposal and its characteristics as well as their alignment with the department’s roadmap. The second phase involves the project’s comparative value relative to competing projects within the group. Our findings showed that the main prioritization decisions are made during the second phase, when the members produce and document their proposed prioritization list. The third phase focuses on a project’s impact as part of a program or its interdependencies with other IT projects in the organization.

The special characteristics of IT projects render prioritization a complex decision-making problem. We saw at both departments that technological interdependencies make it difficult to separate and compare specific projects’ dimensions or characteristics. This confirms previous findings (Elbanna, 2010) that projects are not fully defined entities, which makes comparison between projects even more difficult. In addition, underlying project complexities increase the perceived uncertainty of a project’s outcome (Howcroft and McDonald, 2007). This was most clearly observed in Department A. As we observed in Department B, IT projects are only partially quantifiable since intangible benefits cannot generally be accurately measured. Acknowledging the importance of such intangibles, any rank ordering of project proposals becomes contestable (Espeland and Stevens, 1998; Tallon and Kraemer, 2007; Karpik, 2010). When the various prioritization lists were not comparable on the basis of evidence, comparisons were made on other bases in order to limit contestability.

In an attempt to overcome the above, managers employ four tactics when using evidence in their decision-making processes. Knowing that reality is wider and more complex than what any cost-benefit analysis can express, managers discretely use their judgment as a means of including other aspects to influence the ultimate decision. It is worth noting that, in a very few cases, and only under specific conditions, did the decision makers rely solely on evidence to make a decision. This usually happens when projects have simple technical solutions, low costs, and tangible benefits with a return on investment within one or two years. The managers refer to these projects as ‘‘low-hanging fruit.’’ Table 3 presents how various perceptions of evidence lead to the use of four different tactics in the prioritization process.

The judgments used in the four tactics involve a number of judgment devices (Karpik, 2010). Judgment devices mobilize private information, expertise, and interpersonal trust to support decision makers in shaping individual judgments and reaching collective prioritization decisions for IT projects. Five judgment devices were observed: networks, sponsors, labels. domain expertise, and forums (see Appendix C for a detailed definition of each judgment device). Managers consult personal networks to substantiate a certain ‘reading’ of the data and to build trust in their own impressions. They seek to transfer responsibility for making judgments by locating and relying on experts and sponsors. They infer the importance of the project from the soundness of its presentation at the meeting. Furthermore, managers judge the value of a project by labeling the underlying problem or situation in terms of seriousness and criticality. All such efforts help make prioritization decisions possible. Depending on how managers perceive evidence, they use a device or a configuration of devices for or against an IT project prioritization decision.

During the competitive decisions phase, we observed different uses of tactics and judgment devices in the two departments due to the different natures of each department’s IT projects. In Department A, where projects consist of incremental improvements to the loan platform, we found that judgment devices were used to provide more information on a project’s alignment with the departmental roadmap and its impact on the platform. As a result, when there were doubts about the costs or benefits of a project, judgment devices were used to emphasize its centrality relative to the roadmap as a keystone for future projects. In Department B, where projects were more product and customer driven, judgment devices were used to describe or reframe a project’s intangible benefits and to allow decision makers to rank projects that could not be directly compared. It seems that when IT projects are innovative and incommensurable, managers use judgment devices to make a project more attractive and desirable, highlighting its value-adding attributes on an organizational level.

In the final phase of the prioritization process, we observed that IT committee members used the tactics of supplementing and substituting evidence with judgment devices. These members were somewhat distanced from the calculation of costs and benefits, which (might) result in significant errors in interpretation. They thus avoided engaging in interpretations. Moreover, reframing was not practiced because the IT Committee was the ultimate approval body, reportable to no one. If the members disagreed with the cost-benefit analysis, they could simply substitute it with other judgment devices without the necessity of reframing.

After considering the findings for the different phases of the prioritization process, a common observation is that decision makers are doubtful of the validity and relevance of evidence in the form of costs and benefits in decision making. It is thus rather mystifving that. although participants expressed these doubts concerning evidence, the doubts were not a focus of attention and discussion at the meetings. Deconstructing the designed procedure by neglecting the evidence was not an option, but neither was taking the evidence literally. Presenting the evidence came to constitute a constructive starting point, necessitating the development of alternative criteria for promoting or discarding project proposals. The tactics thus provided participants with an additional toolbox for the prioritization decisions. The evidence did not in itself enable rational deci sions, but the presentation and perceptions of evidence set in motion the tactics that led to prioritization decisions.

## Theoretical contribution

Our findings contribute to the academic discussion on the scope of evidence-based management and in particular the use of evidence in strategic decision-making in the IS field. As mentioned above, the IT project portfolio – more specifically, the prioritization process – is recognized ‘‘as an integral component of IS strategic planning’’ (Daniel et al., 2014, p. 97). Much of the research on IT project portfolios, as explained in the literature review, offers prescriptions grounded mainly in a rational, evidence-based management approach (Howcroft and McDonald, 2007). By systematically describing the interplay between evidence and managerial judgment, we shed light on how evidence is used in practice during the IT project prioritization process. This may be a long way from the ideals of rational choice, based on evidence and calculation, without the involvement of biased interference from decision-makers (Archer and Ghasemzadeh, 1999). But it may also approach an achievable degree of rationality under real world conditions – conditions that render the theory of rational choice a poor theory because it excludes and ignores the very processes that enable choices to be made.

Previous studies recognize the problems of quantifying the costs and benefits of IT projects (Jeffery and Leliveld, 2004), yet they do not investigate how managers cope with the problem of incommensurability. Our study shows that when projects are treated as non-comparable entities, financial data is insufficient since it functions to reduce projects to comparable numbers. This, in turn, renders analytical portfolio methods insufficient criteria for the project prioritization process, confirming previous findings (Farbey et al., 1999; Howcroft and McDonald, 2007). As seen in the interview quotes, allowing subjectively, individually, or socially constructed evaluations of projects and their rank ordering play a role in a priority decision is not a source of distortion but instead a source of ‘objectivity’ – ‘objectivity’ in the sense of being meaningful in a world full of ambiguity and uncertainty, underlining the need for more interpretive portfolio methods (Frisk et al., 2014) and the inclusion of more perceptual measures in the process (Tallon and Kraemer, 2007). This is not to deny that individual biases and stakeholders’ interests may make the outcome suboptimal in some respects; however, any other means of accomplishing the prioritization decision task would be suboptimal in other respects. To neglect the role of judgment and social interaction would not be a path toward better decisions and would significantly reduce our ability to learn from the empirical processes of making portfolio choices and prioritizing IT projects in practice.

This study went beyond some of the traditional concerns of the IS community – such as how to effectively select and prioritize projects and portfolios (Archer and Ghasemzadeh, 1999) – by highlighting the longitudinal nature of the prioritization process and examining the interplay between evidence and managerial judgment during this process. The study reveals how this interplay is deeply implicated in completing the prioritization task. By utilizing the concept of judgment devices alongside the concept of evidence, we have enabled a rich understanding of the decisions involved and how managers reach them. Rather than stopping at the conclusion that rational methods alone are insufficient (Smithson and Hirschheim, 1998; Walsham, 1999; Howcroft and McDonald, 2007), the focus on judgment devices provides insight into how individual and collective judgments are shaped and illustrates how project prioritization becomes possible when evidence is not enough. Judgment devices are important for the IT prioritization process because they enable the introduction of social, political, and cultural dimensions necessary for obtaining a full picture of the prioritization task (Farbey et al., 1999; Howcroft and McDonald, 2007). The tactics of using evidence and judgment devices highlight the interplay between rational and interpretive perspectives (Frisk et al., 2014) in IT project prioritization.

Recently, Kester et al. (2011) claimed that different decision-making models (i.e. evidence-based, opinion/judgment-based, and power-based models) are simultaneously present in prioritization processes. However, Kester et al. (2011) do not clarify how the interactions between these models play out and how managers accommodate them in the decision-making process. Our study sheds light on the interactions between these models by depicting the interplay between evidence and judgment devices. This interplay provides an in-depth understanding of the mechanisms used in prioritization processes.

The study also contributes to the evidence-based management literature by demonstrating the limited scope of evidence-based practices when decisions involve highly uncertain outcomes and projects that are incommensurable. While the current literature examines decision-making by focusing on evidence-based, analytical approaches (Rousseau, 2006b), we show that decision-making is an interplay between evidence-based, reliable, and valid calculations on the one hand and subjective evaluations based on judgment on the other. Analytically, we can distinguish evidence from judgment, but in practice, they seem to fade into an interplay characterized by the tactics described above. Decision makers use different tactics depending on how they perceive the available evidence. Tingling and Brydon (2010) claim that evidence can make, inform, and support decisions without taking into account decision makers’ perceptions of evidence. We show that managers’ perceptions of evidence influence how evidence is used in the decision-making process. We found that different perceptions of evidence lead to different tactics (i.e. the interplay of evidence with judgment devices), which allow decision makers to reach prioritization decisions.

Finally, our findings support Briner et al. (2009) in suggesting that evidence-based management is more than just the practical application of scientific evidence and domain knowledge. In particular, we have investigated how managers reach an IT project prioritization decision when evidence is not enough and judgment devices come into play. Evidence remains important during decision-making, but it does not necessarily determine the outcome of the decision. It gains saliency because it becomes a central focus of discussion, reflection, and negotiation. In this sense, evidence does not close the dia logue but instead starts and directs the dialogue prior to a decision being made.

## Practical implications

Our findings show that the process of reaching a prioritization decision is dominated less by calculation than by individual judgment and social interactions. The organizing of an elaborate, gradual process of prioritization itself testifies to the inadequacy of evidence in the form of cost-benefit calculations. Such evidence must be supplemented, substituted, interpreted, negotiated, and ‘subjectified’ into something that people trust to be fair, correct, and constructive

We identified a process-oriented toolbox of devices that can be used in many situations, sometimes by supplementing and other times by substituting, reframing, or interpreting evidence. This toolbox is used when the underlying ambiguity of a project’s characteristics (i.e. incommensurable properties due to complexity, interdependencies, or intangible benefits) challenges the accuracy and reliability of evidence for measuring specific project parameters.

The interplay of evidence with judgment devices during IT project prioritization decisions brings managerial judgment to the center stage. Our focus on judgment devices emphasizes the important role that judgment plays both in decision-making and in how evidence is used in strategic decisions. More and more organizations and practitioners focus on and idealize the role of evidence in strategic decision-making, selection, and prioritization decisions. This study calls on practitioners to cater not only to evidence-based management in organizations but also tools and methods supporting the use of managerial judgment. Focusing on which judgment devices managers do and do not use and how and when they use them can help managers make better-informed decisions while making the process more transparent and comprehensible to members of the organization. Focusing solely on rational methods and ignoring judgment devices when communicating the IT project prioritization process does not allow the organization to reflect on its actual practices and instead imposes a rational process that only confuses and frustrates organization members who continually stumble upon a different reality. In complex situations and when dealing with loosely defined problems, judgment is the most important managerial decision-making tool. It is thus important for managers to reflect on how judgments are informed and shaped as well as which sources and devices are used.

## Conclusion and further research

We investigated how managers use evidence in IT project prioritization. Our findings highlight the interplay between evidence and managerial judgment when reaching a decision on IT investments. We identified a process-oriented toolbox of judgment devices and four tactics characterizing the interplay between judgment and evidence in IT project prioritization. We find the scope of evidence-based practices to be constrained in decisions that involve highly uncertain outcomes and projects that are incommensurable.

Our findings should be interpreted with caution since they are based in a single longitudinal case study. This allowed for an in-depth understanding of the specific process in question but may not be directly transferrable to other organizations. Further research should be conducted in different contexts to investigate the applicability of the judgment devices and tactics identified in this study of strategic decisions. Our findings should be further explored in the area of IT portfolio management. Additionally, current technological developments enable alternative approaches to collecting data to inform an organization’s strategy. Future research should investigate how new technologies may influence the decision-making practices uncovered by this study.

## C.1. Definitions of judgment devices found in this study

A network consists of a decision maker’s friends, contacts, and colleagues. The decision maker communicates informally with this network to better understand a project proposal by acquiring reliable information and to shape the collective judgment of the prioritization group.

Sponsors are people who hold power in the organization deriving from their positions or expertise, which in turn serves as a guarantee for the information they provide. The greater the number of sponsors or the greater their authority in the organization, the more substantial their influence on the collective judgment and the more confident decision makers felt about their collective choice.

Labels are names or categories associated with a project’s attributes and/or nature, which help to quickly designate and select the type of project in question.

Domain expertise comes from people who provide specific information concerning a project and its respective domain context.

The forum involves the presentation activities of a project, which bring decision makers together with the prioritization group representatives (chairmen and development directors). This device shapes the collective judgment of the IT committee members through the shared experience of the presentations. In particular, the presenters used the forum to influence IT committee members. Meanwhile, the committee members used the forum to better understand the available evidence and projects as well as to gain commitment from the presenters in the implementation of their plans, building trust as the members moved forward to make a decision on the final portfolio.
