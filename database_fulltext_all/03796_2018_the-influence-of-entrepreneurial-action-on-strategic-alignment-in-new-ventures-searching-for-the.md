## Introduction

The alignment of IT strategy with business strategy has been among the top concerns of business leaders for several decades (Kappelman et al., 2014; Niederman et al., 1991). This interest from practitioners has stimulated researchers to produce a voluminous body of literature where definitions and dimensions of alignment have been proposed, measures and models have been developed, and antecedents and outcomes have been identified (Chan and Reich, 2007). Interest in alignment is driven by the reality that there are important organizational performance benefits for firms that achieve a high degree of alignment (Gerow et al., 2015).

Our purpose in this paper is to examine the development of strategic alignment in the specific context of new ventures. We choose to focus on alignment in new ventures for two reasons. First, we do so to examine whether different patterns of entrepreneurial action in such firms have implications for the development of strategic alignment. Researchers studying new ventures have observed that there are discernible differences in the way that entrepreneurs launch and grow their businesses and have therefore constructed multiple theoretical perspectives to explain patterns of entrepreneurial action (Fisher, 2012). Our objectives in examining new ventures with varying patterns of entrepreneurial action are to understand whether some patterns of entrepreneurial action are more likely to lead to a high degree of strategic alignment than others, and also to understand whether the organizational activities and processes that lead to strategic alignment differ in firms that display differing patterns of entrepreneurial action. Second, we focus on strategic alignment in new ventures in order to explore the genesis of alignment. One specific area of interest within strategic alignment research is the study of how alignment develops and changes in an organization over time. Researchers have described stages of growth in how firms strategically use IT (Burn, 1993), phases in the pursuit of alignment (Street, 2006), and a model of punctuated equilibrium where extended periods of stability with evolutionary change are interrupted by short periods of rapid transformation with revolutionary change (Sabherwal et al., 2001). Few, if any though. have examined the origin of strategic alignment from its very beginning within a firm. To date, and to our knowledge, all research on alignment examines existing processes and routines, within both small and large firms, and takes place in a context where the organizations studied are already in existence. By examining new ventures, our objective is to develop a deeper understanding of the inception of alignment and observe the decisions made and actions taken as alignment begins. The research question guiding our efforts in this paper therefore asks: ‘‘How does the pattern of entrepreneurial action exhibited in a new venture form and then shape the development of strategic alignment?”

The paper is organized as follows. We first review relevant literature, beginning with a description of strategic alignment and a discussion of new ventures, then move to explain theories of entrepreneurial action as well as these theories’ potential implications for IT strategy development and strategic alignment. In the methodology section, we describe our positivist case study approach. The case studies describe the formation and growth of two new ventures, focusing on the patterns of entrepreneurial action at each. We analyze our case studies using the alternate templates methodology (Allison, 1971) and highlight that our two firms represent differing patterns of entrepreneurial action. We go on to note both the differing ways in which strategic alignment is pursued and the outcomes at each firm. In the Discussion section, we build on our results to describe patterns of alignment-related activities at each type of new venture, and to explain differences and similarities in the degree of alignment that is achieved. Most important, we also observe how aspects of the new venture context provide clues about the manner in which alignment will be pursued at a firm. Our primary contribution is thus a description of how the pattern of entrepreneurial action in a new venture shapes the development of strategic alignment.

## Literature review

## Strategic alignment

Alignment exists when an organization’s goals and activities and the information systems that support them remain in harmony (McKeen and Smith, 2003). Alignment between business and IT is thus the degree of fit, integration, or consistency among a firm’s business strategy, IT strategy, business infrastructure and processes, and IT infrastructure and processes (Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993). Examining these components, researchers have identified six specific types of alignment: (1) the integration of business strategy with IT strategy, which is referred to as intellectual or strategic alignment; (2) the integration of business infrastructure and processes with IT infrastructure and processes, referred to as operationa alignment; (3) the integration of IT strategy with IT infrastructure and processes, referred to as IT alignment; (4) the integration of business strategy with organizational infrastructure and processes, referred to as business alignment; (5) the integration of business strategy with IT infrastructure and processes, which is a type of cross-domain alignment; and (6) the integration of IT strategy with organizational infrastructure and processes, also a type of cross-domain alignment (Gerow et al., 2015, 2014).

In the current study, we focus primarily on the first type of alignment mentioned above, the alignment of IT strategy with business strategy. This type of alignment has been formally defined as ‘‘. . .the degree to which the information technology mission, objectives, and plans support and are supported by the business mission, objectives, and plans” (Reich and Benbasat, 2000, p. 82). We refer to this type of alignment as strategic alignment, emphasizing that we examine the alignment of strategies rather than the alignment of infrastructures or processes as in the other five types of alignment (Baker et al., 2011; Sabherwal et al., 2001).<sup>1</sup> We focus on the alignment of strategies because of the paramount importance of strategy in shaping the overall purpose, mission, processes, and structure of a firm. The vision for and of a firm can have considerable influence on the activities and practices that initially shape alignment.

While much research has been conducted on alignment, with multiple comprehensive reviews extant (Chan and Reich, 2007; Gerow et al., 2015, 2014; Karpovsky and Galliers, 2015), what remains understudied in this body of work is the man ner in which alignment begins to take shape, the activities that support it, and then how it develops and changes in an organization over time. There are recognized stages of growth in how firms strategically use IT (Burn, 1993), and phases through which organizations pass in the pursuit of alignment (Street, 2006). There is also a punctuated equilibrium model of strategic alignment that explains that extended periods of alignment stability are interrupted by short periods of rapid transformation that prompt re-alignment (Sabherwal et al., 2001). Nevertheless, a study of how alignment is initiated, and whether there is variation across firms in how this takes place, is yet to be undertaken. It is this need that we seek to address

New ventures represent an ideal context in which to investigate the genesis of alignment. The inception of alignment, and the processes and activities that support it even in its earliest stages will be clearly on display in new ventures. The ways in which alignment originates may differ from how it is promoted and supported in more commonly-studied established firms. We now therefore turn to a discussion of new ventures.

## New ventures

New ventures are the specific context of this study. Researchers nevertheless recognize that new ventures share some common characteristics with two other distinct categories of firms, entrepreneurial firms and SMEs. We pause here to distinguish between these three categories. The term new venture generally refers to the origination of an independent entity, a new profit center within an established multi-business firm, or a new joint venture that is considered a market entrant by competitors or potential customers and with anticipated returns in future time periods (Gartner, 1985). An entrepreneurial firm is one that has a deliberate strategic intent to (1) create value by recognizing and exploiting opportunities, (2) develop novel combinations of resources, and (3) produce some valued outcome that can be applied to a practical problem (Shane and Venkataraman, 2000). An SME is a firm that is defined in terms of its size and resource characteristics. SMEs are defined in Europe as ‘‘enterprises which employ fewer than 250 persons and which have an annual turnover not exceeding 50 million euro, and/or an annual balance sheet total not exceeding 43 million euro” (European Commission, 2003). In the United States, the criteria for SMEs vary depending on industry, ownership structure, revenue, and number of employees; generally, the number of employees must be less than 500 (Small Business Administration, 2013).

While these three categories of new ventures, entrepreneurial firms, and SMEs share some common characteristics, they still have substantive differences (Shane and Venkataraman, 2000; Zahra, 2005). Not all new ventures are small, such as when an existing large firm spins off a division or launches a joint venture with another large entity. New ventures display varying levels of entrepreneurial activity depending on the novelty of their product or service. Some are highly entrepreneurial, developing a completely new product or service, while other new ventures are only slightly entrepreneurial, exploiting existing opportunities with an existing product. For example, a new restaurant, a tailor, or a small market/convenience store represent existing types of businesses and provide existing products and services. Such new ventures would be found at the less-entrepreneurial end of the spectrum of new ventures. Similarly, not all entrepreneurial firms are new ventures or SMEs, since many existing, large firms may be entrepreneurial in character. And finally, not all SMEs need be new ventures since some firms remain small for many years, and SMEs may not be entrepreneurial at all

Because new ventures are the focus of this study, and are built in differing ways, we now review literature on entrepreneurial strategy, followed by the literature on enacting that strategy in patterns of entrepreneurial action. We do so in order to explore how these theoretical perspectives on business origination and growth may have implications for the inception and ongoing support of strategic alignment.

## Entrepreneurial strategy

Entrepreneurial strategy describes a deliberate process where individuals and teams create value by bringing together novel combinations of resources to exploit opportunities in the environment that results in new ventures, products, services, processes, and technologies (Morris, 1998). Entrepreneurship also refers to a process of creating new means of production, opening new markets and new sources of supply, creating new ventures, and either doing new things or doing things that are already being done in a new way (Schumpeter, 1934). Similar definitions refer to the ability to recognize and act on new opportunities (Kirzner, 1973), the ability to engage in novel acts of innovation as part of a firm’s core strategy (Drucker, 1985), and as the process of taking on financial, psychological, and social risks in creating something new and valuable and receiving the resulting monetary and personal rewards (Hisrich and Peters, 1998). Researchers also recognize the limitations in defining entrepreneurship as a singular characteristic, and more practically view it as a continuum where firms exhibiting significant opportunity-seeking behavior anchor one end of the continuum, and firms with no opportunityseeking behavior anchor the other (Barringer and Bluedorn, 1999). New ventures can be examined in light of their entrepreneurial strategy and type of entrepreneurial action because all new ventures are to some degree entrepreneurial.

To the extent that an organization experiments with new ideas (Brown and Eisenhardt, 1997), co-evolves with emerging industries (Rindova and Kotha, 2001), and follows experiential, flexible, improvisational strategies (Eisenhardt and Tabrizi, 1995), that organization can be characterized as entrepreneurial. Entrepreneurial organizations are compelled to avoid – for as long as possible – the organizational inertia and institutionalism effects that inhibit fast response and experimentation in established firms (Dobrev et al., 2003). For entrepreneurial firms, the absence of well-understood business rules or models can be beneficial because there are fewer constraints. The more entrepreneurial the firm is, the less likely it is to be limited in its activities by established patterns of activity, and the more likely it is to pursue new opportunities that other firms miss (Ahuja and Lampert, 2001). Thus, an entrepreneurial organization is one that has a deliberate strategic intent to create value by recognizing and exploiting opportunities in the environment by developing novel combinations of resources and producing some valued outcome that can be applied to a practical problem.

## Patterns of entrepreneurial action in new ventures

How entrepreneurial strategies are sanctioned and enacted depends on the action<sup>2</sup> of entrepreneurs and the cumulative activities of their venture. By observing patterns of decision-making within new ventures, Fisher (2012) and others have examined compared and contrasted various theories of entrepreneurial action (Andries et al 2013: Nummela et al 2014: Sarasvathy, 2001, 2008). These patterns are often divided into either traditional theories, referred to as the ‘‘causation” perspective on entrepreneurial action (Casson, 1982; Kihlstrom and Laffont, 1979), and emerging theories, which are referred to as the ‘‘effectuation” (Sarasvathy, 2001) and ‘‘bricolage” (Baker and Nelson, 2005) perspectives on entrepreneurial action.

The causation perspective is the traditional perspective on entrepreneurial action. The entrepreneur identifies and evaluates opportunities, establishes goals, formulates a business plan, acquires resources, and generally takes a rational, deliberate, and intentional approach to new venture creation (Baker et al., 2003; Delmar and Shane, 2003; Katz and Gartner, 1988; Sarasvathy, 2001, 2008; Shah and Tripsas, 2007; Shane and Venkataraman, 2000). In contrast, the effectuation perspective explains that in uncertain and dynamic environments, the traditional causation model is inappropriate. The entrepreneur’s goals develop and emerge over time rather than being planned beforehand, and the entrepreneur focuses on the means at his or her disposal rather than on goals (Sarasvathy, 2001, 2008; Sarasvathy and Dew, 2005). These means are the resources available to the entrepreneur, including knowledge, skills, and social networks. They also include physical, human, and organizational resources (Barney, 1991), and the entrepreneur focuses on these means because they are relatively stable and under the entrepreneur’s direct control, a key consideration in uncertain environments. The bricolage perspective on entrepreneurial action provides yet another contrast to causation. Bricolage means ‘‘making do by applying combinations of resources at hand to new problems and opportunities” (Baker and Nelson, 2005, p. 33). In penurious environments, the bricoleur-entrepreneur considers the available inputs, the regulatory and institutional constraints, the existing customers, and develops novel recombinations of these elements to create and build new markets in uncertain environments (Baker et al 2003). Table 1 provides additional detail on these three perspectives.

Given these three very different perspectives on building a new venture, it seems that there would be potentially farreaching implications for the way that strategic planning processes and strategic alignment develop. One potentially fruitful area of inquiry is what implications the causation, effectuation, and bricolage perspectives have for IT in the new venture. The new venture context will reveal whether there are specific patterns of action that are more (or less) likely to yield a high degree of alignment.

For example, it might be expected that only causation-type entrepreneurial action yields a high degree of strategic alignment because only this type of entrepreneurial action leads to clear strategic plans and well-defined goals, which are commonly seen as prerequisites for strategic alignment. It might also be expected that effectuation-type entrepreneurial action makes a high level of strategic alignment very difficult to achieve because it occurs in less-predictable environments, and is characterized by entrepreneurs’ experimentation rather than consistency. Finally, it might be expected that bricolage-type entrepreneurial action leads to extremely low levels of strategic alignment because of severe resource constraints, and also because of the makeshift responses to challenges and opportunities.

Discernable differences in alignment outcome because of a particular pattern of entrepreneurial action will be noteworthy – as would be a finding that all entrepreneurial action leads equally to alignment. We now search for evidence that the pattern of entrepreneurial action shapes strategic alignment in the following description of our methodology and two indepth case studies.

## Methodology

A retrospective case study methodology following an exploratory positivist approach (Dube and Pare, 2003; Yin, 2009) was selected for this research. The cases were originally developed as part of a larger research study on strategic alignment processes (citation omitted during review). There were four reasons for choosing the case method. First, the literature comparing types of entrepreneurial action is still growing and case studies are a useful technique for exploring and validating emerging research concepts (Yin, 2009). Second, patterns of entrepreneurial action take shape through a complex process involving multiple influences within a single firm (Mintzberg and Lampel, 1999), and since case studies involve in-depth interactions with founders and other key leaders, researchers gain valuable insights these leaders’ perspectives, yielding a richer understanding of the phenomena of interest. Third, retrospective case studies, which have been useful in previous IS and entrepreneurship research (Andries et al., 2013; Fisher, 2012; Sabherwal et al., 2001), are often considered a practical and valid option for collecting data that describes a long period of an organization’s history because of the risk of participants losing interest and the long time commitments required for multi-year longitudinal data collection (Golden, 1992). Fourth and finally, a descriptive positivist approach was used because it matched the purpose of our research: applying the alternate templates method (Allison, 1971) to describe the IT-specific implications of traditional as well as emerging models of entrepreneurial action.<sup>3</sup>

## Context and cases

## Site selection

Two private firms were identified as research sites from a pool of eight organizations (HealthCo and TechCo<sup>4</sup>). There were three reasons for selecting these two organizations: the founders and senior management were willing to be involved and provide detailed information about the company, information included detailed data about the start-up period, and the organizations fit the entrepreneurship definition of opportunity, novelty, and practical outcome, Owners and senior managers at both firms indicated that their company faced significant risk of the unknown when they started operations (Hisrich and Peters, 1998). Each firm created a new business model (Schumpeter, 1934), and had, by the time of the data collection, demonstrated an ability to manage rapid change (Pavlou and El Sawy, 2010). Additionally, IT was identified as central to these firms’ business strategy and success. Thus, these firms fit the characteristics of new ventures, display entrepreneurial activity, and represent contexts where the development of strategic alignment should be transparent.

## Data collection

At each site, data was collected at the organizational, individual, and technological levels of analysis. Following established guidelines (Yin, 2009), three types of data were collected. Primary data was developed through interviews with managers. Interviews at HealthCo included every member of the executive team, as well as the two founders, the original corporate accountant, the first VP of Business Development, the Head of IT, and consultants. At TechCo, the founder, CEO, COO, IT Head, and Programming Head were interviewed. Secondary data came from examination of internal sources, including IS department performance measures, user surveys, consultant reports, outsourcing contracts, service level agreements, and similar documents. External historical data was drawn from newspapers and magazines. Table 2 lists types of data collected at each level of analysis

## Constructing the case study timelines and narratives

The data collected from each case was synthesized into complete individual case narratives. ATLAS-ti software was used to assist in the data analysis, which followed a two-step process of data coding using a pre-existing set of codes, followed by a second step where the data points were organized chronologically from the start of the case timeline to the end. We followed the four-step data analysis strategy for retrospective case analysis described in Sabherwal et al. (2001), which itself is based on the Miles and Snow strategy typology of Prospectors, Analyzers, and Defenders (Miles et al., 1978). The HealthCo narrative describes the important events and the evolution of IT over a 17 year period (1984–2001). The TechCo narrative also highlights important events over an 8 year period (1997–2005) while describing how its IT changed during that period.

After developing the case narratives, the alternate templates methodology was applied. This methodology is a process where different theoretical frameworks (‘‘alternate templates”) are used to describe an actual set of events. The explanatory powers of each theory are then compared to determine which theory best explains the events. The method was popularized by Allison (1971) in his study of decision making during the Cuban Missile Crisis. This methodology has been used in the current study to see which of the three different theoretical frameworks (‘‘alternate templates”) of causation, effectuation, or bricolage best explains the events observed in the new ventures. Codes developed for each of the three types of entrepreneurial action were used to analyze the cases and identify which theory explained the pattern of entrepreneurial action on display (Fisher, 2012). Using these codes, the first and third authors independently coded the case narrative for each organization. The second author independently compared the coding results and highlighted discrepancies; this discussion continued until 100% agreement was reached on the coding. Additional details of the case-study coding process appear after the case study summaries, and in this paper’s Appendix A.

## Case #1 HealthCo<sup>5</sup>

HealthCo was founded in 1984 as a small business in Western Canada, with a vision to provide home-based nursing care for clients who required nursing assistance but were not considered sick enough to be hospitalized. The initial identification of the underserved home-health niche would ultimately be one of the keys to the firm’s growth. By 2001, the company operated a network of more than 60 franchise and corporate offices across Canada, generated revenues of over \$40 M (CDN\$), and was the largest Canadian-owned home health care provider. The growth of HealthCo is noteworthy given the highlyregulated industry and the presence of established healthcare providers and government entities.

The company progressed through three periods of development, starting with a period of business model development (1984–1988). Growth involved developing a franchise network (1989–1996), and once that occurred, the last step was to consolidate operations into a stable and profitable corporate structure (1997–2001). ## Business model period (1984–1988)

With no established business model to guide them, the founders spent significant time searching for clients by identifying and developing the types of services these clients wanted and working to generate a reliable source of revenue from these services. Their novel approach to healthcare delivery represented a shift in the healthcare industry. While home healthcare became commonplace by the 2000s, HealthCo was one of the early entrants into this segment of the industry. When the company was founded, few government or healthcare stakeholders seemed to take the new company seriously:

‘‘I had quite a bit of newspaper coverage, a lot of media asking a lot of questions because [HealthCo was a new type of business], and I think a lot people thought I would just fall on my face, basically”

[HealthCo founder]

As the company grew it moved from the founder’s basement to a separate office and added more service staff. All business decisions and company functions were centralized to be responsive to changes in the business environment while also staying reasonably informal as the business developed. The founders tried to remain as flexible as possible so the business strategy emphasized innovation, flexibility, new services, and market development. The carefully-focused, opportunity-seeking orientation is summarized by one of the founders, who summarized this early period by noting,

‘‘the whole thing was around providing a quality service to as many Canadians as possible.”

[We Care founder]

The IS strategic orientation in this period was focused on cost reduction through operational efficiency. The structure of the IT function was centralized. IT-related decisions were made by one of the co-founders, who was a computer science professor at a nearby university. Even though it was a new venture in a time before personal computers were popular, a small IT infrastructure was in place with a Macintosh PC and early versions of MacWrite and Peachtree accounting software were used to keep track of revenues and expenses. The decision to build the business on Apple infrastructure was influenced by one of the founders' university sabbatical at Apple Computer in California where he spent a vear in residence, coming back to Canada with an insider’s understanding of the potential of small business computing. While HealthCo’s IT may seem primitive by modern standards, they were an early adopter of personal computers and productivity software for functions such as accounting and office correspondence. Increasing office productivity and administrative efficiency were the dominant IT management strategies in the early stages of the company. The founders had not yet thought of IT tools as a differentiator or as providing a competitive advantage; instead IT was seen as essential for productivity in a business that was trying to keep its costs down and lessen the financial risk to the founders. Decisions about which technologies to invest in were based on simple heuristics such as ‘‘adopt new technology in step with increased business volume if it occurs” and ‘‘adapt technol ogy usage based on emerging needs and local opportunities.”

The growth-oriented opportunity-seeking, Prospector-type business strategy was moderately aligned with the low-cost IT strategy. ## Franchise network growth period (1989–1996)

A 1988 decision to transition to a franchise company rapidly changed the structure and function of HealthCo. By the beginning of 1992, HealthCo consisted of a network of 18 franchises. The company continued to follow a growth strategy as it developed its franchise network in this period. Recognizing the risks from an uncertain business environment and a private health care industry that was still a relatively new, management followed an informal problem-oriented business planning process so that the strategy could be perfected as experience grew. Learning-by-doing, prospecting for new business, developing the franchise network, and aggressively marketing services were key elements of the company’s strategy. By 1997, HealthCo had defined its decentralized franchise model, grown to 50 franchises, and was generating \$20 million (CDN\$) in revenue annually.

During this time period, the business model of selling franchises to nurses and health care professionals revealed that these people were not typically experienced in running an office. To support a Prospector growth strategy that relied on practitioner business owners without management experience, in 1990 and 1991 the founder-CEO made plans with a local firm to develop an innovative franchise management information system. The IT function of HealthCo would now support growth by automating office process like scheduling, billing, collections, and employee payroll so health care professionals could focus on their core strengths of delivering health services. Scheduling was a vital, time-intensive process central to operations:

‘‘You need a system for scheduling . . . to take a caregiver and match them up to a client who wants maybe an elderly person, a non-smoker, [etc. etc. ] . . . So what we really wanted to do was [create] a scheduling [system] where we did some matching and then from that schedule generate the bills and the payroll. That was the key thing absolutely essential. Totally essential.”

[HealthCo founder]

By 1992, the scheduling application, code-named Wizard, was introduced. This application became the key to attracting new franchisees. IT strategy changed from efficiency and cost effectiveness in the first phase of the firm’s existence to a focus on growth and innovation as the Wizard system offered an opportunity for HealthCo to differentiate itself from competitor because it offered fast, flexible, and accurate scheduling and accounting:

‘‘[Wizard] played a major role, and there would have been a lot of people scared off if that hadn’t been available to them, in terms of buying [a franchise] in the first place. . . . It was an excellent software program to have, . . . I guess by today’s standards with [internet systems] around this doesn’t sound so significant, but back in those days it was. They were probably even ahead of their time and that is why HealthCo was so competitive with the big players out of the [Untied] States.”

[HealthCo executive]

Alignmentrelated business processes were gradually becoming increasingly routinized (‘‘systematized” in the founder’s vernacular). ## Corporate development period (1997–2001)

Revenue growth and new franchise starts slowed in 1997. A new CEO was named as one of the founders stepped down from this role. With a new CEO, HealthCo then transitioned its focus from franchise building to franchise management. The franchise network had achieved national coverage, new competitors were entering the market, and increasing time was spent evaluating future directions. The maturing of the home healthcare market led to more purposeful environmental scanning and rationalized investment decisions. The large decentralized franchise network needed to be more carefully monitored; business strategy took on the characteristics of an Analyzer.

IS management became increasingly systematized, formalized, and centralized. HealthCo executives, which by 1999 included none of the original founders, included a dedicated IT manager who planned, budgeted, and managed all technology-related decisions for the business. Differentiation and innovation (particularly through the Wizard scheduling system) were still emphasized. Strategic IT experimentation even increased as HealthCo became involved with technology partners to try out new technologies such as mobile workforce software. However, cost-effectiveness became an additional criterion for IT investment and sourcing decisions. The Analyzer business strategy paired well with a hybrid IT strategy of differentiation, growth, and innovation in concert with a low-cost focus. A moderately high degree of alignment resulted.

In 2000, the new President & CEO returned HealthCo to a growth-oriented strategy as he began aggressively implementing a new plan of buying back selected franchise offices. The structure of HealthCo thus shifted from a decentralized franchised model to a hybrid with both franchised and corporate-owned offices. New services were developed such as palliative care and preventative health, and a series of pilot projects ranging from home-based telehealth to Internetbased marketing and health assessment services were experimented with. Business strategy could best be described as returning to that of a Prospector.

Consultant reports and internal task force reviews led to replacement of the Wizard system in the early 2000s with an offthe-shelf product from a major software provider. The IT strategy changed to a low-cost orientation. Planning responsibility was held by the CFO and an IT manager who developed a new strategy where IT was now a source of cost efficiency. Differentiation and innovation through IT were no longer pursued:

‘‘I came to the conclusion that IT isn’t as proprietary today as maybe it was. Our unique factor is going to be with the health care service, the programs we talked about. That is where I want to excel. A lot of us are going to have the same kind of back office, and I like to think of course that we are more efficient than the guy next door.”

[New HealthCo President & CEO]

By 2001, the entrepreneurial, growth-oriented focus of this period no longer included the IT function of HealthCo. IT was no longer viewed as a driver of growth or competitive advantage and an opportunity was missed to achieve more than a moderate degree of alignment.

## Case #2 TechCo

TechCo was founded in 1997 as a start-up focusing on computer-generated (CG) effects in film production (advertising commercials, television programs, and feature films). Many early projects were for television commercials, generating revenue in the range of \$1000–\$10,000 per project. In 1998, TechCo had the opportunity to create visual effects for a major TV miniseries, earning the firm a 1999 Emmy award. When TechCo took on this project in 1998, the firm had 5 employees and \$168,000 in annual revenue. Following this success, TechCo was contracted to produce visual effects for a major motion picture, generating much higher revenue, but also placing them in competition with larger, more established Hollywood competitors. The firm grew as their reputation and project portfolio expanded. By 2004, TechCo had grown to approximately 80 employees and \$8 M (CDN\$) in revenue, with CG projects in commercials and feature films as well as live action production, and with offices in multiple Canadian provinces, the US, and Australia.

From its founding, TechCo had to find creative ways to address the capital-intensive nature of the CG visual effects industry. Firms operating in this industry require not only highly-specialized digital animation software, but also large server clusters for digital rendering – often as many as 100+ individual servers linked as a single virtual rendering computer. The competitive requirements in the CG industry in the 1990s were unforgiving to under-funded new ventures. So with low startup capital and small initial projects, TechCo faced a major challenge. In addition to this initial challenge, firms were required to innovate by producing previously-unseen visual effects and effects with greater sophistication in terms of finer resolution, more precise color, and more realistic motion. All this was required simply to stay in business. Each step of innovation required significant investments in software and hardware. As TechCo responded to these challenges, they progressed

## Business model period (1997–2000)

TechCo officially began operations in February 1997. With a \$20,000 loan from a local bank and a carload of assorted computers and networking equipment, TechCo managed to land several projects for local television commercials in the first year. Their business strategy was to actively prospect for work. Because no standard CG visual effects business model existed, the founders were forced to be flexible and learn as they went, with little routinization or formalization. The company had to increase capacity quickly each time a project was started because project schedules were unpredictable and hard to forecast. No projects were ever turned down, even if the company’s existing infrastructure was insufficient:

‘‘the very first thing [with] this one project . . . a \$2000 project, we basically had to spend \$1000 of that on buying an editing program and we used that same editing program for years. . . . It ran on NT stuff like that, it was a very early program. Once again, it was a very cost effective solution. We could have gone and bought, like, an Avid<sup>6</sup>[that] was like \$30 + thousand – and really what was the difference? So we steered clear of that. We found another solution

[TechCo Founder]

The capital-intensive nature of the CG industry, coupled with the Prospector business strategy, meant that IT needed to support growth and innovation but in an ultra-low-cost manner. Revenue from projects was immediately reinvested in IT hardware and software. TechCo purchased only what was absolutely necessary for immediate projects. More specifically, TechCo followed a low-cost S-I-R (Salvage–Innovate–Reuse) IT strategy where software and hardware were purchased as needed for each project. Low-cost PCs were purchased and networked together to create higher capacity ‘‘virtual computers” for visual effects work rather than purchasing more-expensive, higher-capacity computers to compete against larger firms like PIXAR and Industrial Light and Magic (ILM). Environmental IT scanning involved constant checking of vendor websites, eBay, and technology magazines for new software and hardware announcements, as well as subsequent discount announcements. Typically, a project would be received, IT requirements determined, and depending on the project size (basically a proxy for importance), IT decisions would then be made regarding purchases, support provided, and how existing IT was to be reused or reallocated.

Business and IT decision making were centralized and reasonably well-integrated, primarily as a result of the two founders having dual roles in both business and technology planning during the first three years of operation. Both founders were

## Hollywood outsider period (2000–2003)

On the basis of their growing capability and award-winning skill, TechCo was approached at the end of 1999 and asked if they were interested in working on a larger Hollywood project. A visual effects (VFX) company that was originally contracted to provide the opening sequence for the coming year’s Hollywood summer blockbuster had backed out of the project and left the production company scrambling for a replacement. True to its ethos of never declining a project, TechCo accepted and was suddenly thrust into a new segment of the industry with large, successful, and very innovative competitors. By the end of this time period, TechCo would have three Hollywood movie projects in process and new offices in Vancouver and Los Angeles.

While TechCo was still characterized in this time period by growth, innovation, learning-by-doing, and rapid resource reconfiguration, the business model nevertheless evolved in several ways. TechCo added a live-action TV production division to its core VFX business. Also, proprietary software solutions that TechCo had developed were licensed to VFX software vendors. This provided a slightly more stable source of revenue to counterbalance the uneven project-based work. Along with this product and market development, there was an increasing focus on internal business operations. It was during this time period, after the founders hired a CEO, that a formal business plan was finally created. Decision-making remained centralized among the CEO and two founders. Business strategy remained that of a rapidly-growing, Prospector-oriented new venture.

In terms of an IT strategy, TechCo remained a cost-sensitive, growth-oriented innovator. The S-I-R (Salvage-Innovate-Reuse) strategy remained unchanged from the previous years. At the same time, IT management practices during this period remained opportunistic as the company looked for projects which benefited from its IT experience and infrastructure. For example, IT staff waited until particular video production tasks were held up by some performance limits (e.g., rendering speed) and then focused time and technology on addressing that specific problem. These IT strategy practices helped preserve scarce financial resources. Decisions were centrally coordinated among the founders, CEO, new IT Manager, and Software R&D Manager.

Using this structure, and by developing routines and processes suited to their strategy and constraints, TechCo was able to stay very current in the industry and remain aware of potentially influential trends as early as possible. They accomplished this by closely monitoring competitors, by continuing to scan for IT hardware and software on technical websites and in trade journals, and by repeatedly hiring interns from the local university’s computer science program to find out what programming and IT innovations were leading-edge. Significant experimentation still continued as necessity dictated. Trial and error was the dominant strategy as IT experiments typically operated ‘‘in production” on the same networks that were being used for current projects

TechCo’s hybrid IT strategy would have been best-suited to an Analyzer business strategy – or the Prospector business strategy could have been best-paired with a pure growth and innovation strategy (Sabherwal et al., 2001). With the combination of Prospector business strategy and hybrid IT strategy, alignment nevertheless remained in the same previous moderately-high state.

## Corporate development period (2003–2005)

The frequency with which TechCo’s projects were seen on both the large and small screen increased in the years from 2003 to 2005. Multiple Hollywood motion pictures as well as TechCo’s own live-action productions (at least ten shows per year) continued. Critical mass was being achieved within the R&D department as a variety of specialized software pro grams for both VFX animation as well as computer hardware management were marketed for sale. TechCo was gaining a reputation as an innovative provider of high quality VFX in certain areas such as liquid flow animation and the company received government support to further develop this pioneering expertise in the industry. TechCo could be described as having moved from an earlier period of learning how to compete as a visual media production business between 2000 and the end of 2002 to exploiting that experience with a diverse array of productions, products, and services from 2003 to 2005.

During this time period, TechCo grew to 3 divisions (VFX, Live Action, and Commercials), opened a new office in Sydney, Australia, and updated its strategic plan. Individual project schedules, many of which were still finalized on short notice, continued to dominate the company’s planning cycles and this motivated business processes such as staffing and IT investment to remain flexible and informal rather than routinized. Efforts to create more stable routines for budgeting, project management, IT support, and software R&D were beginning to take shape, but often still assumed secondary priority when new VFX and live action projects started. Centralized governance through the founders and CEO remained in place. The opportunityseeking Prospector strategic stance remained in place as well, as is evidenced by industry-shaping innovation, growth through product and market development, uneven growth, low routinization, and multiple flexible technologies. It is note worthy that by this time it was possible for TechCo to begin adapting their strategies to be more efficiency-oriented with less revenue volatility but they chose to keep following the earlier S-I-R approach to remain open to new types of business that might arise.

Over time, TechCo’s IT infrastructure was gradually transformed into mature server farms for data storage and processing, with machines running both commercial and proprietary software capable of providing visual effects for Hollywood feature films. Management of the IT environment had transitioned from being primarily concerned with trial and error and investment to a focus on efficiently managing the strong infrastructure that now existed. For TechCo, it was established practice to pursue innovation through the least-expensive and most cost-effective technology available. Principles such as ‘‘develop through use, tune as necessary” encapsulated their opportunity-seeking, learningby-doing, flexibility-focused approach. An unchanged IT strategy alongside an unchanged Prospector business strategy kept alignment in a moderately high state even though the company itself had grown from a new venture to a ‘known name’ in the Hollywood VFX industry.

## Assessing strategic alignment

Strategic alignment examines the degree of fit or congruence between business strategy and IS/IT strategy (Luftman and Brier, 1999; Palmer and Markus, 2000; Reich and Benbasat, 2000). Business strategy is commonly assessed using the Miles et al. (1978) typology of (1) Prospector, (2) Analyzer, and (3) Defender (Sabherwal and Chan, 2001). IS/IT strategy can be categorized by assessing whether a firm has (a) a low-cost strategy; (b) a strategy of IT for differentiation, growth, alliance, and innovation; or (c) a hybrid strategy where a low-cost emphasis is combined with a focus on differentiation, growth, alliance, and innovation. The theoretically ideal pairings are that a Prospector strategy should be paired with an IS/IT strategy for differentiation, growth, alliance, and innovation; an Analyzer strategy should be paired with the hybrid low-cost strategy plus IT for differentiation, growth, alliance, and innovation; and a Defender strategy should be paired with a low-cost IS/IT strategy. Each of these theoretically-ideal pairings represent a high degree of strategic alignment (Sabherwal and Chan, 2001).

To assess strategic alignment at HealthCo and TechCo, we examined the ways that IT was being used to develop the organization and grow the business according the characteristics listed in Table 5. We also identified whether the firm had a Prospector, Analyzer, or Defender-type business strategy. The business strategy characteristics that we searched for in our case narratives appear in Table 6.

## Results

## Causation

Causation-type actions were found primarily in the HealthCo case. Every causation-type indicator was found to be moderately or strongly evident. In particular, evidence of business plans, the creation of a vision for the new venture, and the development of project plans were strongly evident. This evidence indicates that HealthCo was deliberate in its planning activities to achieve strategic goals. It also indicates that HealthCo applied rational decision making processes to determine actions that were appropriate for their long-run strategic outlook. In addition, strong evidence was found for HealthCo implementing control processes, particularly cost control. Also, moderately strong evidence was found for environmental scanning activities such as gathering information about market size and competitors. Each of these activities support processes to identify and assess long-run opportunities. These indicators reveal that HealthCo perceived itself to be in a relatively stable environment which allowed it to rationally consider longer-term strategic initiatives

TechCo, on the other hand, provided limited evidence of causation-type entrepreneurial actions. Only two indicators were strongly evident. Most indicators were not found, and instead much evidence revealed that this firm’s actions contrasted with the causation approach.

## Effectuation

Interestingly, strong evidence was found for effectual actions at HealthCo. As HealthCo developed and became a franchise organization, it used a number of ‘‘experiments” to test its products, its services, and the way it would deliver those services. This appears to be a result of the business switching to a franchise structure as it attempted to find the right mix of services and pricing to enable it to be successful. In addition, as HealthCo became a franchisor, it exhibited flexibility in adapting to opportunities as they presented themselves, but at the same time entered into the necessary structured agreements with franchisees and suppliers. All these activities are indicative of a new venture that experienced a degree of uncertainty as it developed.

## Activities supporting strategic alignment

TechCo, with its bricolage-style, evolving, unplanned, sense-and-respond approach to growth and development unsurprisingly displays alignment activities that are characterized as ‘‘emergent” rather than ‘‘intended” (Karpovsky and Galliers, 2015, p. 5). For instance, TechCo displays evaluating-type actions when it responds to unforeseeable opportunities by scanning emerging technologies and focusing on tools to enable future projects by ultimately repurposing existing hardware. TechCo displays learning actions when it learns from past project experiences, intuiting and interpreting changes in the competitive environment. Furthermore, TechCo displays decision-making as decisions are made regarding resource allocation as needs arise, and with management playing a key role in deciding to pursue specific projects. All of these activities are classified as ‘‘emergent” (Karpovsky and Galliers, 2015).

HealthCo, with its more rational, planned approach to development and growth primarily displays alignment activities that are ‘‘intended”, with fewer of the ‘‘emergent” activities. (Karpovsky and Galliers, 2015, p. 5). HealthCo displays developing activities as they choose to develop the Wizard scheduling system in-house. The firm also displays reconfiguring as it centralizes, decentralizes, then recentralizes, reversing structural changes in the firm (Hirschheim and Sabherwal, 2001). HealthCo displays reconfiguring by outsourcing the functions of the Wizard scheduling system in response to growth. This new venture displays strengthening by training users on Wizard and by bringing business and IT staff together for communication. Signalling is in evidence as the firm creates an IT function to help manage growth. Thus, each of the four ‘‘intended” activities are on display. Of the ‘‘emergent” activities, evaluating, learning, decision-making, and negotiating appear when HealthCo ascertains whether Wizard can support future operations, also scanning emerging IT to ultimately find a commercially-available system to replace Wizard. Thus, HealthCo displays primarily ‘‘intended” behaviors, as one might expect from a causation-type firm, but augments these with some ‘‘emergent” behaviors arising from its effectuationstyle, calculative, evaluative approach to dealing with uncertainty.

At a higher level, our case evidence reveals the presence of both planned, top-down strategy as well as emergent, iterative strategizing. HealthCo actively and deliberately prospected for business in a nascent market, custom-building an in-house IS to support and enable their franchise business model. Over time, they centralize, decentralize, and re-centralize in response to growth, market shifts, and leadership changes. HealthCo thus demonstrates not only strategy, but also strategy-aspractice. At TechCo, their Prospector stance is less deliberate than at HealthCo, but TechCo nevertheless displays a guiding principle of salvage-innovate-reuse, and a growing strategic sensibility as their aggressive opportunism is refined over time to focus principally on specialty visual effects. proprietary software, and live-action work, While TechCo most clearly dis: plays strategy-as-practice and the influence of day-to-day activities to shape the firm, they also demonstrate some awareness of high-level strategy. Thus, our evidence supports prior research that identifies the limits of traditional, rational, planned, long-term strategic planning and argues for an greater recognition of IS strategizing and strategy-as-practice (Marabelli and Galliers, 2016; Peppard et al., 2014; Renaud et al., 2016). Furthermore, we observe that patterns of entrepreneurial action, as they are indicated by a firm’s business and IT activities, can be described in terms of emergent strategy (Baker and Niederman, 2014; Chen et al., 2010; Mintzberg and Waters, 1985)

## Summary

Our results indicate that HealthCo displays causation and effectuation entrepreneurial action, while TechCo, in contrast, displays bricolage-type entrepreneurial action. Additionally, both firms exhibit congruence in strategies as well as activities that support and promote strategic alignment. Thus, there is definitive evidence of moderate to high levels of strategic alignment across the time periods examined, albeit with some variation.

## Discussion

The purpose of our research was to examine how strategic alignment forms and develops in new ventures. Based on the extant literature and our limited personal experience with strategic alignment in new ventures, we expected that strategic alignment would only be possible for new ventures that faced relatively stable competitive environments, where development of a business strategy resulted from careful planning, and where deliberate IS/IT planning was tied directly to business strategy. For example, researchers maintain that alignment cannot readily occur if the business lacks a formalized strategic business plan (Lederer and Mendelow, 1989; Reich and Benbasat, 2000; Teo and Ang, 1999; Vitale et al., 1986), and state that planning comprehensiveness and sophistication leads to alignment (Chan et al., 2006). Others have noted that even in SMEs, where planning is often carried out informally (Lefebvre and Lefebvre, 1988), clearly-defined vision and goals are neverthe less important steps preceding alignment (Cragg et al., 2002). In other words, we anticipated that only causation-type entrepreneurial action could yield strategic alignment.

Please cite this article in press as: Street, C., et al. The influence of entrepreneurial action on strategic alignment in new ventures: Searching for the genesis of alignment. J. Strateg. Inform. Syst. (2017), http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jsis.2017.06.002

We expected that effectuation-type entrepreneurial action that occurs in less predictable environments, and where entrepreneurial opportunities are created through a process of enactment and iterative learning, would make it very difficult for a high degree of strategic alignment to be achieved because of the volatility of business plans and processes that would need to be put in place and used for the firm to survive. We expected that this would translate into a struggle to constantly adjust IS/ IT activities to what the business is trying to do, and that misalignment would characterize firms of this type.

Finally, we expected that new ventures that exhibited bricolage-type entrepreneurial action would have no recognizable strategic alignment at all. Bricolage firms develop in penurious environments. Given these constraints, entrepreneurs are forced to ‘‘make do”, recombining and repurposing limited resources that are available to them – including IS/IT resources. This constant state of adapting to available resources makes any business planning almost unmanageable. We expected that the improvisational nature of these firms would make it very difficult, if not impossible, for IS/IT plans to align with business plans. In many cases, there simply are no business plans, only short-term tactics and actions based on available resources to address immediate competitive pressures

To our surprise, our expectations were not met. Indeed, the findings from our in-depth study of two new ventures indicates that (1) moderate to high strategic alignment is achievable in both firms, (2) the type of entrepreneurial action that was taken by each of these two firms did not preclude alignment – a high degree of strategic alignment was still achieved, and (3) differing starting conditions faced by the two firms appeared to impact the types of entrepreneurial action that were taken and the manner in which strategic alignment was achieved. In other words, strategic alignment was realized for causation actions but also for effectuation and bricolage actions. The question now is, ‘‘how did these three very different types of entrepreneurial action result in moderate to high alignment in these two new ventures?”

To answer this question, we have turned to our case evidence. For instance, our case evidence reveals that HealthCo developed business plans for franchising, created an IT function within the organization, and then deliberately decided to develop their ‘‘Wizard” information system in-house. Their IS/IT decisions clearly took into account the perspective and needs of franchisees and their expected level of office management inexperience. In addition, they strengthened their IS IT capabilities over time, improved their in-house systems, trained users, and reconfigured the IT function as the firm grew over time. Thus, we observe that in this firm, when strategic alignment is pursued in the midst of causation-type activity, it will likely be done as a part of traditional strategic IS planning (Grover and Segars, 2005; Segars and Grover, 1998), and SISP success factors will be evident from the founding of the company onward. Top-down strategy formulation and IT strategy plans were cooperatively developed. To enact strategy, appropriate IT infrastructure was acquired and upgraded when possible. IS/IT capabilities became robust, standardized, and keyed to best practices. Alignment activities can be characterized as intended, deliberate, and instrumental (Karpovsky and Galliers, 2015).

In contrast, when strategic alignment at HealthCo was pursued during a period of effectuation-type activity, it was through experimentation, iterative learning, and strategic co-evolution. Where effectuation-type behaviors were observed, we noted that the structure of the IS/IT function developed through adaptation to change, as the key scheduling function was moved to a commercially-available information system after evaluating commercial options against the existing in-house system. The limited means and resources available to the business as it franchised led to low-cost strategies for IS procurement and operation, clearly limiting ‘‘what could be done.” And finally, while planning took place to find ways to use IS to gain efficiencies, without business precedents to follow, iterative learning also shaped the IS function. Thus, in periods of effectuation-type activities, adaptation and experimentation were evident. The end result, strategic alignment, may be the same as with causation-type entrepreneurial action, but the activities by which it was pursued were different.

Finally, when a bricolage-type firm like TechCo pursued strategic alignment, it was done through creative recombination and repurposing of resources – almost the antithesis of the planned, rational approach of SISP at the causation-type firm – and somewhat at variance with the received wisdom on pursuing strategic alignment. At TechCo, revenue from one project was invested in IS infrastructure for subsequent projects with no investment planning undertaken prior to a project’s approval. IS investment decisions were ad-hoc, and evaluations were made in response to what projects were available, what goals were achievable, and what resources could be re-used from prior projects. Iterative learning from one project to the next was clearly on display. A high degree of alignment was achieved in a tactical, rapid-response, reactionary manner. At TechCo, the project-oriented nature of the business, combined with significant capital constraints, made planning difficult, with leaders not knowing what the business would require or have the resources for from one quarter to the next The end goal of alignment was nevertheless achievable. IT innovation and creative re-use were clearly on display in the bricolage-type firm we studied and an alternate path to strategic alignment can be observed.

In summary, the three types of entrepreneurial action at each of these firms led to consistent and identifiable, but differing and distinct patterns of IT strategy development and IT resource deployment. At new ventures exhibiting causation-, effectuation-, and bricolage-type entrepreneurial action, strategic alignment can develop through a variety of different mechanisms and activities, but will nevertheless follow distinctive, typified, and recognizable patterns

## The genesis of alignment

In this research, we studied two new ventures in order to examine how alignment may be initially constructed. In each case setting, the founders could articulate the business strategy and value proposition. Additionally, businesses strategies in each new venture required creating an IT foundation to support continuity and growth. Why did the founders in each case choose different paths to alignment?

Please cite this article in press as: Street, C., et al. The influence of entrepreneurial action on strategic alignment in new ventures: Searching for the genesis of alignment. J. Strateg. Inform. Syst. (2017), http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.jsis.2017.06.002

Deconstructing the theories of entrepreneurial action reveals that the causation, effectuation, and bricolage approaches have different assumptions about how founders perceive their starting conditions. Under the causation approach, the assumption is that the future follows a predictable path and that outcomes can be anticipated; this favors a traditional rational planning approach. The effectuation approach acts on a differing assumption, that the future seems unpredictable with cause and effect unclear, thus favouring a risk-reduction strategy of iterative learning to limit losses. With bricolage, founders perceive resource constraints as particularly salient, which motivates the ‘make-do’ approach where planning is neither necessarily rational nor risk-limiting. Environmental predictability has been studied as a contingent factor between alignment and organizational performance (Bergeron et al., 2001; Choe, 2003; Johnston and Carrico, 1988; Kearns and Lederer, 2004; Knoll and Jarvenpaa, 1994; Peak et al., 2005; Yayla and Hu, 2012), but has not to our knowledge been studied as a predictor of alignment decisions. Our results augur for a theoretical explanation that the founders in each new venture perceived the environment surrounding their respective business opportunities in fundamentally different ways, and made distinct choices about alignment activity based on those perceptions.

Our case evidence also indicates that the experience and backgrounds of the founders influenced their perceptions of business opportunities and the associated risks. These perceptions ultimately led to different approaches toward alignment. When the business opportunity involved innovating in an established and regulated healthcare environment where the rules of patient care and financial compensation were entrenched but the specific rules of how a private company could deliver care were absent, the founders, a professional registered nurse and a computer science professor, were uniquely knowledgeable about the potential application of IT. They purposefully and deliberately adopted a well-understood franchise model to grow their venture using new technologies in a risk-aware manner. When a different business opportunity involved innovating in an incipient computer graphics industry with no dominant business model, the recently-graduated, inexperienced founders with no personal contacts in the TV or motion picture industries hunted for individual projects that could be completed by using or repurposing existing IT resources. Perception of risk has not to our knowledge been studied as a factor involved with strategic alignment or as a predictor of alignment decisions.

In each case, the founders interpreted how predictable the environment was and what they should do, with HealthCo electing for sophisticated office automation and TechCo adopting a ‘‘salvage-innovate-reuse” IT strategy. These choices led HealthCo to a set of intended, deliberate, instrumental alignment activities with occasional adaptive, evolutionary responses; and led TechCo to exclusively emergent, unplanned, evolving activities. Our cases support the idea that perceptions of environmental predictability, business risk, and resource endowment influenced the founders’ approaches to both business growth and strategic alignment. Ultimately, we argue that the starting conditions and entrepreneurs’ experience shaped the type of entrepreneurial action, which in turn determined which specific activities would initially be used to facilitate alignment.

## Practical implications

The primary contribution of our paper is a description of how the pattern of entrepreneurial action in a new venture shapes the development of strategic alignment. From this description, we provide four suggestions for leaders of new ventures. Our first suggestion is to be self-aware. To the leaders of new ventures, we would ask, ‘‘How are you trying to grow your new business? Are you taking a traditional planned approach, envisioning some initiatives A, B, then C as stepping stones toward a goal? Are you following an established pattern of growth for an established market? Or are you instead trying to grow opportunistically but cautiously; expanding, but within constraints and in response to perceived risk and uncertainty in the competitive environment? Or are you expanding as aggressively as resource constraints allow – growing however and whenever vou can2" Being self-aware and reflective about the manner of a new venture's growth – that is, about the pattern of entrepreneurial action – is an important first step for the new venture’s leader to move toward strategic alignment and capture value from IT. In both of our cases, leaders of the new ventures were able to articulate characteristics of the markets they were operating in as well as the reasons for their particular pattern of action.

Building from this, our second suggestion is to be IT-aware. To the new venture leader, we would ask, ‘‘How can IT help you reach your destination? Is your destination a particular strategic goal? A particular competitive position within an industry? A particular financial goal? Is there a particular problem you are trying to solve? Do you know which IT – what infrastructure and what applications – are most appropriate to help you reach your goals?” In both of our cases, IT was integral to the vision of the new venture leaders/owners. They understood how IT would enable them to achieve their goals and they engaged in environmental scanning to identify new technologies that would be valuable and relevant for their firm

At the same time that specific IT is being identified, it is important to also ask, ‘‘How can IT be integrated into your firm in a manner that is consistent with your style, your company ethos, and with your specific type of entrepreneurial action?” Numerous decisions turn on being IT-aware – decisions about technology and infrastructure; decisions about developing in-house systems, outsourcing application development, or choosing cloud-based solutions; and decisions about how to implement and use IT. These decisions should ideally be made in a way that supports not only strategy and tactics, but also culture and values.

## Limitations and future research

The primary limitation of our research is that our conclusions and theorizing are based on two in-depth case studies Although these cases provided a rich source of evidence for entrepreneurial action and strategic alignment, a larger number of cases, and cases from additional industries would provide a more secure foundation from which to generalize our results. Additionally, while case study research is well-suited to emerging research concepts (Yin, 2009), is ideal for complex processes with multiple actors (Mintzberg and Lampel, 1999), and is common in entrepreneurship research (Andries et al., 2013; Fisher, 2012), other methodological approaches might provide additional insights.

An additional limitation is that while we have emphasized here the role of the competitive environment and perceptions of risk on a new venture’s entrepreneurial action, other factors may also be influential, possibly including the personal characteristics of the entrepreneur, industry maturity and regulation, and others. Our intention is not to provide an exhaustive discussion of the drivers of entrepreneurial action, but to show how a pattern of entrepreneurial action, once chosen or evident, shapes the formation of strategic alignment. Future research could investigate in more depth the other factors that could influence entrepreneurial action and their impact on strategic alignment.

Future research could also examine how entrepreneurial action impacts an entire range of IT capabilities beyond alignment. Based on the conclusions in this study, we conjecture that differing types of entrepreneurial action lead to differing IT capability profiles. Also, our study may have implications not only for new ventures, but also for startups, spinoffs, microfirms, SMEs, and/or family businesses, each of which share some degree of commonality with new ventures. Further research could examine the impact of entrepreneurial action and the implications for strategic alignment in these types of firms.

Perhaps more significantly, from our case data we observe that the received wisdom regarding the importance of strategic planning to strategic alignment seems not to apply as strictly in new ventures. Our study may have identified an important boundary of generalizability for alignment research that has been conducted in the context of established (and often large) firms. The benefits of strategic planning for new ventures remain evident in stable and predictable competitive environments. However, our study also strongly affirms what has been said about the importance of creativity, responsiveness, and agility in strategic alignment (Tallon and Pinsonneault, 2011; Teo and Ang, 1999). Our data indicates that these qualities are particularly important at the inception of alignment and may mitigate a lack of detailed planning. A related point is that for established organizations that want to become more agile, entrepreneurial, or dramatically change their business strategy, we recommend research on actions that are best suited to ‘resetting’ strategic alignment. Researchers should endeavor to provide guidance to companies that decide they need to change markets or otherwise rework their IT and business strategies.

## Conclusion

Entrepreneurial action varies from one new venture to another How this action shapes the origin and development of strategic alignment in new ventures is the focus of this paper. Our cases revealed that different patterns of entrepreneurial action had implications for the alignment activities in one new venture compared to a second new venture. Our analysis indicates that not just one type of entrepreneurial action leads to strategic alignment, but that all three, causation, effectuation, and bricolage, have the potential to support a new venture’s efforts to align strategies. What our analysis also supports is that starting conditions and entrepreneurs’ experience shape the type of entrepreneurial action, which in turn determines which activities are initially used in new ventures to facilitate alignment. We look forward to additional work to develop a deeper understanding of how different patterns of entrepreneurial action affect strategic alignment and different types of IT capabilities in new ventures.

Working collaboratively, the researchers compared the results from each coder and examined the elements where disagreement existed. The researchers discussed and resolved discrepancies, noting where agreement did not occur in their respective versions of Table A.2.
