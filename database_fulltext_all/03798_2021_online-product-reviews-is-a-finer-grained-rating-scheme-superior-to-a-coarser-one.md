# ONLINE PRODUCT REVIEWS: IS A FINER-GRAINED RATING SCHEME SUPERIOR TO A COARSER ONE?<sup>1</sup>

Murat M. Tunc Department of Management, Tilburg University Tilburg, THE NETHERLANDS {m.m.tunc@tilburguniversity.edu}

Huseyin Cavusoglu and Srinivasan Raghunathan Naveen Jindal School of Management, The University of Texas at Dallas Richardson, TX, U.S.A. {huseyin@utdallas.edu} {sraghu@utdallas.edu}

Product review platforms in online marketplaces differ with respect to the granularity of product quality information they provide. While some platforms provide a single overall rating for product quality (also referred to as the single-dimensional rating scheme), others provide a separate rating for each individual quality attribute (also referred to as the multidimensional rating scheme). The multidimensional rating scheme is superior to the single-dimensional rating scheme, ceteris paribus, in reducing consumers’ uncertainty about product quality and value. However, we show that, when sellers respond to product ratings by adjusting their prices, compared to the single-dimensional rating scheme, the multidimensional rating scheme does not always benefit consumers, nor does it necessarily benefit sellers or society. The uncertainty associated with quality attribute rating and the extent of differentiation between competing products determines whether a finer-grained multidimensional rating scheme is superior to a coarsergrained single-dimensional rating scheme from the consumer, seller, and social planner perspectives. The main driver of the results is that more (less) granular and less (more) uncertain information exposes (hides) underlying differentiation, or a lack thereof, between competing products, which, in turn, alters upstream price competition in the presence of heterogeneous consumer preferences. The results demonstrate that focusing on the information transfer aspect of rating schemes provides only a partial understanding of the true impacts of rating schemes.

Keywords: Online product reviews, competition, retail platforms, analytical modeling, economics of IS

## Introduction

Online platforms and third-party review sites provide product reviews to mitigate consumer uncertainty about the quality of products sold on those platforms (Hong and Pavlou 2014, Kwark et al. 2014). Product reviews are especially valuable when consumers have heterogeneous preferences about product attributes and competing products differ in these attributes (Chen et al. 2017). In such cases, product reviews can help consumers find products that offer better value. Online platforms typically use both structured reviews that employ ordinal ratings and unstructured reviews that use free-form text to elicit and display information from reviewers.

Existing platforms differ with respect to the granularity of the information they provide via reviews. For instance, on Amazon and Yelp, the structured part of reviews consists of an overall aggregate rating, referred to as the single-dimensional rating scheme (SD hereafter). Other platforms such as TripAdvisor provide ratings on individual product attributes as well, referred to as the multidimensional rating scheme (MD hereafter). For instance, TripAdvisor rates restaurants on four attributes: food, service, value, and atmosphere. Clearly, MD provides finergrained information than SD. While each rating scheme has its own merits and drawbacks, the purport of reviews is to efficiently transfer product information to prospective buyers so that they can make more informed purchase decisions.

MD has been found to be better than SD in transferring product information to consumers and in mitigating consumer uncertainty (Chen et al. 2017, Ge and Li 2015, Archak et al. 2011). Assessing rating schemes on the basis of reduction of consumer uncertainty is useful; however, it provides only a partial characterization of the true impact of a rating scheme. We demonstrate in this research that the rating scheme plays a significant role in shaping price competition between upstream sellers, which, in turn, affects sellers, consumers, and society at large. Thus, we extend the current literature by explaining the potential strategic implications of MD and SD on various stakeholders.

We examined the impact of MD and SD by developing a game-theoretical model of a context in which two competing firms sell their products in a marketplace that charges a commission on the sales price. Each product was characterized by two quality attributes. Consumers had heterogeneous preferences for these attributes and products could also be differentiated along these attributes; consumers were also uncertain about product quality. Under MD, a separate quality rating for each attribute of each product was provided, whereas, under SD, a single aggregate quality rating for each product was provided.

We are mainly interested in the long-term implications of optimal pricing decisions of a duopoly in response to online reviews, which signal product quality. Various factors, such as sellers’ marketing efforts and price, can influence the quality signal and introduce noise into the quality signal. We modeled these influences using review precision as a parameter in order to focus on the differential impacts of SD and MD.

Our study reveals several interesting findings that have important implications. The impact of SD or MD on platforms, as well as on sellers,<sup>2</sup> consumers, and society, depends critically on the extent of product differentiation and rating precision. Product differentiation refers to how similar products are on the two quality attributes. Rating precision measures the extent of error associated with quality attribute rating and it is equal to the inverse of the rating variance.<sup>3</sup> MD offers higher profits to sellers than SD only when products are differentiated on at least one attribute; the rating precision is low if products are not differentiated. The impact of the rating scheme on consumers is qualitatively opposite to the impact on sellers. That is, if MD is superior to SD from the sellers’ perspective, then MD is inferior to SD from the consumer surplus perspective. Therefore, while consumers have more granular information under MD versus SD, consumers are not necessarily better off under MD versus SD. Interestingly, we find that MD does not necessarily improve social welfare (i.e., reduce the consumer misfit costs) compared to SD, even when the ratings have a high degree of precision. When products are partially differentiated (i.e., differentiated on one attribute only), then SD offers higher social welfare than MD. Finally, an increase in rating precision enhances the superiority of MD (SD) from the sellers’ (consumers’) perspective when products are perfectly differentiated but has the opposite effect when products are either partially differentiated or not differentiated. Similar to sellers, an increase in rating precision enhances the superiority of MD (SD) from a societal perspective when products are perfectly (partially) differentiated but hinders the superiority of MD when products are not differentiated.

The main intuition for the above results arises from how rating schemes influence consumers’ perceptions of competing products. Clearly, when consumer preferences are heterogeneous, differentiated products can help sellers target different consumer groups and reduce the intensity of price competition between them, thereby helping sellers, as well as the platform that receives a commission from the sellers. MD highlights, and could possibly amplify, any underlying differentiation of competing products. On the other hand, when products are not differentiated, MD also exposes the similarity between products. In contrast, SD masks the differentiation by forcing consumers to aggregate the ratings of attributes. Thus, even when products are homogeneous, SD still reflects heterogeneous consumer preference and therefore fails to expose the similarity between products. Sellers exploit heterogeneity in consumers’ perceptions of product quality, which are influenced by the rating scheme. Essentially, price competition between upstream sellers can be fundamentally different under the different rating schemes. Consequently, the impact of any rating scheme goes well beyond consumer uncertainty reduction, and the strategic implications on seller competition affect all parties in the marketplace, including sellers, platforms, consumers, and society.

## Relationship to Prior Literature

This research note is broadly related to the literature on online product reviews, which has generally shown a positive relationship between (single-dimensional) product ratings and sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Liu 2006, Duan et al. 2008, Luca 2016). However, studies have also found no relationship or negative relationship between ratings and sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Sun 2012, Duan et al. 2008). Some have explained these mixed findings by pointing out that SD ignores the multidimensional aspects of product quality and heterogeneous consumer preferences for these dimensions (Archak et al. 2011, Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011, Moghaddam and Ester 2011).

Research on MD has shown that MD is more effective in transferring consumer experience than SD in that MD helps consumers find products that better fit their preferences (Archak et al. 2011, Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011, Moghaddam and Ester 2011, Ge and Li 2015, Chen et al. 2017, Dai et al. 2018). Moreover, MD suffers less from rating bias than SD (Ge and Li 2015). Some other studies have proposed techniques to uncover individual dimensional ratings (Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011, Moghaddam and Ester 2011) and effectively combine multidimensional ratings into a single-dimensional rating (Dai et al. 2018). Our work differs from this stream of literature in that we focus on the strategic implications of a rating scheme on upstream competing firms through analytical modeling, whereas this literature has focused on the influence of a rating scheme on downstream consumers via empirical methods.

Our study is more closely related to the modeling literature on the impact of product reviews on product pricing strategies (Chen and Xie 2008, Li and Hitt 2010, Li et al. 2011, Sun 2012, Jiang and Guo 2015, Kwark et al. 2017, Li 2017). Chen and Xie (2008) analyzed how a seller should adjust pricing strategy and the amount of information provided to consumers in response to consumer reviews. Li et al. (2011) showed that online product reviews influence firms’ pricing decisions for products with repeat purchases. Kwark et al. (2017) showed that retailers can use the upstream pricing scheme (wholesale versus agency) as a strategic tool to benefit from online product reviews. Li (2017) identified conditions under which revealing average ratings is more profitable than not revealing them. All these studies consider SD. Li and Hitt (2010) and Jiang and Guo (2015) considered the influence of product prices on reviews and sellers’ choice of rating schemes based on such influences. Li and Hitt (2010) examined the impacts of providing an overall product rating that is influenced by price and the impacts providing a separate product quality rating that is not influenced by price on a firm’s optimal pricing decisions and consumer welfare. They found empirical evidence that overall product ratings can be substantially biased by price effects. Jiang and Guo (2015) analyzed firms’ review system design and product pricing strategies, particularly in terms of which rating scale cardinality to use and whether to offer granular review reports. They showed that the like/dislike type of a binary review scheme is good for niche products, whereas the 1-5 (or 1-10) stars type of granular scheme is better for mainstream products. Moreover, when consumer misfit cost is low, Jiang and Guo showed that offering overall ratings only without granular review reports is superior from the firm perspective.

Our work differs from and builds on the modeling literature on online reviews in several ways.<sup>4</sup> First, our notion of singledimensional and multidimensional rating schemes is different from the rating schemes considered in prior literature. For instance, in both Li and Hitt (2010) and Jiang and Guo (2015), the product quality has a single dimension. In Li and Hitt (2010), the more granular rating scheme provides the absolute rating on this quality dimension and the less granular rating scheme provides an overall rating that is influenced by price. That is, the key difference between SD and MD in their model is whether the rating is price adjusted or not. In Jiang and Guo (2015), more granular review reports about product quality imply that consumers can better infer the product fit. In contrast to these papers, we provide a multidimensional quality in that each product has two dimensions of quality and consumers are uncertain about quality in both dimensions. Furthermore, unlike Li and Hitt (2010), we consider uncertainty in individual ratings and explicitly examine the impact of rating precision. In contrast to Jiang and Guo (2015), we formally model various market types (e.g., when products are perfectly differentiated, partially differentiated, and undifferentiated) and consider a competitive marketplace as opposed to the monopoly setting. Essentially, we provide a novel mechanism, based on heterogeneity in consumers’ relative weights assigned to multiple quality attributes, through which SD and MD affect the perceived differentiation between the competing sellers. Such a mechanism has not been articulated in the prior literature.

Finally, our work is also related to the research on product information disclosure by firms. Sun (2011) found that a monopolist does not always reveal horizontal location information when the quality of its product is publicly known. Hotz and Xiao (2013) considered a context in which products are differentiated in a vertical quality dimension and a horizontal fit dimension and consumers are uncertain only on the quality dimension. They showed that competing firms may choose not to reveal quality, regardless of whether they produce a high- or low-quality product, if consumers’ preferences on fit and quality dimensions are highly correlated. Our work differs from the product information disclosure literature in several ways. In contrast to Sun (2011) and Hotz and Xiao (2013), we consider a context in which products differ on two vertical quality dimensions and consumers are uncertain on both these dimensions. Therefore, while the question of whether to provide information (i.e., rating) at an aggregate level using SD or at the individual attribute level using MD arises in our context, such a question does not arise in prior studies, because uncertainty exists in only one dimension in them. Moreover, the mechanism that alters the intensity of competition between sellers is different in our context. For instance, in Hotz and Xiao (2013), products are already differentiated on the fit dimension and the impact of quality information disclosure is driven by whether or not it reduces the differentiation. In our context, the extent of perceived differentiation can differ under SD and MD, even when there is no (true) differentiation between products.

## Model

We consider an online marketplace in which a platform sells two competing products from two sellers.<sup>5</sup> Products are characterized by different quality attributes, and consumers have heterogeneous preferences regarding these attributes. The platform deploys an online review system that provides quality ratings for products. The platform charges firms a commission that is a percentage of the sale price for each unit sold. We assume a fixed marginal production cost, normalized to zero, for each seller.

## Consumer Utility and Consumer Types

Consumers belong to one of two segments: (1) comparison shoppers, and (2) loyal consumers (Chen and Xie 2005, Kwark et al. 2014). Comparison shoppers (shoppers hereafter), each of whom has a unit demand, use the reviews, in addition to their own assessment of product quality, to decide which of the two products to purchase. On the other hand, loyal consumers are loyal to one of the two sellers and either purchase the product from the seller that they are loyal to or do not purchase at all.

Shoppers differ in their preferences regarding the relative importance they attach to each of the quality attributes. ## Timing of the Game

The game consists of three stages. In the first stage, the reviewers provide their ratings according to the rating scheme. In the second stage, firms simultaneously set their prices $p _ { i } ,$ after observing the reviewers’ ratings. In the final stage, shoppers and loyal consumers make their purchase decisions after observing prices and reviewers’ ratings. We assume that the base utility is high enough that each shopper purchases one of the two products under both rating schemes.

The game sequence implies that prices represent sellers’ tactical decisions in the sense that sellers can change their prices easily. In fact, empirical findings show that sellers react to reviews and change prices accordingly (Jiang and Wang 2008). Furthermore, the game sequence implies an examination of the long-term implications of rating schemes where the reviews have stabilized and additional reviews have little influence on the distribution of the mean rating. This is in contrast to models that examine the short-term implications of reviews and hence assume that sellers can use pricing strategies to influence later reviews (e.g., Li and Hitt, 2010). Thus, our timeline is consistent with our goal of providing guidance regarding the rating scheme design, which is a longterm decision for platforms that does not consider sellers’ short-term review manipulation strategies through price and other means.

Consumers know their own segment and type. Sellers do not know either the segment or type of any consumer but they know the distributions. All other model parameters are common knowledge. All players are risk neutral. Table D1 in Appendix D summarizes the notations used in this paper.

As shown in Proposition 1, whether sellers and platforms realize higher profits under MD versus SD depends critically on whether the firms are differentiated on at least one attribute. This finding is the result of a subtle impact of the rating scheme on shoppers’ perceptions of product differentiation. We explain the intuition behind this impact in the following paragraphs.

Consider SD. Shoppers form their perceptions about the qualities of (and hence the utility derived from) the two products using a single overall rating for each product. Since reviewers differ in the relative value they assign to the two attributes, the aggregate reviewer rating for each product follows a normal distribution with a positive variance. Note that the difference in ratings between the two products follows $\begin{array} { r } { N o r m a l ( \frac { q _ { 1 } ^ { a } - q _ { 2 } ^ { a } + q _ { 1 } ^ { b } - q _ { 2 } ^ { b } } { 2 } , \frac { ( \hat { q _ { 1 } ^ { a } } - q _ { 1 } ^ { b } ) ^ { 2 } + ( q _ { 2 } ^ { a } - q _ { 2 } ^ { b } ) ^ { 2 } } { 1 2 N } ) } \end{array}$ when $\sigma _ { r } ^ { 2 }$ is zero and $\sigma _ { 0 } ^ { 2 }$ approaches infinity. We further note that the variance in ratings exists whether or not the firms are truly differentiated; this variance is solely driven by heterogeneous consumer preferences for the attributes. For instance, the quality of ?? and ??, respectively, for attributes ?? and ?? for both products implies no (true) differentiation between firms. Yet, the rating difference has a positive variance of $( H - L ) ^ { 2 } / 6 N$ This positive variance in the rating difference implies that there is some probability that the ratings can suggest that the two products are not homogeneous. When the ratings for the two products are different, sellers exploit this perceived differentiation (whether the differentiation is real or not), which leads to positive expected profits for sellers.

On the other hand, in the case of MD, shoppers learn the true quality of each attribute for both products from the reviews when $\sigma _ { r } ^ { 2 }$ is zero. Thus, if products are not truly differentiated, shoppers will also realize the absence of differentiation between the two products. Therefore, sellers will engage in intense price competition, which would lead to low profits for sellers under MD. If products are truly differentiated, then shoppers will realize the true extent of differentiation, which would lead to higher expected seller profits, depending on the extent of differentiation.

Figures 2(a) and 2(b) illustrate how the rating scheme affects the shoppers’ perceived differentiation between the two products in (1) a differentiated market, and (2) an undifferentiated market, respectively. Figure 2(b) shows that there is a probability mass of one at zero for no perceived differentiation under MD, but a positive probability for nonzero perceived differentiation under SD. Thus, SD enables sellers to take advantage of the perceived differentiation between the two products in an undifferentiated market. Figure 2(a) shows that the perceived differentiation is uniformly distributed under MD, implying a larger variance under MD compared to a normal distribution under SD (albeit with the same mean as that of the distribution under MD). As a result, sellers are able to benefit more under MD than under SD in perfectly and partially differentiated markets. The net effect of the impact of a rating scheme on shoppers’ perceived differentiation is that the differentiation falsely implied by SD benefits sellers if products are not truly differentiated. In contrast, by enabling shoppers to realize the true differentiation, MD benefits sellers when products are truly differentiated.

An important implication of Proposition 1 is that providing finer-grained information to consumers does not always benefit sellers. Clearly, finer information is never inferior to coarser information in resolving consumer uncertainty about product qualities. However, when sellers respond strategically to reviews by adjusting their prices, they may be hurt under MD, although MD is more consumer friendly (in terms of resolving consumer uncertainty) than SD.

Proposition 2. The consumer surplus is (i) higher under MD than under SD in an undifferentiated market, and (ii) lower under MD than under SD in perfectly and partially differentiated markets.

Proposition 2 reveals that the comparison between the two rating schemes along the consumer surplus dimension is qualitatively the opposite of that along the seller profit dimension. That is, consumers enjoy a higher consumer surplus under MD than under SD only when products are not differentiated. The intuition for Proposition 2 follows from the discussion that comes after Proposition 1. The impact of a rating scheme on price competition between firms, i.e., the competition is more intense under MD than under SD in an undifferentiated market, partly explains this finding. However, we note that the consumer surplus is determined by misfit costs incurred by shoppers as well as price. Clearly, MD can be better than SD for enabling shoppers to find the product that matches their attribute preferences and hence offers higher utility. Therefore, if MD induces more intense price competition between sellers and also reduces misfit costs, then consumers (shoppers as well as loyal consumers) are better off under MD than under SD. Proposition 2 shows that, in a differentiated market, the adverse impact of MD on consumers in the form of less intense price competition compared to SD offsets any benefit in terms of reduction in shoppers’ misfit costs such that consumers are worse off with superior information.

Proposition 2 is a key result with significant implications in light of the commonly held view that MD benefits consumers who have heterogeneous preferences (Chen et al. 2017). However, our findings reveal that it is not necessarily the case. We show that if sellers are able to differentiate themselves in terms of their offerings (i.e., specialize in different attributes) and adjust prices in response to reviews, then consumers are actually hurt by the finer product ratings enabled by MD. Indeed, Archak et al. (2011) show that firms can mine textual reviews to understand the relative importance assigned by consumers to various product attributes and derive significant pricing power. Thus, when sellers are able to respond to reviews and adjust prices in this manner, the conclusion that MD always benefits consumers may not be true.

Proposition 3: Social welfare, defined as the sum of the total seller profit and consumer surplus, is (i) higher under MD than under SD in a perfectly differentiated market; (ii) lower under MD than under SD in a partially differentiated market; and (iii) higher under MD than under SD in an undifferentiated market.

When products are not differentiated, shoppers’ total misfit cost is the same under both rating schemes. However, loyal consumers’ welfare is higher under MD than under SD because of the higher demand created by lower prices. Consequently, social welfare is higher under MD than under SD in an undifferentiated market.

Proposition 3 reveals an interesting result that social welfare is higher under SD than under MD in a partially differentiated market. Moreover, it also shows that finer-grained information does not necessarily reduce the overall shopper misfit cost. The explanation for this result is as follows: Suppose that Product 1 is high quality and Product 2 is low quality for attribute ?? and that both products are high quality for attribute ??. In this case, Product 1 is superior to Product 2 for all shoppers regardless of their preference. Under MD, it is profitable for Seller 1 to target shoppers that attach high importance to attribute ?? and leave shoppers that do not highly value attribute ?? to Seller 2. Consequently, even though shoppers that buy Product 2 have higher gross utility for Product 1, they end up buying Product 2, which offers a higher net utility because its lower price offsets its smaller gross utility compared to Product 1. On the other hand, the aggregation of ratings under SD masks the superiority of Product 1, resulting in lower variability in rating difference compared to MD. Consequently, Seller 1’s targeting ability diminishes under SD compared to MD; thus, Seller 1 will be motivated to target a larger shopper base by decreasing the price. Therefore, more shoppers end up buying Product 1, which results in higher social welfare under SD than under MD.

Propositions 1-3 provide significant insights into the conditions under which sellers, consumers, and social planners will prefer finer-grained quality information when consumers rely on reviews to make their purchase decisions. Naturally, another question of interest is the degree to which one rating scheme is superior to the other from the perspectives of different stakeholders. We find that the superiority of a rating scheme—i.e., the magnitude of the difference in the payoffs between the two rating schemes— for each player depends on the difference between high and low levels of quality (i.e., ?? − ??). Proposition 4: An increase in (H-L) (i) increases (decreases) the superiority of MD over SD for sellers in a perfectly differentiated market (undifferentiated market); (ii) decreases (increases) the superiority of MD over SD for consumers in a perfectly differentiated market (undifferentiated market); (iii) increases the superiority of MD compared to SD for social planners in an undifferentiated market; (iv) may increase or decrease the superiority of MD (SD) over SD (MD) for sellers, consumers, and social planners in a partially differentiated market, and (v) may increase or decrease the superiority of MD over SD for social planners in a perfectly differentiated market.

Proposition 4 shows that a larger difference between high and low levels of quality generally enhances the superiority of the rating scheme, which leads to a higher payoff for each stakeholder. Recall from Proposition 1 that sellers realize higher profits under MD versus SD if and only if firms are differentiated. Thus, when products are perfectly differentiated, a larger $( H - L )$ increases the attractiveness of MD over SD. On the other hand, when products are not differentiated, a larger $( H - L )$ increases the attractiveness of SD over MD. In effect, if we consider $( H - L )$ as a proxy for the extent of quality differentiation (i.e., between high and low levels of quality), then quality differentiation complements product differentiation regarding the relative impacts of the two rating schemes on sellers in perfectly differentiated and undifferentiated markets—higher quality differentiation enhances the attractiveness of the rating scheme preferred by sellers. Thus, sellers have significant incentives to deploy MD in a market characterized by differentiated products and high levels of quality differentiation. The impact of quality differentiation on consumer surplus is opposite to the impact on seller profit. When MD hurts (benefits) consumers relative to SD, an increase in quality differentiation exacerbates (enhances) consumer surplus. The impact of quality differentiation in partially differentiated markets is nonmonotonic and depends on model parameters.

## Conclusion and Implications

We summarize the key findings of our study in Table 2. The table shows the rating scheme (MD or SD) that offers the highest seller profit, consumer surplus, and social welfare. It is clear from the table that (1) product differentiation, and (2) review precision play important roles in determining whether finer-grained review information is better from the perspective of each stakeholder.

The above findings have important implications for both academics and different stakeholders such as sellers, platforms, and consumers. From an academic perspective, focusing on the information transfer aspect of the rating scheme, which relates solely to the consumer side, provides only a partial picture and can be misleading. Our findings demonstrate the need to examine how the rating scheme affects both sides of the marketplace, i.e., consumers and sellers. The study provides several testable hypotheses about the effects of rating schemes on sellers and consumers. In particular, it shows that the impact of reviews varies depending on the rating scheme, market characteristics such as firm differentiation, and product type as it relates to the precision with which reviewers can ascertain product quality. For instance, credence goods have more uncertainty associated with them than experience goods which, in turn, have more uncertainty associated with them than search goods. While the existing studies on the influence of reviews have largely focused on product sales, our study points to a need to account for seller profitability and other factors identified in this paper for a more complete understanding of the role played by online reviews.

From the seller and platform perspectives, the results suggest that there is no one rating scheme that is appropriate (i.e., offers the highest profit) for sellers across all product markets. Some product markets are highly amenable to differentiation, such as those that sell products with high variety (for example, TVs, computers, and other electronics). A finer-grained rating system could be more profitable to sellers in these markets. However, some other product markets may not be easily susceptible to differentiation. For instance, competing hotels or restaurants located in geographical proximity may not be able to differentiate in terms of the location attribute. In such cases, a coarse-grained rating system such as SD may be a better choice for sellers and hence platforms. Consequently, platforms must carefully assess market characteristics before adopting a rating scheme for consumer reviews.

Finally, from a consumer’s perspective, our study cautions against a commonly held view that a more refined rating scheme that provides individual ratings on various product features is always beneficial to consumers (Chen et al. 2017, Li and Hitt 2010). Even though such a scheme enables consumers to obtain a clearer understanding of products, consumers are not necessarily better off with more granular product information. Consumers should realize that sellers also take advantage of this granular information and engage in less severe price competition to attract consumers.

Clearly, we used a stylized parsimonious model that captures the essential aspects pertaining to the research question we examine. Consequently, we abstract away several details related to the review generation process and the factors that influence this process. Furthermore, we made other assumptions, such as two sellers and uniform versus linear distribution of consumer preference. Despite the limitations, our analysis provides rich insights that extend the understanding of the impacts of reviews on marketplace participants.

Our study can be extended in several ways. A valuable extension is to test the findings of this study empirically. The study suggests several testable empirical hypotheses. For instance, the moderating role of product differentiation regarding the impact of the review rating scheme on product sales needs empirical investigation. Analogously, the moderating role of product type and review precision in terms of how the rating scheme affects product sales requires empirical testing as well.

The study could also be theoretically extended. One direction would be incorporating both short- and long-term seller decisions in the model so that the rating schemes could be compared in terms of both short- and long-term impacts. A potential model adjustment would be to add an additional stage in the current game sequence where firms initially set a price which, in turn, affects reviewers’ ratings. Such a dynamic model in which the initial price affects reviewers’ ratings and firms subsequently react to reviews by adjusting prices could capture the interplay between short-term decisions and the long-term implications of review schemes. In such an extension, firms would strategically decide whether they should seek short-term profits or be more forwardlooking to accomplish long-term objectives. We leave this model extension for future research.
