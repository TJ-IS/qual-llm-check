## Abstract

User-website interactions in service contexts create opportunities for positive online experiences that can foster brand equity, nurture trust, and eventually lead to long-term relationship building. The purpose of this study is to examine the concept of “online customer experiences”, “flow”, and its mediating role in influencing customers’ loyalty to a hotel booking website. To achieve this goal, a theoretical model was developed, which suggests that online flow in services is formed by both hedonic and utilitarian features. According to study results, hedonic and utilitarian features affect the flow experience positively. Study results underline that the hedonic features have a stronger effect on the flow experience compared to utilitarian features. The findings imply that when customers experience flow in online hotel booking services, they generate a feeling of trust towards the website. Positive experiences with the website enhance the brand equity. These findings can help researchers and service providers understand the flow concept in online hotel booking services.

Keywords: flow experience, hotel booking services, e-loyalty, hedonic website features, utilitarian website features, brand equity

## Introduction

The Internet has amplified the opportunities to advance the hotel industry (Ip, Leung, & Law, 2011). By driving the adoption of online booking services, hoteliers can achieve high return on investments (eTourism, 2011). Despite the apparent popularity of the Internet as a distribution channel in hotel booking services (Inversini & Masiero, 2014; Lee, Guillet & Law, 2013; Law, Leung, Lo, Leung, & Fong, 2015), loyalty levels to hotel websites have declined since 2002 (Cole, 2011). Forrester research reported that

40% of business travelers are not loyal to any travel company (Cole, 2011). Lee et al. (2013) highlight that 69% of visitors in 2009 booked their accommodation online, this represents a 50% increase from 2008. Though, among those who used hotel booking services, only 20% used a hotel website, demonstrating a significant decrease. In recent years, many hotels have experienced increases in bookings through online travel agent channels such as Expedia, Orbitz, and Travelocity (tnooz, 2014). Moreover, more travelers are choosing to book places to stay through alternative lodging websites like Airbnb. It is estimated that 14% of travelers booked a private home, condo or apartment for at least one of their trips in 2013 (Rosenbloom, 2015). The decline in hotel website booking when compared to online travel agents is also due to the fact that most hotel brands offer identical products and services with little differentiation among themselves (Victorino, Verma, & Plaschka, 2005). Also, Morosan and Jeong (2008) investigated hotel booking websites and their findings indicate that travelers favor intermediary websites over hotel-owned websites for reservation purposes. The 2015 ACSI Travel Report, featuring customer ratings for hotels and online travel agencies, reveals that online travel agencies rate higher in customer satisfaction overall than hotel websites (Rudnansky, 2015). Therefore, it is necessary for hotel brands to develop websites that can attract customers, and encourage online booking and loyalty (Jeong, Oh & Gregoire, 2005). It was found that 24% of annual online revenue is lost globally due to poor online customer experience (Gregoriadis, 2011). With the importance of customer experience to business performance, less attention has been offered to the development of the online customer experience that results from the customer interaction in online service encounters (Rose, Clark, Samouel, & Hair, 2012). The online customer experience is

important for hotel booking services (Bilgihan & Bujisic, 2014). Consumers may seek both hedonic and utilitarian benefits when making online reservations, which potentially lead to a “flow” state. Previous research studies have examined flow in e-commerce little research attention (Carlson & O’Cass, 2011).

To this end, this research integrates a model of flow that aims to develop loyalty to hotel booking services. The term of flow was first defined by Csikszentmihalyi (1975) as a situation where individuals act with a sense of total control, concentration, deep involvement, and enjoyment. Later studies adopted similar definitions, claiming that flow is the enjoyable experience that people feel when acting with total involvement (Hung, Chou & Ding, 2012). Previous research studies evoke that applying the concept of flow underdeveloped field (Guo, 2004; Inversini & Masiero, 2014; Nusair & Parsa, 2011). This is especially the case for services such as hotel stays, tourist attractions, and travel packages (Gao & Bai, 2014). Considering the fact that tourism is becoming the leading driver in e-commerce business as it accounts for 43.9% of the B2C e-commerce markets (Gao & Bai, 2014) it could be argued that hotels could provide compelling experiences through their websites. A well-designed website conveys positive brand perceptions in the mind of customers (Bilgihan, Okumus, Nusair, & Bujisic, 2014).

A unique characteristic of online shopping for services suggests that customers have to base their judgments on information presented (e.g. room pictures, virtual tours) on the websites. Specifically, consumer purchases are usually based on appearance such as pictures, images, quality information, and video clips of the product (Hong, Thong, &

Tam, 2004; Kolesar & Galbraith, 2000). Consequently, the promise of online hotel room reservation depends, to a great extent, on the online experience, the user interface and how people interact with the service setting (Griffith, Krampf & Palmer, 2001; Hong et al., 2004).

Flow experiences are important to keep customers satisfied in the context of services (O’Cass & Carlson, 2010). In order to compete with online travel agents, hotel brands need to offer compelling online experiences to foster customer purchase intent and brand loyalty. Delivering better online interactions provides an opportunity for the hotel brand to offer its customers an optimal shopping experience where the visitor is deeply involved in the shopping activity and is completely immersed in it (Gao & Bai, 2014). Flow is an important construct for the explanation of consumer behavior in the computerresearch is to enhance the understanding the concept of flow and the customer experiences for hotel-owned online services. The primary aim of this study is to develop a theory-based model of flow experience that enhances trust and brand equity, and also encourages customer loyalty. More specifically, this study examines (1) the direct impact of hedonic and utilitarian website features on the flow experience; (2) the influence of hedonic website features on brand equity, and the impact of utilitarian website features on trust; (3) the influence of flow experience on brand equity, trust, and e-loyalty. For the purpose of this study, the lodging industry was chosen as an illustrative context of service industries.

## Literature Review and Hypotheses Development

Pine and Gilmore (2011) suggest that offering a unique experience is the key to winning the hearts and minds of consumers. Their work designates that consumers appreciate the experience more than the actual tangible value. Consequently, the experience is a vital element of the overall product/service being purchased (Bharwani and Jauhair, 2013; Gopalani & Shick, 2012; Morosan, Bowen, & Atwood, 2014; Ramanathan, 2012; Ritchie, Tung and Ritchie, 2011; Rust & Lemon, 2001). Accordingly, researchers examine the consumer’s shopping experience in online services with the “flow” construct as a potential measure to the consumer’s experience (Ding, Huang & Verma, 2011; Novak et al., 2000; Rose, Clark, Samouel & Hair, 2012; Teng et al., 2012).

As stated earlier, the term flow describes the situation when people act with a sense of total control, concentration, and deep involvement. Consumers’ interactions with hotel brand-related stimuli on the website can lead to a state of flow (Hoffman & Novak, 1996, 2009; Huang, 2003; Novak, Hoffman & Duhachek, 2003; Skadberg & Kimmler, 2004). Flow variables are related to attractive outcomes in online service contexts, such as positive affect (Chen, 2006), positive perceptions and attitudes toward websites (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000; Huang, 2003), exploratory behavior with increased learning (Skadberg & Kimmel, 2004), and future intentions to revisit and repurchase (Siekpe 2005; Wu & Chang, 2005).

## Utilitarian Features and Trust

Trust is successful in reducing uncertainty and risks (Chang, 2013; Pavlou, 2003; Suh & Han, 2003) and creating a sense of safety (Nikander & Karvonen, 2001). A successful website is one that magnetizes customers and makes them feel that the site is trustworthy, dependable, and reliable (Becerra, Badrinarayanan & Kim, 2013). The design quality of the website positively affects trust to the website. Ha and Stoel (2009) indicate that trust and enjoyment are the critical beliefs when it comes to online shopping. On the other hand, many consumers may prefer not to purchase online mainly due to the risks associated with conducting business online (Gefen & Straub, 2003). Consumers' trust towards e-tailers is possible when consumers believe that the technology usage is reliable and credible (Utz, Kerkhof, & van den Bos, 2012).

Previous studies indicate that improvements in the ease of use and utilities of a websites nurture trust in the minds of customers (Chien, Chen & Hsu, 2012; Roy, Dewit & Aubert, 2001). Cyr (2008) suggests that utilitarian user interface design variables are key antecedents to website trust. Similarly, Lowry et al. (2008) report that utilitarian website quality features such as navigation and functionality positivity impact trust. Therefore, it is proposed:

H4: In hotel booking services, greater utilitarian features correspond to greater trust Flow Experience, Brand Equity, Trust, & e-Loyalty

When people experience positive emotions, autonomic arousal takes place, which leads to cognitive broadening where one’s attention, thinking, and behavioral repertoires (e.g., explore, play) are broadened or expanded (Fredrickson et al., 2003). Consequently, the positive emotions that arise from the flow experience of interacting with an ecommerce website is expected to increase consumers’ learning about the brand strengthen their association to the brand. Nah, Eschenbrenner, DeWester and Park (2010) find a positive relationship between the flow experience and brand equity in service contexts. Therefore, it is proposed that:

H5: In hotel booking services, greater flow experience corresponds to greater brand equity

Flow in online environments reduces the possibility of undesirable consequences, such as negative attitudes and website avoidance (Dailey, 2004). Hampton-Sosa and Koufaris (2005) examine the effect of a firm’s website on a customer’s development of trust after a visit to the website. Their findings reveal that website appeal and smooth transitions are the significant predictors of trust, which in turn has a significant effect on intention to use the website in the future. Thus, it is expected that flow experience would positively influence trust:

H6: In hotel booking services, greater flow experience corresponds to greater trust

Smith and Sivakumar (2004) propose that flow experience impacts shopping behaviors including repurchase intentions. Similarly, in an online study, Wu and Chang (2005) indicate that, “flow was positively related to transaction intentions” (p. 942). Rose et al. (2012) study the implications for enhancing loyalty and repeat business in online contexts. Their study results indicate that in order to build customer loyalty in online contexts, e-retailers must provide a compelling online customer experience (flow) continuously over time. Additionally, Ilsever et al. (2007) propose that flow experience would impact e-loyalty. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H7: In hotel booking services, greater flow experience corresponds to greater loyalty Trust, Brand Equity, and e-Loyalty

The notion of brand equity emphasizes the significance of developing a long-term focus within brand management (Wood, 2000). Accordingly, Feldwick (1996) provides a classification of brand equity as a measure of the strength of the consumer’s attachment to a brand as a description of the associations and beliefs the consumer has about the brand. A strong brand name can facilitate consumers to differentiate among brands as a strong brand name conveys the quality of a product (Aaker, 1996). It is empirically confirmed that brand equity directly influences brand loyalty (Nam, Ekinci & Whyatt, 2011; Ramanathan and Ramanathan, 2011). We expect similar results for hotel booking services. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H8: In hotel booking services, greater brand equity corresponds to greater loyalty.

Trust plays a significant role in determining a customer’s actions regarding a service website. Empirical research has shown that trust increases customer intentions to purchase a service or a product from a company online (Jarvenpaa, Tractinsky & Saarinen 1999; Lynch, Kent & Srinivasan, 2001). It is hypothesized that customers who trust a company are more likely to use the website, whether for a repeat visit to the site or to make an actual purchase, and the more a consumer trusts a service provider, the more likely the consumer is to continue the relationship (de Ruyter, Moorman & Lemmink, 2001). Consistent with the aforementioned findings, Kim, Jin, and Swinney (2009) report that online trust is strongly related to loyalty. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H9: In online hotel booking services, greater trust corresponds to greater loyalty

## Method

A standardized, self-administered questionnaire was adopted from previous studies (see Table 2). Later, a focus group that consisted of a convenience sample of industry professionals, doctoral students, undergraduate students and academicians was asked to evaluate the items on the questionnaire, and a pilot study was conducted before administering the final questionnaire. The online questionnaire included sections for each of the constructs as well as a section on the respondent’s demographic information. Intercepted respondent’s completed questionnaires were used to check for face validity (Hair, Black, Babin & Anderson, 2010) to identify any problems with the design of the questionnaire, to determine if there were any grammatical or spelling errors, and to ensure that respondents understood the directions and questions. Based on the results of these steps, minor revisions were made before distributing the final questionnaire.

Wengraf (2001) highlights the importance of explaining the phenomenon to participants in their own language or dialect, as opposed to the language of the researcher and research community. Confusions could have resulted from engaging potential informants in a discussion about flow without first explaining the meaning of the term (Pace, 2004). Therefore, the following short narrative description of flow was presented to all survey participants:

“The word “flow” is used to describe a state of mind sometimes experienced by people who are totally involved in some activity. One example of flow is the case where a user is shopping online and achieves a state of mind where nothing else matter but the enjoyment. You are completely and deeply immersed in it. Many people report this state of mind when web pages browsing, on-line chatting and word processing .

A final version of the self-administered questionnaire was then created using the online questionnaire software, Qualtrics, and a marketing company was contacted to distribute the link for the online questionnaire. The questionnaire was sent to 20,000 randomly selected US individuals who were interested in purchasing travel products. After one month, 1298 responses were collected for a response rate of 6.5%. The first question of the questionnaire was for screening purposes to ensure that the respondent had booked a hotel room in the past year from a hotel-owned website. As only 40% of the subjects had booked a hotel room online in the last year, the data analysis consisted of only 520 respondents. After inputting the data into SPSS, it was determined that 9 questionnaires were missing substantially large amounts of data and were consequently eliminated. This brought the total number of usable questionnaires to 511. In the context of SEM, recommendations for sample size range from as low as five respondents for each observed variable to as high as 100 (Jaccard & Wan, 1996). Since it is recommended to use different datasets for the confirmatory and exploratory factor analyses (Hair et al., 2010), 150 of the respondents were randomly selected for exploratory factor analysis, and 350 of the subjects were randomly selected for the SEM data analysis. A sample size of 350 respondents is considered sufficient for SEM data analysis (Hair et al., 2010).

## Measurement Items

Items to measure the focal constructs of the study were derived from multiple disciplines including information systems, marketing, and consumer behavior (see Table 2 for constructs and measurement items). Since these dimensions have not been previously examined in an online hotel booking setting and based on the recommendations of the focus group to include additional items, the authors decided to use EFA to uncover the underlying items of the guests’ hotel booking experiences. EFA analyzes the structure of the interrelationship (correlations) among the items by defining sets of variables that are highly interrelated (Hair et al., 2010). EFA is helpful in providing insight into the structure of the measurement items and proposed model by establishing the factors and indicators to be used. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was then used to perform an exact test on the measurement theory by identifying the association between indicators and constructs (Hair et al., 2010). In other words, CFA allows the researcher to specify the items associated with each construct and the correlations between the constructs.

There are three main steps in conducting EFA. These steps include 1) assessment of the suitability of the data for factor analysis, 2) factor extraction, and 3) factor rotation and interpretation (Pallant, 2005). Two issues considered when determining the suitability of the data were sample size and the strength of the relationship among the items (variables) (Pallant, 2005). In determining the sample size, two factors were taken into account. First, because this study combines exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, it is recommended that the factor analysis be performed using separate data sets (Hair et al., 2010) because the separate data sets allow the researcher to test the theoretical construct under consideration. Using the same data set merely fits EFA results directly into the CFA. Therefore, an initial sample was examined using EFA, which was subsequently followed by another drawn sample for the CFA. It is recommended that a sample size of n=150 is sufficient for EFA given that there are several high loadings marker variables (above 0.80) (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2001). Second, to address the concerns of the inter-correlations among the items, two statistical measures were generated to help assess the factorability. These include the Kaiser-Myer-Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity (Pallant, 2005). To determine if the data were suitable for EFA, the correlation matrices were examined and the Kaiser-Myer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling and the Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity were calculated. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was .905, which is above the recommended value of .6, and the Barlett’s test of Sphericity was found to be significant $( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 6 6 6 ) =$ 6709.637, p<.01). The diagonals of the anti-image correlation matrix all exceeded .5, thus supporting the inclusion of each item in the factor analyses. As an approximately normal distribution was evident for the composite score data in the current study, the data were well suited for parametric statistical analyses.

Structural Equation Model

SEM involves developing measurement models to define latent variables and then establishing relationships or structural equations among the latent variables. The structural model was based on the measurement model obtained from the CFA results. Six latent constructs (hedonic features, utilitarian features, flow, brand equity, trust and eloyalty) and 21 observed variables were used to test the model. In SEM, the significance of the path coefficient in the model provides support for hypothesized relationships among the constructs. Similar to the CFA, because the normality assumption was met, the maximum likelihood estimate method using AMOS 20 was used to test the theoretical model.

The goodness-of-fit measures were used to assess the overall structural model fit. The overall fit indices for the proposed/base model were acceptable with Chi-square/df equal to 2.7, RMSEA equal to 0.069, NFI equal to 0.927, CFI equal to 0.947, IFI equal to 0.947, and RFI equal to 0.908. All of the fit indices for the base/proposed model indicated an acceptable structural model fit. Specification search (the process of finding the model of best fit) was not needed for the main study as the data initially fit the model (Marcoulides & Drezner, 2003).

## Discussion and Conclusions

Investigating the consumer experience in the online service contexts is an emerging area. Although we examined online hotel booking services, our findings may be generalizable to other service contexts. The focus of this research was to develop and test a theoretical model that examines the antecedents and consequences of the flow experience for hotel booking services. The findings of this study highlight the importance of pleasurable features of a website in creating positive online customer experiences because these features are related to the fun, playfulness, and pleasure that users anticipate from a service website. While such characteristics are important in creating flow, utilitarian elements are also critical. Therefore, they should not be discarded when trying to enhance the online customer experience. The traditional approaches in brickand-mortar commerce are not applicable in online service contexts. Rather, it is the constructs such as interaction, participation, immersion, and engagement that are important for services. As e-commerce matures, key aspects of online shopping shift from static to more interactive components. The advent of HTML 5, Web 2.0, Flash,

Ajax, Silverlight, landing pages, and online widgets help websites to enhance customers experiences. Marketers and website developers are encouraged to take cues from developed entertainment industries, such as movies and video games, to bring more seductive attributes to website design as positive online experiences build both brand equity and trust for services.

Although this study examined the flow experience in hotel booking services, the findings may be generalizable to other service contexts to some extent. This study contributes to extant research on online experiences in several ways. While previous studies in the fields of psychology and marketing have applied the flow construct to study how the flow experience influences customers’ assessment and behaviors (Korzaan, 2003; Novak et al., 2000), how service websites could foster flow was not explored in depth. Furthermore, previous studies have not investigated the impact of the various website features on flow experience for service purchases. Therefore, this study tested a model with precursors of flow experience in e-services by establishing a link between website features and the flow experience.

As Hoffman and Novak (2009) note, as the trends in technology continue to expand, new digital applications and a host of exciting new research opportunities for marketing and consumer behavior scholars continue to expand as well. It is important to note that because the online experience is a consumption event in and of itself, it is more interesting than traditional service websites for many consumers. It is necessary to remember that online shopping generates flow only under limited conditions (Hoffman & Novak, 2009), and accordingly, the model in this study highlighted the importance of not only hedonic features but also utilitarian features. Furthermore, it was established in this study that flow develops trust and leads to brand equity.

This study should help online marketers distinguish between factors, namely shopping experiences in services. Customer interactions with service websites create opportunities for creating positive experiences that can foster trust and brand equity. Whether products and services are provided in the traditional or virtual environment, customer interactions with environmental settings influence their emotional responses as well as their purchasing behaviors. For example, a hotel website’s design and graphic presentations are comparable to the overall ambience of the physical environment of the hotel that affects customers’ perceptions. In other words, the design of a website provides cues about a service company. The online shopping experience is also characterized by a complete absorption in what one does (in this case online shopping) and it is related to the utilitarian features of the service website that help consumers have a sense of personal control and an intrinsically rewarding experience. Thus, navigation, functionality, and reliability play important roles in creating positive experiences in online contexts. The findings of this study also suggest that the hedonic aspects of a website are important for fostering a positive brand equity. This highlights the importance of the aesthetics of a website in forming and sustaining a positive attitude towards a brand. On the other hand, utilitarian features are important precursors of online trust, which in turn positively impacts loyalty to the website.

This study encounters a number of limitations. It is believed that the use of a limited market sample and industry category (i.e., hotels) limit the generalizability of these findings to service industry-wide. In the current research, only a limited number of flow antecedents were reported. Moreover, there may be moderating variables such as the respondents’ familiarity to e-commerce or whether they belong to a hotel brand’s loyalty program. Also, further research may consider personal innovativeness and level of expertise as potential moderators.

Results of this study should promote future research. For example, the applicability of this model to other service contexts, such as e-tailing and e-banking, might be a possibility for future investigation. As the direct impact of the flow experience to loyalty was not significant in this study, future research might consider why flow experience only mediates e-loyalty. The sale of tablets and smartphones is on the rise. Mobile devices account for a rapidly growing share of online sales, and are expected to contribute to strong e-commerce sales growth in the future, therefore, online experiences of mobile devices should open doors for future research. Future studies may build models with different dependent variables, such as intent to purchase. Additional research may consider manipulating the flow experience with experimental designs. Finally, one can draw attention to the comparison of product and services’ online purchasing experiences.
