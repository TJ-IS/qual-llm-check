# Determinants of accepting wireless mobile data services in China

June Lu <sup>a,\*</sup>, Chang Liu <sup>b</sup>, Chun-Sheng Yu <sup>a</sup>, Kanliang Wang

<sup>a</sup> School of Business Administration, University of Houston-Victoria, Victoria, TX 77901, United States

<sup>b</sup> College of Business, Northern Illinois University, Dekalb, IL 60115, United States

<sup>c</sup> The School of Management, Xi’an Jiaotong University, Xi’an, Shaanxi 710049, China

Received 1 October 2006; received in revised form 26 September 2007; accepted 17 November 2007

## Abstract

Data services via wireless networks and mobile devices have experienced rapid growth worldwide. We investigated the factors influencing adoption of wireless mobile data services (WMDS) in China and tested our model for explaining adoption intentions there. We argued tha individuals form their intention to adopt WMDS under the influence of wireless mobile technology, the social environment, persona innovativeness of IT, trust awareness, and the facilitating conditions. We examined the simultaneous effects of these five influences on beliefs in the context of wireless Internet data services via mobile phones. Survey data were collected from 1432 participants in several metro cities across China. The findings suggest that WMDS adoption intention in China is determined by consumers’ perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use of WMDS. Theoretical and practical implications are included in our paper. Published by Elsevier B.V.

Keywords: Mobile data services; Wireless Internet; Technology adoption; China; Belief antecedents; Mobile phones

## 1. Introduction

Wireless mobile data services (WMDS) refer to all types of digital data services via wireless networks accessible through any type of mobile devices. China now has the largest mobile communication network in the world in December 2006, the number of mobile communication subscribers in China reached 461.082 million, and the ownership of mobile phones was 35.3% [34]. A strong current trend is in Internet data services delivered via mobile phones because the wireless telecommunication infrastructure is now more completely developed than its fixed-line counterpart and mobile phones are more affordable [37].

Past studies have indicated that having mobile data capability and actually using it are two different things. For Chinese mobile operators, a subscriber is not necessarily a user; despite a 37.7% increase in mobile phone users from 2004 to 2006, there was a 3.87% decline in average revenue per user (ARPU) per month after promotion of simple mobile data services, according to a report of the Chinese Ministry of information industry (MII). This drop suggests that the apparent success has not guaranteed effective use of WMDS in China; this is, of course, important and has strategic implications for business practitioners. However, little is available for identifying the important determinants of implementing WMDS or for predicting the further development of WMDS in China.

In the United States and Europe, a number of theoretical models have been developed to attempt to understand factors impacting the acceptance of information and communication technologies (e.g., [10,17,26,32]). Among these, TAM is relatively robust in explaining IT adoption behavior. However the original model has had to be revised to examine other aspects. We therefore explored factors influencing the adoption of WMDS in China and introduced a model for explaining adoption intentions in China.

## 2. Background: Mobile communication and WMDS in China

## 2.1. General development of mobile communications in China

China’s mobile services are mostly provided by China Mobile and China Unicom corporations. China started its mobile telephone business in November, 1987 with just over 150 mobile subscribers. It took a decade for its number of subscribers to jump to 10 million. With 206 million mobile phone subscribers in 2002, China had become first in the world for its use of the mobile market. Since then mobile value-added services have skyrocketed at roughly a 100% annual rate. The development of China’s mobile market can be divided into three stages.

## 2.1.1. Initial development (1987–1993)

This was characterized by satisfying an urgent customer demand for mobile analog phones. The clumsy handset became a symbol of wealth and social status. Only business people in big cities and foreign corporations could afford the services [38]. China Telecom was the state-owned enterprise allowed to offer local and long-distance services.

In 1990s, the reforms that opened the market attracted huge foreign investment. Motorola, Nokia, and Ericsson together held 70% of the mobile handset market, while second tier suppliers like Siemens, Philips, and Alcatel accounted for another 20%. Domestic brands did not appear until 1998 [11]. By 1993 there were less than 700,000 mobile phone subscribers in China.

## 2.1.2. Rapid development (1994–2000)

The Chinese government decided to actively support the development of the domestic mobile communications industry in 1994, establishing China Unicom Corporation, or LianTong, to provide wireless and value-added services [29]. It became the driving force behind the transition from an analog network toward widespread use of digital global systems for mobile communications (GSM) services. In 1995, these services were launched in Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin and Guangzhou. In 1996, automatic roaming service was provided to 15 provinces and cities. The price of a mobile phone was only 900 Chinese yuans (roughly 110 US dollars). Service charges also experienced a sharp drop due to competition. In 1999, China Telecom’s mobile division was spun off to become China Mobile [16], which focused on code division multiple access (CDMA) technology, touted to replace dial-up technology so that China Mobile could link directly to its customers, bypassing the fixed-line network of China Telecom. CDMA could provide better voice quality and reduce electromagnetic radiation. Since then, an increasing number of local manufacturers appeared in the mobile communications market. Three domestic brands – Bird, TCL, and Konda – were ranked in the top 10, both in industry output and sales. With a fully established nationwide GSM wireless network, the Chinese mobile market was recognized as the largest in the Asia Pacific area.

## 2.1.3. Transition to data services (2001–2006)

China Unicom and China Mobile had collectively led the revolution of the mobile communications industry in China. In 2001, China Unicom also entered the CDMA market. Both companies launched wireless application protocol (WAP) for wireless services and offered a variety of valueadded data services. By the end of 2002, the total number of subscribers reached 206.3 million [27]. By the end of 2003, the number of mobile users in China exceeded the number of fixed-line users.

Since then, the mobile communication market has continued to grow. Intense market competition among domestic and foreign mobile phone manufactures required the government to introduce a license policy to control mobile imports and network entry.

Now, local wireless access services are well received, even in small cities and some remote villages. As the market has matured, the possibility of third generation wireless products has started to influence technology choices. China is actively expanding its CDMA networks nationwide and upgrading its GSM-based infrastructure to prepare for highspeed mobile data access, video on demand, and other services.

## 2.2. WMDS in China

Among all services, simple message service (SMS) (the transmission of text and its immediate display on the mobile device) was received enthusiastically by consumers. The short message business has been booming since 2000, when mobile phone users sent over one billion messages. People are showing a growing preference for the short message, which has become one of the major channels to extend Lunar New Year’s greetings to relatives and friends. Both China Mobile and China Unicom reported a significant increase in 2006, with 429.7 billion short messages, representing 41.1% growth over the previous year. According to the statistics for the first 9 months in 2006, the proportion of the revenue increase from China Mobile’s new mobile services was 20.8% [30].

Multimedia message service (MMS) (including multi media ring-tones) and WAP services have also grown rapidly. MMS, an upgraded version of SMS that allows users to send color photographs, images, voice, sounds and text, has grown steadily. Downloading and sending multimedia messages and ring-tones brought huge profits to mobile operators. For example, the color ring-tones business brought over 2.6 billion Chinese yuan (roughly US \$330 million) for China Mobile for the first 6 months in 2006 alone.

In addition to SMS and MMS services, mobile operators in China also allow users to browse wireless Internet for shopping, entertainment, sports news and movies, and financial information. Although there are more than 461 million mobile users, Internet users reached only 137 million in 2006 [7]. WMDS users represent only 12.4% of the total Internet user population. There is thus an urgent need to understand consumers’ beliefs and perceptions toward WMDS and to create the environment for forging acceptance of WMDS services.

## 3. Theoretical framework and research model

In our work we examined user acceptance by considering intention to use rather than actual use because WMDS is still at an early stage, with limited adoption and use in China. Fig. 1 presents our research model.

## 3.1. Perceived usefulness (PU) of WMDS

PU is considered to have a direct effect on intentions to use a technology. It is a measure of the individual’s subjective assessment of the utility offered by the technology.

Jiang et al. developed Utilization of the Internet Technology Model to explore user acceptance of Internet. They confirmed that utilization of the Internet was positively related to PU. However, they only investigated the use of the hardwired Internet and were centered on actual usage rather than the intention to use. Lu et al. [24] presented a theoretically-based research model for the Wireless Internet. They suggested that PU had a direct effect on intention to use.

Despite the high penetration rate of wireless mobile phones in China, most mobile phone users reported that they did not use any data service other than SMS and MMS in 2006, but that 77% of the Chinese mobile users wanted to buy 3G handsets in 2007 if the phones could provide useful mobile services such as high-speed Internet access, wireless positioning, video calls, mobile TV program watching, and multiple audio file formats for music downloading. Thus, we hypothesized:

H1. Perceived usefulness of WMDS has a significant positive effect on intention to use WMDS in China.

## 3.2. Perceived ease of use (PEoU)

PEoU involves an individual’s assessment of the effort involved in using a system. PU and PEoU are distinct but related constructs. Improvements in PEoU may contribute to improved performance. This suggested that PEoU would have a direct, positive effect on PU. However, Chau excluded PEoU from his model but admitted that ease of use plays an important role in the initial stages and thus PEoU may be salient only in the early stages of using a new technology. Considering that WMDS technologies are at the early stages for use, PRoU will be important for most mobile users. Indeed, a 1999 mail survey among 800 professionals conducted by Embedded Solutions in England found ease of use among the top five factors for determining use of wireless handheld devices [8]. A phone survey in 2004 by China Commercial Network among 1000 customers revealed that ease of use was among the most important factors when exploring the functions of a mobile phone [6] Consequently, we made the following hypotheses:

H2. Perceived ease of using WMDS has a significant positive effect on intention to use WMDS in China.

H3. Perceived ease of using WMDS has a significant positive effect on perceived usefulness of WMDS in China.

## 3.3. WMDS technology

We proposed a conceptual construct, WMDS technology, to help study the impact of technology on user intentions to accept WMDS. WDMS technology is a network of radioconnected devices and servers offering voice, information, and other Internet services. Operationally, it mainly consists of WMDS’ functionality and its interfaces.

## 3.3.1. WMDS functionality

WMDS has some functions that provide special Internet services. Specifically, wireless mobile services add new dimensions of localization, personalization, and immediacy to the Web. These features are major considerations for mobile users who are considering using WMDS. For instance, swift exchange of messages, immediate access to current news and financial data, multimedia enriched content, and information about nearby restaurants, gas stations, and movie theaters is available, based on user profiles. Service trends also include real-time traffic updates via removable wireless handsets and more user-centric and succinct content. Such location-based personal mobile services are increased by using generic over-the-air datasynchronization to collect, collate, and deliver information.

## 3.3.2. Interface design

While mobile handsets can provide access to an array of new WMDS applications, they impose limitations, such as small screen size, limited screen resolution, and cumbersome input mechanisms. The user interface, therefore, becomes central in application delivery.

Any aspect may have some effect on user satisfaction and mental effort, which ultimately shapes the overall impression of usefulness and importance of a WMDS. Therefore, we saw them as critical system determinants of both perceived usefulness and PEoU toward WMDS.

H4a. WMDS technology has a significant effect on perceived usefulness in terms of system functionality and interface design.

H4b. WMDS technology has a significant effect on perceived ease of using WMDS in terms of system functionality and interface design.

## 3.4. Personal innovativeness in information technology (PIIT)

PIIT is another research construct that has been shown to influence perceptions of usefulness [2]. Personal innovativeness in some individuals means that they willing to take risks. In innovation diffusion research, highly innovative individuals have been shown to be active seekers of new ideas. They are able to cope with high levels of uncertainty and develop more positive intentions to accept [31]. Agarwal and Prasad defined personal innovation in IT as the willingness of an individual to try out any new IT. They postulated that individuals with higher levels of innovativeness could be expected to develop more positive perceptions about the methods in terms of advantage, ease of use, compatibility, etc., and, have increased intentions to use it.

Therefore, we proposed the following hypotheses:

H5a. PIIT has a significant effect on perceived usefulness of WMDS in China.

H5b. PIIT has a significant effect on perceived ease of using WMDS in China.

## 3.5. Facilitating conditions

Facilitating conditions involved two dimensions: resource factors, such as time and money, and technology factors, involving compatibility issues that constrain usage. It has been posited that behavioral intention and IT usage would be less likely if less time or money is available and when technical incompatibility exists. Facilitating conditions are believed to include the availability of training and provision of support.

Facilitating conditions, however, can also be viewed as an external control in the environment. Behavior could not occur if the environment prevents it or if the facilitating conditions make the behavior difficult. Policies, regulations, and legal environment are therefore all critical to WMDS acceptance. Both businesses and consumers who engage in mobile data services or mobile commerce activities need legal and regulatory protections as they do when conducting traditional business transactions. In China, government influence via MII has been in regulating all wireless networks and thus the entire mobile industry and operations.

The effect of facilitating conditions on behavior has not been proven. Our interest was to test the effect of facilitating conditions, with perceived usefulness and ease of use. We therefore stated two hypotheses:

H6a. Facilitating conditions have a significant effect on perceived usefulness of WMDS in China.

H6b. Facilitating conditions have a significant effect on perceived ease of using WMDS in China.

## 3.6. Social influences

In Taylor and Todd’s study, social influences were defined as the effect of other people’s opinion, superior influence, and peer influence. Further, subjective norms, social pressures to perform or not perform a particular behavior, also are considered to influence behavioral intentions. Image is derived from research on diffusion of innovations. Moore and Benbasat defined it as the extent to which use of an innovation was perceived as enhancement of status.

Mobile users are often in social situations. According to a survey, new mobile phone models had changed from being communication equipment to fashionable accoutrement. Many people in China changed mobile phones to show that they followed the latest trend and thus improve or maintain their social status. To test these possibilities, we proposed the hypotheses:

H7a. Social influences have a significant effect on perceived usefulness of WMDS in China.

H7b. Social influences have a significant effect on perceived ease of use of WMDS in China.

H7c. Social influences have a significant effect on intentions to adopt WMDS in China.

## 3.7. Mobile trust

As business marketers place emphasis on building longterm relationships with their customers, mobile trust has assumed a major role [12,13,36]. It is a complex social phenomenon that reflects technological, behavioral, social, psychological, and organizational interactions between various agents. All business transactions require an element of trust, especially those conducted in the virtual and mobile environment.

## 3.7.1. Security

WMDS depends on wireless Internet and is exposed to a more danger, since hackers may intercept signals. Consumers would not pay for goods or services over a wireless medium if financial and personal information could not be securely transmitted. Wireless transport layer security (WTLS), public-key infrastructure (PKI), certificate authority (CA), wired equivalent privacy (WEP), device independent smart card, and wireless biometric services have emerged as common solutions. These help to authenticate the sender of a message, encrypt and decrypt messages, and provide a core infrastructure to verify that only the authorized user is making the transaction. The Chinese network-security products market has experienced fast development, with the scope of the market expected to reach to 7.8 billion Chinese yuan by early 2010 [31].

User concerns are mostly centered on protection of data transmission and storage. Security problems encountered include illegal data capture during transmission, illegal users and/or illegal networking, malicious data visit, loss of terminal devices, etc. Since wireless mobile applications in China are mostly mobile data services, the data security issue is more evident and needs improvement in both regulations and technology.

Recently, viruses have been transmitted via SMS, and have targeted phones manufactured by Nokia Corp. and some SIM cards. Practitioners believe it is not yet a greater concern than any other wireless threat. However, user security awareness is generally low in developing countries. Many businesses sell mobile data services without proper security protection.

## 3.7.2. Privacy

Privacy concerns often arise with new IT that support enhanced capabilities for collection, storage, use, and communication of personal information]. Reports of concern about privacy on the wireless Internet are increasing.

A recent study indicated no significant differences between US and Taiwan customers on privacy concerns regarding online activities [22]. Wireless Internet technology amplifies the power of individuals. In this new environment, individuals might be more aware of and more outspoken with their desires, aspirations, and perceptions of their rights, regardless of their different cultural backgrounds.

## 3.7.3. System reliability

In addition, overall system reliability also contributes to user-perceived mobile trustworthiness; it serves as the basis for mobile system trust. Wireless mobile system reliability is interpreted as the probability that the system remains successful in achieving its intended objectives within a given period of time and under a given set of conditions [33]. A wireless mobile system, by its nature, is vulnerable to many types of interferences. As wireless mobile systems become a major information system in China, the need will arise for measurement of their reliability.

Trust as an antecedent belief has been held as influential on perceived usefulness [14]. Chircu et al. [9] argued that trust was an important determinant of PEoU. The underlying logic was that trust reduced the need to understand, monitor, and control the situation, making adoption easier.

Mobile trust is expected and should have a direct effect on WMDS adoption intentions and an indirect effect on intentions mediated via perceived usefulness and PEoU. Thus, we stated hypotheses:

H8a. Mobile trust has a significant effect on perceived usefulness of WMDS in China.

H8b. Mobile trust has a significant effect on perceived ease of using WMDS in China.

H8c. Mobile trust has a significant effect on intentions of adopting WMDS in China.

## 4. Methods and results

A survey study was conducted in 2005 to validate the research model. The target innovation was the Internet data services directly available to users via mobile phones. Participants were individuals living in five cities across China.

\- Hangzhou (in the most developed southeast coastal region),

\- Chengdu (in the southwest region),

\- Xian (in the northwest region),

\- Jinan (in the east coastal region), and

\- Xiaoshan (a city in the southeast region).

\- Three coauthors contacted the universities and organizations in these different regions in China. Local university classroom instructors were asked to collect the completed questionnaires. In addition, five professional questionnaire collectors were hired to help. Because we had to contact those cities one by one, it took about six months for all completed questionnaires to be collected and sent to us in the United States. All research subjects received Chinese language survey questionnaires. Of 1498 completed survey questionnaires, only 66 were invalid or incomplete. Of 1432 valid data entries, 481 were from Xian, 112 from Jinan, 543 from two universities in Hangzhou, Zhejiang, 56 from CEOs and managers in a management training class in Xiaoshan, Zhejiang, and 240 from Chengdu state-owned institutions. For the university samples, most were graduate students. Most subjects were students, with instructors as the data collectors; comparison of important demographics, such as age, gender, mobile device possession, experience and length of computer use did not show any statistically significant difference between student and non-student subjects.

## 4.1. Construct operationalization

All the constructs had been validated in prior research studies in the United States. The three-item intention scale, the four-item ease of use scale, and the six-item usefulness scale were adapted from the work of Davis. Items of social influences were adapted from the work of Venkatesh and Davis. The facilitating conditions scale was adapted from the work by Thompson et al. [35] and of Taylor and Todd, and the four-item scale on PIIT was developed from the work of Agarwal and Prasad All items included in the survey were measured on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 7, with ‘‘1’’ for strongly disagree and ‘‘7’’ for strongly agree. The English version of the survey instrument is shown in Appendix A.

## 4.2. Data analysis and results

The data were first analyzed using SPSS 14.0 for Windows. Three categories of demographic variables were considered: device use (experience of use), personal data (age, gender, income, employment), and organizational data (business type, job title, ownership). Our survey shows that only 23.2% of mobile users who responded had one or less years of experience, most had two or more. Of the respondents, 73.5% were between the ages of 20–40. 59.6% were male; 49.2% had a full-time job. Less than twothirds provided income information, with 57.5% earning less than 3000 Chinese yuan (roughly 363 US dollars) a month. The respondents came from more than 14 fields. Most were working in service areas, financial institutions, or government agencies and were professionals and managers at different levels. Ownership of the organizations they worked varied from government agencies and state-run organizations to private, foreign/joint ventures, and corporations.

All the absolute univariate skew indexes were well within the range for normal distribution [20]. In order to ensure that the variables in each construct were internally consistent, reliability assessment was carried out using Cronbach’s alpha. Internal consistency reliability coefficients for research constructs were well above the commonly accepted level of 0.70 [28], except for PIIT (0.627). The data are listed in Table 2.

This helped us to observe whether the conceptual framework had provided an acceptable fit to the empirical data.

Discriminant validity was determined when indicators loaded more strongly on their corresponding construct than on other 6s in the model. ## 4.2.2. The path model

To find out whether the core structure of the model fit the empirical data set, a structural model was first built including major constructs such as intention, perceived usefulness, and ease of use. The model revealed a strong fit (GFI = 0.956, AGFI = 0.936, IFI = 0.955, TLI = 0.943, CFI = 0.954, RMSR = 0.037, and RMSEA = 0.063).

All the antecedents and relevant relationships were then added to the core model to test the full structural equation portion. The regression weight results revealed that social influences does not exert significant impact on either perceived usefulness or perceived ease of use as hypothesized. The social influences construct comprised two elements: image and norm. When each of these was allowed to relate to the construct of usefulness separately, none of the path coefficients was significant ( p = 0.578, p = 0.221). Social influences, however, were found to have a significant negative impact on intentions (0.216, p < 0.000). As an additional finding, PIIT related significantly to the intention construct $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ as postulated by Agarwal and Prasad.

The path coefficients and explained variance for this alternate structural model are shown in Fig. 2. Together, the predictors such as WMDS technology, mobile trust awareness, facilitating conditions, PIIT and social influences explained 41% of the variance in perceived ease of use. WMDS technology combined with the facilitating conditions, ease of use, mobile trust awareness, PIIT, and social influences accounted for 67% of the variance in perceived usefulness. The perceptual beliefs of usefulness and ease of use plus PIIT and social influences collectively explained 61% of the variance in the major dependent variable, intention to adopt WMDS.

Our empirical data strongly supported the core structure of the research model. The entire alternate model provided a detailed account of the key forces underlying respondents intention to adopt wireless mobile data services. Facilitating conditions had a significant impact on user perceived usefulness of WMDS. This is generally consistent with findings in the US. Facilitating conditions have been identified as more influential than many other factors on infusion or adoption of IS innovations (see [5,18]). A study on adoption of WAP services in Taiwan for mobile commerce also revealed facilitating conditions to be a critical factor influencing WAP services adoption behavior [15]. In that study, policies, regulations, and legal environment were considered a critical aspect of external facilitating conditions for WMDS acceptance. This finding corresponds to our discovery of the power of government policy control and corporate regulations in China. In fact, when mobile users travel, their usage patterns are dependent on regional, national or international regulations and on standardization of the physical and data-link layers of telecommunication in a country. In our study, this aspect of facilitating conditions was positively recognized by the participants. Another point worth attention is that, compared to the other variables in this domain, the effect of the cost of using WMDS received a much higher mean (M = 6) from respondents. To promote WMDS in China, more reasonable service charge plans have to be implemented.

Contrary to our hypothesis, facilitating conditions do not have significant impact on perceived ease of using of WMDS in our study. The respondents did not regard this construct important in forming or changing their perception of ease of using WMDS. The high cell phone penetration in China provides favorable facilitating conditions for the acceptance of WMDS. Since convenience and ease of use have been widely assumed and the advantage of WMDS was perceived as easy to use, it is thus understandable that the respondents did not take facilitating conditions as necessary in alleviating their mental effort in learning and using the services.

All three hypothesized relationships of mobile trust were supported by the empirical data. Of the six mobile trust indicators, only the two on security received a mode of five for agreement. People in China did not have a strong sense of individual rights until recently. In terms of privacy protection, they could hardly have any high expectations and therefore were unlikely to express any strong opinions. In the US, the wireless mobile trust construct was found to influence adoption intention significantly and directly [25]. In our study, a significant direct relationship emerged again ( p < 0.000).

PIIT had a significant effect on perceived ease of using WMDS as hypothesized. And there was significant positive causal relationship between PIIT and intention to adopt WMDS ( p < 0.001). PIIT has been used in a number of recent studies in the US (e.g., [21,23]). Agarwal and Prasad believed that individuals with higher PIIT could be expected to develop more positive perceptions about the target innovation.

## 6. Conclusion and implications

Our empirical data showed that intention to use WMDS via mobile phones in China was dependent on an individual’s perceptual beliefs about the target services in terms of their usefulness and ease of use, and also relied on PIIT and mobile trust belief. In China, the technology supporting such data services constituted significant and stronger influence over potential users’ perceptions about WMDS than other predictors (such as facilitating conditions, mobile trust of the wireless system, and PIIT). Social influences in terms of image and subject norm do not generate important impacts on an individual’s perceptual beliefs about WMDS, but had direct impact on WMDS adoption intentions.

From a perspective of theory advancement, we confirmed some salient predictors of key beliefs in wireless mobile technology acceptance. A few predictors explained 41% and 67% of the variances in instrumental beliefs. Our alternate model explained 61% of the variance in intention. These findings suggest that the major part of the hypothesized model applies, though it is not as accurate in explaining the acceptance decisions as expected.

For practitioners such as the mobile communication operators and data service providers, our results are useful in understanding the impact of region- or country-specific strategies in implementing WMDS. Practitioners need to focus their attention on commitment to improving WMDS technology for contingent adoption decisions. To encourage use, data service charges need to design with a facilitating mechanism built into the system for ready use. Practitioners also need to be more responsive to data security concerns in the wireless mobile sphere and be more careful in helping to improve mobile trust belief.

Previous studies in the US have indicated different effects of social influences, PIIT, and perceived usefulness. It will be interesting to see if the same effects will take place in China.
