# Multidimensional analysis model for a document warehouse that includes textual measures

Martha Mendoza <sup>a,b,</sup>⁎, Erwin Alegría <sup>a</sup>, Manuel Maca <sup>a</sup>, Carlos Cobos <sup>a,b</sup>, Elizabeth León <sup>c</sup>

<sup>b</sup> Electronic and Telecommunications Engineering Faculty, Universidad del Cauca, Colombia

<sup>c</sup> Engineering Faculty, Universidad Nacional de Colombia, Colombia

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 15 October 2012 Received in revised form 6 February 2015 Accepted 8 February 2015 Available online 16 February 2015

Keywords: Document warehouse OLAP Textual measures Text warehouse

## a b s t r a c t

Data warehouses and On-Line Analytical Processing tools, OLAP, together permit a multi-dimensional analysis of structured data information. However, as business systems are increasingly required to handle substantial quantities of unstructured textual information, the need arises for an effective and similar means of analysis. To manage unstructured text data stored in data warehouses, a new multi-dimensional analysis model is proposed that includes textual measures as well as a topic hierarchy. In this model, the textual measures that associate the topics with the text documents are generated by Probabilistic Latent Semantic Analysis. while the hierarchy is created automatically using a clustering algorithm. Documents are then able to be queried using OLAP tools. The model was evaluated from two viewpoints — query execution time and user satisfaction. Evaluation of execution time was carried out on scienti<sup>fi</sup>c articles using two query types and user satisfaction (with query time and ease of use) using statistical frequency and multivariate analyses. Encouraging observations included that as the number of documents increases, query time increases as a lineal, rather than exponential tendency. In addition, the model gained an increasing acceptance with use, while the visualization of the model was also well received by users.

## 1. Introduction

Data warehouses are widely used today for analyzing large amounts of data in a variety of contexts (e.g. marketing, health, education, and research [29]). The tool most commonly used for querying available information in data warehouses is On-Line Analytical Processing (OLAP), due to its ease of use and capacity to conduct <sup>fl</sup>exible analysis of the data in real time [23,25]. It has been routinely applied in many different domains [9,12,14,21,22,26,31].

The amount of unstructured data has risen signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly due to growth of the internet, more document information in business systems, and observations and comments added to object-relational databases. It is thought that no more than 20% of information extracted from data warehouses relates to simple numeric data, i.e. the remainder is hidden in nonnumeric data, or documents [27]. Unfortunately, conventional OLAP tools do not allow adequate management of this information in data cube queries. In the area of health, for example, information relating to the medical treatment of a patient usually comprises name of patient, prescribed medication, appointment date, diagnostic, and observations. This latter <sup>fi</sup>eld, the observations attribute, is unstructured and in modeling a data warehouse is usually omitted or, in the best case scenario, modeled as a descriptor. Regardless, it cannot be used in OLAP operations (drill-down, roll-up, slice, dice, and pivot). As a result, business analysts are often mak ing decisions while omitting this important information stored in texts.

In the research <sup>fi</sup>eld, to inquire about the state of the art of a particular subject, it is necessary to engage a great amount of documents, some of which fail to contribute in the desired way. Were it possible to store a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c article with its percentage of relation to certain areas or subjects, this would allow researchers to review articles closest to the subject about which they are inquiring. The handling of unstructured text opens the possibility of conducting a more complete analysis of the information existing in organizations.

A key challenge that data warehouses and OLAP tools face is thus to incorporate unstructured data and textual measures so they are navigable in the warehouse [5]. This paper proposes a multidimensional model that incorporates textual measures — generated with Probabilistic Latent Semantic Analysis (PLSA) — in combination with an automatically-created topic hierarchy. The model thus allows documents to be queried using OLAP operations. In addition, a prototype of OLAP is put forward that permits querying a set of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c articles stored in a warehouse.

The paper is organized as follows: Section 2 presents the most relevant related works. The architecture of the proposed model is outlined in Section 3. Section 4 explains the technical aspects of the model. Section 5 discusses in detail the creation of textual measures and the aggregation function for each of them. Section 6 presents the automatic creation of the topic-hierarchy using an evolutionary clustering algorithm, while Section 7 explains how to obtain the probabilities needed to relate the documents to the topics. The results of the evaluation, based on textual measures are found in Section 8. Section 9 presents visualization of queries conducted in the document warehouse using an OLAP tool. Section 10 presents conclusions and future work.

## 2. Related work

The research conducted on text analysis using OLAP tools can be divided in two categories [30]: text as categorical data, and text as an OLAP component.

In the approach that treats text as categorical data, document classi<sup>fi</sup>- cation methods are applied to class labels within a category. This model allows drill-down or roll-up operations on the category dimensions, but uses a traditional multidimensional cube (only with structured data). In addition, the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation process in these proposals requires training data (documents previously assigned to categories) and this information is not available in every scenario. There are several representative works for this approach. Cody et al. [3] from IBM in 2002 proposed some general ideas of how to integrate text mining algorithms with OLAP. Nonetheless, in this work the cubes are still the traditional ones and with a low integration level (at an external level) of both technologies. In addition, the subject of ef<sup>fi</sup>cient materialization of the cubes that handles the text dimension is not addressed. There is also McCabe's work [19] from 2000, focused on information retrieval, which presents the terms occurrence in the document as a fact for the multidimensional model, with dimensions for the term and document; hierarchies for time and localization; and a category dimension that contains a subject category for the term. The measure is the weight of the term within the document (term frequency). The main objective of this research consists in conducting searches by terms within the document, combined with time and location. In this respect, Tseng and Chou [27] presented in 2006 a general framework for a document warehouse (data sources, pre-processing of documents and data presentation). They de<sup>fi</sup>ne a dimensional model for this type of warehouse. The dimensions are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed in three categories: ordinary dimension, with the set of keywords that allows users to locate the desired documents directly; a metadata dimension that gives the document information (e.g. title, author, publisher, date, etc.); and <sup>fi</sup>nally the category dimension. This last dimension can be either a WordNet-based hierarchy, or one de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the user. The fact table is formed by a keyword composed of the foreign keys of the dimensions previously named, attributes used to derive the document measures (term frequency), and a Document ID column that represents the document identi<sup>fi</sup>er (a foreign key from a dimension that holds all the documents identi-<sup>fi</sup>ers and the <sup>fi</sup>le path). In this model the table granularity is by keyword, which makes user navigation through the document very complex. In addition it only contemplates counting measures, not a probabilistic measure that allows giving a better approximation and relevance of the subjects addressed by the document.

In turn, Franck Ravat et al. [8] propose a multidimensional model where they integrate an aggregation function, with the advantage of combining qualitative and quantitative analysis, i.e. the keyword analysis from a publication, with the objective of providing a view of the content of the publication. They provide a function that aggregates a set of keywords into a smaller set of more general keywords. This process is based on a conceptual model that provides: concepts or subjects adapted for supporting no-numeric textual measure as a hierarchical representation of the concepts analyzed with the help of ontologies, and a new concept of OLAP textual aggregation processing unit with the use of domain ontologies. This model requires ontology management, which makes it more complex since it corresponds to a concept domain hierarchy where each node represents a concept and each edge between nodes models an “is a” relationship. The model proposed in this paper introduces measures of the document content different from the traditional TF and IDF. The aggregation function of these textual measures is created by means of MultiDimensional eXpressions (MDX) and traditional programming language, besides the granularity of the fact table is at document level and not at a term level (keywords) as stated in these works, which allows greater query power on the data.

In recent years, there has been a great interest in the approach of text as an OLAP component that uses OLAP technology to exploit the text data that is stored on a Multidimensional Text Database (MTD). One of the main contributions is framed on Content Management Systems (CMS) with the Alkis et al. [1] proposal, called Multidimensional Content eXploration (MCX) from 2008. In MCX, a document repository from a CMS is used, and when the user executes a query, IR and OLAP techniques are mixed to generate reports of the kind “the k most relevant results”, along with text summaries through a <sup>fl</sup>at term dimension. This work focuses on the ef<sup>fi</sup>cient cube process, since the queries can involve hundreds, thousands or even millions of documents. To extract the most representative terms of the collection, it uses the most frequent terms or phrases on the query on real time and based on a random document sample, which makes it viable (scalable) in queries with large amounts of documents. For document analysis, the multidimensional cube is built in run-time and two types of dimensions are modeled: static, previously known in the application area, and dynamic, based on terms or phrases stored in the documents. The main drawbacks of MCX lie in the fact that the frequent terms and phrases do not hold enough meaning for the users and it does not allow a hierarchical exploration of the topics from the collection hindering analysis by the expert.

Subsequent to MCX, in 2008, a model called “Text Cube” [13] was proposed. This model combines traditional IR techniques such as calculating TF-IDF [15], stop-word removal, stemming, the use of a vocabulary that is speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the application area, WordNet as a general level ontology to convert terms into concepts and an inverted index of documents. With this data. it registers the TF and IDF in a cube dimension. thereby allowing summarizing (a subject of transcendental importance in OLAP [18]), and it navigates using the main OLAP operations (drilldown, roll-up, slice and dice) on this dimension. In this proposal, a Text Cube is formed using both traditional and IR measures, in addition to dimension hierarchies and new term hierarchies (given by experts from the application domain, where the semantic levels and relationships among the terms in the text are speci<sup>fi</sup>ed). In this model the term hierarchy behaves like an organization hierarchy (the depth of the hierarchy can vary from one branch to another) in which pull-down and push-up operations proposed by the authors can be executed. This hierarchy is used to calculate the summaries (abstracts) of the cells, but since there is no dimension related to this hierarchy in the fact table, it is not possible to explore the text data related with this hierarchy using OLAP operations. Unlike Text Cube, the investigation proposed in this paper has the topic dimension, which allows text navigability with classic OLAP operations. In addition, the topic hierarchy, unlike the term hierarchy, is automatically de<sup>fi</sup>ned, since in the majority of cases it is very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for users to de<sup>fi</sup>ne them beforehand.

After Text Cube, Zhang et al. [29,30] in 2009 proposed a new data model called “Topic Cube” that combines OLAP with a probabilistic topic modeling to allow a multidimensional modeling that includes a hierarchical text dimension. The new text dimension allows analysis of the cubes from a textual topic hierarchy organized with an improved implementation of the EM (Expectation–Maximization) clustering algorithm. In the cube materialization, location, time and topic dimensions are presented. They have a hierarchy where the standard drill-down and roll-up operations can be executed as in any other dimension of the model. This model requires previously having the text database and the topic hierarchy. Then, all the topic hierarchy documents are mapped and a measure for the document is calculated for each cell. The model proposed in this paper generates the topic hierarchy from a particular algorithm, which obtains the information required for materialization without using an expert in the area; the model will also allow the user to reach the lowest granularity level (i.e. the document).

## 3. Proposed document warehouse model

Document warehouses ought to provide the infrastructure to enable business executives to correctly organize, understand and categorize documents and aid them in strategic decision making [27]. A new model is therefore proposed that integrates unstructured text data in a warehouse. The general architecture of the model (Fig. 1) comprises four main processes: Topic Hierarchy Building, Probabilistic Measures Calculation, Extract–Transform–Load (ETL), and Multidimensional Cube Building. Each takes data or documents as input, converting them to input for the processes that follow. All are executed in an offline scenario before using an OLAP visualization tool.

In Topic Hierarchy Building, two algorithms process the document set to obtain the topic hierarchy (Fig. 2):

(1) Cosme (step 1) allows:

(a) Conversion of a PDF document into a text (.TXT) <sup>fi</sup>le,

(b) Cleaning of the text <sup>fi</sup>les, removing strange characters resulting from the conversion, and

(c) Generation of a series of XML <sup>fi</sup>les (Metadata) that contain all the relevant data from the documents (Fig. 3).

(2) A hierarchical version of the Iterative Global-Best Harmony Search K-means algorithm (IGBHSK) [2], this version receives the TXT <sup>fi</sup>les generated by the Cosme (Step 1) algorithm as input and builds a multilevel hierarchy. The hierarchical IGBHSK carries out:

(a) Preprocessing of text <sup>fi</sup>les,

(b) Construction of Terms by Documents Matrix (TDM) based on TDF-IDF [15], and

(c) Recursive Clustering of documents in order to generate the hierarchy with its respective labeling

The algorithm then produces the required outputs for the next processes, i.e. a topic hierarchy with its respective tags (Fig. 4a), an XML <sup>fi</sup>le with the clustered documents (Fig. 4b) and the TDM matrix.

Three things are worth mentioning here: (i) if the original <sup>fi</sup>les are in a format other than a text <sup>fi</sup>le, they must be converted somehow, just as is done with PDF <sup>fi</sup>les, and the Cosme (step 1) algorithm adjusted in the conversion process of PDF format to TXT for continuing with the algorithm, (ii) the topic hierarchy does not necessarily have a <sup>fi</sup>xed level for its structure since the depth level depends on the amount and similarity of processed documents, (iii) to generate the topic hierarchy, an algorithm different to modi<sup>fi</sup>ed IGBHSK can be used depending on the needs of the model, and (iv) the hierarchy can be generated manually by experts in the relevant area and the documents then assigned to the lowest level of the hierarchy using a document categorization algorithm [10,20,28].

The second process, Probabilistic Measures Calculation (Fig. 5) is responsible for executing the PLSA algorithm [24], receiving as input the topic hierarchy, clustered documents and TDM matrix, generated by the modi<sup>fi</sup>ed IGBHSK. When the PLSA model is applied to a set of documents represented in the TDM matrix, it normalizes the matrix and extracts a set of probabilities that characterize each topic in the hierarchy (in this case, the topics from the lowest level of the hierarchy). This means that the process allows assigning a probability to every document according to the topic in which it is assigned, and additionally, helps obtain and assign words with their probability to each topic. PLSA uses the EM algorithm [6,17] to improve the probabilities of the topics in the documents P(d|z) and the probabilities of the words in the topics P(w|z) according to a series of iterations. As an output, the probabilistic data needed by the next process is stored in a TXT <sup>fi</sup>le.

ETL (Extract–Transform–Load) [11] (Fig. 6) references the loading of the data into the relational database. Initially the Cosme (step 2) algorithm receives topic hierarchy, clustered documents, document metadata and the probabilities (P(d|z), P(w|z)). These <sup>fi</sup>les are combined to generate the multidimensional structure de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the model (XML for each dimension, see Section 4). An ETL tool then realizes the loading of the data directly in the relational database of the warehouse, by means three sub-processes: extraction, transformation and loading. Extraction is responsible for recovering the data from information sources (in this case, XML <sup>fi</sup>les). Transformation applies a series of business rules and/or functions (i.e. data type conversion) to the extracted data to convert them into data that will be loaded. Loading is the moment the transformed data is loaded into the relational database. The de<sup>fi</sup>ned restrictions (e.g. unique values, referential integrity, mandatory <sup>fi</sup>elds, and domains) are then applied to guarantee the quality of the data. A relational database is <sup>fi</sup>nally obtained with loaded data.

The fourth and <sup>fi</sup>nal process is the Multidimensional Cube Building (Fig. 7), which receives the relational database of the data warehouse. The sub-processes of the multidimensional cube are the creation of dimensions, text measure creation in MDX (language for advanced querying), creation and execution of the aggregation function realized using an assembly (more details of these last two are found in Section 5), and processing of the multidimensional cube. Finally, using an OLAP tool or report generator, users can visualize and analyze information in an intuitive way, generating multidimensional queries (ad hoc queries) with movable rows and columns or standard reports with <sup>fi</sup>xed rows and columns. The multidimensional cube provides a structure that allows <sup>fl</sup>exible access to the data in order to explore, analyze their relationships, and use information from a number of analysis perspectives, in this case information from textual measures of the documents.

## 4. Multidimensional model

The multidimensional model [11] comprises a central Fact Table, Assignation, related to the set of dimension tables by foreign keys in a Star Scheme (Fig. 8). The fact table has a granularity at the document level, with standard dimensions, a topic dimension, three many-tomany (M–M) relationships with dimensions, and textual measures with probabilities values obtained using PLSA.

## 4.1. Standard dimensions

The model presents a number of dimensions that relate to relevant information from a research article, such as: document dimension (Document) with name (document title) and document type, author dimension (Author) with name and email, date dimension (Date) with publishing date, location dimension (Location) with city and country of publication of the document, and word dimension (Word) with all words from the stored document set.

## 4.2. Topic dimension

The topic dimension (Topic) allows storing the topic hierarchy, de<sup>fi</sup>ned for the document set, modeled as a father-child hierarchy with automatically de<sup>fi</sup>ned topics. A hierarchic model facilitates 1:N (one-to-many) relationships so that a parent can have more than one child, located on the same level, while a child can have just one parent, located on the level immediately above. The lowest level of the hierarchy relates to the document according to the topics contained within it.

## 4.3. M–M relationships

An M–M relationship exists between Assignation and author dimension, via a bridge table (Author-Group Bridge) in which speci<sup>fi</sup>c authors are stored (scienti<sup>fi</sup>c articles often have more than one author). Such a relationship exists too between the fact table and topic dimension via Topic–Document-Group Bridge, since a document can be related with more than one topic. 4.4. Measures of the fact table and the topic and word dimension bridge tables

The fact table shows a calculated textual measure (Topics\_ Probab\_TM) that represents the probability a document has of being related to a topic. This allows the end user to visualize the percentage of the document related to each topic and thus identify key topics. Another measure (Documents\_TM) facilitates obtaining documents with their percentages according to the dimensions related in a query, for example getting documents by topic. The bridge table between topic and word dimensions features a further measure that shows probabilities of a word within topics (Word\_Probab\_TM). This helps identify the words that are most relevant in a topic. These measures allow qualitative and quantitative analysis to be carried out, improving decision-making for organizations and end users.

## 5. Textual measures and aggregation function

Regarding the model proposed, the Topics\_Probab\_TM, Documents\_ TM and Word\_Probab\_TM measures and the aggregation function that allows the different OLAP operations to be executed are de<sup>fi</sup>ned. These measures are created using an expression in MDX [16], to interact with data storage and recover mechanisms in runtime in the multidimensional cube.

Each measure has an associated aggregation function, by default a sum. An aggregation function can be personalized to address special requirements, in this case to average probabilities of topics repeated in a query, using a stored procedure that receives topics with their probabilities in TXT format (generated from a MDX measure) and <sup>fi</sup>nds repeated topics to average their probabilities. These calculated measures then are able to clearly show topics or documents with their respective probabilities for any chosen dimension, and words with their probabilities for the topic dimension.

## 5.2. Documents\_TM Measure

Suppose each cell of cube C represents a TM. This measure is constructed from a set of documents D, associating with each the probability of topics that the document covers separated by a colon (:) and documents by a semicolon (;). The aggregation function permits average percentages of repeated documents to be calculated (Fig. 10).

The TM measure (Documents\_TM) uses the pTopic\_Document attribute of the Topic–Document-Group Bridge (between Topic and Document). In this case a stored procedure is not used, because the percentage value is located in the pTopic attribute and it is not necessary to split the text strings. The formal expression of the TM measure is shown in Eq. (6).

$$
T M = \left\{D _ {1}: P _ {D _ {1}}; \oplus D _ {2}: P _ {D _ {2}}; \oplus \dots \oplus D _ {k}: P _ {D _ {k}}; \oplus \dots \oplus D _ {B}: P _ {D _ {B}}; \right\}\tag{6}

## 6. Topic hierarchy

In this research the topic hierarchy was built using a hierarchical version of the IGBHSK algorithm [2], which is based on a combination of global best harmony search as a global strategy for searching the solution space, k-means for <sup>fi</sup>nding the optimum value in the local search space, Bayesian information criteria as a <sup>fi</sup>tness function, and the FP-Growth algorithm to reduce the high dimensionality in the document vocabulary. IGBHSK was selected for creating the topic hierarchy because of its good results compared with Carrot2 algorithm and because in addition to de<sup>fi</sup>ning the document groups, it generates labels for each group, which is important for the topic dimension of the model proposed. However, IGBHSK is a partitional algorithm and does not generate a group hierarchy. Since the model needs a hierarchy that allows user navigation, it was necessary to modify IGBHSK to obtain the topic hierarchy and then associate each document to the lowest level of the hierarchy (Fig. 12). Each iteration of this algorithm clusters a subset of documents. Each cluster is then grouped in the next level of the hierarchy and so on. In this way building the hierarchy is done taking into account the topics contained in the entire set of documents (Fig. 13 presents the hierarchical version of IGBHSK). Resulting from the modi-<sup>fi</sup>ed IGBHSK, a three level topic hierarchy was obtained, which was then loaded in the topic dimension.

## 7. Probabilities using PLSA

To obtain the probabilities, the PLSA algorithm [24] is used. This algorithm proposes a probability model given a set of documents $D =$ $\{ d _ { 1 } , d _ { 2 } , d _ { 3 } , . . . , d _ { n } \}$ with words in a vocabulary $W = \{ w _ { 1 } , w _ { 2 } , w _ { 3 } , . . . , w _ { m } \} ,$ , ignoring the order or sequence in which a word appears. The data from the document collection can be summarized in a document-word A matrix of co-occurrence, where the $A _ { i j }$ element indicates the number of times the w term appears in the $d _ { i }$ document. This document collection representation is called vector space model and is used by many text recovery algorithms based on keywords. PLSA introduces the latent semantic variables $Z = \{ z _ { 1 } , z _ { 2 } , . . . , z _ { k } \}$ . Fig. 14 shows the basis for the probabilistic model. The model known as aspect model has two forms: the asymmetric and the symmetric forms. In this research, the asymmetric form is used since, as suggested by Hoffman, if the cardinality of z is smaller than the amount of documents, the model can be asymmetrically parameterized according to the de<sup>fi</sup>ned probabilities.

Given the aspect model, the joint probability on the matrix $A =$ $D \times W$ (where “×” is the operator of the Cartesian product) is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as shown in Eq. (14):

$$
P (d, w) = \sum_ {z \in Z} P (z) P (w | z) P (d | z).\tag{14}
$$

In this way, the relationship between two documents can be expressed by P(d|z) and P(z), instead of the original P(d, w). The outputs from the PLSA algorithm are the best estimate P(d|z), P(w|z) and P(z). By following the likelihood principle, the best maximization estimation of the log-likelihood function is determined using Eq. (15).

This algorithm can be implemented using the Expectation– Maximization (EM) algorithm, which alternates the expectation step (E-step), where the later probabilities P(z|d,w) are computed for the latent variable z, based on the current parameter estimation; and a maximization step (M-step) in which parameters P(d|z), P(w|z) and P(z) are updated given the later probabilities in the E-step; the algorithm is repeated until it converges. For the execution of the PLSA algorithm, Hang Chen's implementation [4] from the University of Illinois was used. It requires as input the data from the co-occurrence documentword matrix generated by the IGBHSK algorithm to <sup>fi</sup>nd the P(w|z) and P(d|z) probabilities. This process is done for each topic in the lowest level (leaf node) of the de<sup>fi</sup>ned topic hierarchy.

## 8.3.2. Multivariate analysis

Multivariate analysis is a set of methods that allows simultaneous analysis of two variables observed in any research, allowing the study of multivariate information, in the sense that several variables are measured for each individual or study group. Component analysis is a multivariate technique that allows selecting the information within a set of interest variables in new independent variables. This means that the analysis is centered on reducing the dimensionality of the data space, but is also used to monitor and track the main components obtained, to compare against the hypothesis established in a multivariate analysis study and to identify atypical data in the dataset.

## 10. Conclusions and future work

As a contribution to overcoming dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in the management of unstructured text in data warehouses, a multi-dimensional analysis model that includes textual measures and a topic hierarchy was proposed. The three textual measures incorporated enable percentage probability <sup>fi</sup>gures to be obtained for the respective key aspects — the topics (in relation to dimensions implied in the query), the documents (also to the dimensions) and the words (in relation to each topic). Modi<sup>fi</sup>ed IGBHSK and PLSA, the algorithms used to generate the topic hierarchy and probabilities respectively, can be replaced by other algorithms according to the requirements of the model, since these processes are realized during the loading of the data in the warehouse.

The model was evaluated from two viewpoints — query execution time and user satisfaction. Query execution time shows a promising lineal tendency as the number of documents increases. As regards user satisfaction with the model, high acceptance was recorded relating to ease of use — in the presentation, format, navigation and analysis of the measures; to query time; and also in relation to the overall perception of the model. Further, as demonstrated by the multivariate analysis for ease of use, as the user interacted more with the model, acceptance increased. The multivariate analysis also recon<sup>fi</sup>rmed satisfaction with query time.

As future work, it is hoped to conduct further evaluation of the model using many more documents (millions of records) that would consolidate identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of tendency as regards query execution time when textual measures are used. It would also be of value for the OLAP tool to include a control or library in order to improve textual measure handling, allowing measures to be observed as a list, or customizable (where probability ranking can be shown in typical OLAP charts easily understood by the user). Elsewhere, inclusion of incremental document loading in the ETL process, to update database information, would mean that whenever new documents are added, general architecture processes need to be carried out for only the new documents, thereby signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reducing processing, clustering and loading times.

To conclude, the integration of unstructured text within a data warehouse is complex since data warehouses traditionally have managed numeric measures that permit mathematical operations to be performed on the data. Nevertheless, the quest for such integration remains a vibrant research subject since successful integration of unstructured information would allow end users to gain access to relevant information stored in texts of interest, thereby bringing missing information into the decision making process.
