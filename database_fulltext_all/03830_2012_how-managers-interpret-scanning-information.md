## A B S T R A C T

When scanning, managers acquire and collect information about their environment to keep informed of its evolution, make decisions, and sometimes to anticipate changes. We attempted to understand this process and assess how French managers interpret scanning information to evaluate its importance. Our effort resulted in: (1) sixteen factors used by managers to make sense of the signs and signals they perceived when scanning; (2) a heuristic model of the process; and (3) two instances of the model. Our results suggested that ad hoc training should be provided and IT use should be fully investigated to enhance managers’ scanning skills if organizations wish to develop an ability to detect weak signs/ signals, anticipate plausible change, and increase strategic decision effectiveness.

## 1. Introduction

Strategic scanning involves watching important events and trends in the environment; this knowledge can help top executives in identifying and understanding strategic threats and opportunities, and in making strategic decisions [12].

There are two complementary ways of scanning: Focused Search (when managers are already involved in a decision-making process) and Scanning (when managers have no specific decision yet likely to guide a Focused Search).

Scanning information and evaluating whether it is important may help managers detect signals that indicate unexpected future opportunities, threats, or risks, and thus prepare for their arrival [5].

Many previous behavioural studies have discussed the methods that managers use, their scanning frequency, and how personal characteristics influences information acquisition but they have not investigated how managers interpret and make sense of the signs and signals and how they might interpret the effect of the information.

The aim of our research was to offer insights into the area of scanning. From a theoretical view point it was an attempt to open the ‘‘black box’’ and understand managers’ cognition when they scan information. Our work should thus contribute to increasing strategic scanning projects success [9], decision-making effectiveness [11] and organization performance [7].

## 2. Theoretical background

The environment is sometimes turbulent (to varying degrees) depending on the organisation. Evolution and changes result from events and interdependencies that result from human behaviours, decisions, and actions. Every change or event produces reactions, which produce signals [13], possibly weak ones, and signs [4], some of which can be taken as early warning indicators.

## 2.1. Signals and weak signals

Individuals and organizations voluntarily send out signals to inform stakeholders, influence judgements, anticipations and behaviours, and provoke reaction. On the one hand, some are sent to reduce information asymmetry, motivated by legal requirements, conventions, or deliberate communication strategies. On the other hand, some signals are emitted to take advantage of information asymmetry: they include rumours or even have fraudulent intentions, being intentionally false, deceptive or misleading.

Thus, for individuals perceiving signals, they:

\- can provide information about a situation, its causes and possible development,

\- may be vague and their reliability difficult to assess,

\- may have unknown or obscure motives,

\- may be difficult to assess,

\- may be weak because they convey unusual, unexpected, fragmentary and incomplete information,

\- convey ambiguous information; thus they need to be interpreted,

\- contain anomalies, discordances and singularities that extend outside the normal frames of reference,

\- may have no statistical validity.

Thus, weak signals are imprecise and vague. Noticing and using them for decision-making present difficulties that can be discouraging to managers who prefer strong and reliable signals. But the stronger the signal, the less it is anticipatory and the shorter is the recovery window.

## 2.2. Signs

Of course communication can be involuntary, unconscious and non-verbal. Individuals and organisations are constantly interacting and cannot prevent themselves from producing signs in one form or another.

A sign is an indication, a spontaneous event, an involuntary manifestation of behaviour, decision, action or change. It is composed of a signifier (a perception perceived through at least one of the five senses) and a signified (the meaning of a sensory perception). It can also be extrinsic and one therefore has to interpret sensory stimuli in order to make sense of them. Thus, the way in which individuals interpret signs is an essential step in noticing and interpreting.

A sign can be ambiguous and equivocal. The relationship between the actions that produce the sign, the intentions or decisions from which they result, and their potential implication for the future can be difficult to identify and interpret. The causal chains and potential trajectories can be vague. Therefore, scanning the environment looking for signs of potential evolution and changes present difficulties.

## 2.3. Perceiving and selecting signs and signals

The detection of signs or signals can be partially structured and formalised. A target can be established and key words identified to specify expectations and priorities for scanning and browsing, information sources can be selected according to their perceived relevance and reliability, and selection criteria can be specified in order to evaluate the information’s perceived importance, relevance, reliability and novelty [3,6,14].

These approaches contribute to the construction of organisational filters. Specifically, they help guide information browsing, perception, selection and classification. They are needed to break up a continuous flow of actions and information, and produce a reasonable body of signs/signals that are relevant, processable, and useful for interpretation and decision-making. On the whole, they help evaluate information’s attributes, but not its meaning.

## 2.4. Interpreting and making sense of signs and signals

Whether signs/signals are noticed or not depend as much on contextual factors as on prevailing individual and collective concerns, frames of reference, knowledge structures and personal characteristics. But noticing a sign/signal is not enough to determine the intention and action that produced it, to anticipate its potential implications, and to evaluate how it can be useful in decision-making.

The same sign/signal can be produced by numerous different actions and events that can each be part of a number of plans or trajectories. A priori, the problem lies not so much in the absence of meaning as in the number of plausible meanings. Information ambiguity and equivocality thus raise the question: How can we evaluate the nature of the change that might occur and what sense can be made of it for its use in decision-making?

By the time a person notices a sign/signal, he/she is not sure that any change will materialize. The action or event that produced the sign/signal can help confirm or support previously known trends but they may also define an emerging situation. The changes that managers perceive are often uncertain or dubious in nature. Information uncertainty thus raises the question of how to evaluate risk, likelihood, and probability that any change will materialize.

## 2.5. Research goals

We considered that information depends on its context and may be an artefact, or mental representation, as noted by the individual perceiving the signs/signals. Thus it is subjective.

It is also the product of latent, tacit and often unconscious thought, during which the individual interprets the signals and signs, making sense of them for making decisions.

Our research focused on managers’ thoughts when they were scanning, noting, and making sense of signs/signals (see Fig. 1). It does not focus on the interpretation by the managers. It is concerned with identifying the way that managers associate a sign/ signal with a response that may result in future change. Previous studies have examined the role that information plays in scanning but they have not attempted to examine the underlying cognitions.

Although the concept of cognition always relates to thought, studies have diverse subjects. Specifically, they have examined managers’ cognitive predispositions (e.g. their personal characteristics – cognitive styles, values, attitudes, etc. likely to influence how information is interpreted), cognitive structures (e.g. the contents of the mind in terms of mental representations, mental and cognitive maps, etc.), or cognitive processes (the workings of their mind in making judgments, developing cognitive structures, and making decisions).

By cognition, we mean elementary brain activity likely to be involved in the process of interpreting and making sense of signs/ signals. Evaluation of information reliability, relevance and importance are examples of elementary cognitive activities used to explain how managers select and evaluate scanning information. Our goal was to identify a more complete set of cognitions used to evaluate the character of scanning information.

Thus our research questions were:

\- What elementary cognitions do managers use to evaluate perceived signs/signals and interpret their potential anticipatory character? and

\- What interpretive heuristics can be deduced from them?

## 3. Methodology

Given its focus, the methodology was split into two parts (see Fig. 2).

## 3.1. A qualitative study identifying the elementary cognitions to interpret signs/signals

A focus group approach was used; it consisted of eight French managers with at least eight years experience in the area. The group was assembled for a period of nine months and took part in three focus group meetings (see Fig. 3). The goal was to stimulate knowledge amplification cycles in conformance with the SECI model [10]; this asked the managers to reflect on the thoughts they used to interpret and make sense of anticipatory information. Recurring underlying questions resulted in 16 items.

The second step was to validate the 16 items during individual interviews. 19 face-to-face interviews with French managers were conducted (see Appendix A). Our goal was to assess the items’ intelligibility, relevance, and completeness. Depending on their availability and interest in the research subject, the interviews lasted from thirty minutes to two hours.

This phase confirmed the 16 items generated by the focus group. A quantitative study validated them on a larger scale and determined their role in interpretation heuristics.

## 3.2. A quantitative study to determine an interpretation heuristics

175 questionnaires were administered to managers over two years. 96 were collected face-to-face during the first year, 79 through a Web survey the second year. This represented a good sample of the manager population in terms of its age, gender, professional background, industry, and occupation (see Appendix A).

The 16 items were measured with a Likert scale (between 1 – never and 5 – always).

The first step was to update a general heuristic model stemming from the 16 items generated by the qualitative study. To do this, we proceeded in two steps.

1. We made an exploratory principal component analysis of the 16 items using SPSS 20. Our goal was to identify the main axes around which the items were structured, and from which the managers interpret information. A confirmatory factor analysis was then conducted. In order to get reliable parameters estimates, we relied on a systemic bootstrap procedure (500 random replications). We found that each factor loading was highly significant (t test > 2). Our goal was to confirm that this structure had an adequate data fit, by examining goodness-of-fit indices such as the GFI, AGFI, and RMSEA.

2. Simple, descriptive statistics (means, standard deviation) were calculated for the 16 items structured around their factorial axes. Our goal was to perceive their relative position in the managers’ interpretation process. This led to the construction of a model for understanding how managers interpret and make sense of signs/signals, and how they evaluate the anticipatory character of the information.

The second step was to identify heuristic incidences according to individuals. Thus, we tried to determine if individuals all adopt the same heuristics or if they use different ones. To determine the number of groups of managers exhibiting distinct heuristics, a cluster analysis was conducted using the ‘‘K Means’’ method. To determine and qualify the different heuristics identified, F tests and a discriminant analysis were carried out. These results helped to determine the typology to be validated. ## 4. Results

## 4.1. Items measuring elementary cognitions

Of the 16 items (Table 1) 9 are similar to criteria previously identified in the literature while 7 have not been mentioned in previous studies of strategic scanning.

## 4.2. Our proposed model for interpreting signs/signals

Our exploratory factor analysis confirmed the relevance of the 16 items and revealed a 4-factor structure (with all of the item communalities >0.5, and a cumulative explained variance of 64%). This suggested that information evaluation was based on 4 distinct dimensions (see Table 2).

The CFA thus supported the results with very satisfactory validity indicators (RMSEA, GFI and AGFI).

Factor 1 concerned the evaluation of the information’s significance in terms of the risk of plausible change. Managers felt that the plausibility of the perceived change (reach), its impact on the current balance of their markets (substitution), and the time in which their company could react (reactivity) were important. They also imagined that different plausible change scenarios (variety) mattered. But it appeared that information of a surprising, unexpected, and singular character (surprising) was seldom observed.

Factor 2 concerns the evaluation of the information’s meaning in terms of the nature of plausible change. Some managers very frequently considered the ‘‘intention’’, ‘‘movement’’, ‘‘opportunity’’, and ‘‘risk’’ that information could possibly signify. They also considered what the information was likely to suggest about an actor’s ability to provoke a change.

Factor 3 concerns the evaluation of information’s perceived quality. Some managers considered the ‘‘reliability’’, ‘‘relevance’’, and ‘‘importance’’ of information, and its ‘‘novelty’’.

Factor 4 concerns information’s perceived currency. Some managers considered its ‘‘freshness’’ and to a lesser extent its ‘‘repetition’’. But others were only slightly aware of the perceived currency of information.

It is remarkable that items are taken into account with frequencies ranging from medium to high.

Factors 1 and 2 (e.g. nature and risk of plausible change) show how managers question the meaning of information – the signified – to evaluate the possibility of change (the sign/signal signified). Factors 3 and 4 (e.g. perceived quality and freshness) show how they question information attributes – the signifier – to evaluate its intensity (the sign/signal strength). Numerous authors have written that managers and CEOs filter information according to its quality, reliability, relevance, perceived importance, etc., but their observations are fragmentary. This four-factor structure can be interpreted as an information interpretation and interpreting heuristic model. Its main contribution is to federate and structure the item and factor set. It is modelled in Fig. 5 below, where the size of the characters used for each item relates to the frequency with which individuals consider them (see Table 3).

## 4.3. Are there different heuristics, depending on the individual?

Analysing the items’ standard deviations gives a glimpse of the disparities between managers in terms of how frequently they use these elementary cognitions to interpret information. A cluster analysis and discriminant analysis were conducted in order to ascertain the extent to which individuals from the sample refer to the same heuristic models or different ones. After several cluster analyses with different numbers of groups, a two-distinct group solution was finally identified on the basis of the F-test results and discriminant analysis (see Table 3). The classification index of individuals in their initial in-group following the discriminant analysis is 96.4%, or 169 of the 175 managers in the sample.

Eight of the 16 items discriminate the two groups of individuals (see Table 3, variables in bold). The interpretation of these two groups is mainly based on a study of the 8 discriminant variables. It also takes account of the groups’ means on the other 8 items (see Table 3 and Fig. 5).

With the exception of the item ‘‘surprise’’, group 2’s means are clearly lower than group 1’s for all items (differences between 0.93 and 1.46 points). Thus, the results of the cluster analysis show that information interpretation and interpreting are established differently, depending on the individual, according to the frequency with which all items are used. The groups are therefore differentiated more on a frequency of use continuum than on the nature of the items used themselves (see Fig. 6).

In spite of its discriminant character, the difference between the two groups’ means for the item ‘‘surprise’’ (0.56) is very slight. The individuals rarely consider the information’s singular, surprising or unexpected character (a mean of 2.97 for group 1 and 2.42 for group 2).

Group 1 (112 people) frequently consider the information’s attributes (its quality and currency, factors 3 and 4) as well as its meaning (the nature of plausible change and its perceived risk, factors 1 and 2). The means for the four factors’ items are very high (ranging from 3.46 to 4.56) with the exception of the item ‘‘surprise’’ (2.97). ## 5. Discussion, implications and conclusions

In our research we identified a model of information interpretation that combined two complementary dimensions of information evaluation: one that evaluates its strength, and the other its meaning. Thus Focused Search and Scanning modes of data acquisition and retrieval might require two distinct but complementary evaluations with four different ways evaluating them, as shown in Table 4.

## 5.1. Insights into focused search and scanning heuristics

Focused Search (or seeking) takes part when managers are already involved in a decision-making process, and look for information to better understand the decision context, choices and implications. First a situation that must be decided on is identified, questions are raised and asked, and then a Focused Search is engaged. The objective is to find and gather reliable and nonambiguous information to give as precise and clear answers as possible to the questions managers wonder. Thus, information evaluation is both analytic and deductive:

\- Information attributes are evaluated in an analytical way to question their reliability, relevance and importance according to the decision and questions the search is focused on. The more certain and the stronger the sign/signal, the more it catches attention and seems worthy of interest.

\- The meaning of information is evaluated in a deductive way to question its significance and the insights it provides into the nature and risk of (plausible) change. The less ambiguous the sign/signal and the more it seems useful to answer specific questions and take decision, the more it catches attention and seems worthy of interest.

On the other hand, Scanning (or general browsing of data) is a sort of pre-attentive monitoring or exploration without any particular decision to take or question being identified to guide the research. The objective is to be vigilant to discrepant signs/ signals that might manifest in the peripheral vision and could eventually help identify, discover or anticipate plausible changes in the environment.

Thus, information evaluation is both heuristic and inductive:

\- Information attributes are evaluated in a heuristic way to question the sign/signal freshness and novelty. At the time when the sign/signal is perceived, whether it is certain and reliable seems less important than the novel and fresh pieces of information it carries, compared to the perceiver’s actual state of knowledge and beliefs.

\- The meaning of information is also evaluated in an inductive way to question whether the sign/signal could eventually signify (early) warnings about plausible and unexpected changes.

## 5.2. Managerial implications

Previous research showed that managers use a Focused Search more often than they use a Scanning mode. We also found this to be true. Thus developing managers’ Scanning ability could help improve the organisation’s peripheral vision, notice signs/signals, identify early warnings, anticipate plausible changes, and react and adapt rapidly. Therefore, managers with a personal ability for Scanning should be identified and trained when organizations want to identify discrepancies rapidly and anticipate possible discontinuities and breakdown, and improve decision-making effectiveness and organisational performance.

The development of new IT tools also contributes to reinforce scanning practices. For instance, thesaurus, intelligent agents, datamining, business intelligence and lexical analysis contribute to facilitate and increase the performance of digital information retrieval and acquisition.

On the one hand, they help evaluate both the reliability and relevance of information, and its freshness and novelty. They provide robust solutions to support and reinforce analytic and heuristic evaluation. Generally speaking, they help evaluate information uncertainty. But they cannot yet evaluate signs/signals importance, which is more subjective and interpretative an evaluation.

On the other hand, the support provided by IT in considering the meaning of information to evaluate the plausibility of change is more questionable. Some IT can help to contextualise, complement, aggregate, segment and even model digital information to create and suggest meaning. Therefore, they contribute to support deductive evaluation. Yet, the representations they suggest still need to be interpreted and made sense of. Their utility seems even more questionable concerning inductive evaluation, when signs signals are rare, ambiguous, unclear and equivocal, and when the warnings about plausible unexpected changes depend more on the ‘‘signified’’ managers associate to them, than on its intrinsic significance. Whether IT are yet efficient for inductive evaluation and Scanning is debatable.

Finally, IT may be helpful for Focused Search and Scanning only if, at a certain point in the process, information is digital. In every other situation, when signs/signals are cognitive perceptions rather than digital information, IT does not help.

## 5.3. Limitations and avenues for future research

Like any study based on surveys, the declarative data collection method has bias and limitations. By presenting respondents with 16 items, we are asking them to think about how they interpret and make sense of scanning information, when in all likelihood it is a question of cognitions that are latent, tacit and somehow unconscious. We also ask them to evaluate the items’ frequency of use. But we know neither how they evaluate this frequency, nor which specific contexts, situations and experiences come to mind in the moment when they are responding to the survey.

One of the four factors (i.e. information currency) is composed of only two items, and this might be insufficient for characterising it. Other items close in meaning could conceivably be associated with it. Such items were identified neither in the literature, nor during the qualitative phase. They could be the subjects of future research.

The item ‘‘surprise’’ is used by all respondents with a low frequency. This item may not have been well understood by respondents. It might be useful to reformulate it and make it clearer.

As mentioned in the literature background (see Fig. 1), many variables are likely to influence how managers interpret and make sense of scanning information that were studied in this research such as: the managers’ personal attributes [1,8] (e.g. his/her age, gender, culture, education, cognitive style, experience, position and level of responsibility), the organisational variables (e.g. the type of company, the industry, the level of uncertainty, turbulence and complexity [2]), the nature of the information (e.g. whether it is formal or informal, the type of information source, the richness of the media), and the action and decision-making concerns managers are involved in.

## 5.4. Conclusion

We studied the way that an individual scans and interprets possible important signs/signals from the environment. The 16 elementary items, the heuristic model, and the distinction between Focus Search and Scanning were all found to be important in the process. This suggests that IT support cannot substitute for human cognitive efforts of interpretation and evaluation of signs/signals but should be designed to help reduce managers’ overload and support the extraction, from the everyday information flow, of signs/signals with strong added value, but in reasonable amounts to be exploited, useful, and valuable for managerial decision-making.
