# CAMPUS EMERGENCY NOTIFICATION SYSTEMS: AN EXAMINATION OF FACTORS AFFECTING COMPLIANCE WITH ALERTS<sup>1</sup>

Wencui Han

Department of Business Administration, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, 350 Wohlers Hall, 1206 South Sixth Street, Champaign, IL 61820 U.S.A. {wencuihan@gmail.com}

Serkan Ada Beysehir A. A. Faculty of Business, Selçuk University, 42700 Konya, TURKEY {serkanada@selcuk.edu.tr}

Raj Sharman and H. Raghav Rao

Management Science and Systems Department, University at Buffalo, 325 Jacobs Management Center, Buffalo, NY 14260-4000 U.S.A. {rsharman@buffalo.edu} {mgmtrao@buffalo.edu}

The increasing number of campus-related emergency incidents, in combination with the requirements imposed by the Clery Act, have prompted college campuses to develop emergency notification systems to inform community members of extreme events that may affect them. Merely deploying emergency notification systems on college campuses, however, does not guarantee that these systems will be effective; student compliance plays a very important role in establishing such effectiveness. Immediate compliance with alerts, as opposed to delayed compliance or noncompliance, is a key factor in improving student safety on campuses. This paper investigates the critical antecedents that motivate students to comply immediately with messages from campus emergency notification systems. Drawing on Etzioni’s compliance theory, a model is developed. Using a scenario-based survey method, the model is tested in five types of events—snowstorm, active shooter, building fire, health-related, and robbery—and with more than 800 college students from the Northern region of the United States. The results from this study suggest that subjective norm and information quality trust are, in general, the most important factors that promote immediate compliance. This research contributes to the literature on compliance, emergency notification systems, and emergency response policies.

Keywords: Compliance, campus alerts, emergency notification systems, information quality trust, scenario based survey

## Introduction

In recent years, we have witnessed a growing number of college campus-related emergency incidents. Examples include criminal incidents (e.g., shootings and robberies), natural disasters (e.g., fire and snowstorms), and healthrelated incidents (e.g., disease outbreaks) (Mitroff et al. 2006). (See Appendix A for some examples of recent campus-related emergency incidents.) Between 2005 and 2009, approximately 30 campus-related shooting incidents occurred worldwide, and nearly 135 people were killed in these incidents (Infoplease 2012). Research conducted by the U.S. Federal Bureau of Investigation indicates that between 2005 and 2008, some 220 counts of murder or negligent manslaughter, more than 14,000 sex offenses, 19,900 robberies, and 4,045 arson incidents were reported to the Department of Education in compliance with the Clery Act (Drysdale et al. 2010). In addition, the U.S. Department of Education reports that between 2009 and 2011, more than 6,700 fire incidents occurred in higher education institutes in the United States.<sup>2</sup>

Many U.S. college campuses have implemented emergency notification systems (ENS) (e.g., e-mail, text messages, Short Message Service [SMS], voice messages, and websites) to promote campus safety in response to these incidents, to adhere to the mandates established as part of the Clery Act,<sup>3</sup> and to respond to both the pressure from the government and their own deep desire to make campuses safe places for students. Emergency notification and alert systems, in their current form, are band-limited (128 characters) information systems that allow short text messages to be disseminated to a variety of devices, such as computers and mobile phones, very quickly, thereby facilitating decision making in timesensitive situations. (Some sample messages from these systems are provided in Appendix E.) However, less attention has been paid to how effective these systems have proven to be in generating proper actions upon receipt of the notifications (alerts). Merely deploying an emergency notification system on a college campus does not guarantee that it will be effective. Alerts may have to be immediately complied with by the recipient to minimize loss of health or property—or one’s life. Oftentimes the act of verification of the alert may waste precious time; thus it is important to understand the various factors that influence immediate compliance upon receiving the alert. The study presented here addresses the following research questions: What are the important factors influencing students’ ENS-message compliance intentions? How does the level of trust in the quality of information affect compliance intention when the information is received? Does the importance of these factors change in different types of incidents? What we can do to improve immediate compliance?

This research makes several important contributions to the field. First, it introduces the notion of compliance to the emergency context. Most compliance research in the management information systems (MIS) field focuses on individuallevel information technology (IT) or IT security policy compliance, as influenced by organizational communications, such as fear appeals (Johnston and Warkentin 2010; Johnston et al. 2015). This paper addresses the individual response to organizational messages (persuasive communications). Further, it explores the critical factors that influence compliance under different scenarios. It contributes to theory by adapting Etzioni’s compliance theory (1961a, 1961b, 1961c) to an emergency notification system context and by incorporating the concept of information quality trust in three dimensions (information relevance trust, information actionability trust, and information criticality trust). Trust in notifications through electronic channels—specifically, a band-limited (128-character) context—under conditions of time pressure has not been studied in the prior literature. Finally, this paper contributes to practice by suggesting ways that campus administrators might improve the extent of immediate compliance with campus emergency notifications. (Note: Of course, such emergency notifications may also include messages that provide information and do not require compliance from the students. Our paper, however, is devoted to messages that ask students to take action.)

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we briefly review the relevant literature. We then present the research model. In the subsequent section, we detail the instrument development. We next test the research model and describe the data analysis and the results. Finally, we discuss the findings, their implications, and future research directions.

## Related Work

Prior research in the field of disaster and emergency response has focused on several topics: emergency response systems design and system effectiveness evaluation (Chen et al. 2007 2012; Hale 1997), the adoption of communication channels for emergency response (Ada et al. 2010; Sullivan et al. 2010), resource allocation (Chen and Sharma 2012; Chiu and Lin 2010), response management and coordination (Kim et al. 2007; Maldonado and Maitland 2010; Rowan 1994; Waikar and Nichols 1997), and human–computer interaction (Carver and Turoff 2007). Emergency management, however, remains an understudied area (Janssen et al. 2010), and the academic literature dealing with campus emergency notification systems is sparse.

Some researchers have studied the design of emergency notification systems in the context of campus-related emergencies. For example, Sharpe (2009) highlights the success of the Illini Alert System, focusing on ease of use as an important element in system design. Leonidis et al. (2009) present a semantics-based, context-aware notification system that provides personalized alerts to graduate students. Gulam and Murray (2009) show that it is the effective usage of textbased emergency notification systems, rather than merely the deployment of such systems, that makes the university community safer. Lee et al. (2013) investigate the factors that impact students’ adoption of Short Message Service (SMS) for emergency notifications. Their study shows that social psychological factors—such as attitude toward, subjective norm associated with, and perceived intrusiveness of SMS notifications—affect students’ adoption of SMS emergency notifications.

The issue of compliance has been studied in the information systems (IS) domain in the area of IS security policy (Bulgurcu et al. 2010; Johnston and Warkentin 2010; Kwon and Johnson 2014; Myyry et al. 2009; Puhakainen and Siponen 2010; Spears and Barki 2010). There is now a growing body of literature specific to this area that addresses these issues from several perspectives. Some researchers have investigated the factors that deter noncompliance behaviors (Boss et al. 2015; Li et al. 2010; Willison and Warkentin 2013), whereas others have focused on the factors that motivate compliance intentions by employees in organizations (Herath and Rao 2009a, 2009b; Johnston and Warkentin 2010; Kajava and Siponen 1997; Warkentin et al. 2011; Willison and Warkentin 2013).

Although prior information security research has provided insight into compliance behavior, the context of the existing body of research has not addressed campus-related emergency incidents, nor does it account for the intention to comply with emergency notifications in time-sensitive situations. Employees are governed by organizational rules and regulations more strictly than are college students; there is always an awareness of the repercussions of noncompliance and the possibility of punitive action for those who do not comply with emergency notifications. Campus administrators (including campus police) and faculty members do exhibit means of control through their day-to-day roles in classrooms and other university spaces as authorities who are attempting to accomplish the mission of the university. For most students, there may be no perceived separation between the levels of authority that university officials exhibit in other situations versus during extreme events. However, university rules cannot always be strictly enforced, and those who project a sense of power and attempt to enforce compliance normally do not have the explicit authority over students to force those students to comply with messages.

Prior research in the area of IT governance has identified two basic governance structures: centralized governance and decentralized governance. A strict centralized governance design places all decision-making authority within a central IS organizational body, whereas a strict decentralized governance design places all decision-making authority within the confines of the individual business units or processes (Brown and Grant 2005; Brown 1997). Borrowing from this stream of literature, we classify the university as an organization with a decentralized governance structure in which individuals have more discretion in their compliance decisions.

Emergencies are unpredictable interruptions that threaten physical security, safety, and well-being (Zdziarski et al. 2007), unlike typical cyber-security-related breaches. In turn, human response to warnings comprises a complex social process (Tierney 2000). Understanding what motivates students to subscribe to and then comply with emergency notifications would, therefore, make a contribution to student safety.

## Conceptual Model Development

## Background

Etzioni (1961a, p. xv) defined compliance as “a relationship consisting of the power employed by superiors to control subordinates and the orientation of the subordinates to this power.”

Organizations differ in terms of the means employed to make subjects comply. For example, they may use coercive power, remunerative power, or normative power (Parsons 1957).<sup>4</sup> Coercive power is the “application, or the threat of appli cation, of physical sanctions such as the infliction of pain, deformity, or death; generation of frustration through restriction of movement; or controlling through force, the satisfaction of needs such as those for food, sex, comfort, and the like” (Etzioni 1961a, p. 5). Remunerative power is “based on control over material resources and rewards through the allocation of salaries and wages, commissions and contributions, fringe benefits, services and commodities” (Etzioni 1961a, p. 5). Normative power “rests on the allocation and manipulation of symbolic rewards and deprivations through employment of leaders, manipulations of mass media, allocation of esteem and prestige symbols, administration of ritual, and influence over the distribution of acceptance and positive response” (Etzioni 1961a, p. 5).

Based on the major types of power an organization uses to control its employees, it can generally be classified as either a coercive organization, a utilitarian organization, or a normative organization. Typical coercive organizations include concentration camps, prisons, and custodial mental hospitals.

Examples of utilitarian organizations include factories, mines, banks, and insurance companies, which generate pressures to comply using remunerative mechanisms. Organizations such as hospitals, universities, and religious organizations are normative organizations that use organizational norms to achieve organization goals and mission. Very few organizations are completely coercive, utilitarian, or normative. Instead, most organizations represent a blend of these three ingredients. When an organization is more normative, we tend to classify it as being normative but recognize that it is not entirely devoid of governance rules and regulations. Compliance is also obtained from employees as a consequence of coercive pressures, but this force is much weaker than normative pressures.

Normative power involves vertical relationships between actors who have different ranks (Parsons 1951). In the model developed in this paper, pressures stemming from administrators and faculty members and transmitted to students can be perceived as an application of vertical normative power. Horizontal relations conceptualized as “social power” are more common among actors who have perceived equal rank; for example, students may influence their peers through horizontal normative power. Schools are organizations whose primary goal is transmission of culture; consequently, normative power is most appropriate for leadership in educational institutions. For normative power to be effectively utilized, it must evolve through collaboration among all school stakeholders (Etzioni 1961b).

Organizational culture, in some cases, tends to introduce normative power. Students in universities, while not being employees, are attached to those organizations and feel pressure to comply with their instructions during extreme events, just as employees do. University personnel such as faculty members and administrators (including campus police), despite not possessing the explicit authority to force students to comply immediately with messages, are perceived as authority figures and are often seen as having control during campus-related extreme events due to their day-to-day roles in classrooms and other university settings. In turn, students may experience vertical pressure to comply with university officials’ orders. They may also feel pressure from friends, whom they perceive as equal in rank, as an outgrowth of social power.

Compliance differs from many other behaviors in the sense that it is based on more than just one individual’s risk–benefit assessment. That is, it also involves the relationship between the commander and the follower, as well as between the organization and the members. In the organizational environment, organizational power is an important factor in predicting compliance. Etzioni’s (1961c) theory examines the forces that influence people’s compliance behavior. For example, the theory addresses not only the normative forces at work, but also the influence of coercive power and remunerative power, which can explain the forming of intention. In this paper, we use Etzioni’s theory as a lens through which to view campus security information systems; we adapt the theory to the emergency notification system context, here focusing on the normative organization. In a normative organization, coercive power and remunerative power are not the dominant types of power; that is, noncompliance with emergency notifications cannot be punished through the application of physical or financial sanctions. In an extreme event situation such as a campus emergency, threats to a student come from the incident itself, which may cause loss of life or property; threats do not arise because the organization imposes sanctions. The safety threat derives from the potential harm to the student’s safety or health, and the financial threat is factored in as loss of property. Schools can convey the potential safety and financial threats posed by these incidents through notifications.

The dependent variable in this study is ENS-message compliance intention. It is difficult to measure actual behavior, as much of the attention during extreme events focuses on safety, and there is no mechanism for measuring actual compliance. Further, extreme events are low-probability events that occur without prior warning for the most part; thus, preplanning to collect compliance behavior data is very difficult. In addition, questions about whether the notification is a “false alarm” may hamper the ability to measure compliance. However, given that behavioral intentions correlate significantly with behavior (Ajzen 1985, 1991), several studies in the IS security field (Bulgurcu et al. 2010; Herath and Rao 2009a, 2009b; Thompson et al. 2006) have adopted behavioral intention as a predictor of, or surrogate for, actual behavior. In the emergency incident notification compliance context, what matters is whether compliance is immediate versus whether it occurs after verification (i.e., delayed compliance). Intrinsically, verification implies that the recipient may not trust the message completely and seeks to ascertain the truth or correctness by examination, research, or comparison.<sup>5</sup> This process of verification leads to delays and loss of precious moments in responding to the warning. Typically, true noncompliance is rare.

To summarize, drawing from Etzioni’s compliance theory, coercive power can be mapped as a perceived safety threat (in a normative organization such as a university, physical sanctions are not easily attainable, but it is possible to influence people through fear of threats to their safety that may result in pain and death). Remunerative power can be mapped as perceived financial threat (a university cannot really mandate penalties for noncompliance with emergency alerts, but it can influence people by underscoring and disseminating the potential threats of financial loss). Normative power can be mapped as a subjective norm. We posit that a student’s ENSmessage compliance intention is associated with the subjective norm, the perceived safety threat, and the perceived financial threat. In this situation, the question is not one of compliance with an alert versus ignoring the alert; rather, the focus is on immediate compliance versus verifying first and then complying after this intermediate step. It should be noted that we study compliance not in a regular operational context, but rather in an emergency context. As such, we argue that the factors influencing compliance intention and their importance are different in this context than in the regular, day-to-day context.

## Hypotheses Development

ENS-message compliance intention, the dependent variable of this study, is categorical in nature. Specifically, the two categories of compliance intention are (1) intention to comply immediately and (2) intention to verify first and then comply. (The compliance intention variable was initially designed with three categories, where the third category was intention to ignore. Given that less than 1 percent of students chose to “ignore the message,” we found complete noncompliance to be minimal in our study.) Intention to comply immediately is defined as the student’s intent to follow the direction suggested in the emergency notification immediately upon receiving the message. Intention to verify first and then comply is defined as the student’s intent to first contact other people or sources to verify the information in the notification, and only then comply with the message.

Subjective norm refers to the perceived social and peer pressures to perform or not perform certain behavior. As mentioned earlier, normative power is one of the three main forces that influence people’s compliance behavior in organizations. Normative power refers to the concept that the behaviors and beliefs of others can influence one’s own behavior. The notion that a person’s intention to perform a behavior is influenced by the expectations of others has been widely adopted in the literature dealing with IT-related behaviors. A subjective norm is based on normative beliefs (Ajzen 1985). Normative beliefs refer to the perceived behavioral expectations of important referent individuals or groups such as a person’s spouse, family, friends, and (depending on the population and behavior studied) teachers, doctors, supervisors, or coworkers. These normative beliefs, in combination with the person’s motivation to comply with the different referents, determine the prevailing subjective norm (Ajzen 1985). Adopting the same concept from the theory of reasoned action (TRA; Ajzen 1985), subjective norm is formulated as normative belief times motivation to comply with the referent. In the campus emergency notification response context, students’ intention to comply (or to verify and then comply) with emergency notifications may be influenced by their perceptions of the expectations from their parents, teachers, classmates, and other people important to them. This norm can be formed through training, drills, and other educational activities to teach students the expected behavior in emergencies. News about these types of incidents on other campuses will normally generate discussions on social network sites, among students, and in students’ family settings. These discussions help form the norms of expected behavior as well. If students believe that immediate compliance with notifications is the expected behavior, then they will be more likely to demonstrate such compliance. Therefore, we propose the following:

## Hypothesis 1: Subjective norm positively affects the intention to comply immediately.

Threat has been described in the literature as a negative psychological emotion that results from the belief that negative consequences are possible, probable, or inevitable. In the context of emergencies (Lazarus and Folkman 1984), perceived safety threats refer to the perception of the probability and the severity of campus emergencies’ impacts on one’s physical safety and health. Safety risk perception has been identified as one of the key factors determining a person’s behavior in responding to emergencies (Dovidio et al. 1991; Wu 2009). Campus emergencies have the potential to physically hurt someone or even cause loss of life. Higher levels of safety threats may, therefore, lead students to comply immediately with campus emergency notifications. In a normative organization such as a university, physical sanctions are not easily attainable, but it is possible to influence people through fear of threats to safety that may result in pain and death. Hence, we propose the following:

## Hypothesis 2: Perceived safety threat positively affects the intention to comply immediately.

Perceived financial threat refers to the perception of potential financial loss due to security-related incidents. Remunerative power influences people in the form of financial loss or gain in concordance with the agency perspective. The idea of rational action has generally been taken to imply a conscious social actor engaging in deliberate calculative strategies. In turn, the rationalist perspective argues that economic incentives influence human behavior (Scott 2000). An organization may levy penalties or dangle incentives that can foster compliance among its employees. For example, prior researchers have shown that financial sanctions, or perceived financial losses related to noncompliance, may motivate compliance intentions (Bulgurcu et al. 2010; Herath and Rao 2009a). As mentioned earlier, a university cannot really mandate penalties for noncompliance with emergency alerts, but it can influence people by underscoring and disseminating the potential implications of threats of financial loss. Students’ personal belongings might be damaged in a campus emergency incident, or they might get sick or hurt and need to pay for health care. Students may have different levels of financial threat perceptions, however. For example, those students who are paying for their own tuition might be more concerned about financial loss. Likewise, international students typically spend more money on tuition and traveling, so they might also be more concerned about financial loss. In incidents such as snowstorms, the financial threat might be less direct—for example, medical treatment for a physical injury caused by the snowstorm or repair of a car due to the storm. Although the student’s attitude toward the financial threat might differ in each incident, it becomes part of the calculation as to whether immediate compliance is needed. We therefore propose that students’ compliance behavior will be shaped by the rewards and punishments that are encountered:

## Hypothesis 3: Perceived financial threat positively affects the intention to comply immediately.

In a variety of online contexts, researchers have explicated the relationship between trust and behavior. For example, Pavlou and Gefen (2004) argue that information trust facilitates online exchange relationships. Further, consumer trust continues to be a key issue advancing the proliferation of Internet shopping (Gefen and Straub 2003). In fact, various studies have suggested that trust has a positive influence on purchase intentions and actual buying decisions (Lim et al. 2006). We suggest that it is, therefore, rational that an individual’s trust in alerts during emergencies—situations that are fraught with uncertainty and risk—would influence the alert receiver to act in accordance with the message. The prior literature has not explicitly incorporated information quality trust as a part of Etzioni’s compliance theory. However, in his paper about transparency and regulation, Etzioni (2010, p. 401) discusses the importance of informational quality trust when people have to rely on experts or leaders for information:

The public does not have the cognitive capacities to determine which intermediary provides information that is better processed than another. Reference here is not to deliberate manipulation, but to differences in the quality of the information processing due to differences in access to primary information, resources available to the analysts, their skills and training, and the assumptions they make.

In a campus emergency context, students need to make immediate decisions based on the instructions provided, so their trust in the quality of the information is a critical factor. Trust is a complex subject relating to belief in the benevolence, integrity, competence, reliability, and other qualities of the trusted person or service (McKnight et al. 2002). The definition of informational trust depends on the research context. For example, prior research regarding online agency recommendations and consumer channel choices refers to informational trust as a person’s beliefs about the reliability, credibility, and accuracy of information (Choudhury and Karahanna 2008; Dashti et al. 2010). In our context, trust toward the notification messages focuses on the quality of the information. For students to take immediate action as instructed, they need to believe that the messages are critical—that is, that the messages correctly convey the urgency, severity, and timing of the incident. They also need to trust that the instructions provided in the messages are actionable. Many notifications, such as state- and city-wide emergency alerts, are sent to a large region. In such cases, people do not always follow the instructions in these alerts immediately, because they believe that the instructions are not directly relevant to them. For students to pay attention to campus alerts, it is important for them to believe that the notifications are relevant.

These properties of information are important aspects of information quality as it relates to fostering trust. Indeed, some of them have been studied in other contexts (DeLone 2003; Lee et al. 2002). Actionability and criticality are especially important elements of information quality for emergency notifications. We define information quality trust as students’ beliefs about the relevance, criticality, and actionability of emergency notification messages. Information relevance trust in our context relates to how germane the information is, which in turn influences whether it is regarded with confidence by the message recipient. Trust in information relevance also relates to the importance and congruity of the information and the believability or confidence it fosters. Information actionability trust in our context relates to the confidence in the information to make decisions or act on the basis of the information. Information criticality trust in our context relates to how timely, urgent, and crucial the information is to make it believable. These definitions are based on thesaurus.com and adaptations of the work by Parker et al. (2006) and Singh et al. (2009).

Our study focuses on immediate compliance. The survey results suggest that students will ultimately comply with the instructions in the message, but the factor that differentiates the “immediate compliance” response from the “verify first” response is trust. Emergency notification systems are human– machine participatory systems. The compliance with such messages differs from the compliance observed in normal organizational contexts. Therefore, in addition to the previously mentioned three hypotheses, we argue that immediate compliance with notifications, without verifying the information via alternative sources, will depend on the trust placed in the information. Put simply, lack of trust leads to delayed compliance. Delay occurs when students spend time verifying the details using other sources at their disposal prior to compliance. Hence, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 4: Information quality trust positively affects the intention to comply immediately.

Prior research in various fields has pointed out that compliance might be affected by past experience (Bonsall and Parry 1991; Cummings et al. 1979; Maue et al. 2004). Past experiences can influence students’ compliance intention in various ways. For example, based on their own unique past experiences, students might make different assessments of the severity of an incident as well as of the relevance of notifications. Hence, we included past experience as a control variable in the model.

## Research Method

In this study we used mixed methods to investigate the factors motivating immediate compliance with campus alerts. First we conducted a survey and performed quantitative analysis to test our research model. Following the quantitative study, we conducted a focus group to provide deeper understanding of the research question.

The research process is discussed in detail in the following sections.

## Instrument Development

Having proposed the previously described model (see Figure 1), we then developed and validated an instrument to measure compliance intention (Kim et al. 2007; Moore and Benbasat 1991; Smith et al. 1996; Straub 1989). We pretested this instrument, with this effort then being followed by a pilot test and technical validation of reliability and construct validity. Finally, a full-scale survey was administered through Qualtrics in several universities in the northern United States.

## Item Creation

To improve the validity and reliability of the survey, we employed several processes. First, we adapted questions from prior validated research as much as possible. For items that were not available as part of prior research, we created items to measure the constructs in our study. Subjective norm was modeled as a formative construct, perceived safety threat and perceived financial threat were modeled as reflective constructs, and information quality trust was modeled as both a first-order formative construct and a second-order formative construct.

Second, we interviewed four experts working in the field (the chief of the university police, the vice president of university communications, the emergency planning manager of the university, and the chief executive officer [CEO] of a company that offers emergency notification solutions). We had three rounds of meetings with them. The interviewees gave us suggestions on the research as well as on the questionnaire.

Third, we asked several students for their opinions on the instrument, such as who might influence their behavior intention (referent groups for subjective norm) (Mathieson 1991). Fourth, we consulted with professors from the university who typically deal with survey instrument design to ensure the way the questions were phrased was appropriate for eliciting proper responses for the factors being measured. Finally, we created the instrument based on the literature review as well as on the feedback from the field experts, students, and instrumentation experts. Table 1 presents the types and the sources of the measurements.

## Scenario Design

To test our hypotheses, we used scenario-based surveys to measure the variables. Five scenarios were selected for the study (the selection process is detailed in the next paragraph). Each participant was randomly presented with one of five scenarios. After the participants read the scenarios, they indicated their perceptions on both the independent and dependent variables based on the scenarios. Scenario-based methods are commonly used in security and business ethics research (Banerjee et al. 1998; Hovav and D’Arcy 2012; Siponen and Vance 2010), because it is difficult to assess actual deviant behavior in the workplace.

With each scenario, the subjects were asked to indicate their compliance intention upon receiving notifications if the scenario happened to them: “If you were Jeff, what would you do?” The answers were captured as a categorical variable: (1) intention to comply immediately, (2) intention to verify first and then comply, or (3) intention to ignore (the third category was eliminated in the final analysis, because only a very small percentage of students chose this category).

## Pretests and Pilot Test

After the development of the first version of the instrument, four rounds of pretests were executed, with 15 students participating in each round; these students were drawn from the target sampling frame. Comments about the survey regarding its clarity, length, structure, and other aspects were collected during each round. Based on the feedback from the pretests, we modified and refined the survey questions by adding, rephrasing, or eliminating questions.

A pilot test was then conducted using the revised version of the instrument. In total, 200 students participated in the pilot survey, and the preliminary analyses from the measurement model led to further refinement of the survey instrument. Final data collection was conducted after the instrument had been validated. Appendix B presents the constructs and measurement items used to measure them.

## Main Field Test

We distributed the survey online through a survey-collecting service company. The participants were university students from the northern region of the United States. We used a filter question to eliminate any student who was not aware of any types of campus alert systems. To make sure the student could relate to the scenario, we also included a reality check question, “Can you imagine a similar scenario happening at your university campus?” If the student chose no, his or her survey was excluded. We ultimately received a total of 821 completed and usable surveys (164 cases for the snowstorm, active shooter, building-related, and health-related scenarios; 165 cases for the robbery scenario).

Based on the work of Peduzzi et al. (1996) and Long (1997), the following guideline can be used to calculate the minimum number of cases that should be included in studies using logistic regression: Let p be the smallest of the proportions of 0 or 1 cases in the population and k be the number of covariates (the number of independent variables); then the minimum number of cases to include is N = 10k/p. In our study, we had five independent variables, and the lowest p was for the building-related incident scenario. Thus p equaled 0.36, which suggested that we needed a sample size of at least 139. All other cases required a smaller minimal sample size. Hence we had enough samples to conduct the logistic regression analysis.

## Instrument Validation

To test the psychometric properties of the measurement scales, we used the SmartPLS 2.0 software package (Ringle et al. 2005). SmartPLS is a component-based path modeling software based on the partial least squares (PLS) method. PLS-based applications readily handle both reflective and formative constructs. Thus we felt this software package was appropriate, as our model included both reflective and formative constructs. We implemented measures consistent with the nature of the constructs (Boudreau et al. 2001; Gefen et al. 2000).

## Validation of Reflective Constructs

We modeled information quality trust as a formative firstorder construct and a formative second-order construct; perceived safety threat and perceived financial threat were modeled as reflective first-order constructs. Subjective norm was calculated by multiplying the aggregation of motivation to comply with reference i by the normative belief concerning referent i (3 motivation to comply with reference i \* normative belief concerning referent i). Many studies in different IS-related contexts have indicated that trust has many dimensions and have modeled trust as a second-order formative construct (Hsu et al. 2007; Vance et al. 2008). We likewise felt that trust regarding the information quality of emergency notifications is better defined as a formative construct.

We first analyzed the measurement properties of the reflective constructs—that is, perceived safety threat and perceived financial threat—following standard procedures. We also used chi-square tests to evaluate the homogeneity of the samples for the five scenarios. The chi-square tests indicated that there were no significant differences in gender ratios (chisquare test statistic = 0.24; p = 0.99; result was not significant at the 0.10 level) or academic status ratios (chi-square test statistic = 5.04; p = 0.28; result was not significant at the 0.10 level).

We used the pooled data from the five scenarios to test the instrument. The individual item loading and average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct were examined to ensure that reliability and convergent validity were established for the individual items. As shown in Table 5, all of the item loadings were greater than 0.60 (Backhaus et al. 2003; Hair et al. 1995). It has been recommended that factors should have a Cronbach’s alpha value that is greater than 0.7 in established studies and greater than 0.6 in exploratory studies (Fornell and Larcker 1981; Kahai and Cooper 2003). Table 6 shows that the Cronbach’s alpha values were at an acceptable level in our study, and the AVE values were higher than the recommended value of 0.50. Therefore, we conclude that the convergent validity requirement was satisfied.

We also established discriminant validity. We compared the square root of the AVE values for each construct to the other correlations in the correlation matrix. As shown in Table 6, all the diagonal elements of the correlation matrix were greater than the off-diagonal construct correlations. In addition, we performed an exploratory factor analysis and reviewed the factor analysis table (Table 5). Following the recommendations of Gefen and Straub (2005), discriminant validity was established, since all the items loaded at least 0.10 less in other research constructs.

## Validation of Formative Constructs

Information quality trust was modeled as first-order and second-order formative constructs. Formative constructs need to be validated in a manner different from that for reflective constructs (Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001; Petter et al. 2007). Specifically, internal consistency (reliability testing) of indicators is not relevant for formative constructs because the indicators are not reflections of the underlying latent variable. Formative construct indicators are not necessarily correlated; hence, convergent validity also was not relevant in our study. However, discriminate validity can be tested for formative constructs (MacKenzie et al. 2005). To do so, we first examined the significance levels of the item weightings of the formative constructs (Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001). All the weightings were significant at the 0.05 level. We then used variance inflation factor (VIF) statistics to determine whether there was multicolinearity among the items. The results showed that the VIF value was less than three, as recommended (Diamantopoulos and Siguaw 2006).

## Model Testing Results

The results for the overall population presented in Table 8 show that all five models provided a better fit for the data than did the base model (demonstrated by the significant chisquare test for the difference between the –2LL ratios; the base model is the model that includes only the intercept). The results are summarized in Table 9.

The Wald statistic $\mathrm { ( \beta / S E _ { \beta } ) }$ tests the unique contribution of each predictor, holding constant the other predictors. Each significant predictor in our models met the conventional .05 standard for statistical significance. The odds ratio indicates the change in the odds due to a one-unit change in the predictor variable (for example, in an active shooter incident, when perceived safety threat increases 1 unit, the ratio between “comply immediately” and “verify first and then comply” increases 0.83 unit); the 95 percent confidence interval for this ratio is also reported. In our analysis, intention to comply immediately was coded as 1, and intention to verify first and then comply was coded as 0. If both limits of the 95 percent confidence interval for the odds ratio are greater than 1, then we might conclude that a one-unit increase in the predictor variable will lead to an increase in the likelihood of complying immediately. If both limits of the interval are less than 1, then a one-unit increase in the predictor variable will probably lead to an increase in the likelihood of verifying first and then complying.

The response ratio indicates the number of students who choose to comply immediately versus the number of students who choose to verify first and then comply. The results indicate that more students would verify the information presented in ENS messages prior to complying with those messages in the case of snowstorm and health-related incidents. The –2 log-likelihood ratio (–2LL) tests whether the predictors of the model make a difference in predicting the dependent variable. Larger values of the –2LL ratio indicate a poorly fitting model. The SPSS logistic regression procedure computes two –2LL ratios: one for the base model with no predictors in the model and one for the model with predictors. Thus we would expect the –2LL ratio to become smaller as we add the predictors in the model. For this purpose, a model chi-square statistic, which is the difference between the –2LL ratios of the two logistic models, was also reported. A statistically significant model chi-square statistic denotes that the predictors make a significant difference as compared to the base model. The overall correct classification indicates the percentage of correct prediction made by the model. Finally, the Nagelkerke R square value indicates the model’s goodness of fit.

The parameter estimate and odds ratio results indicate that subjective norm positively affected the intention to comply immediately for snowstorm, active shooter, health-related, and robbery incidents. For most of these cases, Hypothesis 1 is supported. However, for building-related incidents, the results were not significant. Building-related incidents differ from other incidents in that most schools conduct drills to inform students about what they should do in such incidents; hence, subjective norm is not a primary motivator for immediate compliance in such scenarios.

The results indicated that perceived safety threat has a significant impact on intention to comply in active shooter and building-related incidents. As shown in Table 2, these types of incidents are fast developing, and the threat to students safety can be severe. Hence in these incidents, safety concern is an important factor motivating immediate compliance. Perceived financial threat is significantly related to compliance intention in building-related incidents. In events such as fire-related incidents, students’ personal belongings can be damaged, and the probability of property loss is high if one is in the affected area. Hence, students are likely to comply with the notifications immediately if they are concerned about possible financial loss. The finding that perceived financial threat is not a significant factor in robbery incidents is a counterintuitive result. The logic at play here might be that the notifications are sent only after an incident occurs; thus, although a financial threat is associated with the robbery incident itself, students may perceive that their own chance of becoming a victim is quite low. Snowstorms and healthrelated incidents are relatively slower-developing incidents, and the perception is that there is a bit more time to avert potential financial losses.

We also tested the impact of gender and academic status, but they did not have a significant effect on compliance intention.

## Discussion

Our quantitative analysis revealed some counterintuitive results. For example, perceived financial threat was not a significant factor in predicting compliance intention in most types of incidents. Following the quantitative analysis, we used qualitative methods (focus group) to further investigate the research questions and provide validation for the results. The procedures and methods used with the focus groups are presented in Appendix C, and some of the key findings from these focus groups are discussed in this section.

Our study indicates that subjective norm and information quality trust are critical factors that positively influence immediate compliance. With regard to subjective norm, our findings show that students’ intentions to comply immediately—as opposed to verifying the information first and then complying—are strongly affected by the perceived expectations of referent groups including friends, parents, faculty, and university administrators, at least in most cases. For example, news about incidents on other campuses generates discussions among students and within families that then shape the norm. Some students in the focus groups said, “I have heard of many campus shooting incidents from the news. My friends and I have discussed these incidents, and we think that following the alert immediately in these situations is very important.”

Further, to test the differences in the influence exerted by different referent groups with regard to immediate compliance, mean contrasts were calculated; the results (mean contrast and significant level) are presented in Table 10. The contrast is computed by subtracting the mean of the group in the row from the mean of the group in the column. For example, –0.65 (Cell: Parents, Friends in snowstorm incident) indicates that the influence of friends is significantly less than the influence of parents in the context of snowstorm incidents. In fact, our results showed that friends have less influence than parents and university officials in all types of incidents. This finding is counterintuitive, as it might be expected that peer pressure in young adults would have a stronger impact (Lu et al. 2003; Scharl et al. 2005). It seems that the context of emergencies changes the impact of peer (friends) pressure. When the influence of parents versus university officials is considered, university officials have greater influence in active shooter, building-related, and robbery incidents. These incidents impact only certain areas on campus. We assert, in turn, that students perceive university officials to be better informed than other information sources about these incidents as compared to snowstorm and health-related incidents. In contrast, when incidents affect the entire community, as is the case with snowstorm and health-related incidents, students are more likely to be influenced by their parents.

Another major finding from our study is that the safety threat posed by an incident is a major factor influencing compliance in fast-developing incidents. Students informed us that they perceive snowstorm and health-related incidents as slower developing. In robbery incidents, as stated earlier, because most notifications are sent only after the incident has already happened, students do not perceive the existence of a safety threat that might otherwise be a factor motivating immediate compliance.

In our adaptation of Etzioni’s model, trust was introduced as an antecedent of compliance. This factor proved to be significant in all the extreme event scenarios. The characteristics of communication channels (such as the character limits for text messages), however, impose limits on the level of trust that can be built by way of content alone. It is important to realize that trust in the channel may have to be fostered by ensuring the relevance of the message, the actionability of the instruction, and the ability of students to assess the criticality of the information, as well as by developing norms (expected behavior) in general. The focus groups revealed that because emergency notification system messages are sent through electronic channels, it is difficult to build trust solely based on the content of the message. With such channels, there is insufficient electronic real estate to provide proof or convincing arguments. Given this constraint, trust has to be present at the information system level or the channel level, and it is important to understand how such trust can be built.

Finally, the different types of emergencies represent different levels of risk exposure for student safety. They are also associated with different levels of urgency and uncertainty. Our results show that the factors impacting the effectiveness of emergency information notification systems vary according to the type of emergency. For example, the perceived safety threat is significant in building-related and shooting incidents; students perceive these types of incidents as fast developing and as potentially having severe impact. Some students in the focus group told us that “A shooting incident is very dangerous, so I would be very concerned about my safety.”

Focus groups also confirmed that most students prefer to get notifications for all types of campus incidents. Also, they trust campus police and security personnel more than they trust administrators and professors in this context.

From a practice perspective, given that normative factors positively influence immediate compliance, we recommend that administrators target referent groups in advertising messages related to campus safety. Referent groups (such as parents and university officials) can be targeted to urge students to comply immediately with alert-related instructions and to assure them that immediate compliance will lead to lower risk and relieve stress on others. Students could be made aware that immediate compliance is an expected behavior during campus emergencies through education and training. These expectations should be made clear to students, a priori, as such instructions help create norms and instill a culture of safety. Parents and professors can help strengthen this belief.

Students clearly expect that the notifications sent through a campus alert system will be relevant; thus it is important to use such systems only for communicating messages related to campus emergencies that affect students. For information privacy reasons, location information for specific students is not available to university administrators. Therefore, information generated by emergency notification systems cannot be targeted to only those students who are directly affected by the threat. Hence, instead of a multicast mode, more of a broadcast mechanism must be utilized. Given that students who are unaffected by the incident will also receive the message, there is inevitably erosion of the expectation that messages will always be relevant to them. To counteract this trend, university officials must find ways or create applications to ensure that voluntary disclosure of location information while students are present on college campuses is achieved. This notion is somewhat controversial and represents an area for further research. From an IT perspective, better notification technologies should be adopted that allow for more accurate targeting of emergency alerts and for provision of mechanisms that clearly and easily convey urgency and threat level.

Our results also show that past experience positively impacts immediate compliance in building-related incidents. Students in the focus groups told us that because drills are held to educate students regarding the actions they need to take in case of building-related incidents, they are likely to immediately comply with notifications related to these events— they did not perceive any need to verify the message’s content in such an event. For campus emergency managers, an implication of this finding is that training and education that help students better prepare for emergencies can improve immediate compliance.

## Limitations and Future Studies

This research has some limitations, which can be addressed in future investigations. First, this study was conducted in the northern United States—a region usually affected by snowstorms. Individuals at other universities in other parts of the world may be more familiar with other types of emergencies. In these cases, alerts may take different forms, including the use of sirens. Our study, although relevant in this context, is not directly applicable, as both the context and the factors motivating compliance are different. Further, the context of our study is U.S.-centric. Culture issues may impact compliance, but this possibility is not reflected in our study. Future studies might address these issues.

Second, we measured compliance as a categorical variable with two categories: (1) immediate compliance and (2) delayed compliance (verify first and then comply). We started with compliance being parsed into three categories: no compliance, delayed compliance, and immediate compliance. Our survey results reported very few responses for “no compliance,” so we eliminated this option from our analysis. However, in other settings for other types of emergencies, “no compliance” may be a valid category. Future studies might further investigate this issue.

Third, future research might address the issue of compliance in this context by investigating the effects of regional characteristics on behavior. Public apathy toward emergency notifications in the broader community context also needs to be studied. The importance of addressing this issue is clear, as exemplified by the noticeable apathy evident after warnings were provided through emergency notifications to the residents of Joplin, Missouri, before a tornado devastated that town (Federal Signal Public Safety Survey 2012).

Fourth, extensions of our study might investigate other aspects that are important to effective emergency response. What is the most effective way to communicate with students? How do we improve the timeliness of notification and deal with the asynchronous nature of communication? What is the most effective channel? How should we utilize local, regional, and national government systems, television stations with weather alerts, and other channels to improve campus emergency response? How should the messages be framed so that the information is perceived as highly trustworthy or elicits more immediate compliance?

## Conclusions

This study makes several contributions to the field of information systems as it relates to emergency notification systems and policies for more effective response. Although compliance has been studied in the IS field, it has not been adequately addressed in time-sensitive contexts. This paper adapts Etzioni’s theory to provide a deeper understanding of the antecedents to immediate compliance. It introduces “information quality trust” as an important predictor of immediate compliance. The results also reveal that the important factors affecting compliance vary by the types of campus emergency.

Our research can be extended to a variety of domains—for example, to the health care domain to study the impact of alerts as a means to foster better adherence to medication regimens. It also has important implications on the effective use of short communications to ensure better compliance.

## Mechanics

Each focus group included two note-takers and a coordinator in addition to six student participants. The answers were recorded by both notetakers using an open coding scheme (Crook and Kumar 1998). The coordinator first introduced the purpose of the study and the focus group, ensuring that participants understood which factors affect ENS-message compliance intention (comply immediately or verify first and then comply) and why these factors are important. Next, the coordinator encouraged all students to actively participate by answering each question. The coordinator guided the direction of the discussion to ensure that the conversation did not digress from the topic and provided necessary explanations. The data from the focus group were entered into a document within 24 hours of each focus-group session.

In addition to the issues discussed in the main body of the paper, the focus groups informed the study on a variety of related issues. For example, we learned from the focus groups that most students prefer to receive notifications for all types of campus incidents. Also, they trust campus police and security personnel more than they trust administrators and professors in this context. For less-urgent incidents, they might seek additional information from other sources such as school websites and news channels, but they felt it remains important for schools to send timely notifications. For example, the students agreed that a snowstorm does not develop as rapidly as a campus shooting incident, but if students are not aware of the changing weather conditions and are driving in a snowstorm, the situation can quickly become dangerous. Hence, timely notifications and compliance remain important in these kinds of incidents. Students also mentioned other factors that might influence compliance—for example, education and training are important to improve immediate compliance; where the campus is located affects the perceived threat posed by robbery incidents; and police on scene, other people receiving the same notification messages, and multiple alerts (such as receiving a text alert and hearing an audible alarm) improve compliance.

## Appendix D

## Assessment of Common Method Bias

In a data collection process, if all data are self-reported and collected through the same questionnaire within the same period of time, common method bias may affect the estimates of the relationship between constructs. To assess common method bias, we employed two statistical methods: Harman’s single-factor test measurement (Podsakoff et al. 2003) and the marker-variable analysis suggested by Lindell and Whitney (2001). We also introduced a procedural remedy to reduce common method bias as suggested by Podsakoff et al. (2003).

First we performed Harman’s single-factor test measurement (Podsakoff et al. 2003). We conducted an exploratory factor analysis to determine the number of factors that were necessary to account for the variance in the variables. If a substantial amount of common method variance is present, either (1) a single factor will emerge from the factor analysis or (2) one general factor will account for the majority of the covariance among the variables. We conducted the test for five scenarios. All five analyses produced more than one factor with Eigen values greater than 1.0. The total variance explained by the factors in these five scenarios was 68.74%, 74.63%, 75.15%, 68.97%, and 66.88%, respectively. When using single-factor analysis, if one factor contributes more than 50% of the total variance, common method bias might exist (Indushobha et al. 2010; Nov and Ye 2008; Xu et al. 2014). In our analysis, the first extracted factor accounted for 45.06%, 40.93%, 44.32%, 43.30%, and 44.53% of the variance in the data; the second explained 12.02%, 14.07%, 16.83%, 15.30%, and 13.04% of the variance in the five scenarios. This outcome indicates that common method variance was not a serious issue in our study.

Harman’s single-factor test has been criticized for having insufficient sensitivity to detect a moderate or small level of the common bias effect (Malhotra et al. 2006; Podsakoff et al. 2003). For this reason, we also carried out the marker-variable analysis suggested by Lindell and Whitney. “Ease of sign-up for campus alerts” was employed as a theoretically unrelated variable to adjust the correlations among the principal variables. Ease of sign-up for campus alerts was measured by the question, “How much do you agree that signing up for campus alerts is easy to do?” The responses were recorded on a seven-point Likert scale. The scales were fully anchored (strongly disagree, disagree, somewhat disagree, neither agree nor disagree, somewhat agree, agree, strongly agree). This was consistent with the measurement of the other independent variables. The results showed no high correlations between the marker and the principal variables (Table D1). Thus we concluded that common method bias was not a serious concern.

We also introduced a procedural remedy to reduce common method bias, as suggested by Podsakoff et al. (2003). We tried to reduce the variance introduced by social desirability or respondent acquiescence by protecting respondent anonymity and reducing evaluation apprehension. We provided survey participants with verbal and written assurance that the survey would be anonymous and that the purpose of the survey was to help improve campus safety.

The dependent variable of our study was a binary variable—ENS-message compliance intention—which was collected for all five types of events. Because the dependent variable was captured in different contexts, common method bias was not of great concern for the dependen variable in this study.
