# Disruptive Information Technology Innovations and the Cost of Equity Capital: The Moderating Effect of CEO Incentives and Institutional Pressures

## Abstract

Disruptive information technology (IT) innovations not only present opportunities, but also cause uncertain impacts on firm risk that affects the equity financing cost of a firm. This paper used longitudinal data from 146 U.S. listed firms that adopted radio frequency identification (RFID), a disruptive technology that enables supply chain process innovation. Results show that firms that adopted RFID significantly reduced their cost of equity capital, and the reduction was stronger for firms with greater CEO incentive-based compensation and coercive pressure. The findings help managers make strategies that maximize the benefits of disruptive IT innovations.

Keywords: Disruptive IT innovations; the cost of equity capital

# Disruptive Information Technology Innovations and the Cost of Equity Capital: The Moderating Effect of CEO Incentives and Institutional Pressures

## 1. Introduction

Disruptive information technology (IT) innovation is defined as an architectural innovation originating in the IT based that has subsequent pervasive and radical impacts on development processes and their outcome [1]. Disruptive IT innovation revolutionizes operation processes that provides firms with major new growth in business, dramatic improvements, and more efficient on unit performance [1-3]. Engaging in innovation activities also help firms to be aware of the latest development, absorb new and related knowledge, and develop dynamic capabilities that equip firms with abilities to handle dynamic market changes [4-6]. However, disruptive IT innovation also has drawbacks that investors may perceive it negatively. The adoption of disruptive IT innovations is risky and expensive, and their benefits are uncertain in the long term. The adoption often leads to disruptions in operations of the adopting firms because its measures may not compatible with the existing systems. The innovation also dramatically modifies a firm’s division of labor and requires new skills and behavior to sustain competitive advantage. Firms have to adjust their thinking and operation processes to meet significant changes caused by the innovation [1, 2]. Any change would encounter resistance from people, who believe that their interests would be compromised following the adoption of those innovations [7]. Moreover, the adoption of disruptive IT innovations requires certain capabilities and resources, firms that do not possess sufficient resources or capabilities for the adoption are likely to fail the adoption and suffer

financial losses from the adoption. In addition, it can take firms years to receive the full benefit of these innovations.

Researchers have been interested in understanding the relationship between IT innovations and economic performance. Early research has examined how IT innovations based measures [e.g., 11, 12, 13], and how the impact varies with contextual factors such as firm and technology characteristics. Mithas et al. [8] examined more than 400 global firms. They found that IT had a positive impact on profitability, and firms had greater success in achieving higher profitability through IT-enabled revenue growth than through IT-enabled cost reduction. Zhu [10] examined the main effects and the interaction effects of e-commerce and IT on firm performance. They found a positive interaction effect between IT infrastructure and e-commerce capability, suggesting that the complementarity positively contributes to sales per employee, inventory turnover, and cost reduction. Li and Ye [9] found that the impact of IT investments on financial performance was positively associated with greater environmental changes, more proactive company strategy, and closer CEO/CIO ties. As for those short-term event studies, Dos Santos and colleagues [12] discovered that innovative IT investments increased market returns, while noninnovative investments did not. Dardan and colleagues [11] found that e-commerce initiatives increased stock return and volume, and the returns were different in good and bad markets. Hayes and associates [13] found that the market reacted positively to initial enterprise resource planning (ERP) announcements and more positively toward ERP investment news from small and financially healthy firms. More recently, empirical studies also have started to examine the impact of IT investments on risk. Dewan and Ren [14] found that IT investment increases both profitability and risk (i.e., variability of profitability), and that suitable firm boundary strategies of diversification and vertical integration in supply chain can moderate the effects

of IT on firm performance in a manner that increases profitability and decreases risk, at the margins.

This study examines the same substantive issues, but takes a long-term event study approach [15] to jointly examine the response of the capital market to disruptive IT innovations and the factors that affect this response. Studying the effect of disruptive IT innovations on the cost of equity capital is important. First, the cost of equity capital is the internal rate of return that the market applies to the future cash flows of a firm to determine its current market value. If the capital market perceives that a disruptive IT innovation improves the risk portfolio of the firm, then the capital market will lower the cost of capital, which will increase the overall economic performance of the firm. Second, the cost of equity capital is a key input in the long-term investment decisions of a firm because it reflects the required rate of return on firm investments of investors [16]. Third, previous studies have suggested that agency cost and institutional pressures could influence the cost of equity capital [17-20]. However, empirical evidence is limited to support such a claim in the context of disruptive IT innovations. Overall, a deeper understanding of the effect of disruptive IT innovation on financing costs and the conditions that reduce this cost has significant competitive implications for a firm.

The present study estimates the impact of disruptive IT innovations on the cost of equity capital and the moderating effect of CEO incentives and institutional pressures on the impact based on longitudinal objective data. Using radio frequency identification (RFID) as the example of disruptive technology for supply chain process innovation [21-26], we apply long-term event study analysis to estimate abnormal change in the cost of equity capital between the adopters and non-adopters. We then conduct hierarchical regression analysis to examine how CEO incentives and institutional pressures affect the magnitude and direction of the abnormal cost of equity capital.

## 1.1. RFID as supply chain process innovation

Due to the disruptive nature of RFID, it is not commonly adopted in manufacturing industries until Walmart launched a mandate requiring its top 100 suppliers to used RFID tags on the cases or pallets of shipments in 2003. An RFID system includes tags and readers, application software, computing hardware, and middleware [27]. RFID allows the automated identification of products by embedding chips with wireless antennas into objects [28] and offers numerous advantages relative to barcodes. Among the advantages are the simultaneous reading of goods, absence of the constraint of line-of-sight tracking, storage of relevant information, and reusability [29]. RFID also dramatically changes the structure of work processes, such as order tracking and fulfillment as well as inventory control [28]. In particular, the tags can store and share relevant information over the Internet in real-time. Therefore, RFID adoption provides firms complete visibility of inventory movement along the supply chain leading to operational and strategic benefits [30]. Moreover, RFID provides real-time intelligence to organizations [31] and enables new business model (e.g., B-to-B ecommerce applications) [32].

RFID is widely viewed as a disruptive technology [21-24] that revolutionizes supply chain processes and practices [25, 26]. For example, Bose et al. [21] discussed that RFID met the two criteria of being a disruptive IT innovation. First, the adoption of RFID incorporated different architectural principles that substitute the existing bar code systemand fundamentally change the supply chain operations of adopting firms. Second, RFID could create several radical services and process innovations during the implementation stage. Krotov and Junglas [24] suggested that RFID introduced a new value proposition that had the potential to become a disruptive technology with a profound impact on business and society. Moreover, Sheffi [33] identified RFID as a disruptive technology similar to personal

computers, which showed six stages in its evolution, namely, (1) fog of innovation, (2) life support for existing technologies, (3) stamp of approval, (4) transition from the old, (5) ubiquity, and (6) the big bang. Sheffi defined RFID development between the first and third stages of the innovation cycle because of its unclear benefits and several unsolved problems such as security and privacy.

Since RFID is a disruptive technology, firms need to overcome several challenges to realize the full potential of RFID adoption [36-38]. For example, firms face high cost of establishing an RFID infrastructure throughout the supply chain because the adoption involves investment in tags, readers and related equipment, network and communication systems, data maintenance and process including changes to existing Enterprise Resource Planning and other related systems [34]. Moreover, the integration of RFID into the supply chain processes of a firm is complex, the adoption destroys a firm’s existing competences when using barcode system, thus requiring to the reinvention of its key processes in the business network [40]. RFID adoption also requires warehouse activities in the supply chain to be completely re-engineered [32]. However, firms are often lack of expertise such as RFID data analysts, RFID systems technicians, and RFID maintenance officers in the business network to implement that adoption [39]. In addition, firms adopting RFID may not instantly experience its benefits [42], and it may take as long as two to three years for returns on investment to begin to appear [43].

RFID has received increasing attention from academics and practitioners since Walmart launched a mandate requiring its top 100 suppliers to use RFID tags on the cases or pallets of shipments in 2003. Previous studies focus on examining the business value of RFID using accounting-based measures, such as operational performance and profitability [e.g., 36, 39, 44, 45], and market-based measures such as stock price [e.g., 21, 46]. For instance, a case study of five Hong Kong fashion retailers by Moon and Ngai [47] indicated improved

operational efficiency and effectiveness and increased sales and profits as major benefits of RFID adoption. Jeong and Lu [46] found an overall positive abnormal stock return on RFID investment announcements over a three-day event window based on 128 RFID investments. However, the impact of RFID on the cost of equity capital and the effect of CEO incentives and institutional pressures on that impact remain uncertain.

## 2. Theoretical development and hypotheses

## 2.1. The impact of disruptive IT innovations on the cost of equity capital

Firms can be financed through equity. The cost of equity is the expected return from the investment of firm equity, such as stock. In the equity market, disruptive IT innovations like RFID may reduce a firm’s cost of equity capital through two ways: the increase of investor base and the reduction of systematic risk. For the former case, prior studies suggest that an increase in the investor base results in lower cost of equity capital [48] because the opportunities for risk diversification are increased [49]. Previous studies also suggest that investment in innovations gain investor base [18]. For example, firms with e-commerce initiatives attracted more investors to buy their shares that increase both stock price and volumes [11]. Similarly, RFID-adopting firms disclose their efforts on the adoption. Such public disclosure enhances media and analyst coverage, and may attract more investor to buy their shares, thus decreasing their cost of equity capital.

Investors make investments by trading off risk and return [17]. In other words, investors maximize their return for a given level of systematic risk or minimize systematic risk for a given level of return. Disruptive technology provides firms with dramatic improvements and more efficient unit performance [3]. For example, RFID technology improves the performance of warehousing and distribution operations and order accuracy by automating operations that used to involve manual handling [50] and freeing them from

human error [51]. Such improvement in operations reduces operating costs, thus reducing systematic risk. The innovation also enhances firms’ ability to handle dynamic market changes and insulate from market downturns. Firms are less vulnerable to competitive forces, and thus reducing systematic risk [52]. Industry reports suggested that RFID adoption could increase sales by 2% – 7%, especially item-level RFID tagging in retail supply chains [53]. If a disruptive IT innovation reduces the level of systematic risk because it improves performance that increases and stabilizes future cash flows, then the market will reward the improved performance with a lower cost of equity capital. Based on the above arguments, we propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1: Disruptive IT innovation significantly reduces the cost of equity.

## 2.2. The moderating effect of CEO incentives and Institutional pressures

## 2.2.1. CEO incentives

Agency theory refers to the ubiquitous agency relationship, wherein the principals (shareholders) delegate work to the agents (CEO) [54]. Such a relationship is bounded by an agreement between the principals and the agents, which specifies the rights of the parties, ways of judging performance, and their payoffs [55]. Two problems are likely to occur between shareholders and the agents. First, the goals of the principals and agents might be conflicting. Second, monitoring what the agents are actually doing is difficult or expensive for the principal because agents have better control over the company resources; this issue is called information asymmetry [54]. Therefore, a firm that lacks alignment between shareholders and agents may result in agency costs, such as perquisites consumption or other opportunistic behavior by managers [55, 56]. Incentive-based compensation such as bonuses and stock options that are highly related to organizational performance [57] may serve as

incentive alignments. Barney [20] found that employee stock ownership reduced the cost of equity capital in Japanese electronics firms because the pay aligned the interests of employees with stockholders.

CEO plays a key role in determining disruptive IT innovation investment as well as the success of the adoption. Based on an agency perspective, investors may lower the cost of equity capital for firms with greater CEO incentive-based pay. First, incentive pay can reduce the threat of CEO opportunism by motivating CEO to engage in actions that maximize firm performance [58]. CEO receives more incentive-based pay will devote more attention to the adoption to ensure its success. CEO is also more likely to make decisions during RFID implementation in ways consistent with outside equity holders’ interests. In addition, incentive-based pay is positively related to information disclosures. Nagar et al. [59] found that CEO’s ratio of stock-based to total compensation and the CEO’s average value of shareholdings are positively related to information disclosures. Enhanced information distribution can increase transparency, reduce uncertainty and information asymmetry, and make monitoring easier. Overall, investors are willing to accept the lower cost of equity capital under greater CEO incentive-based pay [48, 60, 61]. This leads to the second hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2: Firms with higher CEO incentive-based compensation obtain lower cost of equity capital upon disruptive IT innovations adoption.

## 2.2.2. Coercive pressure

Innovations diffuse through institutional pressures in organizational setting. When managers make an investment decision of a promising innovation, one of the key criteria they consider is the institutional environment where their firms are located [62]. Firms do not only compete for resources and customers, but also compete for political power and institutional legitimacy for social and economic fitness [63]. Legitimacy is defined as “a generalized perception or assumption that the actions of an entity are desirable, proper, or appropriate within some socially constructed system of norms, values, beliefs, and definitions” [64, p574]. Legitimacy is critical in accessing various resources from potential exchange partners in the organizational field, such as customers, suppliers, and regulators whom a firm needs to survive [65].

RFID adoption in manufacturing industries is characterized by powerful initiators (i.e., retailers and the government) [41]. Mandates by the U.S. Department of Defense (DoD), U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and retail giants such as Walmart have shaped the institutional environment for RFID adoption. In particular, since the RFID mandate of Walmart in 2003, manufacturing firms are facing increasing pressures to adopt RFID [66, 67]. Previous studies have showed that customers who mandate RFID adoption are in a position to use financial benefits to reward dependent firms for their compliance [25]. Moreover, Scott [68] argued that conformity to the institutional environment could provide legitimacy to firms, allowing them to secure valued resources that lead to better financial performance. The results of previous empirical studies consistently show that firms with legitimacy obtain higher ratings on compliance [69], stock market risk reduction [70], and financial performance improvement [71]. For example, Westphal et al. [69] examined the consequences of conformity on normative adoption (i.e., total quality management accreditation in over 2,700 U.S. hospitals) and stated that adoption was positively associated with legitimacy (e.g., higher ratings on compliance). Therefore, we expect that potential stockholders or other stakeholders will perceive that firms that conformed to the mandate are legitimate and will award these firms with a lower equity financing cost.

Hypothesis 3: Firms facing coercive pressure obtain lower cost of equity capital upon disruptive IT innovations adoption.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Data collection and processing

The event study method has been recently used in IT research to estimate the specific abnormal operating performance caused by an event [72-74]. In this study, we focus on manufacturing firms (SIC code 2000-3999) listed in stock markets in the United States that adopt RFID. We conducted a comprehensive announcement search covering over 8000 listed firms (SIC code 2000-3999) in Standard and Poor’s COMPUSTAT. We searched for RFID adoption announcements containing names of publicly listed firm names and keywords such as “RFID,” “RF-ID,” or “radio frequency identification” from Factiva for 1992 through 2012. According to innovation literature, innovation is an idea, material artifact, or behavior that is new to the firm adopting it [1, 67], we defined RFID adopter as a firm that has applied its first RFID adoption to supply chain. We adopted a systematic review mechanism to ensure data validity. First, we collected and codified all RFID adoption announcements. We then reviewed all of the announcements and verified any ambiguous cases to ensure that the selection and coding of those announcements are accurate and objective.

Following standard practice identified in several previous long-term event studies [72, 75], we focused on the first of multiple announcements made by individual firms regarding RFID adoption. Moreover, we eliminated announcements with confounding events such as new company business, merger and acquisition, and other automatic identification applications (e.g., QR Code) in the supply chain occurring within the five-year confounding window lasting from two years before the event to three years after the event. The sample used in the present study was cross-checked against data on the 108 and 128 RFID

#

investment announcements respectively collected by Bose et al. [21] and Jeong and Lu [46] to ensure completeness of the current sample size. We also cross-checked announcements collected from Factiva with news from other public sources. For instance, lists of manufacturers subject to customer mandates (e.g., Walmart and the Department of Defense), Google News, and related technology periodicals including RFID Journal and the RFID Knowledgebase. After extensive and careful filtering, we obtained 383 publicly listed firms that adopted RFID. Below are examples of such an announcement.

• Sep 13, 2004: Computerworld: Sara Lee Corp. is testing RFID tags on some product cases and shipping pallets to meet a mandate that RFID technology be used on goods sent to Walmart Stores Inc.'s Dallas-area distribution centers starting in January 2005.

• Apr 14, 2008: PR Newswire (U.S.): American Apparel Deploys RFID Solution from Motorola to improve inventory accuracy and streamline operations.

We collected financial performance data from COMPUSTAT, stock data from CRSP, and CEO data from EXECUCOMP. We demanded the performance data to be dated at least two years prior to RFID adoption to match a control firm for data analysis. The number of sample firms decreased to 147 because other firms adopted RFID prior to becoming listed firms or because their data is not available in the database for matching. Out of the 147 announcements, approximately 53% (78 out of 147) of the sample firms proactively adopted RFID due to the system’s benefits, whereas 47% (69 out of 147) adopted RFID passively due to compliance requirements, such as government policies and mandates from their respective business partners. Among the 69 passive adoptions, 33 of the announcements related to mandates from Walmart. Three examples of such an announcement are as follows (abbreviated):

• Mar 4, 2007: The RFID Knowledgebase: Shaw Industries Group, a carpet and rug producer, has contracted ODIN Technologies to design and install an RFID system for supply chain optimization and to also comply with Walmart's mandate.

· Mar $I 6 ,$ 2005: Business Wire: LifeCell implements RFID systems to automate process to meet rigorous FDA label tracking requirements.

• Aug 2, 2006: PR Newswire: Lockheed Martin uses RFID label to meet DoD compliance tagging requirements.

Following previous studies [76], we estimated the cost of equity capital as the expected investor return from the firm’s equity, calculated with the CAPM [77, 78].

3.2. Event study methodology

3.2.1. Data analysis

An event study requires defining the event period. We followed procedures from prior studies to define the event period [e.g., 72, 73]. The year of formal RFID adoption when a firm successfully applied full-scale RFID was defined as year t. Given that full-scale implementation [80, 81], we used t – 1 as the initiating time of RFID implementation and year t – 2 as the base year that is free from the impact of RFID adoption to match the control firms. The definition is aligned with a previous research by Hendricks et al. [81], suggesting approximately one year for implementing a typical SCM system. Since the payback period for RFID projects is between two and three years [43, 82], we detected the impact of RFID on the cost of equity capital up to three years after the RFID adoption (i.e., t + 1, t + 2, and t + 3). Overall, we studied the cost of equity capital for the period starting from two years before RFID adoption and three years after RFID adoption (i.e., from t – 2 to t + 3). We estimated abnormal performance as the sample post-event performance (i.e., actual performance) minus the expected performance. We calculated the expected performance as the sample pre-event performance plus the change of performance of the control firm during the same period [15]. The formula for calculating the abnormal performances as follow:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathrm{AP} (t + j) = \mathrm{PS} (t + j) - \mathrm{EP} (t + j) \\ \mathrm{EP} (t + j) = \mathrm{PS} (t + i) + [ \mathrm{PC} (t + j) - \mathrm{PC} (t + i) ] \end{array} \tag {2}\tag{3}
$$

where AP is the abnormal performance, PS is the actual performance, EP is the expected performance of sample firms, PC is the performance of the control firm, t is the RFID adoption year, i is the base year (i = -2), and j is the ending year of comparison (j = -1, 0, 1, 2 and 3). In our sample, we included announcements with specified actual adoption dates.

While the majority of the announcements were made upon or after RFID adoption, only a few of the announcements were disclosed during the implementation stage. Examples of these two types of announcements are shown below.

• Feb 22, 2004: Times Union: General Electric Co. started using a constellation of inventory process a year ago. (The adoption date in this case was Feb 22, 2003.)

• Apr 28, 2004: CMP TechWeb: Hewlett-Packard will ship cases and pallets of ink-jet printers and scanners with radio-frequency identification tags attached to them to Walmart Stores Inc. beginning Friday. (The adoption date in this case was Friday, Apr 30, 2004.)

• Jun 28, 2004: Home Textiles Today: Mohawk Home later this year will break ground on its seventh finishing and distribution facility, with completion slated for the end of 2005. Both sites will also be outfitted with state-of-the-art technology and equipment, including RFID capabilities, stated Mohawk. (The adoption date in this case was Dec 31, 2005.)

Following previous studies [81], for announcements during the implementation stage, such as pilot, trial, and planning phases, we aligned the adoption time by adding one year (unless adoption date is specified), so that the adoption of RFID can be averaged over one year for a full-scale rollout. After confirming the adoption dates of the sample firms, we compared the adoption dates against the dates of the fiscal year end to estimate the year t.

Following previous event studies [e.g., 72, 74], we reported three statistical test results, including the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank (WSR), sign test, and the parametric t-test. Barber and Lyon [15] have asserted that a nonparametric test is more appropriate than a parametric t-test when the abnormal performance of the sample firms is not normally distributed. Therefore, we took WSR to be the most appropriate test for our study. The discussion below is based on the WSR statistics. The other statistics contained in the tables indicate similar results.

## 3.2.2. Selection of control firms

We employed propensity score matching that has been adopted extensively in economics and statistics to select control firms [e.g., 72, 74]. Propensity score matching is based on the likelihood that a firm will adopt a particular practice [83, 84]. This ensures direct comparisons among firms with similar characteristics (propensity scores) where one firm adopts disruptive IT innovation and the other does not. Such matching process should substantially reduce any remaining selection bias issues.

We first calculated propensity scores from a logistic regression of an indicator variable equal to one if the firm adopts RFID and zero if the firm does not adopt RFID. Given that matching procedures tend to be invalidated if there are too many regressors [85], we only included limited known factors affecting the decision in favor of the disruptive IT innovation based on theories and empirical evidence. These predicting factors were firm size (natural logarithm of the total assets), ROA (operating income over total assets), financial slack (current assets over total assets), SGA intensity (sales and general administrative cost over sales), leverage (debt over total assets), R&D intensity (R&D expenses over sales), sales growth (annual sales growth rate), labor productivity (operating income over number of employees), and inventory days (365 over inventory turnover). All factors were based on data in year t - 2. Firm size, ROA, financial slack and SGA intensity imply the availability of resources for firms to adopt innovations successfully [86]. Firms with more slack resources are more capable of coping with disruption and are thus more likely to adopt disruptive IT innovations. Second, we included leverage and R&D intensity to capture managerial risk taking which is a key determinant of innovations adoption [87]. Third, we included the

## 4.2. Hierarchical regression analysis of the impact of CEO incentives and coercive pressure on the abnormal cost of equity capital

In this section, we used hierarchical regression analysis to examine the influence of CEO incentives and institutional pressures on the dependent variable, abnormal cost of equity capital (i.e., abnormal cost of equity capital of the sample firm over a five-year period). To test hypothesis 2, we used CEO incentive-based compensation, which includes bonuses, stock options, and restricted stock options. We expected that firms with higher CEO incentivebased compensation helps reduce agency cost and obtain lower cost of equity capital. To test hypothesis 3, we created a dummy variable called coercive adoption and coded it as 1 for

firms adopting RFID because of the mandate of customers; otherwise, we coded it as 0. We predicted that potential stockholders or other stakeholders will perceive that firms conforming to the mandate are legitimate and will award those firms with a lower financing cost.

To test the robustness of our results, we included the following individual-, firm-, and industry-level control variables. First, we controlled CEO characteristics such as age, gender, duality, and basic salary. Second, we included the firm-level characteristics of a firm such as previous cost of equity capital, firm size (total assets), ROE (operating income over shareholders equity), Tobins’ Q (the sum of market value of share, preferred stock, and debt over total assets), financial slack, and R&D intensity that could affect firm performance. We utilized data starting from year t – 2 for all these variables. Third, for industry-level variables, we controlled industry competitiveness measured by the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) of industry concentration [90]. The index was measured as the sum of the squared market shares for all firms in an industry group, ranging between 0 and 1. A score approaching 0 indicates the presence of several competitors in an industry. Such industries are considered more complex and competitive. We controlled industry clockspeed [91] and assigned the variable with values of 1, 2, and 3 to represent fast, medium, and slow clockspeed industries, respectively. We also controlled industry sales growth, which was measured as the average performance change in industry sales between year t – 2 and t + 3. Fourth, we created a dummy variable for announcements after implementation stage, which was equal to 1 for announcements published when the implementation of RFID had been completed, and 0 if otherwise. Finally, we controlled the adoption year. Table 3 shows the correlations between various indicators.

## 5. DISCUSSION

## 5.1. Overview and Contributions

This study examines the impact of disruptive IT innovation on the cost of equity capital. Based on a sample of 146 RFID-adopting firms, the financial market accepts firms that adopted disruptive IT innovations with lower equity risk premium. The finding shows that disruptive IT innovations do not only contribute to stock returns in a short term [46, 92],

#

but also reduce the cost of equity capital in the long term. The result indicates that investors tend to believe that the benefits of RFID are greater than the costs in the long term.

We further examined the moderation of CEO incentives and institutional pressures on the effect of disruptive IT innovations on the cost of equity capital. Consistent with agency perspective and empirical findings reported by Barney [20], we found that firms with greater CEO incentive-based compensation obtained lower cost of equity capital. The result indicates that equity market perceives that financial alignment helps reduce agency costs by motivating managers to embrace the interests of shareholders. Similarly, the finding shows that firms conforming to customer mandate received lower cost of equity capital. The result is consistent with that of Bansal and Clelland [70], who reported that high corporate environmental legitimacy experienced reduced stock market risk, and with that of Deephouse [71], who argued that banks conforming to institutional pressures to obtain legitimacy resulted in an increased financial performance.

In general, this study provides important theoretical implications. First, the findings of this study provide one of the first objective evidence of the direction and the magnitude of the effects of such adoption on the cost of equity capital. In particular, this study complements prior IT research that focused on examining financial return using accounting-based measures or short-term market-based measures by adding accumulative evidence to an underdeveloped literature area regarding the relationship between IT investment and the cost of equity capital. These findings help build a better theory regarding the outcomes of disruptive IT innovations and explain the relationship between economic performance and disruptive IT innovation. Second, this study complements considerable previous research that suggests that the link between IT investment and firm performance depends on contextual factors, such as industry competitiveness, industry clockspeed, type of IT, time, and firm size [9, 81, 93-98] by showing how CEO incentives and institutional pressures affect that link.

#

Third, the present study contributes to agency theory. Previous studies have examined the role of executive incentive compensation in mitigating the agency costs [19]. Our result that firms with greater CEO incentive-based compensation obtained lower cost of equity capital indicates that the alignment of interest between CEO and shareholders has a positive role in equity financing. In addition, this study has contributed to institutional theory by providing an empirical account of calculated support for existing institutions. Institutional theory has been applied in the IT literature to explain the antecedents of an IT adoption [66, 99]. However, empirical evidence of the effect of conformity to institutional pressures on financial cost is limited. Our examination of the influence of coercive pressure on the cost of equity capital of the adopting-firms fills this gap.

Regarding practical implications, the results indicating that RFID adoption reduces the cost of equity capital send an encouraging message to firms, particularly those that the cost of equity capital is a significant component of doing business, to adopt disruptive IT innovations which are similar to the case of RFID. Moreover, the results of the hierarchical regression indicating that the equity market imposes less financial risks on adopting firms that conformed to coercive pressure suggest that managers should pursue coercive disruptive IT innovation to obtain legitimacy and improve their cost of equity capital. In addition, the results indicate that setting up a reward mechanism or governance that aligns the interests of the managers with those of the shareholders is important. When implementing disruptive IT innovations, firms should consider designing CEO compensation with a higher ratio of incentive-based pay to obtain lower cost of equity capital.

## 5.2. Limitations

This study has a few limitations. First, the study only included publicly listed manufacturing firms in the U.S. Therefore, the results may be inapplicable to firms in other

#

countries, industries, or non-listed private sectors. Future research can extend the analysis to emerging markets or small- and medium-sized firms. Moreover, future studies can include sample firms across countries to examine other contextual factors such as national culture and government policy, which may affect the impact of disruptive IT innovations on the cost of equity capital. Second, we only included sample firms that announced their adoption to the media. Firms that did not release their RFID investment decisions to the press are excluded from this research. Similarly, a few selected control firms may have adopted RFID without announcing this initiative to the public. Third, certain characteristics related to RFID adoption, e.g., the amount of the investment, were unaccounted in our data because of non-availability. Due to data unavailability, we were also not able to control all confounding events during the event periods, which may lead to biased results. However, such bias would be randomly distributed across all firms (both sample and control firms) and should not have compromised the validity of our results.

Fourth, we used longitudinal data from listed firms to examine the impact of disruptive IT innovations on the cost of equity capital. Given the data availability constraints for public listed firms, we failed to gain an in-depth understanding on the actual processes in the firms we studied. Theoretical research using complementary methods (e.g., case studies, field studies, and surveys) to validate our indicators and develop a conceptual model related to other elements of disruptive IT innovations will be useful in setting an agenda for further empirical research. In addition, we focused on the case of RFID adoption. Future studies may examine the impact and contingencies of other disruptive IT innovations on the cost of equity capital.

## 5.3. Conclusions

#

Longitudinal data from 146 listed U.S. firms that adopted a disruptive technology (i.e., RFID) for supply chain process innovation show a significant reduction on the cost of equity capital. Such reduction was stronger under coercive pressure and greater CEO incentivebased compensation. The findings provide an alternative explanation to observed positive relationship between economic performance and disruptive IT innovations, and contribute to agency and institutional theory by showing the moderation of CEO incentives and coercive pressure on the relationship between disruptive IT innovation and the cost of equity capital. Finally, the findings deepen the understanding of managers on the effects of disruptive IT innovation on financing costs and guide managers on making strategies to maximize the benefits of the adoption.
