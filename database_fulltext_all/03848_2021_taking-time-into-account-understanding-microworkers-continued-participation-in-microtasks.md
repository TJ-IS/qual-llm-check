# Taking Time into Account: Understanding Microworkers’ Continued Participation in Microtasks

Ling Jiang<sup>1</sup>, Christian Wagner<sup>2</sup>, Xingyu Chen<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>York University, Canada, lingjiang2-c@my.cityu.edu.hk <sup>2</sup>City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, wagner@cityu.edu.hk <sup>3</sup>Corresponding author, Shenzhen University, China, celine@szu.edu.cn

## Abstract

Microtask crowdsourcing platforms foster digital assembly lines where microtasks are performed by on-demand microworkers. The sustainability of microtask crowdsourcing platforms and the timely completion of microtask batches hinge on microworkers’ continued participation, but our understanding of microworkers’ continuance remains unclear. Drawing on the theory of the allocation of time and the research on work motivational orientations, we hypothesize that three motivational orientations (i.e., compensation, enjoyment, and microtime structure) are positively associated with microworkers’ perceived relative advantage of microworking, which in turn enhances microworkers’ intent to continue microworking. The relationships between the three motivational orientations and relative advantage are moderated by microworkers’ perceived situational boredom and microwork status (i.e., ad hoc, part-time, and full-time). An online survey on Amazon’s Mechanical Turk provides empirical evidence for the predictive powers, which are contingent on situational boredom, of the three motivational orientations on relative advantage, suggesting that fine-grained microtasks cater to the inherent desire to structure time at a microlevel. We further found that the relationships between motivational orientations and relative advantage differ across ad hoc, part-time, and full-time microworkers. Our study sheds light on theorizing users’ participation by incorporating the time lens and distinguishing heterogeneous effects across temporal contexts and user types. Our findings also provide important practical implications for the design and organization of microwork as well as the governance of the crowdsourcing platform.

Keywords: Microtask Crowdsourcing, Extrinsic-Intrinsic Motivational Orientation, Microtime Structure Orientation, Relative Advantage, Intent to Continue, Situational Boredom

## 1 Introduction

Online labor markets enabled by crowdsourcing platforms are “taking a function once performed by employees and outsourcing it to an undefined (and generally large) network of people in the form of an open call” (Howe, 2006). A variety of crowdsourcing platforms have sprung up in the recent decade. Sites such as Threadless and InnoCentive (Brabham, 2008,

2010) host innovative and challenging projects and offer rewards in the form of substantial amounts of money (Zwass, 2010). In contrast, microtask crowdsourcing platforms such as Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (Ipeirotis, 2010a) and Microworkers (Hirth et al., 2011) foster a paradigm where small and simple tasks are transacted at low pay under a piecerate compensation system (Kittur et al., 2008). Although both InnoCentive and Amazon’s

Mechanical Turk (MTurk) generally fall under crowdsourcing, they feature two distinctive types of crowdsourcing platforms—crowdsourced innovation and microtask crowdsourcing—which differ considerably in the nature of the tasks, the type of workers they target, the amount of compensation they pay, and the mechanisms by which tasks are completed and workers are compensated (Schenk & Guittard, 2011). While “innovative” laborers participate in crowdsourced innovation exchanging professional knowledge and skills with substantial rewards, our study centers on “menial” laborers, namely microworkers, completing low or unskilled work for little compensation (Irani, 2013).

Concerned with microworkers’ unsustainable participation, unpunctual completion of microtask batches, and the considerable controversy over the fairness of pay rates, our study seeks to understand microworkers’ continued participation in microtasks. First, microworkers’ participation follows a long-tailed distribution (Hara et al., 2018), where most of the microtasks are performed by a small group of extremely active microworkers, such that the platform sustainability relies heavily on these microworkers’ continued participation. Although the leading microtask crowdsourcing platforms have attracted a significant number of microworkers, the platforms are subject to high dropout rates and rapid turnover of experienced microworkers, thus threatening their sustainability in the long run (Ma et al., 2018).

Second, microtask crowdsourcing platforms are characterized by a predefined, structured series of microtasks whose outputs can be further used to complete a larger task workflow (Teevan, 2016). Such a workflow forms a virtual digital assembly line consisting of thousands of microtasks, which are always submitted in a batch. As a batch of microtasks approaches completion with fewer microtasks available, its attractiveness decreases and microworkers are less likely to pick up the leftovers. The timely completion of an assembly line pivots on the stable supply of continued participation (Difallah et al., 2014).

Third, microtask workers are typically compensated with small payments (frequently \$0.01 to \$0.10 per task), which raises concerns about the fairness of the microwork pay rate (Deng et al., 2016; Irani & Silberman, 2013). Regarding workers’ participation in microtask crowdsourcing as an economic phenomenon, microtask crowdsourcing platforms primarily trade work for hire without prosocial benefits. In that sense, explanations such as challenge or recognition have yet to serve as critical driving forces, leaving the rationale for the relationship between small payments and microworkers’ continued participation to await in-depth examination.

While the literature has identified both extrinsic and intrinsic motivations as typical antecedents of participation in crowdsourcing (Deng & Joshi, 2016; Kaufmann et al., 2011; Zheng et al., 2011), the unique characteristics of the microtask crowdsourcing work environment (Deng & Joshi, 2016) appear to introduce a third motivational facet: the temporal cost-and-effect rationality for moment-by-moment online behavior. From a deterministic-static view assuming wellestablished and stable preferences (Busemeyer & Townsend, 1993), traditional work motivation theories (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Gagné & Deci, 2005; Steers, et al., 2004) explain workers’ behaviors over a broad time frame (e.g., three months or one year) that is specified in a formal contract. On-demand microtasks, however, allow microworkers to make improvised decisions that do not necessarily complying with the decisionmaking rationale proposed by work motivation theories for salaried employees. For example, the motivation to sign a three-month contract may not necessarily correspond to logging in to MTurk for a ten-minute task; however, monetary rewards play a role in both circumstances. Traditional models of work motivation may be insufficient for explaining micromoment decision-making.

The fit between granular tasks and small blocks of unoccupied time, which has been ignored in the literature, motivates this study’s investigation of the temporal aspect of microworking. Crowdsourced microtasks are at the lowest difficulty level and are fine-grained in time duration (Webster, 2016) such that the IT-enabled task allocation systems of crowdsourcing platforms can efficiently fit microtasks into micromoments with little overhead, thereby bringing temporal structure to discretionary time that might otherwise be occupied by playing games such as Angry Birds or by engaging in an unpaid activity such as surfing the internet (Ipeirotis, 2008; Jiang et al., 2015). On MTurk, for instance, workers perceive microtask completion as a more fruitful way to spend free time than watching TV, playing online games, or idling (Ipeirotis, 2008; Jiang et al., 2015). Although the driver of passing free time productively has been reported in previous studies (Deng & Joshi, 2016; Kaufmann et al., 2011), the literature has devoted little attention to the enabling role of time (e.g., temporal structuring of microtasks) emerging from microtask crowdsourcing.

Apart from the extrinsic and intrinsic motivations, we postulate that the temporal motivation of structuring microtime brings a new perspective and further explains microworkers’ moment-by-moment decisionmaking. In addition to structuring their time at a microlevel, microworkers can engage in microworking to varying degrees at a macrolevel, i.e., microworker status, ranging from ad hoc and part-time to full-time, which can also affect workers’ continued participation.

To obtain a generalizable understanding of the characteristics of participants on microtask crowdsourcing platforms, we investigate individual differences in microworkers’ motivational orientations as antecedents to address two research questions:

1. What motivational orientations affect microworkers’ intent to continue participating in microtasks?

2. How does microworker status affect the relationships between motivational orientation and intent to continue participating?

With our focus on time assigned to microtasking, we ground this study on the theory of the allocation of time (Becker, 1965), a classic theory analyzing household production and the allocation of time within the household, which is in accordance with the discretionary nature of microwork time. Drawing upon the utility maximization rationale underlying the theory of the allocation of time, we identify the relative advantage of microworking over alternatives as the enabler of microworkers’ intent to continue participating and propose microworkers’ motivational orientations as antecedents of their perceptions of relative advantage. Beyond the well-established extrinsic and intrinsic motivational work orientations (Amabile et al., 1994), our study examines microworkers’ inclination to structure small pieces of time moment by moment. In light of prior research on time structure (Bond & Feather, 1988; Feather & Bond, 1983), we introduce microtime structure as a third motivational orientation for microworking. To investigate the nature of the time allocated to microworking at a microlevel, we incorporate situational boredom, i.e., the extent to which microworkers feel bored if they are not participating on microtask crowdsourcing platforms. The relative advantage of microworking is contingent on microworkers’ perceived boredom in specific temporal contexts. At a macrolevel, we distinguish between ad hoc, part-time, and full-time microworkers and compare the differential effects of extrinsic, intrinsic, and temporal motivational orientations on perceived relative advantage. Investigating the time allocated to microworking at both micro- and macrolevels, our research makes theoretical contributions by adding the motivational orientation toward microtime structure to the existing extrinsic-intrinsic orientations and by identifying the dynamics of extrinsic, intrinsic, and temporal motivational orientations for microworkers at varying participation levels.

In Section 2, we review the theory of the allocation of time and the literature on time structure. We then propose our research model and develop corresponding hypotheses in Section 3. Section 4 describes the research method, and Section 5 presents the results of an online survey on MTurk. Finally, we conclude with a discussion of the contributions to research, limitations, and implications of this study.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 Related Work on Users’ Motivations for Online Participation

Microworkers’ participation can be explained not only by well-recognized motivations of online participation in general, but also by factors peculiar to the microwork crowdsourcing context. To provide a comprehensive understanding of what drives people to participate in cyberspace, we review and synthesize a number of representative studies on motivations of users participation in a variety of online contexts (see Appendix A), including the internet in general, online bulletin boards/listervs/panels, firm-hosted user communities, organizational knowledge management systems, communities of practice, online communities, open innovation communities, open source software development, question-and-answer communities, social media sites, crowdsourcing platforms in general, crowdsourcing contest platforms, and microtask crowdsourcing platforms. Our literature synthesis outlines these studies with respect to the research context, participation mode (i.e., voluntary and/or compensated), theoretical lens, dependent variable, motivation factor, and user type. Through integrating the motivations identified in the literature, we compile the major factors into four dimensions: extrinsic, intrinsic, social, and temporal motivation.

Despite the varying labels across studies, the literature commonly identifies certain motivating factors across different research contexts, such as information seeking, financial rewards, knowledge and skill development in the extrinsic dimension; altruism, enjoyment, and fun in the intrinsic dimension; and reputation, peer recognition, and social bonding in the social dimension. Prior studies have also recognized factors pertaining to particular contexts. For instance, career advancement serves as a salient motivation in open source software development (e.g., Krogh et al., 2012; Shah, 2006), whereas commitment plays a key role in sustaining online communities (e.g., Bateman, Gray, & Butler, 2011; Lampe et al., 2010). Regarding on-demand laborers on crowdsourcing platforms, beyond financial rewards as the primary motive, competition (Khasraghi et al., 2020) and security (Deng & Joshi, 2016; Deng et al., 2016) are contextspecific factors driving crowd workers’ participation.

By contrast with the extrinsic, intrinsic, and social dimensions that dominate the research focus in the literature, the temporal dimension has drawn limited attention in only a few studies. With the advent of the internet, the temporal motivation (e.g., passing time) was investigated in Papacharissi and Rubin’s (2000) seminal work on the general motives of computermediated communication (CMC). With the emergence of a variety of enduring online platforms, however, studies on users’ participation became predominately grounded on motivation and social theories (e.g., selfdetermination theory, organizational commitment, social capital theory), leaving the temporal perspective out of theoretical accounts. Nevertheless, people’s attempts to make use of time may have been embedded in the existing motivation theories, such as introjected and identified motivations in self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Gagné & Deci, 2005). It is worth noting that the temporal concern in the literature is predominantly regarded as a backdrop underpinning higher-order motives, yet the role of time as a primary driving force has not been fully recognized. Given the importance of the specification of when things happen (Mitchell & James, 2001), researchers have called for incorporating time as a new lens into theory building (Ancona et al., 2001; George & Jones, 2000). Given that the temporal perspective has largely faded from view, our study brings the time lens back to enrich the understanding of users’ online participation.

## 2.2 Theory of the Allocation of Time

Studies of the economic analysis of time allocation stem from Becker’s (1965) seminal work on household production. Becker proposed “A Theory of the Allocation of Time” to analyze how individuals allocate intrahousehold time to either work or nonwork activities. All activities were conceptualized as commodities and priced according to the value of associated market goods and of time spent. A play, for instance, was considered as a commodity with the (fractional) input of actors, script, theatre costs, and the playgoer’s time to produce the commodity “enjoyment.” Sleeping was also a commodity, with the input of a bed, housing, and the sleeper’s time to produce “rest.” The core assumption of the theory is that households are both producers and consumers. They combine different types of consumption goods and time to produce commodities (e.g., home-cooked meals) during nonwork hours (Chiappori & Lewbel, 2015). All commodities produced by households directly enter the household production function. Through allocating time to different activities, households maximize the overall utility derived from the production functions of chosen activities (i.e., commodities) produced by a set of market goods and time inputs, which are subject to constraints on time and budget. Assuming that the household can convert time into money by allocating less time to consumption and more to paid work, the household utility function eventually encounters a single resource constraint, which is termed as “full income” referring to the income from investing all the time and resources in earning income without any consumption.

Since the market goods and time inputs used in each activity are the primary variables for utility function, the cost of time input plays an important role in maximizing utility. Distinguishing between different types of time (e.g., daytime from nighttime, weekdays from weekends), Becker’s theory emphasizes that the cost of time varies according to the type of time and the household’s foregone earnings associated with that time. Due to the unobservability of the cost of time, a widely adopted approximation is the household’s market wage, which is exogenous to the allocation of time (Chiappori & Lewbel, 2015). According to the traditional labor-leisure division, changes in the market wage of time can result in a substitution between hours worked and leisure time. A rise in market wage signals an increase in forgone earnings, meaning that individuals will probably prefer to allocate more hours to work than leisure. Given a predefined spectrum of activity ranging between income and leisure, Becker’s theory assumes that an individual’s preference for household activity remains constant.

While the theory of the allocation of time offers a theoretical analysis of the choice faced by a household, empirical studies of time allocation in the time-use research have described patterns of time allocation at the activity level across multiple countries (Harvey & Gromo, 1986; Harvey et al., 1984). Through analyzing the data on time allocation in the form of retrospective time diaries, time allocation studies summarize a basic taxonomy of activities: work activities such as market work or household work, personal care such as sleeping or eating, and leisure activities such as playing sports or watching television (Juster & Stafford, 1991). These frequent activities constitute individuals’ repertoires for time allocation.

Following Becker’s household production model, time allocation studies have proposed models to estimate the time demands for different activities such as sleep (Biddle & Hamermesh, 1990) and watching television (Corneo, 2005), plus a variety of other discretionary activities (Yamamoto & Kitamura, 1999). To further distinguish housework from leisure in the nonwork time domain, prior studies formulated a trichotomy of work in the market, work at home (home production), and leisure (Gronau, 1977), the latter two being nonwork activities.

The emergence of work opportunities online blurs the differentiation between work-for-pay and nonwork activity. Crowdsourcing platforms provide access to online labor markets and thus to work away from the regular work environment during nonwork hours. Differences in psychological cognition (e.g., preferences, perceptions, and expectations) between traditional workplaces and online labor markets might affect individuals’ perceptions of the work context and their behaviors in the latter; thus, we examine the allocation of time to microtask crowdsourcing through a psychological lens with a focus on time (Camerer, 1999).

## 2.3 Research on Time Structure

Since people want to have a sense of what lies ahead (Hagestad, 1986), the need for structure is inherent to human nature (Kelvin, 1981). Temporal structuring allows people to plan, organize, segregate, prioritize, and balance activities, and therefore influences all human behaviors and attitudes by setting up background expectancies for a normal life (Zerubavel, 1985). For an unemployed worker, the ability to structure time is the best single predictor of mental health (Hepworth, 1980). Similarly, people are most likely to experience boredom when they are idle (Grubb, 1975). Filling one’s time with interesting activities can reduce the perception of boredom (Vodanovich & Watt, 1999).

However, most individuals have difficulty occupying all of their time in a structured way. Prior qualitative research has found that people’s spare time is characterized by a lack of structure (Kay, 1989). The findings of time-use surveys show that people in many countries have time on their hands that they do not know what to do with (Baxter, 2011). Because of the inherent desire to structure time, difficulty filling discretionary time may bring about disorientation, feelings of helplessness, and problems with mental health (Kielhofner, 1977). Therefore, a variety of coping mechanisms have emerged for the use of unstructured time, such as watching television (Kubey, 1986; Robinson, 1981) and accessing social media (Bolar, 2009; Gilroy-Ware, 2017).

To examine the degree to which individuals use their time in a structured manner, Feather and Bond (1983) proposed the concept of time structure, which they defined as the extent to which individuals perceive their use of time to be structured and purposive. Individuals who score low in time structure have difficulty structuring time in a way that achieves their goals, whereas those who score high in time structure are more likely to occupy their time by engaging in meaningful activities. To operationalize the construct of time structure, Bond and Feather (1988) developed the Time Structure Questionnaire (TSQ), to measure the individual’s sense of time structure. Prior research on time use has investigated the positive effect of time structure on people’s psychological and mental status, such as self-esteem, optimism, and mental health (Bond & Feather, 1988; Hepworth, 1980); on work and life satisfaction (George, 1991); and on individual behaviors, such as Type A behavior (Bond & Feather, 1988; Mudrack, 1999) and performance (Macan et al., 1990). A lack of time structure is associated with a variety of psychological and emotional problems, such as boredom, psychological distress, hopelessness, and depression (Bond & Feather, 1988; Feather & Bond, 1983; Vodanovich & Watt, 1999).

## 3 Research Model and Hypotheses Development

Drawing on the theory of the allocation of time, we identify the relative advantage of microworking as the key antecedent of the intent to continue. In line with prior research on the Work Preference Inventory (Amabile et al., 1994), we include compensation and enjoyment orientation, which are subfactors of extrinsic and intrinsic motivational orientations, respectively, as two antecedents of relative advantage. Based on the research on time structure, microtime structure, which represents the temporal motivational orientation, serves as the third antecedent of relative advantage. The relationships between each of the three motivational orientations and relative advantage are moderated by situational boredom at the microlevel and by microworker status (i.e., ad hoc, part-time, or full-time) at the macrolevel. Taken together, we formulate the research model (see Figure 1) to explain microworkers’ continued participation in online labor markets. The key constructs are described in Table 1. Our outcome construct, microworkers’ intent to continue, is adopted from information systems (IS) continuance studies (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004). We explain the research model and corresponding hypotheses in detail below.

## 3.1 Effect of Relative Advantage on Intent to Continue

According to the theory of the allocation of time (Becker, 1965; Gronau, 1977), a household activity is conceptualized as the production of a commodity through combining time and market goods. The utility of engaging in an activity is a function of market goods and time inputs. The goal of allocating time to a specific activity is to maximize the utility accrued from participating in the activity. Although the theory of the allocation of time is primarily applied to costbased or price-based economic modeling, we assume that the underlying rationale of utility maximization is generalizable to individuals’ decision-making (Schoemaker, 1982). Individuals are thus expected to maximize as much as possible the utility derived from allocating time to a set of possible activities (Simon, 1972). Given a period of time and a set of activities, maximizing the utility of the time allocated to a specific activity becomes a key criterion for people in choosing an activity to participate in. The chosen activity should have an advantage over alternatives to maximize the time value. The utility function proposed by the theory of time allocation suggests that the utility of participation in an activity varies with the costs, such as market goods and time inputs, associated with it.

When the time at people’s disposal is given as a constant, the utility rests primarily on the tradeoff between the input of market goods and the value derived from participating in the activity. Alongside benefiting from the economic value realized in the market, activity participants also perceive the value of participating subjectively, i.e., as creating “psychic income” (Becker, 1965). To maximize the utility of time allocation, participants compare the utility of the focal activity against alternatives according to their perceived value of each activity, and eventually choose the activity with a relative advantage (i.e., the highest utility). The concept of relative advantage has its roots in research on diffusion of innovation and is defined as the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being better than its precursor (Rogers, 1983). IS researchers have used this concept to examine the adoption of IT innovation (Moore & Benbasat, 1991). In this study, relative advantage refers to the extent to which participating in one activity is perceived as being better than participating in alternative activities within given time constraints.

Individuals with firsthand experience in microworking have accumulated realistic perceptions of microwork outcomes. When they can derive more benefit from completing a microtask on a crowdsourcing platform than from alternative activities, the optimal choice is to continue participating. The value of time for an individual in a given situation is conditioned by the activities that are being traded (Cesario, 1976). When microworkers assess the trade-off between participating in crowdsourcing platforms and alternative activities, they choose the activity with a relative advantage. Considering the time spent on microtask crowdsourcing platforms, other activities, such as dealing with household chores or playing online games, might fit into the same time period, but the opportunity to make money from the time allocated to microwork makes it more advantageous than the alternatives and it stands out as the preferred activity. If it were not the preferred activity, participants would probably discontinue participating and switch to another activity with a higher level of utility. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1: Relative advantage is positively related to the intent to continue.

## 3.2 Effects of Extrinsic and Intrinsic Orientation on Relative Advantage

The dichotomy between extrinsic and intrinsic motivation has been well-established in motivation theories (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Gagné & Deci, 2005; Steers et al., 2004). Although individuals’ motivations toward activities have been studied as consequences of the contexts in which individuals engage in those activities, both theoretical statements and empirical evidence have suggested that stable individual differences in motivational orientation also exist (Amabile et al., 1994). Apart from the situational stimuli driving human behaviors, intrinsic-extrinsic motivational orientations, as traitlike individual differences that are stable across time and situations, also determine how people perceive and behave toward their work. For instance, individuals who are intrinsically oriented are more likely to select work they are interested in and perceive enjoyment from performing the work. In contrast, extrinsically motivated people are more likely driven by external incentives, e.g., salary, praise, and reputation.

To investigate individual differences in work orientations, Amabile et al. (1994) developed the Work Preference Inventory, which assesses intrinsic and extrinsic motivational orientations as two primary scales, each of which is broken down into two subfactors. The two components of extrinsic motivational orientation involve compensation and outward (oriented toward the recognition and the dictates of others), whereas the subscales for intrinsic motivational orientation refer to enjoyment and challenge. Given that microtasks are simple and participation is anonymous, microworking lacks challenge and recognition. Therefore, our study examines compensation and enjoyment as extrinsic and intrinsic motivational orientations, respectively.

Completing tasks that are compensated by money is typical in the workplace. Microworkers who are extrinsically oriented toward compensation are more likely to acknowledge the benefit of microworking in terms of money (Durward et al., 2020). Additionally, completing microtasks can also produce enjoyment (Deng & Joshi, 2016). For instance, completing a task that is designed as a game or a puzzle is quite similar to playing a computer game (De Benetti & Miettinen, 2012). Such enjoyment derived from completing microtasks on crowdsourcing platforms reflects the orientation of intrinsically motivated microworkers. Taken together, microworkers’ extrinsic and intrinsic motivational orientations govern their perceptions of the relative advantages of microworking. Compared with possible alternatives that may also fit into the time slot, monetary rewards and potential enjoyment give microwork a relative advantage among available activities. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H2: Compensation orientation is positively related to relative advantage.

H3: Enjoyment orientation is positively related to relative advantage.

## 3.3 Effect of Microtime Structure Orientation on Relative Advantage

Although users’ online participation can be driven by overarching extrinsic and intrinsic motivations, activities on a particular platform may bear unique characteristics that result in context-specific driving forces. Both general and specific factors can contribute to a full understanding of users’ participation. Contrasting with the disproportionate amount of scholarship focusing on well-established extrinsic and intrinsic dimensions, the temporal dimension, initially taken into account as one of the CMC motives (Papacharissi & Rubin, 2000), lacks in-depth investigation in the literature (see Appendix A). Delving into the role of time in driving users’ continued participation, our study situates users inherent desire for time structure at a microlevel in the context of performing microtasks in the crowdsourcing market.

In light of the positive latent consequence of employment through imposing a time structure on the working day (Jahoda, 1981), we posit that microwork introduces a microtime structure for discretionary time. Drawing upon previous research on time structure (Bond & Feather, 1988; Feather & Bond, 1983), the concept of microtime structure uncovers an understudied reason for participating in microtask crowdsourcing. Prior research defines “time structure” as the extent to which individuals perceive their use of time to be structured and purposive (Bond & Feather, 1988; Feather & Bond, 1983), with a focus on time at a macrolevel. We adapt the concept of time structure to the microlevel from a motivational orientation perspective, i.e., a microtime structure orientation, which refers to the extent to which individuals tend to make use of small blocks of time in a structured and purposive way.

Microtime structure serves as a third motivational orientation (coupled with extrinsic and intrinsic orientations), aligning with the temporal flexibility of microworking. People have an inherent desire to occupy unstructured time, especially time in small blocks, with activities (Kubey, 1986; Robinson, 1981). Instead of simply passing the time, people with strong microtime structure orientation prefer to engage in an activity if the activity can fit into the time slot, especially when the time is more specific and more fine-grained (e.g., several minutes during a coffee break) than general time periods (e.g., a lifetime or a day).

Microtasks on crowdsourcing platforms are characterized by small granularity (Hoßfeld et al., 2011). Task completion requires little time (e.g., minutes) and pays a small amount of money (e.g., cents). Microworkers freely select tasks and complete them within short intervals, which offers a means to fill short intervals of time with something meaningful or enjoyable (Ipeirotis, 2008). Compared with working in offline labor markets that involve explicit costs such as commuting time and transportation costs, participation in microtasks involves less overhead, thereby offering a more convenient choice for occupying discretionary time. The marginal overhead required to enter a microtask crowdsourcing platform and the flexibility in the duration of available microtasks create opportunities to structure unoccupied time on a moment-by-moment basis, allowing microworkers to organize small time slots in a purposive way.

The construct of microtime structure orientation proposed in this study captures this unique propensity toward time allocation enabled by participation in microtask crowdsourcing. Instead of regarding the best alternative as a constantly recognizable choice, Svenson (1992) assumes that a decision involves the selection and creation of a candidate that is sufficiently superior. When a small piece of time is unexpectedly available, the decision to allocate the time to an activity hinges on the specific context. To avoid going through a time-consuming and effortful cognitive process, individuals likely make trade-offs between maximizing utility and filling the void, thereby choosing a superior action intuitively on a moment-by-moment basis. Although many other activities may also fit into micromoments, e.g., replying to emails, running an errand, or connecting with friends and family, microworkers benefit from allocating microtime to microtasks by a virtue of being plugged into and unplugged from the ambient technology autonomously and effortlessly, thus rendering microworking more advantageous than alternatives on a moment-bymoment basis. For those who are motivationally oriented toward filling discretionary time with meaningful activities on a moment-by-moment basis, the productive use of microtime through microworking may stand out as a salient advantage. Thus, we hypothesize:

H4: Microtime structure orientation is positively related to relative advantage.

## 3.4 Moderations of Situational Boredom

Apart from focusing on microworkers’ motivational orientations across situations, we adopt a time allocation perspective and examine the situational factor to better understand the effects of motivational orientations on relative advantage. According to the theory of time allocation, individuals allocate a specific time slot to an activity that can maximize its utility, if such an activity is available. Thus, the question turns to explaining the circumstances under which microwork maximizes the utility of the microtime. When people’s spare time is fragmented into small segments, such as a few minutes, the time constraint narrows the choice of activities that fit. Because the time spent on microtasks is so short, microworking may be the only choice, other than idleness, to occupy short time intervals, and lack of activity can lead to boredom. We therefore examine microworking through microworkers’ perceptions of boredom if they do not perform microwork, namely situational boredom.

Given the discretionary nature of the time spent on microworking, we base the concept of situational boredom on the research on perceptions of boredom in leisure (Iso-Ahola & Weissinger, 1987, 1990), which defines leisure boredom as “the subjective perception that available leisure experiences are not sufficient to instrumentally satisfy needs for optimal arousal” (Iso-Ahola & Weissinger, 1990, p. 4). Adapting the definition from leisure in general to the time allocated to microwork, our study defines situational boredom as the extent to which individuals perceive boredom if they are not performing microtasks. While leisure boredom describes a negative state of mind caused by two conflicting perceptions in leisure (i.e., too much time available versus too little to do), situational boredom focuses on the perceived unavailability of other activities for a specific time period, which is then filled with microwork (Hahn et al., 2019). With stronger feelings of boredom in a given time slot, microworkers will perceive more relative advantage from performing microwork. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H5: Situational boredom is positively related to relative advantage.

Situational boredom also reflects microworkers moods when they are engaged with microtasks (Qiu et al., 2020; Zhuang & Gadiraju, 2019), which may influence how they assess the relative advantage of microworking. When individuals perceive a high level of boredom, their unsatisfied need for optimal arousal causes the desire to “have something to do.” A microtask can easily attract the participation of such individuals because they would be likely to perceive the relative advantage of “doing something practical” over “having nothing to do.” The benefit of relieving boredom (e.g., “having something to do”) likely outweighs the monetary rewards in such cases, which then becomes an extra bonus rather than a necessary incentive. Therefore, we predict that the effect of compensation orientation on relative advantage is weakened by situational boredom.

In contrast, to cope with situational boredom, a microworker’s craving for internal arousal likely increases. An activity producing arousal (i.e., enjoyment) yields a relative advantage by filling the void with positive emotion. If a microworker is motivationally oriented toward enjoyment, situational boredom makes the relative advantage of arousal offered by microworking more understandable. In that case, the effect of intrinsic motivational orientation toward enjoyment on relative advantage would be strengthened in boring situations (Feyisetan & Simperl, 2019). Therefore, we hypothesize:

H6a: Situational boredom negatively moderates the relationship between compensation orientation and relative advantage, such that the relationship is weaker when situational boredom is high rather than low.

H6b: Situational boredom positively moderates the relationship between enjoyment orientation and relative advantage, such that the relationship is stronger when situational boredom is high rather than low.

The relationship between microtime structure orientation and relative advantage is also a function of situational boredom. Situational boredom occurs in specific temporal contexts, which are also the settings for microworkers’ motivational orientation toward microtime structure. When microworkers experience boredom in a specific time interval, those who are motivated to structure the time purposively are more likely to appreciate the option of microwork (Cai, 2017). In the presence of the perception of boredom, microwork appeals to individuals with an inherent orientation toward microtime structure, thus yielding a relative advantage over passing the time aimlessly. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H6c: Situational boredom positively moderates the relationship between microtime structure orientation and relative advantage, such that the relationship is stronger when situational boredom is high rather than low.

## 3.5 Moderations of Microworker Status

Our literature synthesis in terms of user type (see Appendix A) suggests that users’ participation patterns at a macrolevel also indicate different motivation mechanisms. Underlying the different labels of user type across studies are some frequently identified user types, such as heavy versus light users (Leung, 2003; Roy, 2009) and hobbyists versus professionals (Jeppesen & Frederiksen, 2006; Shah, 2006). A few studies have found empirical evidence for the differential effects of motivations across user types (e.g., Lai, 2019; Lampe et al., 2010; Ye & Kankanhalli, 2017), signaling the necessity of accounting for user type to understand the heterogeneity of users’ motivations. In the microtask crowdsourcing context, our study distinguishes user types by examining microworker status as an equivalent to employment status in the traditional workplace.

Although there is no formal definition of employment status on microtask crowdsourcing platforms, individuals’ varying levels of microwork indirectly signal their status. We identify three types of microworker status based on participation levels: ad hoc, part-time, and full-time. Ad hoc microworkers participate on an impromptu basis, whereas full-time microworkers treat microwork as a full-time job and devote a substantial amount of time to it. Between ad hoc and full-time microworkers are part-time microworkers, who participate to a moderate extent. Participants self-select their microworker status, which indicates their pattern of participation in microwork. These patterns influence the way that they perceive the relative advantages of microworking.

The time that ad hoc microworkers spend is more transitory, compared with part-time and full-time microworkers. When the time at their disposal is on an ad hoc basis, it is difficult for individuals to organize this time productively (Kielhofner, 1977). For instance, filling ten minutes with meaningful activities seems more spontaneous and difficult than filling half a day. Since the difficulties in structuring time are related to its duration, the benefit from overcoming that difficulty varies with the duration of the time. As the difficulty of occupying time moment by moment increases, the benefit of structuring that time increases. If microworkers have limited time available, their perceived relative advantage of microworking is more strongly related to their motivational orientation toward microtime structure and their perception of situational boredom, compared to part-time and fulltime microworkers. Thus, we hypothesize:

H7a: The relationship between microtime structure orientation and relative advantage is stronger for ad hoc microworkers than for part-time and fulltime microworkers.

H7b: The relationship between situational boredom and relative advantage is stronger for ad hoc microworkers than for part-time and full-time microworkers.

Microworkers may be predominantly motivated by extrinsic compensation across all three types of microworker status. Some microworkers may view microwork as their primary means of earning a living (Brawley & Pury, 2016) and some microworkers may treat microwork like a job with a regular work schedule. However, viewing microwork as a job (e.g., full-time status) is not necessarily synonymous with viewing it as one’s primary source of income (Keith et al., 2019). The findings of the existing research (Keith et al., 2019) show that those who regard microwork as a job are more likely to be sustained by intrinsic enjoyment. In particular, if microworkers cultivate a quasi-employment relationship with a crowdsourcing platform, they become less sensitive to monetary rewards even in face of wage cuts (Chen & Horton, 2016), suggesting a weaker orientation toward compensation. Additionally, the considerable amount of discretionary time available for full-time microworkers renders the need to structure microtime less urgent. Since full-time microworkers are less likely to attend to extrinsic and temporal motivations, orientation toward intrinsic motivations is more likely to play a paramount role. Therefore, compared with ad hoc and part-time microworkers, the enjoyment orientation is likely to exert a stronger effect on relative advantage for full-time microworkers. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H7c: The relationship between enjoyment orientation and relative advantage is stronger for full-time microworkers than for ad hoc and part-time microworkers.

## 4 Research Method

## 4.1 Measurements

Our research model comprises seven constructs: compensation orientation, enjoyment orientation, microtime structure orientation, situational boredom, relative advantage, intent to continue, and microworker status. Microworker status was measured by the self-reported participation level (i.e., the number of hours spent on microworking per week). Referring to the weekly work hours in the traditional workplace, we classified microworkers who spent fewer than 10 hours on microwork per week as ad hoc, those ranging between 10 and 20 hours per week as part-time, and those microworking more than 20 hours per week as full-time microworkers.

Except for microworker status, a 7-point Likert scale ranging between strongly disagree and strongly agree was used to measure the constructs through multiitems (see Appendix B). Specifically, compensation and enjoyment orientation were measured by the subscales of the Work Preference Inventory developed by Amabile et al. (1994). We measured microtime structure using the time structure instrument TSQ (Bond & Feather, 1988; Feather & Bond, 1983) by anchoring time at a fine-grained level. Relative advantage was measured by an instrument established in prior studies (Moore & Benbasat, 1991), adjusting the context from IT innovation adoption to microtask crowdsourcing participation. Items measuring situational boredom were adopted from a leisure boredom scale (Iso-Ahola & Weissinger, 1987, 1990) and anchored to the specific context of microwork. Intent to continue was measured by items widely used in IS continuance studies (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004).

In addition, we measured several control variables, including demographics (i.e., gender, age, employment status, education), microwork tenure (i.e., how long they had been microworking on MTurk), microwork intensity (i.e., number of human intelligence tasks completed on a weekly basis), microwork granularity (i.e., minutes on a session basis and on a microtask basis), and microwork earning (i.e., hourly microwork income and microwork pay satisfaction).

## 4.2 Data Collection

To test the proposed research model, we surveyed workers on the highly popular Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (MTurk) microtask crowdsourcing platform (https://www.mturk.com/mturk/). On MTurk, socalled requesters publish “human intelligence tasks” (HITs) to potential workers. HITs are generally small and simple in nature, such as tagging objects in an image, assessing the relevance of documents, or transcribing podcast clips. Workers choose freely from HITs available at any point in time and complete a HIT in exchange for a small payment (Ipeirotis, 2010a).

With the growing popularity of conducting surveys and experiments on crowdsourcing platforms, numerous studies have evaluated the quality of data collected through these sources. Their findings provide empirical support for the validity and reliability of online labor portals for scientific research (Behrend et al., 2011; Sheehan, 2017). The MTurk crowdsourcing marketplace in particular has been analyzed in several prior studies (Ipeirotis, 2008, 2010a; Kaufmann et al., 2011; Ross et al., 2010). Given its popularity and recognition as a useful research testbed, we chose MTurk as our target platform and distributed the survey as a HIT to MTurk workers.

Concerned with the potential bias that a HIT in a survey format is most likely to recruit workers who prefer completing surveys to other task types, we collected the data sample through two separate rounds of surveys. The first round targeted workers who rarely or never complete surveys (termed as “non-surveytakers”), whereas the second round targeted workers who frequently completed surveys (termed as “surveytakers”). We specified this preference in worker background within the HIT description for each survey round. We also filtered out workers who appeared to misrepresent themselves by asking a qualifying question about the specific type of tasks workers most frequently completed. Workers answered the qualifying question by selecting one option from a list of task types defined by MTurk. In the round targeting non-survey-takers, a worker who selected “survey” as the task type most frequently completed was immediately eliminated. Similarly, in the round for survey-takers only, workers who selected an option other than “survey” as their most frequent task type were immediately eliminated as well.

The survey was administered as a HIT posted on MTurk in March 2014. While 541 workers clicked the link for our survey, 389 workers passed the qualifying question. Of these, 54 workers did not submit the survey and 44 workers submitted incomplete responses. Among the 291 completed responses, 31 responses with outliers on four variables (i.e., weekly microwork hours, weekly microwork HITs, session duration, and weekly microtask number) were excluded using the interquartile range (IRQ) method (Rousseeuw & Croux, 1993). The final sample contained 260 respondents (see Appendix C). The considerable difference in the number of total clicks between the two survey rounds (316 versus 225) shows that more survey-takers tried to represent themselves as non-survey-takers, than the opposite. The fact that 35% of participants (111 of 316) failed the qualifying question targeting non-survey-takers in the first round versus 18.2% failing in the second survey round indicates that without the qualifying question the entire sample would have been skewed toward survey-takers.

## 5 Data Analysis and Results

## 5.1 Sample

Sixty-five percent of the respondents were male, 50% were between 20 and 29 years old, 65% had an education level of a bachelor’s degree or higher, and 54% were fully employed (see the sample demographics in Table 2). In line with the demographics reported in prior MTurk research studies (Ipeirotis, 2010b; Ross et al., 2010), many of our respondents were highly educated, fully employed, young workers. We also asked questions to solicit microworkers’ self-reported participation intensity on MTurk and microwork pay satisfaction (on a 7-point

Likert scale). As the descriptive statistics of these behavioral variables shown in Table 3 indicate, our sample of microworkers covers a wide range of participation levels.

## 5.2 Measurement Model

## 5.2.1 Construct Validity and Reliability

We assessed the multi-item constructs in terms of convergent validity, discriminant validity, and reliability. As Table 4 shows, all average variance extracted (AVE) values exceed 0.5 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981), and all composite reliability (CR) values are greater than 0.6 (Bagozzi & Yi, 1988), indicating convergent validity and reliability. Table 4 also presents the descriptive statistics and intercorrelations for all research variables. The latent variable correlations confirm discriminant validity as well, since the square root of AVE for each construct exceeds any interconstruct correlations (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). The factor loadings and cross-loadings calculated from the confirmatory factor analysis (see Appendix D) also exhibit adequate discriminant and convergent validity, in that each factor loading on its own construct is significantly higher than the crossloadings between factors (Chin, 1998). To further test the potential multicollinearity, we found that all the variance inflation factor (VIF) values (ranging from 1.091 to 1.691) are less than 3.3 (Diamantopoulos & Siguaw, 2006). These metrics suggest that the psychometric properties of all constructs are within acceptable boundaries.

## 5.2.2 Common Method Bias Test

Since the survey measured constructs using selfreported items measured on 7-point Likert scales, we tested for possible common method bias using Harman’s single factor test (Harman, 1976; Podsakoff & Organ, 1986) and the common method variance factor test (Podsakoff et al., 2003). According to Harman’s single factor test, common method bias is present, when a single factor accounts for more than 50% of the variance of all constructs (Harman, 1976). We performed a principal component factor analysis including the items of all constructs (Podsakoff & Organ, 1986). The total variance explained by any single factor is 35%, which indicates no excessive common method bias. Second, we included a common method factor in the research model and calculated each indicator’s variances explained by its principal construct and by the method factor (Podsakoff et al., 2003). The average variance of indicators explained by the construct is 0.712, while the average variance explained by the method is 0.004 (see Appendix E), which suggests that common method bias is not a serious concern for this study.

## 5.3 Structural Model

We tested the hypotheses by formulating a structural equation model and running the model in SmartPLS 3.0. The results of the partial least squares (PLS) model are shown in Figure 2. According to these results, intent to continue is significantly associated with relative advantage $( \beta = 0 . 5 8 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , which is significantly associated with compensation orientation $( \beta = 0 . 4 8 8 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 ) .$ enjoyment orientation $( \beta = 0 . 1 6 4 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , microtime structure orientation $( \beta = 0 . 1 1 0 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , and situational boredom $( \beta = 0 . 2 0 4 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , supporting H1, H2, H3, H4, and H5. Our results indicate that situational boredom negatively moderates the relationship between compensation orientation and relative advantage $( \beta = -$ $0 . 1 8 5 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and positively moderates the relationship between microtime structure and relative advantage $( \beta =$ $0 . 1 1 2 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , while the interactive effect of situational boredom and enjoyment orientation on relative advantage is not significant. Thus, H6a and H6c are supported, but H6b is not. Among the control variables, our results show that microwork pay satisfaction $( \beta = 0 . 1 1 3 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ has a significant effect on intent to continue. Overall, a total of 52.7% (R<sup>2</sup> = 0.527) of the variance of relative advantage and 43.0% $( \mathbf { R } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 4 3 0 )$ of the variance of intent to continue are explained by the variables.

To test the moderating effects of microworker status, we conducted a multigroup analysis of the research model for ad hoc, part-time, and full-time microworkers, respectively. We classified the full sample into three subsamples in terms of weekly hours spent on MTurk: ad hoc, part-time, or full-time microworkers (see Table 5). On average, ad hoc microworkers spent 6.27 hours, parttime microworkers spent 16.7 hours, and full-time microworkers spent 33.99 hours per week. To examine whether the constructs in the research model vary across the ad hoc, part-time, and full-time groups, we conducted a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test in conjunction with the post hoc analysis using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (Tukey’s HSD) Test (Kramer, 1956), which allows for unequal group sizes in the sample. The results in Table 6 show that compensation orientation is significantly higher for part-time and fulltime microworkers than for ad hoc microworkers, while enjoyment and microtime structure orientation do not differ across the three groups. Full-time microworkers have higher levels of situational boredom than the other two groups, whereas ad hoc microworkers have lower level of relative advantage than the other two groups. With respect to intent to continue, we found a significant difference between ad hoc and full-time microworker groups.

We then evaluated the research model using these three subsamples, respectively. The results in Figure 3 and Table 7 show that the relationship between compensation orientation and relative advantage remained significant for the three groups. The effect of enjoyment orientation on relative advantage is significant for full-time microworkers only $( \beta = 0 . 3 1 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , whereas the effect of microtime structure on relative advantage is significant for ad hoc microworkers only $( \beta = 0 . 2 7 5 , p <$ 0.01), lending support to H7a and H7c. With respect to situational boredom, the effect is significant for ad hoc (β $= 0 . 2 1 7 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and part-time $( \beta = 0 . 2 2 1 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ microworkers, but not for full-time microworkers, partially supporting H7b.

Given the range of tasks on MTurk and microworkers’ corresponding task specialization, our survey-HIT may have attracted a specific group of respondents—those who were interested in filling out surveys—although the full sample covered the community of HIT-workers that complete all kinds of tasks (see the “Most frequent HIT $\mathrm { t y p e } ^ { \mathrm { , } \mathrm { , } }$ in Table 2). We performed a t-test to compare the latent values of the constructs between the non-surveytakers (first round) and the survey-takers (second round) in our sample. Other than the difference in situational boredom, there was no significant difference in means of the latent values between the two groups (see Appendix F). As the standard deviations suggest, the distributions of the latent values for survey-takers and non-surveytakers follow similar patterns. We also tested H1, H2, H3, H4, and H5, as well as the effects of control variables for survey-takers and non-survey-takers, respectively (see Appendix F). The results show that while H1, H2, and H3 are significant for both survey-takers and nonsurvey-takers, H4 is significant for non-survey-takers only and H5 is significant for survey-takers only. The impacts of age, education, and microwork pay satisfaction are significant for non-survey-takers. Therefore, our sample, which included both surveytakers and non-survey-takers, avoided the potential sampling bias of survey-taker dominance.

## 6 Discussion

## 6.1 Key Findings

The results, summarized in Table 8, provide evidence that microworkers’ motivational orientations in terms of compensation, enjoyment, and microtime structure have a positive impact on their perception of the relative advantage of microworking, which enhances their intention to continue participating. Apart from the wellestablished extrinsic-intrinsic motivational orientation, microtime structure orientation lends support to microworkers’ temporal rationale in evaluating the relative advantage of microworking. In that sense, microwork serves as a device for intentionally structuring granular time to produce superior utility over alternatives. The predictive power of microtime structure orientation on the relative advantage of microworking reflects the importance of the innate human desire to make use of time in moment-by-moment decision-making.

Taking into account the specific temporal context, microworkers’ perception of situational boredom also significantly enhances their perceived relative advantage of microworking. Situational boredom, on the one hand, weakens the relationship between compensation orientation and relative advantage; on the other hand, it strengthens the relationship between microtime structure orientation and relative advantage. While motivational orientations represent stable individual differences across time and situations, situational boredom enriches our understanding about how motivational orientations interact with the specific context of microworking. Exposed to a feeling of boredom, individuals’ desire for compensation is crowded out by the need to cope with the boredom. However, we did not find that situational boredom moderated the relationship between the enjoyment orientation and relative advantage. One possible explanation is that individuals may be motivated by other intrinsic orientations (e.g., knowledge and skills) instead of enjoyment.

Looking at participation intensity at a macrolevel, we found differences in both variables and relationships across ad hoc, part-time, and full-time microworkers. The differences across the three microworker groups (see Table 6) suggest that microworkers’ continued participation is partly related to full-time microworking, i.e., a microwork pattern equivalent to full-time status in the traditional workplace. The ad hoc and full-time groups, as two ends of the spectrum of microworker status, differ in compensation orientation, situational boredom, and relative advantage, thereby illuminating the heterogeneity across microworker types in terms of motivational orientation, temporal context, and subjective evaluation of microworking.

With respect to structural relationships, whereas ad hoc microworkers are more likely to be motivated by structuring microtime and avoiding boredom, full-time microworkers are more likely to be oriented toward enjoyment. Indeed, the relationship between enjoyment orientation and relative advantage was not significant in the ad hoc and part-time subsamples and the relationship between microtime structure orientation and relative advantage was not significant in the part-time and full-time subsamples. As the proposed hypotheses varied with the status of microworker, our study suggests that it would be worthwhile to delve deeper into different groups of microworkers to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the microtask labor market.

With the exception of microwork pay satisfaction, the control variables had no significant effect on intent to continue for the full sample. The relationship between hourly microwork income and intent to continue was not significant, indicating that the actual microwork’s pay rate exercises little influence on microworkers’ continuance decisions. However, microwork pay satisfaction had a significant effect on intent to continue for the part-time subsample (β = 0.224, p < 0.05), showing that microworkers’ affective response to microwork pay plays a role in continued participation, in addition to relative advantage. However, the relationship was not significant for ad hoc and full-time microworkers. A possible explanation is that, under the circumstances of either ad hoc microworkers’ casual participation or full-time microworkers’ active engagement, microworkers may either be indifferent to microwork pay or routinize the practice of microworking. In this regard, microworkers at the opposite ends of the participatory spectrum are less likely to be affected by microwork pay, decreasing the effect of microwork pay satisfaction on intent to continue. Age had a significant effect on intent to continue for the part-time subsample only, suggesting that older part-time microworkers are more likely to continue microworking.

Two control variables, education level $( \beta = - 0 . 1 5 7 , p <$ 0.05) and employment status $( \beta = - 0 . 1 6 2 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ exhibited significant negative effects on ad hoc microworkers’ intent to continue (see Table 7). The results suggest that ad hoc microworkers who are highly educated or fully employed in the traditional workplace are less likely to continue microworking. However, such relationships, which are arguably reasonable, were not significant among part-time and full-time microworkers, indicating that different mentalities regulate microworkers’ decision-making and behavioral patterns. Ad hoc microworkers may regard microwork as a nonessential activity that should meet certain expectations and if expectations are not met, they can easily discontinue their participation. Part-time and full-time microworkers, in contrast, are more engaged with microwork and take it more seriously, such that their internal intention takes over regardless of their education level and offline employment.

## 6.2 Contributions to Theory

This study makes several theoretical contributions. First, our study addresses the neglected view of time at the microlevel—i.e., we introduce microtime structure orientation as a new theoretical lens to explicate microworkers’ continued participation. The relationships between the three motivational orientations and relative advantage connect the macrolevel decision-making driven by extrinsicintrinsic orientation with the microlevel decisionmaking contingent on activity context and user type. At the core of time structure is individuals’ inherent desire to organize unoccupied time. Our study reconceptualizes time structure by focusing on the orientation to allocate time on a moment-by-moment basis and explains how this microtime structure orientation affects the perceived utility of time. Microtime structure captures microworkers’ proactive desire to make use of granular time, which differs from the passive use of free time identified in the literature (e.g., Brabham, 2012; Lai, 2019; Papacharissi & Rubin,

2000). The advantage of allocating microtime to microworking depends not only on microworkers orientation toward making money, pursuing enjoyment, and making use of time, but also on the temporal context, e.g., as a coping strategy to deal with a boring time slot. In addition, our study identifies the heterogenous effects of extrinsic, intrinsic, and temporal motivational orientations across distinctive user types (e.g., ad hoc, part-time, and full-time microworkers). While compensation orientation spills over into all three types of microworker, enjoyment orientation sustains full-time microworkers whereas microtime structure orientation is salient for ad hoc microworkers.

Second, our study advances the understanding of users participation beyond the context of crowdsourcing, given the generalizable nature of the constructs in our research model. The three motivational orientations (i.e., stable and enduring individual traits across contexts), coupled with relative advantage as a widely used decision-making rationale, render our findings generalizable to users’ participation beyond crowdsourcing. As ubiquitous access to the internet continues to expand the repertoire of available activities, users’ inclinations to make use of granular periods of time will increasingly take a salient role in driving online participation rather than providing a backdrop for it. The moderations of situational boredom and microworker status demonstrate the nuanced motivational mechanisms by which users’ inherent orientations interact with external contexts. Taken together, placing users’ inherent orientations within the temporal context and distinguishing user types in theorizing users’ motivations for online behaviors are worthwhile endeavors.

Third, our study informs the relaxation of certain assumptions in Becker’s theory of the allocation of time. The influence of microtime structure orientation on relative advantage suggests that the cost of time, which Becker’s theory assumes to be exogenous to the allocation of time (e.g., market wage as an approximation), could also be affected endogenously— for instance, through microtime structure orientation in the context of our study. The cost of time is likely higher for people who are more motivationally oriented toward microtime structure. Meanwhile, microtasks could also relax the temporal constraints neglected in Becker’s theory. In particular, when time is characterized by boredom, microworkers may be motivated by the desire to both make money and make good use of time. As a means of coping with boredom, microworking transforms boring time segments, which may not be traditionally counted as a disposable resource, into time of value, thus making the immutable fixed amount of time budget malleable and blurring the traditional laborleisure division. Relaxing these assumptions enables rethinking how the ad hoc utility of time spent on microtasks affects people’s decisions to allocate time on a moment-by-moment basis. We thus incorporate the temporal context into the utility evaluation to emphasize the necessity of theorizing about temporal characteristics to advance our understanding of individuals’ decision-making (George & Jones, 2000; Mitchell & James, 2001).

## 6.3 Implications for Practice

This study also has significant implications for practice. Our findings suggest that complex tasks could be decomposed into small, simple, crowdsourceable activities that fit people’s motivational orientations toward making money, pursuing enjoyment, and structuring fragmented, unoccupied time. This paradigm would dramatically change the granularity and organization of work on the internet by splitting jobs into short, low-paying microtasks that could be rapidly completed by microworkers (Hoßfeld et al., 2011). Some studies have proposed strategies to make the splitting up and recombining of complex human computation tasks implementable (Kittur et al., 2011).

Aside from offering monetary rewards, microwork providers could cater to people’s desire to structure microtime by designing and framing microtasks to be more meaningful and enjoyable (Kaufmann et al., 2011; Zheng et al., 2011). To fulfill microworkers’ temporal (time structure) and intrinsic (enjoyment) motivational orientations, tasks should not only be microstructured but also gamified to increase their appeal (De Benetti & Miettinen, 2012). Then, instead of launching Angry Birds, people might quickly pick up a ten-minute task from a crowdsourcing platform, earn ten cents and thereby also raise their score in the “most money earned” list on that platform. Such behavior would be similar to that of casual game players described by Anderson (2012).

The temporal flexibility and granularity of microtasks on crowdsourcing platforms could enhance the versatility of an individual’s discretionary time, especially concerning small blocks of time, which differ in terms of the range of activities that can be completed within them (McGrath & Kelly, 1992) as well as in the underlying value that individuals attach to them. The task duration and difficulty dictate in part the possible temporal contexts. For instance, completing a short survey can be completed during a coffee break for fun, whereas transcribing a twominute audio segment takes more time and effort, meaning that microworkers would probably perform the task after dinner as side work. Understanding the varied temporal contexts, which are contingent on the nature of the task and differ in terms time value, could shed light on the best time to publish tasks and how much to pay for tasks. Therefore, it would be worthwhile for task providers to estimate the potential temporal contexts for a given task, choose the appropriate publishing time for target workers, and price the task in consideration of highly likely temporal contexts accordingly.

Furthermore, ad hoc and full-time microworkers’ different motivational orientations suggest that task providers should design microtasks with different characteristics to attract different types of microworkers. Tiny, bite-size tasks may easily attract ad hoc microworkers, whereas time-consuming but interesting tasks may draw the attention of full-time microworkers. In particular, if task providers intend to retain experienced full-time microworkers, integrating intrinsic benefits into microworking may be a promising strategy.

Clearly, how microtask providers decompose, organize, and especially reward online work can fundamentally impact employment and the economic welfare of workers: outsourcing takes tasks away from regular employees and provides microworkers with subminimum wages. Such challenges must be recognized and addressed by reexamining the governance structure of platforms, particularly given the rapid growth of microtask crowdsourcing as an economic activity.

## 6.4 Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the generalizability of the results is limited by the collection of data from a single site (i.e., MTurk). We anchor the research context to microtask crowdsourcing platforms, and make no claims regarding other crowdsourcing models, such as crowdsourced innovation. Second, we investigate microtime structure by revising the conceptualization and operationalization of time structure in the literature. The validity and reliability of microtime structure should be verified in future studies. Third, given that the proposed research model explains 52.7% of the variance of relative advantage and 43.0% of the variance of intent to continue, there must be other factors explaining the remaining portions. Understanding these other factors would help to generate a more comprehensive explanation of microworkers’ attitudes and behaviors toward microtask crowdsourcing.

## 7 Conclusion

Revealing the mechanisms of microworkers’ continued participation is crucial if microtask crowdsourcing platforms are to maintain long-term development, ensure timely completion of microtask batches, and understand the rationale underneath microworkers’ acceptance of micropayments. Our findings explain the circumstances under which microworkers with different motivational orientations continue participating on microtask crowdsourcing platforms. Our conclusion is that, among the activities available for a brief, unoccupied time period, participation in microworking offers a superior choice for many users that caters to microworkers’ motivational orientation toward compensation, enjoyment, and microtime structure on a moment-bymoment basis. Thus, microworkers’ willingness to continue participation can be rationalized as the result of utility maximization situated in specific contexts. The extent to which microworkers are concerned about monetary rewards is contingent on their motivational orientation and on situational characteristics. Such a time-centric decision-making rationale is applicable to users’ online participation beyond crowdsourcing.

In practical terms, as competition for qualified microworkers increases, tasks will increasingly be reformulated as easy, lightly structured, leisure activities with economic, knowledge, and hedonic benefits. Hence, we expect the gamification of online work at a fine-grained level. Given the importance of microtime structure, crowdsourcing platforms should seek to enable even better uses of microworkers’ small segments of discretionary time. A likely design outcome would be mobile device-friendly platforms (Chatzimilioudis et al., 2012) to attract people with highly fragmented time that would otherwise be spent idle or playing casual games, e.g., during transit. In the same vein, we also expect to see attempts to better structure tasks, improve task search, and reduce microtask platform participation overhead in order to fit microtasks even better into small segments of available time.

The driving forces of compensation, enjoyment, and microtime structure orientation across ad hoc, parttime, and full-time microworkers shed light on substantial heterogeneity in terms of subjective interpretations of microworking, as well as on the time allocation mechanism of users’ online participation in general. We expect microtask crowdsourcing to initiate a significant rethinking of online participation at a microlevel and hope this study contributes to this reconceptualization and theorization. We also anticipate significant changes in the practice of online activity design and in the governance of users participation.

## About the Authors

Ling Jiang is an assistant professor in the School of Information Technology at York University. She received her PhD in information systems from the College of Business at the City University of Hong Kong. Her research interests focus on online peer-production communities and crowdsourcing markets. In particular, she explores core contributors withdrawal from online communities and crowd workers’ continuous participation in microtask crowdsourcing platforms. Her research has been published in Journal of the Association for Information Science and Technology, Internet Research, and in the proceedings of several leading IS conferences.

Christian Wagner is the chair professor of social media in the School of Creative Media at City University of Hong Kong. He specializes in the development and study of creativity support, collective intelligence, knowledge management, and the use of computer games for learning. His work has been published in several top IS journals, such as MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, Communications of the Association for Information Systems, and Journal of the Association for Information Science and Technology, among others.

Xingyu Chen is an associate professor and deputy head of the Department of Marketing in the College of Management at Shenzhen University. She received her PhD in industrial engineering from the Nanyang Technological University (NTU) in Singapore. Her research focuses on interdisciplinary topics including online user behaviors and social media marketing strategies. Her research has been published in Marketing Science, Journal of Marketing Research, Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, etc. She has also been awarded Outstanding Youth Scientist of Shenzhen University, Nanshan Overseas High-level Talents (Pilot Talents), and Overseas High-Caliber Personnel (Peacock Plan) of Shenzhen.
