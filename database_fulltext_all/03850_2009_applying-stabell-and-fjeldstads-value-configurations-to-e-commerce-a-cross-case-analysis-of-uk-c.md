# Applying Stabell and Fjeldstad’s value configurations to E-commerce: A cross-case analysis of UK comparison websites

Des Laffey <sup>a,\*</sup>, Anthony Gandy <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Kent Business School, University of Kent, Canterbury CT2 7PE, United Kingdom

<sup>b</sup> ifs School of Finance, 8th Floor, Peninsula House, 36 Monument Street, London EC3R 8LJ, United Kingdom

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 5 March 2009 Received in revised form 18 October 2009 Accepted 18 October 2009 Available online 5 November 2009

Keywords: Comparison websites Value configurations Click chains E-commerce

## a b s t r a c t

This paper analyses the comparison website sector in the UK using what Stabell and Fjeldstad (1998) term the three generic value configurations: the value chain, the value shop and value network. Drawing on secondary material and case studies from the United Kingdom, the key finding is that these frameworks all offer partial explanations of value creation in comparison websites. An amended version of the value chain termed the click chain, outlined by Laffey (2009), is most effective in explaining the source of online traffic for comparison websites and in explaining their costs and revenues. This is an important finding as the literature has identified the value chain as being of limited relevance outside of the manufacturing sector. In addition the value shop offers insights into how the sector deals with more complex products such as mortgages and life insurance, whilst the value network helps to explain the growth of comparison websites. The paper also suggests how this framework can be used for e-commerce markets in general before it concludes.

## 1. Introduction

Comparison websites, also known as aggregators and bots, enable users to specify their requirements and then be presented with a range of prices from product providers, typically with the ability to click through to make a purchase. Comparison websites appeal to both users and product providers or retailers.<sup>2</sup> They act as specialised search engines and enable users to save both time and money instead of visiting multiple websites. In turn product providers receive a supply of customers who, by going through the comparison process, should be more likely to purchase a product.

Through this dual appeal comparison websites are changing the manner in which consumers research and purchase products. Research by the Internet Advertising Bureau UK stated that they accounted for 10% of online sales in the UK in 2008. The impact on some sectors has been even more radical with 25% of car insurance policies in the UK sold through comparison websites in 2007 (Financial Services Authority, 2008).

## 1.1. Rationale

In spite of this impact there is relatively little research into comparison websites in the business and management field. Literature searches conducted for an earlier article revealed that most research in this area has been concerned with the technical issues involved in aggregating data over the Web (Laffey, 2009).

A relatively small number of papers were retrieved from Information Systems journals. Maes et al. (1999) take a consumer buying behaviour approach and argue that software agents (the term they use) will be most important for non-differentiated products, perishable products or surplus inventory. Madnick and Siegel (2002) make the point that all organisations face the threat of aggregation, which has become far easier with advances in software. They analyse the relationship between aggregators and what they term aggregatees (the website whose information is being compared) and offer guidance to all participants. Kocas (2003, 2005) highlights the role of loyalty in dealing with the challenges posed by comparison websites and argues (2005, p. 129) that ‘‘firms with a large percentage of loyal customers should never attempt to use price promotion to attract switchers”. Wan et al. (2007) analyse comparison sites using the analogy of the food chain, developing a schema to categorise sites according to the nature of the assistance and information they provide to users. This includes objective information (for example, prices), subjective numeric information (for example, customer ratings) and subjective text information (for example, customer reviews). Su (2007) offers a framework which outlines strategies for decision making in conditions of incomplete information; expected value, brand seeking and price aversion. This is influenced by the Marketing literature being very similar to a framework presented by Tellis and Gaeth in their 1990 Journal of Marketing paper.

The area of comparison websites does not seem mature enough for the style of literature review recommended by Webster and Watson (2002) whereby a field is analysed by concept, and, if necessary, down to the further level of unit of analysis. However, drawing on their approach a distinction can be made between the many technical papers on aggregation identified in earlier research (Laffey, 2009) and the articles identified in this paper which mostly have a marketing orientation.

The Webster and Watson approach is useful, however, in that it can point the way for further areas of research which draw on established theoretical perspectives in Business and Management and Information Systems. One such perspective is value creation which is considered by this paper.

## 1.2. Objectives and structure

The paper considers how value creation through comparison websites can be explained through the three generic value configurations identified by Stabell and Fjeldstad; the value chain, the value shop and the value network (1998). Stabell and Fjeldstad’s framework has been chosen for the following reasons. Firstly, the paper has been highly influential in the field of Business and Management with Google Scholar showing 507 citations in October 2009, including citations in top tier journals in both Information Systems and other Business and Management disciplines. Second, earlier research by one of the authors, Laffey (2009) noted that certain aspects of value creation in comparison websites seemed, at first examination to fit with Stabell and Fjeldstad’s additional value configurations, the value shop and the value network, which suggests they are worthy of further examination. This earlier research had focused on the adaptation of one of the value configurations Porter’s value chain, to a framework termed the click chain to explain the operations of a comparison website.

This paper’s contribution lies in applying the click chain framework more broadly, to three other case companies and other sectors – and in analysing the sector using the other generic configurations of Stabell and Fjeldstad. However, it is recognised that the use of theoretical frameworks in this way can lead to a rigid approach, or, as Walsham stated (1995, p. 76), ‘‘only seeing what the theory suggests”.

The structure is as follows. Firstly, the paper covers the theoretical frameworks. The methodology and background to the case study companies is then outlined. Cross-case analysis is then presented within the three value configurations and key points are highlighted in the discussion before the paper concludes.

## 2. Theoretical framework: value configurations

## 2.1. Value chain approaches

Inevitably, most discussions of value creation start with the Porter value chain given its status as a standard tool of analysis in academia and business. Value is measured very simply: what buyers will pay for a product. Superior value comes from either offering differentiated products which can justify a premium price or by being a cost leader (Porter, 1985). The role of the value chain is to break down the firm into activities to understand how to create superior value, i.e., cost reduction or differentiation. These activities are distinguished into primary activities, those which create value, and support activities which enable the primary activities to take place.

Information has long been seen as central to the value chain. Although Stabell and Fjeldstad do not focus on this aspect in their paper, it is worthy of discussion here as this paper considers online businesses. Porter and Millar (1985) suggested that IT is present in all activities in the value chain and is “transforming” the value chain, Rayport and Sviokla (1995) introduced the idea of the virtual value chain in which information is more than ‘‘a supporting feature” and can actually become a source of value. They argue there is a virtual value chain (the marketspace) which mirrors the physical value chain (the marketplace). The virtual value chain can create new value, as for example in the case of access to additional material by an artist online. Rayport and Sviokla also argue that some activities can be moved from the physical value chain to the virtual value chain, giving a range of examples including research and development for manufacturing.

Firm value chains are also part of wider value systems as the firm requires inputs from suppliers, created through their value chains, and also provides inputs for the value chains of buyers, either other firms or consumers.

The dot com boom would see challenges to traditional thinking, driven by trends in the investment and start-up community, with terms such as e-strategy and business model gaining credence. However, the dot com bust provided Porter with an opportunity to reassert the relevance of the value chain and his other frameworks in the Internet age. To Porter the Internet was simply an enabler which increased efficiency but did not rewrite the rules of strategy. Indeed its lowering of entry barriers and intensification of competition actually made his models of strategy even more important (Porter, 2001). As an example, where Porter and Millar (1985) had listed telemarketing and remote terminals for sales staff under Marketing and Sales, Porter (2001) listed several applications made possible by the interactive and low cost nature of the Web including product configurators, dynamic pricing and customer feedback.

The value chain has also been criticised at a more fundamental level by academics. Whilst it is generally seen as a good fit for traditional manufacturing, the value chain has been criticised for its sequential nature, a failure to capture interdependencies and lack of relevance for the service sector. Stabell and Fjeldstad highlight the limits of the value chain by using the example of the insurance industry, asking ‘‘What is received, what is produced, and what is shipped?” (1998, p. 414). Chaffey (2007) also developed an amended value chain model which drew on the work of Deise et al. (2000). This model takes into account the importance of services, the impact of technological advances and also incorporates links with consumers.

## 2.1.1. From value chain to click chain

Laffey (2009), using a case study of the financial comparison website Moneynet, shows that with some adjustment for information flows rather than physical flows, an amended version of the value chain – termed the click chain – can effectively model aspects of value creation in the comparison website sector in the UK.

In this analysis, users, termed clicks, are seen as the product of comparison websites and value is measured by the willingness to pay for such clicks by the product provider. The click chain thus focuses on a refinement process of transforming users into higher quality ‘‘clicks”, who are more likely to buy a product, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

2.1.1.1. Primary activities. Inbound clicks – This term describes visitors to the comparison website. There is a crucial distinction here between paid clicks, which involve a payment to a third party, and free clicks which do not. Other things remaining equal free clicks are preferable as they will be more profitable.

Operations – This describes the matching process between users and product providers. Data is collected from product providers using methods which range from technological solutions to manual methods. Technology solutions include screen scraping, effectively automated copying and pasting data from product providers’ webpages – which is an error prone solution – and the use of XML to link to back-end systems which is far more efficient and accurate. Manual methods, as used by Moneynet, involve the use of specialist staff to collect and interpret data from product providers which has the advantage of checking that data is shown in a like-for-like format. For an interesting discussion of legal and regulatory issues in the reuse of data (see Zhu and Madnick (2009a,b)).

Outbound clicks – This describes the process by which a user is transferred from the comparison website to the product provider. This requires technology linkages as the user needs to be transferred to the relevant page on the providers’ website which should be populated with their data. Tracking is also required as the comparison websites charge for the linking facility, most commonly through a fee for customers who are converted and/or a per click payment.

Marketing and sales – Comparison websites market themselves to two distinct audiences, consumer and business. They offer the opportunity to save money to consumers through public relations in the media whilst marketing their service to businesses as a cost effective way of acquiring customers.

Service – An example of service given by Porter (1985) is product adjustment. An example identified in earlier research in this field is through links from comparison websites to financial advisors.

2.1.1.2. Support activities. Laffey (2009) identified two of the secondary activities as being distinct in the click chain as compared to the value chain; namely, procurement and technology development. As comparison websites derive revenues from payments for outbound clicks, the purchase of paid clicks (i.e., procurement) is a crucial aspect of their business, with paid (sponsored) search and affiliates of particular importance. Paid search is payment on a per click basis for position next to the organic search results in Google AdWords and, to a lesser extent, Yahoo and MSN. Affiliates are third parties that are paid for generating traffic and operate through websites, email and search. Comparison websites typically pay affiliates when the traffic they generate clicks through to the product provider.

Technology is fundamental to the operation of comparison websites from the provision of comparisons, the tracking of users and improved user experience to attract and retain users on the site.

2.1.1.3. External value systems. Laffey (2009) categorised the sources of inbound clicks to Moneynet as a series of (value) click chains as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Direct users to the comparison site are seen as ideal as they incur no direct cost – important given the expense of paid clicks. Moneynet obtained 40% of their users directly for which public relations and effective service for existing customers was seen as crucial. Direct users were also seen as being of higher quality than users obtained from other sources.

Laffey (2009) identified the comparison sector as refining clicks from search engines which described broad markets, for example credit card, into a specific market segment. Both methods of search were used by Moneynet and generated 30% of overall traffic, with organic search representing the majority of traffic. Paid search had been used heavily when it was new with clicks sold onto product providers but the costs had increased as product providers competed for paid search clicks

Affiliates were a further form of traffic for Moneynet, representing 5% of the total. However, they were seen as generating low quality traffic and being associated with fraud, whereby clicks are generated solely to generate revenue not because of genuine interest in the product.

## 2.2. Alternative value configurations

After they review the value chain concept and reveal its limitations, Stabell and Fjeldstad (1998) present two additional value configurations, the value shop and the value network.

## 2.2.1. Value shop

Professional service environments such as medicine, the law, and education are seen as offering a good fit with the value shop. The shop is used as a means of description to illustrate that, firstly, whilst many solutions offered are standard, unique problems can be dealt with and, secondly, to signify that the client is involved at all stages of the value creation process.

Information asymmetry between professional and client is a fundamental characteristic of value shops, a position which may concern the ability to diagnose an illness, to advise upon contracts or to recommend the key academic literature in a given area. This information asymmetry leads to a situation in which the provider tells the buyer what they need. This means that the products of value shops have experience and credence qualities, with the buyer only able to evaluate some aspects of the product after purchase/provision (experience) with some aspects impossible to evaluate (credence) (Nayyar, 1990).

Problem solving is seen as iterative and is interdependent with other activities, as is the case when a number of medical professionals are involved in a diagnosis, with spiralling activity cycles, the patient being possibly referred on from their doctor to a consultant, and then to a surgeon.

The organisation of the value shop involves a core of experienced professionals, who can provide oversight and intervene with difficult cases, along with more junior staff. This organisational structure illustrates another characteristic of the value shop, that primary activities often cannot be made distinct from support activities, as for example the senior professional is also managing junior staff.

The automated nature of e-commerce makes the value shop configuration more difficult to apply to online activity. Afuah and Tucci (2000) acknowledge this when giving MyYahoo as an example of an online value shop, noting that it is not targeted at individual customers. However, online computer support as offered by Online PC Support Ltd. (http://www.onlinepcsupport.co.uk) can be seen as having a value shop logic, whereby a technician, using remote software to control a user’s PC and peripherals, will solve problems such as a slow computer or spyware.

## 2.2.2. Value networks

Value networks look at how value is created through a mediator who enables connections to be made, with examples being financial institutions, telecommunications companies and postal services.

In the e-commerce sphere classic examples of value networks are eBay, Betfair (the person-to-person betting website), and the more recent example of Zopa, a UK based financial marketplace, in which people lend and borrow.

Stabell and Fjeldstad describe the mediator as a club manager who controls entry to the network. This nature of this role will vary from relatively light touch for admittance (as in the case of eBay), to strict enforcement (Zopa, for example, credit checks its members). The mediator also enforces standards of behaviour, which is clearly important to maintain trust in the networking service.

Network externalities are clearly crucial given that a critical mass is required, with standardisation required to match a large number of users in an efficient manner. The need for network externalities means that it may be difficult to charge fees, although all the above firms have charged a commission since launch. Whilst Stabell and Fjeldstad see payment being at two levels, access to the network (some type of standing charge), and for use of the network, this is not typical of e-commerce, where free access is the norm, with payment generally reserved for usage.

In the value network mediation activities are performed simultaneously at multiple levels with Betfair, for example, checking that its users have sufficient funds to make a bet and pay out if they lose, before the bet is matched to someone else (Watson et al., 2004)

Stabell and Fjeldstad also identify a layered and interconnected industry structure, giving the example of telecommunications which has different levels of mediating network; network operators, service provider and payment services. As an example eBay relies on other networks; for payments its own PayPal system and credit cards; and for delivery, postal systems.

Network promotion and contract management: This concerns who is invited to join the club, through promotions, selection of customers and the rules and contracts which are enforced.

Network infrastructure: This involves activities involved in operating the mediation service, which will vary from firm to firm. Two support activities within technology development are highlighted by Stabell and Fjeldstad. Network infrastructure development includes ‘‘the design, development and implementation” of the network and service development, all contract terms and methods of interaction enabled by the mediator.

## 3. Methodology

As this is still an under-researched area, case studies were seen the most appropriate research method. Eisenhardt (1989) identifies three uses of case studies; to provide description, to test theory and to generate theory. The main aim of this paper is to further test the click chain concept and alternative value configuration frameworks, albeit with a limited number of cases and also, inevitably, to provide description of the industry. The use of cases, which involve significant involvement and judgement by the authors in a real world context, makes the approach in this paper interpretive. It is therefore, somewhat ironic to cite Eisenhardt, as Walsham (1995) noted in his widely cited paper on Interpretive Case Studies, given that she advocated positivistic approaches.

## 3.1. Case selection

Access to comparison websites was clearly a requirement for this research to be successful. In April 2007 Anthony Ball, the co-founder of OneCompare, was interviewed after coming to the attention of the corresponding author through media coverage. Thus the initial stage of this research involved convenience sampling which, although common in social science research, is obviously a limitation.

OneCompare is a mobile phone and broadband comparison website based in Manchester that employs seven people. Ball had also set up previous comparison websites in the financial services field and so was well placed to speak about the subject. This interview was semi-structured and lasted for two hours using questions based on the three generic value config urations and other frameworks to maximise the data collected.

The interview was transcribed and sent to Anthony Ball to check that no sensitive information was included. It was made clear that this information would be used in public research and he was happy to be identified. Key themes which emerged from analysis of the transcript, the OneCompare website and other secondary materials were then identified

For further access, it was decided to focus on services comparisons because of their lucrative nature, as they generate ongoing revenues for product providers in contrast with the one-off income from the purchase of a physical product. Requests were then made to twelve such comparison websites judged to be significant and typical of the sector. This list was developed by first drawing on research by the Resolution Foundation in November 2007 which had identified the eight leading comparison websites in the UK. Three more websites were added to the list by noting the main market participants identified by the publicly listed Moneysupermarket. Finally. a long established comparison website not included in the list was added to enable wider coverage.<sup>3</sup>

The number of requests was based on Eisenhardt’s advice that between four and ten cases usually works well, and in the realisation that access would be difficult. These requests resulted in access to three more organisations as described below.

Barry Holloway, Marketing Director of uSwitch, which employs 125 people, and has a high profile by offering utility comparisons, was interviewed over the phone for 50 minutes in November 2007. He has since left the company. Richard Brown, the CEO of Moneynet, the UK’s first financial services comparison website, which is based in Bromley, Kent and employs 17 people, was interviewed for 1 hour and 15 minutes on his company’s premises in December 2007. Andrew Hagger, Head of PR and Press of Moneyfacts, responded through an email dialogue in December 2007. He too has since left the company. Moneyfacts, the leading provider of financial information in the UK, is a long established firm and supplies financial information to leading organisations and institutions including the Bank of England, Treasury, media and financial organisations. It has operated a financial comparison site since 2000 and in total employs 120 people.

The interview with Richard Brown was recorded and detailed notes were made during the telephone interview with Barry Holloway. These were both transcribed and sent to the respondents for confirmation. It was made clear to the three further respondents that this information would be used in published research and they agreed to be identified.

## 3.2. Limitations

It is noted that there are limitations with the interviews used for this research. Firstly, the views given are those of the individuals concerned, which is noted for commercial reasons, and is also recognised as a limitation of the study. Second, the information provided may well exclude important information which may not be available to what Walsham terms the ‘‘outside observer”, who is conducting interviews (1995). This may be for commercial reasons or simply because the researcher is not a member of the organisation and ‘‘will not present on many occasions” (Walsham, 1995, p. 77). Third, a further point made by Walsham is the role of the interviewer in influencing the interpretations of those being researched. Care was taken not to ask leading questions but inevitably there will be some influence. Fourth, the reliance on remote interview methods with two of the respondents (one through telephone and one through email) is a further limitation of this research but the information was consistent with that obtained in the face-to-face interviews. Finally, case studies, whilst offering high internal validity, are criticised for a lack of external validity because of financial and practical constraints on how many cases can be included (Barnes and Hinton, 2007). It is thus noted that results generated through the use of case studies, especially a low number, cannot be generalised in the same way that an industry wide survey can.

However, this research makes a contribution to the limited literature in this field and will enable the development of more generalisable work. The firms represented in this research are also broadly representative of the comparison sector, with the final three companies having diversified to cover nearly all comparison markets offered for services. The respondents also have detailed knowledge of the industry with two being founders of comparison websites and the remaining two holding senior level positions.

## 4. Cross-case analysis

The cases are now analysed in the three frameworks outlined earlier; the click chain, the value shop and the value network.

A structured set of questions were developed for each of the frameworks, which were used to develop cases on each website by the two authors. These were then compared and discussed to resolve any differences with more detailed cases gradually being developed. Finally, cross-case analysis was used comparing the websites according to the value configurations.

## 4.1. The click chain

## 4.1.1. Primary activities in the click chain

Inbound clicks – As comparison websites generate revenues through clicks to product providers, termed outbound clicks in this model, a major emphasis of comparison websites has been to reduce the cost of inbound clicks by maximising the number of free clicks, that is, those who come directly to the website (covered in ‘‘External click systems” below).

Of the case companies Moneynet and Moneyfacts provided a detailed breakdown of their inbound clicks. Moneyfacts obtained 50% of their traffic direct, and 10% from free links, with Moneynet receiving 40% through this route. Search was important to both firms accounting for 30% of traffic, with no paid search for Moneyfacts and only a small amount (not disclosed) for Moneynet. Moneyfacts did not make use of affiliates with Moneynet only receiving 5% of traffic from them. Finally, advertising generated 10% of traffic for Moneyfacts and 25% for Moneynet.

Anthony Ball of OneCompare did not disclose detailed figures but stated that most users came direct, there was no use of advertising/paid search and that they used some affiliates. Barry Holloway stated that uSwitch used all the methods outlined, but for commercial reasons could not offer a breakdown.

Operations – Once a click has come to the comparison website, they (the user) are asked to enter personal information and to specify their needs so they can be presented with products which are appropriate. Ideally this leads to a click who, more aware of their needs, is guided to suitable products and is, crucially, more ready to buy. As Richard Brown of Moneynet stated: ‘‘someone who comes to us is more likely to do business than if he goes direct to the product provider”.

It follows that the click through rate to the product provider is a measure of success and comparison quality, as is the conversion rate once a click is at the product provider’s website. uSwitch use Tealeaf CX, a tracking tool which enables de tailed analysis of user experience to pinpoint potential problems, for example a page taking too long to load.

Clearly, a key aspect of operations for comparison websites is acquisition of prices, with a range of approaches in use.

\- OneCompare uses screen scraping to collect data. This involves copying data from webpages, which is a slow process, as each page has to be scraped, and error prone. The data is then interpreted by staff before being used.

\- Moneynet uses an in-house team who collect data directly from the product provider.

\- uSwitch collects data from suppliers via XML links for non-financial products. XML allows data to be collected directly from databases in an efficient process, whilst for financial products its partners (for example, Moneyfacts) supply comparison services through XML

\- Moneyfacts also collects its own information and is a long established provider of information to financial institutions, the Government and the media.

Outbound clicks – When a user clicks on a result from a comparison site they are generally taken to the landing page on the provider’s site, a customised application page with their data transferred. Energy comparisons are handled differently here, as due to the complexity of the process the comparison site will handle the conversion as well.

Conversely, a user may simply click away from the comparison site. They may do this for many reasons; they find the website difficult to use, they wish to simply collect information, they do not feel confident with the comparison process, or perhaps because they want more time to reflect upon the situation. This is a limitation of the click chain as it assumes the sequential nature of the value chain. However, this reveals a danger for comparison websites, that users obtain comparisons and then visit the product provider independently, in other words they break the click chain meaning no revenue is generated for the comparison website.

Marketing and sales – Anthony Ball and Barry Holloway both stated that the dual appeal of comparison websites can cause tensions with product providers. To quote Barry Holloway:

‘‘uSwitch’s marketing can create tension as it focuses on putting the customer first and highlights poor value. This could be seen as biting the hand that feeds. uSwitch have never shied away from criticising their own clients”.

Richard Brown of Moneynet had seen an evolution with early resistance from large product providers giving way to an acceptance that comparison websites were ‘‘a cost effective source of traffic”. Andrew Hagger of Moneyfacts was an exception here seeing no tension with product providers.

Service – All these sites focus on ongoing contact with their users with newsletters a common feature across all firms. One-Compare have a deeper involvement with customers through ensuring product providers honour promotional incentives, such as cashback. This reflects the nature of the mobile phone industry as some retailers use promotional incentives which are designed to be difficult to claim, for example through requiring the completion of detailed forms during certain time slots. Interestingly and linking back to Marketing and Sales this focus is on users as the customers and not product providers.

## 4.1.2. Support activities in the click chain

4.1.2.1. Procurement. As comparison sites are paid on the basis of outbound clicks, how effectively they purchase inbound clicks goes a long way to explaining their profitability. Other comparison sites and the product providers with their far greater resources are competing for the same traffic which inevitably increases costs.

Of the websites looked at here, OneCompare and Moneyfacts do not use paid search at all with Moneyfacts going further and making no use of affiliates. The importance of value for paid clicks was clear to Anthony Ball:

‘‘Only an idiot will throw money into something without knowing, to some degree, what they will get back out of it”.

4.1.2.2. Technology development. Comparison websites would not exist without the Web and its associated technology infra structures. The sites here have varying approaches to data collection, as described earlier, but being able to present users with appropriate products quickly is, in the language of operations management, an ‘‘order qualifier” for the comparison sector. A major emphasis of all the sites here has been on improving the user experience with a focus on additional content and features which are all provided through the website. As Andrew Hagger comments:

‘‘The marketplace is starting to get quite crowded and it will be the sites that can adapt more quickly and offer additional content and value added features that will eventually come out on top”.

## 4.1.3. External click systems

4.1.3.1. Direct. A direct user offers the greatest potential for profit, as no payment has been made to obtain them. All interviewees stressed the importance of public relations in raising awareness and thus generating direct traffic: to quote Andrew Hagger of Moneynet: ‘‘The majority of our new users come to us from our extensive PR coverage”. This position was confirmed by Anthony Ball of OneCompare who stated that ‘‘Most customers are attracted through public relations and the name”.

Direct clicks were also identified as being the best quality source of users by Barry Holloway and Richard Brown. This is intuitive as these users are more likely to be aware of the comparison website and to be visiting in order to obtain value.

Media coverage has the further positive impact online, if hyperlinked, of supplying free click-throughs to the site, as is the case in the Moneyfacts example above. As news organisations will have ongoing features on the industries dealt with by comparison sites, there are many opportunities to provide input, for example on how to obtain savings, and thus to obtain wider coverage.

4.1.3.2. Search engines. The relevance of search engines here is in their role as the most popular tool for online research (iCrossing, 2005). Comparison sites play a key role in refining search queries as explained in Section 2.1.1. They also dominate search results for general queries which inevitably leads search engine users to comparison sites.

Organic search is preferable to paid search as it incurs no direct costs, apart from search engine optimisation: to Barry Holloway of uSwitch, ‘‘the ROI is great”. Obviously a key task is to firstly obtain traffic through organic search, a major challenge given the competition from the other comparison sites and the product providers.

Paid search also sees competition from product providers, with their far larger budgets, who compete with comparison websites, a form of disintermediation.

4.1.3.3. Affiliates. The idea behind affiliate sites is that they offer content of interest to the target audience meaning that clicks they generate are interested in the benefits of a comparison site. However, both Barry Holloway and Richard Brown of Moneynet saw the quality of affiliate traffic as generally poor. As Richard Brown states:

‘‘You are paying for clicks and the conversion rates are really poor”.

A notable exception is the website Moneysavingexpert, which provides high quality clicks to OneCompare and uSwitch. Moneysavingexpert, a website ‘‘dedicated to saving you money”, receives extensive media coverage and is regularly in the top 100 UK websites, as measured by the Alexa service, receiving 3 million visitors a month.

Moneysavingexpert preserves the external click chain identified here to maintain its independence and neutrality. As Barry Holloway explains (when talking about energy comparisons):

‘‘It also does not link to product providers and therefore routes people via comparison and other sites”.

Users are also incentivised to go via Moneysavingexpert as they receive substantial promotional incentives from uSwitch which are not available for users who go direct.

## 4.2. Value shops

The fundamental appeal of comparison websites to users, reduction of information asymmetry, is a characteristic of a value shop as is the involvement of users at all stages of the value creation process, noted in the click chain analysis. In spite of these characteristics, the standardised nature of comparison websites does not fit with the value shop’s idea of unique problems configured to deal with unique cases.

However, further examination of the more complex, higher risk products on comparison websites – mortgages and life insurance – shows that it is normal for customers to be referred onto either telephone support or to a financial advisor, which can be seen as value shops.

Moneynet originally launched offering mortgage comparisons but soon realised people wanted personal advice. They directed their comparison efforts towards other financial products, and in the case of mortgages and life insurance referred customers offline with a network of vetted financial advisors paying a fee to Moneynet. These financial advisors are value shops – as each client has particular circumstances which need consideration – and are accessed via a value network controlled by Moneynet. This reflects the shift to online research with the majority of leads for mortgages now coming online (Georgiou, 2007). If the financial advisor is successful in advising the user then the user will then be connected to a mortgage provider, a form of value network.

This represents the two value chains as described by Rayport and Sviokla (1995) but with greater complexity, Initial prod: uct search has moved online (space), away from magazines and financial advisors (place), to search engines, affiliates and then comparison websites, who then route activity back to financial advisors (place).

As with Moneynet uSwitch also does not attempt to offer full mortgage comparisons online. They offer comparisons according to price, user characteristics and other product features but do not offer click through options to the providers. Instead the user is prompted to call an advisor or provide their details to request a call back. The service is provided by a third party mortgage brokerage which uSwitch has partnered. In this situation Barry Holloway believed that over time mortgage comparisons could move online as users become more comfortable with the process. Moneyfacts in contrast enables comparisons with the ability to click through – the click chain – and also the opportunity to go to a mortgage broker which enables them to cater for customers with differing levels of confidence and knowledge.

The products referred to above, mortgages and life insurance, have high credence qualities with high switching costs meaning that the perceived level of risk from the customer is high. In contrast whilst car insurance has high credence qualities, in other words people only realise how good their policy is when they need to make a claim, the switching costs are low, with an annual renewal offering an easy opportunity to switch.

In a sense comparison websites can be seen as automating the services of financial intermediaries (financial advisors and insurance brokers). A key question here is how effectively they do this, especially for products with such high credence qualities. Does a user fully understand the questions on a self-service insurance form? Or is this made clearer on the telephone, with staff trained to probe suggestions of confusion or doubt in the respondent’s answers? This will, of course, vary according to the customer and the agent they are dealing with.

## 4.3. Value networks

The discussion above also demonstrates the mediating role of the comparison website, here linking clicks to financial advisors, but more generally directly to product providers, in the role of a value network, the final configuration. Stabell and Fjeldstad’s description of value networks can be summarised and applied as follows.

## 4.3.1. The club manager and enforcing standards of behaviour

The notion of the club manager is an accurate description of what a comparison website should do in terms of excluding dishonest firms. Anthony Ball stated that OneCompare vetted mobile phone retailers before allowing them to be listed in its comparisons and Moneyfacts warns product providers that attempts to deceive the comparison system, for example by offering deals that are not available in reality, will be punished with exclusion. However, no such controls exist with respect to users, as this would present a barrier and inhibit network externalities.

## 4.3.2. Strong network externalities

Comparison websites create value by matching consumers with product providers, each side being attracted by volume Stabell and Fjeldstad’s point about the difficulty of charging in the ‘‘initial phase” fits with the launch of Moneynet, as Richard Brown suggests:

‘‘There was no payment. In the early days the model was purely advertising. It was an up hill struggle as it was new to everyone”.

However, the setup costs have to be borne, which include the expensive task of building brand awareness. uSwitch spent heavily on TV advertising and a year after their launch were in a situation where ‘‘half their staff were made redundant”, as Barry Holloway states. Of the other case firms, OneCompare entered the market initially as a partner with the market leader Moneysupermarket before building their own traffic, whilst Moneyfacts used its leverage as a key source of financial data. Moneyfacts is a particularly interesting case as their original business, which they still continue, involved collecting data from product providers which was then made available as industry standard publications, meaning that industry participants can see the range of market products and prices. For a mortgage broker this is seeing the range of deals available for clients and for product providers being able to see what their competitors are offering. Effectively Moneyfacts was a comparison service for the business community which then offered the same data to users through its comparison website. This can be seen as a classic case of reducing information asymmetry, as the insurance agent who traditionally negotiates with a client over discounts knows the market prices, and comparison websites now offer this ability to the user.

There is also a trade-off between strong network externalities and commissions as users are attracted by broad market coverage, but some firms refuse to pay commissions. This dilemma has been resolved by uSwitch, Moneynet and Moneyfacts by using versioning (Shapiro and Varian, 1998), in which all product providers feature in comparison results, but only those that pay commissions have an Apply button, which enables click-throughs. OneCompare in contrast only includes product providers that pay commission.

## 4.3.3. Simultaneous mediation and efficient interconnections

Comparison sites have to compare a user’s requirements with various products and enable linking and transfer of the user and their data. This requires an efficient IT infrastructure which can cope with the demands of e-commerce.

Comparison sites receive significant traffic from search engines and affiliates, which can themselves be described as networks. Affiliate networks, such as Buy.at and Commission Junction (used by uSwitch) and Trade Doubler, exist to connect the tens of thousands of affiliate sites and websites which require traffic. Search engines, in particular Google (the most important network on the Web as it is typically the starting point for users), are critical for comparison sites, demonstrated by the volume of traffic they generate for the case companies

## 4.3.4. Standardisation

Standardisation is mentioned by Stabell and Fjeldstad as important, something comparison sites require to enable automation of their services. All these sites achieve this through use of standard forms with their users and tracking URLs to show where users came from (to make payment) and have gone onto (to receive payment).

## 4.3.5. Primary activities of the value network

4.3.5.1. Network promotion and contract management. For network promotion comparison websites use the methods best described in the click chain section earlier. The non-contractual status of users noted above can cause problems for comparison sites, as some users view information and then visit the provider site independently with a loss of revenue for the comparison site. This is possibly a larger problem for those case companies which use versioning, that is all of them except OneCompare, as they are raising awareness about products for which they will not be paid (which, of course, is the reason that they do not provide a linking opportunity).

4.3.5.2. Service provisioning. In contractual terms this can capture the product provider’s relationships with the comparison website in terms of linking with appropriate potential customers and being able to track such customers.

Stabell and Fjeldstad (1998, p. 429) state billing involves measuring ‘‘customers’ use of network capacity in volume and time”, however, billing in the comparison market does not really work in this way. Some firms in the sector are introducing monthly charges simply to appear, but none of the firms discussed here have adopted such an approach. Payment varies from sales commissions, to payment for applications/quotes, to payment per click and also payment per lead for the connected financial advisors discussed above. However, on the customer side there is no payment at all.

## 4.3.6. Network infrastructure

In the context of comparison websites this involves the Web and IT infrastructure of the comparison websites, their ability to supply product information and finally their ability to efficiently connect their users through to product providers. There are clearly differing approaches to the use of technology amongst the case companies in terms of acquiring product data, as detailed under Operations in the click chain. However, all sites have to be able to cope with the demands of e-commerce, with Moneynet revealing they interact regularly with 700,000 users, Moneyfacts stating they receive over 500,000 users a month and OneCompare receiving over 2 million unique visitors a year. uSwitch, the largest of the sites analysed here, with a well known national brand, witnessed one of the unique challenges of e-commerce, namely spikes in demand, in April 2008 when traffic to its site increased by 57% compared to the day before as a result of a PR campaign which reached the national media (Davies, 2008)

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Click chain

The routes by which users come to comparison websites are modelled effectively by the amended click chain as are the associated costs and revenues. The click chain analysis highlights the crucial relationship between search, a general purpose tool, and comparison sites, which can be seen to some degree as specialised search engines. Search engines have inherent limits, as it can be difficult to express requirements and characteristics for (often complex) products in a basic search box. As most searches only consist of a few words (Ozmutlu et al., 2004; Farber, 2004), there is inevitably an inefficient matching process, something which becomes more severe as product complexity increases. Comparison websites can transform general needs as expressed by search using numerous filters such as income, residential status, credit history, and preference for early settlement charges.

The click chain thus highlights the importance of achieving high rankings in search engines for comparison websites. Another key issue is maximising the proportion of direct visitors, as they incur no payment, and the role of public relations in achieving this, an area which to the knowledge of the authors has not been researched.

Interestingly some product providers refuse to be listed, most prominently Direct Line and Norwich Union in the UK. This means, following the logic of this paper, that these firms have to maximise traffic from other sources, directly, through search, and from affiliates. It is therefore unsurprising that they both reward affiliates generously with Direct Line paying £70 per car insurance sale and Norwich Union up to £65. These are extreme cases, as most organisations are listed on comparison websites, but shows that organisations need to make strategic decisions about the relationships they have with them (Madnick and Siegel, 2002).

## 5.2. The value shop

The value shop is effective in describing the role of the comparison site in reducing information asymmetry along with the close involvement of users at all stages of the comparison process. The links to financial advisors also show the relevance of the value shop concept, which is reinforced by the origins of Moneynet, and other leading comparison sites as financial advisors. Comparison websites provide financial information, which clearly there is a large demand for, in a way that would never be viable in a face-to-face or telephone environment.

## 5.3. The value network

The value network is most useful in focusing on the growth of comparison websites, and their continuing appeal to product providers and users. The UK comparison sector is extremely effective in this way as it offers users wide market coverage and product providers access to a ready supply of higher quality customers. However, this is something of a balancing act as comparison websites are funded by the product providers who want to increase their profitability and used by consumers who wish to save money. Some argue that the dual appeal of comparison websites is misleading as they are commercially motivated, but the comparison sites see themselves as offering value to consumers, which ultimately guarantees them payment from the product providers.

## 5.4. Implications and areas for further research

The value configurations referred to here offer opportunities for research in the wider field of electronic commerce. The click chain idea has support from observation of retail websites, with the sources of clicks, direct to URL, affiliates, search, comparison website offering a good starting point for the analysis of customer acquisition. In a broader sense these methods can measure, to a degree, the level of brand awareness as it would seem logical that the more powerful a brand the greater number of direct visitors. Google – the largest firm in the paid click sector – is the most extreme case of the relationship between a brand and direct traffic as it has no need at all to pay for clicks.

Product providers have also shown an interest in comparison sites with Lloyds Banking Group, the largest UK financial services group, launching its own service in 2008 (www.lloydstsbcompare.co.uk) focusing upon insurance and utilities. The comparisons offered are in heavily commoditised markets outside their core banking areas and involve collaborating with rival firms. This supports the views of Maes et al. (1999) who argue that comparison websites will be most important for non-differentiated products. Keeping such a service away from core areas where customer loyalty is strong is also a logical development, when considered in the light of Kocas’s research on customer loyalty (2003, 2005) referred to earlier.

The different routes offered for mortgage comparisons by Moneyfacts – links to brokers and direct click throughs to providers, which take business away from brokers – is a further example of the complexity covered in this paper. Research for this paper also discovered partnerships between comparison websites with Moneyfacts supplying uSwitch with financial data whilst being in competition for users. This discussion suggests that an examination of the roles played by participants in the comparison sector could be a further method of analysis. This could be related to the work of Wan et al. (2007) which looked at comparison websites using the concept of the food chain.

Recent work in the business models field by academics and other writers can also be drawn upon to examine the area of comparison websites. In their work on Business Models, Osterwalder and Pigneur (2002) use Stabell and Fjeldstad’s value configurations in the development of their framework for e-business models which suggests the business models approach could be a further theoretical perspective to develop this area. The Long Tail concept, introduced by the journalist Chris Anderson (2004, 2006) is also seen by some as important in e-commerce. The basic argument is that niche markets – ‘‘The Long Tail” – can grow in importance through factors such as lower search costs, the removal of physical shelf space constraints and innovations in warehousing and distribution. Certainly comparison websites offer access to a wider market for niche players and Anderson actually refers to them in his work. The Long Tail concept has had a mixed response in academic circles; Brynjolfsson et al. (2006) support the concept, which is unsurprising as their earlier work was drawn on in its development, whilst Elberse (2008) does not. In any event an interesting question can be posed based upon this debate, which is the impact of comparison websites on market structure.

Finally, the research in this paper has been from the perspective of comparison websites. Further research has already begun with product providers in financial services which will include interviews and industry wide surveys. The stage after that will be research with consumers to take their views on comparison websites in the value creation process. The fundamental question of how comparison websites are changing the way that consumers research and purchase financial services can be probed in a number of ways drawing, for example, on Su’s (2007) framework as discussed earlier.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper has demonstrated how each of the generic value configurations can be used to offer partial explanations of value creation in comparison websites. The research suggests that the click chain adaption of the value chain offers a useful method of identifying and managing sources of online traffic, particularly in terms of cost and quality which can also be seem as a possible lens of analysis across other retail sectors, as the methods of attracting customers – direct, search, affiliates, comparison websites – are generic. The value shop, although a limited concept in e-commerce, also offers insights through the reduction of information asymmetry enabled by e-commerce, the continuous involvement of the user and referral to physical value shops. Finally, the value network’s emphasis on mediation highlights the value created through network externalities, which comes through attracting both users and product providers in sufficient numbers.

In summary the role of comparison websites, and a myriad of other online, and offline, partners, in connecting these participants demonstrates that value creation, far from being simplified through e-commerce, is actually growing in complexity.
