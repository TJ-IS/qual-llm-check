## Abstract

Digital technologies are reshaping both organizations and our lives as members of society. The resulting changes are often referred to as “digital transformation” (DT)—a concept that has proven difficult to define and explain theoretically. This is especially true because the nature and scope of DT is evolving beyond clearly defined organizational boundaries. Today, information technology (IT) is becoming increasingly accessible and relevant for many more members of society, creating the potential for previously unseen transformations beyond organizations. Accordingly, new theoretical lenses are needed to explain how a broader set of social actors instigate and are affected by transformative change induced by IT. We conceptualize DT as collective social action, a novel lens that aims to expand the focus of DT research beyond organizations as its unit of analysis, with a complementary perspective centered on social actors, their actions, and their impact. We delineate a typology of digital transformation as collective social action that considers the locus of transformation (what?), types of social actor(s) (who?), and the underlying mechanisms (how?). Collectively, the four ideal types constitute a new theory to help apprehend, explain, and predict how DT as collective social action occurs within and outside formally defined organizational boundaries.

Keywords: Digital Transformation, Collective Social Action, Typology, Fintech

## 1 Introduction

It has become commonplace, even fashionable, to use the term “digital transformation” (DT) to describe the many technosocial changes attributed to the adoption and use of information technologies (IT). However, DT has proven difficult to define and explain (Markus & Rowe, 2020), with other concepts ranging from digitization (e.g., Loebbecke & Picot, 2015; Yoo et al., 2010) to digital disruption (e.g., Karimi & Walter, 2015) competing for attention within a fragmented discourse. Prior IS research exploring transformative digital phenomena—in the broadest sense—has contributed significantly to our understanding of IT impacts. However, such research has necessarily been informed and thus aligned with the technological landscape of the last half-century—a time when IT was predominantly applied within the formally defined boundaries of organizations seeking to improve their performance or productivity (Hess et al., 2016; Hitt et al., 2002; Hylving, 2015).

Today, the nature and scope of DT is evolving. For example, within financial services, peer-to-peer lending platforms (Gomber et al., 2018), cryptocurrency communities promoting blockchainenabled decentralized finance (Breidbach & Tana,

2021), and crowdfunding (Conboy et al., 2022), are enabling more members of society to experience the transformative power of IT, thus expanding the nexus of digital transformation. Accordingly, the perspectives and associated theories that IS research uses to explore strategies (Hess et al., 2016), approaches (Berghaus & Back, 2017) and the means that organizations use to execute DT initiatives (Zolnowski & Warg, 2018) need to be expanded to adequately capture the increasingly emergent nature of the phenomenon (Markus & Rowe, 2020). Specifically, as the nexus of DT evolves, new theoretical insights are needed to explain how a broader set of social actors instigate and are affected by transformative change induced by IT (Nambisan et al., 2019). Complementing the existing organizationcentric narrative in our discipline by incorporating the perspectives of social actors engaged in DT would provide new input into current IS research traditions and address long-standing calls to focus on and theorize about the role and impact of transformative digital technologies at a societal level more broadly (Leong et al., 2016; Majchrzak et al., 2016; Demetis & Lee, 2018; Markus & Rowe, 2020).

Our work responds to this challenge by following the well-established process of typological theorizing (Doty & Glick, 1994; Lau & Shaffer, 2021; Leslie, 2019). First, we theorize that digital transformation is a process of collective social action—a concept that remains largely unexplored and undertheorized in DT research. <sup>1</sup> We define collective social action as purposive cooperation among and between social actors (e.g., individuals or groups) who, united through shared values and norms, pursue a joint objective (i.e., instigate transformative change through the use of IT), in their own socioeconomic realities (i.e., a setting within or outside formally defined organizational boundaries). A collective social action perspective does not contradict the organizational frame underlying much of the DT research to date but complements it by shifting the focus from an organization as the unit of analysis to social actors, their actions, and their impact. Second, we use this starting point to propose a novel typology of DT as collective social action. Our typology delineates two dimensions (or “first-order concepts”): the locus of transformation (the what), and social actor(s) (the who), enabling us to identify four ideal types of DT as collective social action. We then explore the “how” (i.e., causal mechanisms) evident for each ideal type in the typology to explain how each ideal type can be viewed more generally as instances of collective social action (Hedström & Swedberg, 1998, p. 21). As such, our typology represents an instantiation of a Type IV theory (Doty & Glick, 1994; Gregor, 2006), which enables IS researchers to apprehend, explain, and predict how DT occurs.

## 2 Towards Digital Transformation as Collective Social Action

## 2.1 Observations of Digital Transformation Research in the IS Literature

Digital transformation (DT) is a central phenomenon of interest to IS research. Not surprisingly, this has led to many related concepts, including “digitization” (e.g., Yoo et al., 2010), “digitalization” (e.g., Bhimani & Willcocks, 2014), and “digital disruption” (e.g., Karimi & Walter, 2015). However, knowledge pertaining to what DT might entail is fragmented and ambiguous, making it increasingly difficult to explain and define the concept.<sup>2</sup>

Many IS studies exploring transformative digital phenomena—in the broadest sense—assume that organizations are driving DT (the who); usually focusing on the conversion of a “predigital” firm (Chanias et al., 2019) into a digitally enabled entity (Hess et al., 2016; Hitt et al., 2002; Hylving, 2015). The units of analysis in these studies are large, established organizations, with substantial access to technical resources (Andal-Ancion et al., 2003; Kohli & Johnson, 2011; Sebastian et al., 2017). For example, extant work has explored their strategies (Hess et al., 2016), approaches (Berghaus & Back, 2017), and the means to execute DT initiatives (Zolnowski & Warg, 2018) (the how). Other DT research has postulated that the locus of transformation (the what) occurs largely inside the boundaries of such predigital firms, where it is generally focused on the strategic adoption/use of technology to instigate deep structural changes (Heracleous & Barrett, 2001), culminating in cultural and identity transformations (e.g., Utesheva et al., 2016) and increased productivity (Hess et al., 2016).

To appreciate why applying such an organizational frame could hinder the future development of knowledge pertaining to DT, we briefly reflect on the nature of theory, the nature of technology, and their implications for theories of DT. We start with a basic observation about the nature of theory—that theorists are naturally constrained by their starting frames. Given that theories reflect an evolving set of ideas that emerge and change over time within and among scholarly communities (Burton-Jones et al., 2021, p. 306), the frames imprinted into a field naturally affect the ideas that theorists choose, develop, and evolve. This is one of the great strengths of a school of thought (McKinley et al., 1999) because it allows researchers to build upon the advances made by prior theorists. However, it can be a weakness when the phenomenon of interest stretches beyond the range afforded by the initial starting frame. Ultimately, we believe this is the case with the phenomenon of DT.

While many IS researchers have already moved beyond organizational contexts (Barrett et al., 1996; Majchrzak et al., 2016), the field has long had a managerial and organizational influence (Davis & Olson, 1985; Hirschheim & Klein, 2012; Keen & Scott Morton, 1978). Given this history and the continued salience of DT in organizations, it is not surprising that most DT researchers have chosen to take on an organizational frame. To illustrate, we reviewed DT research in the Senior Scholars’ Basket of Journals. Table A1 (Appendix) shows that approximately 78% of articles have adopted an organizational frame. While organizational frames have clearly been salient for many prior instances of DT research (Hess et al., 2016), the emergence of collective self-organizing practices in contexts ranging from the blockchain economy (Beck et al., 2018) to peer-to-peer lending markets (Gomber et al., 2018) are also important but have received much less attention. Indeed, researchers can explain very little at present about the evolving and increasingly emergent nature of the DT phenomenon (Markus & Rowe, 2020), including how a broader set of social actors engage in DT—be it inside or outside the organization (Nambisan et al., 2019). However, it is critical to do so because open source, decentralized, and peer-to-peer groups drive DT across many levels of society (Faik et al., 2020); thus, understanding this broader frame of social actors holds great promise for creating new knowledge about DT that complements current organizational-centric frames.

A second way to appreciate the limits of an organizational frame when studying DT is to consider the nature of technology. The philosophy of technology suggests that technology concerns the relationship between “potentiality and actuality” (Chiasson et al., 2018). In this light, the adoption of an organizational frame for DT research could be appropriate if the potential and actual effects of a technology are constrained inside an organization’s boundaries. However, this is a hard assumption to make because the effects of IT are invariably complex and emergent (Gray & Hovav, 2011). Technologies can be powerful forces for shaping people and activities, creating new structures and values, and challenging stakeholders’ values and interests, and may cause unintended and disruptive outcomes (Chiasson et al., 2018; Hassan et al., 2018). Social actors can also reappropriate technology according to their goals to instigate further collective action (Faik et al., 2020; Orlikowski, 1996).

For all these reasons, organizations harnessing the power of technology often find that it spills beyond their control. Zuboff (1988) observed this decades ago regarding managers’ inability to withstand the autonomous power of technology, and it is evident in platform firms today (Parker et al., 2017). Therefore, while the organizational frame is important for DT research, we suggest that our field should consider a broader set of social actors and a broader locus of transformation to provide new insights into the DT phenomenon. We propose that a collective social action perspective on DT is an appropriate pathway for achieving this objective.

## 2.2 Social Actors and Collective Social Action

To clarify how a collective social action perspective can advance DT research, we initially distinguish the concept of social actor from that of collective social action. We then discuss the benefits of a collective social action perspective as it pertains to our discipline’s ability to explain transformative digital phenomena within and beyond organizational boundaries.

Social actors include any individual or group that performs intentional actions (e.g., the use of IT to instigate change) shaped by that actor’s internalized expectation in a social setting (e.g., gains/losses) (Chandler & Munday, 2016). As assemblages of individuals, social actors develop a situation-specific understanding of self—that is, an “understanding of the self as part of a collective” (Slaby & von Scheve, 2019)—through their self-construal (Turner et al., 1987). Through social processes, understandings of the self and the collective stabilize and change over time (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011; Pentland & Feldman, 2008). Specifically, collectives form social structures that provide the conditions for stability, which in turn provide the conditions for transformation and vice versa (Giddens, 1984; Orlikowski, 1996).

Lamb and Kling (2003) advanced one of the most wellknown accounts of social actors in IS research. They took an organizational perspective, defining social actors as “an organizational entity whose interactions are simultaneously enabled and constrained by the sociotechnical affiliations and environments of the firms, its members, and its industry” (p. 218). However, we believe that their organizational focus merely reflected the IS research tradition at that time; clearly, the concept of social actors can apply within and outside organizations. Researchers who have taken such a broader perspective include those who refer to social actors in general (without reference to organizations) (Reix & Rowe, 2002, as cited in Rowe et al., 2004, p. 93) and those using the term to refer to stakeholders other than firms who form collectives to instigate societal change (Breidbach & Tana, 2021).

Collective social action refers to purposive cooperation among social actors (e.g., individuals or groups) who, united through shared values and norms, pursue a joint objective (i.e., instigate transformative change using IT) in their own socioeconomic realities (i.e., a setting within or outside formally defined organizational boundaries). Interest in collective social action has been emerging in IS scholarship (Watson et al., 2010), with recent contributions using the concept to investigate information sharing in platforms (Feller et al., 2017), group norms in social media (Chen et al., 2020; Sæbø et al., 2020), and how consortia instigated the development and diffusion of IS standards (Markus et al., 2006). Regardless of context, collective social actions are necessarily influenced if not limited by structural constraints, including laws and contracts and norms and values (Chandler & Munday, 2016). Indeed, Ostrom argues that social actors partake in collective social action with “a set of norms and values that can support cooperation” (2000, p. 146), recognizing the complex interdependencies within collective social actions and their recursive effect on social actors.

A collective social action perspective is naturally suited to advancing DT research since it is applicable to the organizational frame (as collective social action can occur within clearly defined and established organizational boundaries, Lamb & Kling, 2003), while also reaching beyond it. A collective social action perspective therefore does not contradict current organization-centric DT research. It complements it by shifting the focus from organizations as the unit of analysis to social actors, their actions, and their impact. After all, organizations are just one context in which social collectives form and attempt to instigate change through IT, since any group of individuals who form collectives—be it within a firm with formally defined boundaries (i.e., a bank) or within a loosely coupled social movement (i.e., a group of cryptocurrency activists)—is made up of relational contracts among its members (Adelstein, 2010). Motivated by Markus and Rowe (2021), we now focus on social actors in DT (inside organizations, outside, or both), as these remain largely unexplored and undertheorized.

## 3 A Typology of Digital Transformation as Collective Social Action

Just as the collective social action perspective can account for contexts within and beyond organizations, we sought an approach to theorizing that would be equally suited to that task. We chose typological theorizing because it is naturally suited to understanding different types of situations within a coherent overall framework.

Typological theorizing involves providing and integrating both an overall metatheoretical account underpinning the typology (here, our conceptualization of DT as collective social action) and middle-range theoretical accounts tailored to specific settings (here, the “ideal types” of DT as collective social action within the typology) (Doty & Glick, 1994; Fiss, 2011; Lau & Shaffer, 2021; Leslie, 2019). In theorizing, we iterated between published cases of DT in the academic and practitioner literatures to theorize the nature of DT and the overarching concept of collective social action to interpret and guide our selection and interpretation of cases. This iteration continued until our theorizing stabilized.

To ensure that our typology would cover settings within and outside organizations, we focused on just one industry—financial services—to provide a common basis from which to theorize. The limitation of doing so is that other industries may yield different insights, but we saw this as an inevitable trade-off between generality and specificity (Weick, 1979). The financial services industry is one of the world’s most influential industries and is undergoing profound technological change (Deloitte, 2016; Gomber et al., 2018). Indeed, “fintech” is now associated with major innovations by industry incumbents (e.g., banks) (Basole & Patel, 2018), new market entrants previously unaffiliated with finance (Gimpel et al., 2018), and even members of the public (Breidbach & Tana, 2021). The emergence of ATMs in the 1950s and online banking in the 1990s are well-known historical examples of how finance and technology have coevolved within organizations (e.g., banks) (Arner et al., 2016), while cryptocurrencies (Nakamoto, 2008) and blockchain (Nofer et al., 2017) exemplify the decentralized and emergent instances of DT occurring outside organizational boundaries (Hendershott et al., 2021). Therefore, the financial services context is highly suitable to illustrate our typology of DT as collective social action.

To provide the scaffolding for our theorizing of DT as collective social action, we built on the arguments of Anderson et al. (1999) and Breidbach and Maglio (2016), who propose that any process can be explored through the attributes of the actor (who), artifact (what), and action (how). Therefore, the process of DT as collective social action can therefore be described by exploring the social actors involved (who), the locus of transformation (what), and the mechanisms (how) enabling such change.

First, social actors (who) are the entities who drive, engage, or are involved in DT. Given our goal of theorizing within and beyond organizations, we focus on the extent to which social actors are constrained/ enabled by rigid boundaries (e.g., laws and contracts within or between organizations) or fluid boundaries (e.g., conventions and social norms in decentralized or self-organized groups). The presence of rigid boundaries is part and parcel of being an organization because that is what gives them their identity (Simon, 1991). Just as organizations are created to facilitate fluid coordination internally (Coase, 1937), their creation necessarily creates a boundary that formalizes coordination across the organization. Even new organizational forms created to facilitate fluid coordination must deal with the fact that the creation of the organization inevitably creates boundaries that then have to be traversed and can become resistant to change (Chen & O’Mahoney, 2009).

Second, the locus of transformation (what) refers to the objectives of a transformation, which can be predeterminate or open. Predetermined objectives enable actors to structure and govern the DT toward those objectives, enabling monocentric governance (Ostrom, 1999). In contrast, when objectives are open, actors may neither intend nor even apprehend the DT. Rather, DT emerges nondeterministically from actors’ adaptations and self-organizing behaviors (in a firm, network, region, etc.). To the extent it is governed, we expect polycentric governance (Ostrom, 1999), reflected in a multiplicity of decision centers, rules, and incentives, as well as the emergence of spontaneous order (Aligica & Tarko, 2012; Constantinides & Barrett, 2015; Ostrom, 2010).

Third, mechanisms (how) are the causal processes that enable transformative change (Rowe & Markus, 2021). Inspired by Avgerou (2013), we argue that these can be investigated and tested (or perhaps even falsified) in future research. However, typological theorizing also requires the acknowledgment of macrolevel conditions affecting all ideal types in order to contextualize and bound the overall theory (Doty & Glick, 1994). We therefore expect social, political, and technological macrolevel conditions to shape all the ideal types we depict in Figure 1. For instance, regulatory changes and standards (Scott & Orlikowski, 2021), exogenous shocks like COVID-19 (Mandviwalla & Flanagan, 2021), market competition (Kohli & Johnson, 2011), and the continued evolution of digital technology (Yoo, 2010), all affect DT. The latter is especially important because it can lead to a general shift toward more prominent and observable instances of DT outside organizational boundaries, i.e., a general shift toward the “systemic transformation” ideal type we propose here. This builds on our earlier point that the nature of technology often leads to unintentional, unpredictable effects that slip outside preexisting boundaries. We expect this to continue and even accelerate as developers and architects of emerging digital technologies begin to more fully understand how their design choices enable future growth, innovation, and emergence (Eaton et al., 2015; Rossi et al., 2020; Rowe & Markus, 2021; Schilling, 2000; Yoo et al., 2010). ## 3.1 Prescribed Transformation

Prescribed transformation processes imply that the objective to be achieved through the use or adoption of IT is predeterminate, meaning social actors formulate it ex ante and implement it collectively within the rigid boundaries of an organization. Examples of prescribed transformations abound in the IS literature, with prior work typically referring to IS-enabled organizational transformation (Besson & Rowe, 2012; Poole & Van de Ven, 2004). For instance, Chanias et al. (2019) describe how a group of senior executives of “AssetCo,” a European financial services provider, purposefully cooperated to initiate a digital transformation journey after regulatory changes reduced sales. United by their shared sense of and desire to maintain the continuity of AssetCo’s operations, these social actors facilitated the development of a B2C share trading platform to open up a new sales channel (Chanias et al., 2019). Based on our analysis of DT examples in the literature, we propose that prescribed transformation processes are enabled by three underlying mechanisms: attempts by social actors to achieve business continuity and strategic sustainability, their interpretation of digital technology, and the subsequent development of new capabilities, skills, norms, and routines.

First, prescribed transformation processes typically involve the initial definition and formalization of a long-term business strategy, vision, or objective (Matt et al., 2015) to ensure business continuity and strategic sustainability. As was the case for AssetCo (Chanias et al., 2019), this process is typically accomplished via a hierarchical governing agency (Besson & Rowe, 2012), meaning that a distinct group of social actors (i.e., senior executives) articulate, for example, their desire to reduce costs (Andal-Ancion et al., 2003) or to redevelop the organization’s value proposition (Sebastian et al., 2017). That is, in prescribed transformation, the desire to achieve transformative change through IT is not necessarily the starting point. Instead, prescribed transformation processes are enabled by the shared values and norms of a group of social actors (i.e., senior executives’ desire to maintain business continuity) collectively responding to intrinsic or extrinsic challenges faced by their organization (Koch et al., 2021). The use of IT to solve a given challenge then emerges as a preferred pathway.

Second, a second group of social actors respond to a hierarchical governing agency (i.e., the organization’s IT unit) and obtain the resources needed to achieve the set objectives via an initial temporary IT project. This endeavor is characterized by its dedicated budget, defined team, specific type of IT to be used, timeline, and objectives (Iriarte & Bayona, 2021; Schuchmann & Seufert, 2015). However, as the initial IT project progresses, the social actors holding hierarchical agency (i.e., senior executives) engage in associated decision-making processes in their own socioeconomic reality that require them to interpret the digital technology (Koch et al., 2021) used and judge how its characteristics (i.e., cost, efficiency) may address potentially evolving organizational objectives, thus culminating in a new collective interpretation (Gioia, 1986; Taylor & Crocker, 1981). Chanias et al. (2019) describe this process, outlining how AssetCo’s senior executives extended their initial objective of creating a B2C sales platform (i.e., the “initial” IT project) to the creation of new business models and a digitally enabled workplace because their understanding of digital technology improved. Due to the nature of digital technologies, this collective interpretation of digital technologies is likely to be an ongoing, evolving process (Engesmo & Panteli, 2021) and may result in further iterations of new objectives and technologies. In this sense, while “IT projects” are temporary and constrained endeavors (Iriarte & Bayona, 2021; Schuchmann & Seufert, 2015), prescribed transformation processes transcend these by evolving into larger transformational activities that are enabled by the evolving collective interpretation of digital technologies by a group of agentic social actors.

Finally, if outsourcing the technical aspects of a DT initiative is not an option (Koch et al., 2021), other social actors (i.e., staff within the organization) will need to establish the digital skills to achieve and maintain the ongoing or evolving transformational objectives that agentic social actors define (Sebastian et al., 2017). This requires the proactive development of new capabilities, skills, norms, and routines related to the use of digital technologies (Erden et al., 2008). In AssetCo’s case, senior executives facilitated the development of the required skills and knowledge for their staff through “recurrent, habitual, and routinized” practices like strategy workshops (Chanias et al., 2019, p. 13). Whenever individuals come together to perform activities for a common transformational goal (Erden et al., 2008), further transformative processes emerge. For example, as staff use new digital technologies, organizational structures, roles, and responsibilities or systems change (Engesmo & Panteli, 2021; Hess et al., 2016) and new business processes emerge (Jones & Karsten, 2008), eventually culminating in new organizational routines that result from these collective actions.<sup>3</sup>

## 3.2 Constructive Transformation

Constructive transformation refers to spontaneous and often unexpected change to organizational routines, which is enabled by social actors (i.e., employees) collectively establishing new patterns and norms of digital technology use (e.g., Orlikowski, 1996). The case of “GlobalBank” (Gregory et al., 2018) offers a good example. Gregory et al. (2018) explain how the increasing adoption and use of the iPhone by GlobalBank’s employees in their personal lives changed their perception of mobile technologies provided by the organization (i.e., BlackBerry phones). Specifically, as their experiences with a perceived superior technology—the seamless usability, attractive design, and customizability of the iPhone and its app ecosystem—transcended into the workplace, the values and norms GlobalBank’s employees held regarding the usability and functionality of corporate technology changed. Consequently, these social actors demanded access to superior technologies. We note how Gregory et al. (2018) highlight that GlobalBank’s constructive transformation processes can be traced back to the shared belief its employees collectively developed about technology (i.e., the superior usability of the iPhone), as well as the joint objective they subsequently pursued (i.e., replacing BlackBerry phones)—both key hallmarks of collective social action. GlobalBank ultimately responded by providing a digital self-service platform so that employees could select and install applications on their devices that met the needs of their lived experiences within the workplace. Based on our review of constructive transformation processes in the literature, we postulate that these are enabled by two mechanisms: the emergence of new capabilities, skills, norms, and routines and organizational incentives.

First, constructive transformation processes are enabled by the emergence of new capabilities, skills, norms, and routines from within a group of social actors. As shown in the GlobalBank example (Gregory et al., 2018), social actors intentionally or unintentionally use whatever digital technologies are best suited to achieve their goals. In doing so, their values and perception of how a technology should perform evolve. As social actors acquire new capabilities and skills in using the technology, organizational structures, systems, norms, and routines, change (Nan, 2011). Whereas in “prescribed transformation,” new capabilities, skills, norms, or routines were intentionally developed for a predeterminate objective, in “constructive transformation,” their emergence is nondeterministic and rooted in processes of exploration (Hanelt et al., 2021). We can see this with how banks respond to new technologies like AI, where potential benefits are less certain and setting predeterminate objectives is thus more challenging. We also know that organizations often create units of subject matter experts or designated employees to explore how best to use new IT (Burton-Jones & Gallivan, 2007) and that workers often find novel ways to appropriate IT that depart from organizational prescriptions. This is what happened at “Main Finance,” a banking group that introduced “CleverLoan,” an AI system to automate credit decisions (Strich et al., 2021). Specifically, Strich et al. (2021) explained how “MainFinance’s bankers learned to manipulate the CleverLoan AI system to maintain their final decision-making authority. Such behaviors shift the transformation in unintended directions and require the social actors involved to learn capabilities, skills, norms, and routines to sustain the unsanctioned behavior over time (Cunha & Carugati, 2018).

Second, constructive transformation processes may be enabled by organizational incentives. This is because organizations have the capability to instigate constructive transformation processes from within their employee base. Recognition or financial rewards, but also the need for belonging, are well-known factors that can motivate social actors (here: employees) to contribute to organizational goals (Bimber et al., 2012). In the context of constructive transformation, the emergence of new patterns of IT use can be facilitated through formal training or by implementing technology policies encouraging the exploration of new types of technology. For example, DBS Bank and HSBC launched in-house “Future Tech” and “HSBC University” training facilities, respectively, thus encouraging employees to independently explore technology trends and to identify how these can benefit the business (Ang, 2021; HSBC, 2021). Hackathons are another option, as shown by the Argentinian bank BBVA. In 2020, their “Global Ninja” hackathon assembled over 1,000 participants to develop solutions to improve the bank’s operations or sustainability (BBVA Communications, 2020). Both examples support the notion that outcomes in constructive transformation and the configuration of social actors partaking in this process are open and unconstrained.

## 3.3 Connective Transformation

Connective transformation is change associated with a predeterminate objective that social actors aim to achieve by involving, collaborating, or engaging with a broader set of actors outside rigid organizational boundaries. For example, the “Reddit Rebellion,” a loosely coupled group of social actors, gained prominence in 2021 when they acted on their shared sense of disenfranchisement with Wall Street and battled hedge funds that had shorted shares of the struggling video game retailer GameStop.<sup>4</sup> Specifically, this group of social actors engaged in connective transformation by using Reddit online forums to initially define their premeditated objective (e.g., to initiate a “squeeze” to counter hedge funds betting on a falling GameStop share price) before relying on fee-free brokerage apps like Robinhood to buy GameStop shares, eventually driving a 1600% share price increase, causing substantial losses for the hedge funds involved as well as subsequent regulatory action. Our review of DT examples in the literature suggests that connective transformation processes are enabled by three underlying mechanisms: connection and collaboration among social actors, multiple iterations of trial and error, and challenges and changes in social structures.

First, connection and collaboration among multiple social actors are essential for connective transformation processes to take place. Prior IS research has linked the joint establishment of industry standards by multiple stakeholders to collective social action (Markus et al., 2006; Zhao et al., 2011). Of course, any interaction involving multiple social actors is necessarily more complex than, for example, prescribed transformation processes that take place within the rigid boundaries of individual organizations. This is because such multiactor interactions need to be orchestrated or coordinated while also meeting the demands and objectives of all involved (Parker et al., 2017).

Second, because of their inherent complexity, connective transformation processes tend to be more prevalent in contexts that aim for higher structural impact (e.g., the GameStop case) (Steinfield et al., 2005) and involve multiple iterations of trial and error to find appropriate configurations of social actors (Tan et al., 2020) as they seek to align their collective efforts (Lanamäki et al., 2020). This process can result in friction among social actors, including conflict of interest (Markus et al., 2006). For example, some actors in the Reddit Rebellion aimed to challenge the financial system, while others just joined for personal financial gains.

Third, connective transformation is caused by challenges and changes in social structures (Hinnings et al., 2018; Mutch, 2013), which affect the trajectory of change processes and may impede actors from achieving their predeterminate objective (Eaton et al., 2018; Feiock & Scholz, 2009). Indeed, Robinhood eventually restricted its users’ ability to trade GameStop and other volatile stocks on January 28,

2021, after hedge fund losses accumulated to almost US\$20 billion. In response, several US regulators intervened, issuing 13 civil investigative demands to Robinhood, while The New York Times questioned the future viability of short selling as a strategy amid a class-action lawsuit filed against the app (Kelly & Goldstein, 2021.

## 3.4 Systemic Transformation

Systemic transformation involves macrolevel change that emerges within ecosystems and results from social phenomena driven by digital technologies. As such, systemic transformation is not the consequence of a predetermined problem. In fact, neither the problem formulation nor the transformative change that emerges or even its potential benefit may be apparent at first. Systemic transformation takes place over an extended period and may cause both intended and unintended changes to structures, norms, and values in ecosystems. Therefore, systemic transformation only becomes apparent once new structures, norms, and values, as well as the governance mechanisms or communal rules that shaped the process, are established (Jabr et al., 2014). Consequently, the fact that a systemic transformation is underway may not be immediately noticeable to stakeholders (including researchers).

One example of systemic transformation includes the development, prolific rise, and societal impact of Bitcoin, which led to the development of multiple other tokens and blockchain-based “decentralized finance” ecosystems (e.g., Ethereum). Initially envisioned by Satoshi Nakamoto (2008) in response to the Global Financial Crisis, Bitcoin’s arrival in 2008 can, in retrospect, be viewed as the first step in a systemic transformation that not only saw countries like El Salvador adopting Bitcoin as legal tender in 2021 but also organizations ranging from Microsoft to Starbucks accepting it as payment for goods and services, as well as the emergence of a global cryptocurrency market currently valued at \~US\$1.7 trillion (CoinMarketCap, 2022). While Nakamoto developed Bitcoin as an electronic peer-to-peer payment system that would function without the need for a trusted intermediary (i.e., a central bank), the case of Bitcoin demonstrates how a global community of social actors, including miners, entrepreneurs, and software developers (among others) actively challenged and continue to transform financial services in a manner that, even in hindsight, was unprecedented and unpredictable.

Of the four ideal types we proposed in our typology, systemic transformation is the least studied to date. However, since we believe it is becoming the most pervasive in society, due to the near-ubiquitous pervasiveness of digital technologies in our daily lives (Yoo, 2010), it is thus the most in need of investigation. Based on our review of cases in the literature, we postulate that systemic transformation processes are enabled by three underlying mechanisms: emergent social structures of technology use, combinatorial evolution leading to new iterations of digital technologies, and the establishment of new institutional arrangements.

First, digital technologies have become pervasive and are now firmly embedded in virtually all social systems where their use moderates our experiences and patterns of social interaction (Yoo, 2010), culminating in emergent social structures of technology use. To understand how this could evolve, we need to go well beyond the traditional view of users as social actors who use IT to perform well-defined tasks. Rather, from the perspective of systematic transformation, users of digital technology are social actors embedded in systems and social structures that extend within, across, and outside organizational boundaries (Lamb & Kling, 2003, Yoo, 2010). Social actors may not intend to create transformative change, but the emerging social structures of technology they are embedded in and inadvertently contribute to can create such systematic change. Others have called these processes “deep structures,” referring to the interplay between abstract relations that enable and constrain everyday social actions and the emergent properties formulated by social action and behavior (Fry, 1982; van Maanen, 1979; Silva & Hirschheim, 2007). In the case of Bitcoin, many actors initially adopted it as a speculative financial asset, for payments, or even for illicit purposes (Abramova & Böhme, 2016; Böhme et al., 2015; Glaser et al., 2014; Sipior et al., 2018). As the adoption of Bitcoin increased, the size and functioning of the cryptocurrency market—as well as the type of offerings—increased and became more complex (Tana & Breidbach, 2021; Mai et al., 2018; Miranda et al., 2022).

Second, whenever humans use digital technologies, their actions also shape the characteristics and capabilities of the digital technologies themselves (Rowe, 2018; Winner, as cited in Hassan et al., 2018), culminating in technological innovation through processes of combinatorial evolution (Arthur, 2009), because digital technologies enable social actors to recombine, redefine, or offer new value propositions by leveraging the characteristics digital technologies provide (Fitzgerald et al., 2014; Schallmo et al., 2017).

Eventually, this increases the diversity of digital technologies available to the social actors within a social system (Benkler, 2006), who can then accomplish an even broader range of tasks and achieve a wider variety of outcomes (Bimber et al., 2012). One example is Ethereum’s extension of smart contracts (Buterin, 2014). Other types of cryptocurrencies developed as an alternative to Bitcoin, such as Monero, which offers unique technologically enabled privacy properties (Monero, 2014; Renwick & Gleasure, 2021), have instigated further systemic transformation (i.e., nonfungible tokens commonly use Ethereum).

Finally, as the diversity of digital technologies available to social actors has increased, novel institutional arrangements (i.e., “rules of the game”; North, 1990) causing further systemic transformation have been established. Institutional arrangements include formal codified rules and laws (e.g., attempts to regulate Bitcoin), as well as informal social norms, beliefs, and any other convention that provides social actors with structure when engaging with one another—or with digital technologies (Scott, 2008; Vargo & Lusch, 2016). The diffusion of institutional arrangements thereby helps social actors “make sense” of the transformative change taking place in the context of their realities (Simon, 1978). However, institutional arrangements can also constrain collective social action in the context of systemic transformation when individual actors become susceptible to following set ideologies or ineffective dogmas (Vargo & Lusch, 2016). The most likely outcome is conflict, which further perpetuates any systemic change already taking place—typically in unprecedented directions. For example, since its inception, the Bitcoin community had maintained a common set of rules regarding the cryptocurrency’s technical features (i.e., a set of institutional arrangements). However, this was disrupted on July 21, 2017, when Bitcoin Improvement Proposal 91 suggested increasing the block size, which would increase the technical requirements to mine new Bitcoin. This created a tension in the community that led to a collective of Bitcoin activists performing a hard fork<sup>5</sup> (August 1, 2017) and developing Bitcoin Cash as a spin-off cryptocurrency (Bitcoin Cash Org, 2021). This was followed by another split into Bitcoin Satoshi Vision (BSV) in November 2018 (Bitcoin Association, 2021).

## 4 Discussion

Having presented the DT typology, we now reflect on our theorizing approach, how the proposed theory differs from prior theory, and our theory’s limitations and implications.

## 4.1 Reflections on Typological Theorizing and This DT Theory

Gregor (2006, p. 628) refers to typological theories as “Type IV” theories (i.e., explanation and prediction oriented). The explanations and predictions of such theories are formulated by specifying relationships among constructs that can be tested in reality and potentially falsified (Doty & Glick, 1994, p. 234). Accordingly, we briefly reflect on the constructs and relationships in the proposed typology and on its degree of falsifiability.

Regarding constructs and relationships, the idea in typological theorizing is to simplify the phenomenon down to its essential dimensions or “first-order constructs” without losing the overall picture (Fiss, 2011). We achieved this by focusing on the locus of transformation (what) and social actor (who), as “first-order” constructs. Doty and Glick (1994) explain that “unlike more traditional theories, typological theories do not highlight the hypothesized relationships between the unidimensional first-order constructs and the dependent variable(s). Instead, typological theories highlight the internal consistency among first-order constructs within an ideal type” (p. 234). That is, the ideal types reflect levels of the firstorder constructs that fit or make sense for that type, and the mechanisms involved are the relationships that sustain and enable the phenomenon enacted by that type. For instance, the prescribed transformation ideal type is not only defined by the presence of rigid boundaries and predeterminate objectives; rather, that type of DT also fits and makes sense in the context of those dimensions, i.e., organizational leaders are better able to prescribe DT activities if they know their objectives and if the organizational boundaries are rigid enough to give them sufficient control. Likewise, the mechanisms in prescribed transformation (e.g., developing new capabilities, skills, norms, and routines) enable the transformation to take place. In short, typological theorizing involves searching for sets of concepts and relationships that fit together in distinct ways (allowing us to specify the four ideal types) while also having a common theme (all exemplifying collective social action).

Regarding falsifiability, typological theorizing generates falsifiable insights because its predictions can be tested and refuted (Cook & Campbell, 1979; Lave & March, 1975). Such tests are based on the similarity between a given case and the ideal type (Doty & Glick, 1994, p. 234), and we can consider such tests at the level of overall typology or at the level of each type. At the level of overall typology, researchers could test our prediction that instances of DT will increase in number and consequentiality in the form of systemic (rather than prescribed) transformation, reflecting a general move upward and to the right of Figure 1. Researchers could also test our global perspective that DT can usefully be viewed through a collective social action lens. Meanwhile, at the level of types within the typology, researchers could test for the presence and salience of each mechanism. A mismatch between the mechanisms proposed and the mechanisms that researchers observe (e.g., the transformation occurring without our proposed mechanisms or the proposed mechanisms generating other types of transformations) would refute our theorizing. However, such conclusions need to be drawn mindfully because mechanisms tend to have probabilistic and even interactive effects (Markus & Rowe, 2018; Rowe & Markus, 2021; Øvrelid & Bygstad, 2019, p. 229). However, at present, there are more instances of prescribed DT than systemic transformation in the literature. Therefore, the opportunities for falsifying the theory (and for refining and extending it) are greatest with systemic transformation.

Finally, theories of DT are at an early stage of development (Markus & Rowe, 2021). Given the diverse conceptions of DT in the literature (Vial, 2019), one of our basic motivations was to provide an overarching lens that can support the existing diversity in the literature yet highlight a common theme to support and integrate further study. The collective social action lens offers both advantages. To date, most theorizing of DT has occurred within organizations; we thus need a lens capable of explaining and predicting such instances of DT. At the same time, DT is moving well beyond organizational boundaries, and we need theories of DT to explain and predict them too. In contrast to existing theories, the collective social action perspective enables theorists to study all these types as well as the trajectories among them. We strongly believe this will only grow in importance over time because a truism of modern life is that our work and personal lives intermingle and overlap in increasingly complex ways. In fact, it may be increasingly difficult for future theorists to use a theoretical approach tailored to only one of these environments.

## 4.2 Limitations and Potential New Research Streams

Several limitations and new research streams flow from both the collective social action perspective on DT that we advanced and the typology we constructed.

First, defining DT as collective social action allows theorists to leverage the strengths of that theory (mindful of its limitations). Historically, the central critique of the collective social action perspective was that many individuals gain benefits by not contributing but instead “free-riding” on the participation of others (Olson, 1965). Ostrom (1990) solved much of this theoretical dilemma but opportunities for tightening that part of the theory continue to be found (Willer, 2009). While no theory is free of limitations, the collective social action perspective has proven remarkably powerful over time (Ostrom, 2000, 2010). As a result, tying DT to collective social action enables researchers to draw on its intellectual armory, such as forms of mechanisms (macro-micro-macro models, Hedström & Swedberg, 1998; Cowen et al., 2022) and types of governance (monocentric and polycentric) (Ostrom, 1999, 2010). Future studies could use these concepts to refine and extend the steps we have taken to gain a deeper understanding of the dynamics between individuals and groups in DT.

The proposed typology also has limitations that open new research streams. The main limitation stems from the limited number of factors we considered. While recommended (per Doty & Glick, 1994), this opens many new questions. For example, by scrutinizing the concept of social actor(s), researchers may investigate whether similar or different mechanisms are involved in settings like digital activism, thus generating a better understanding of its activities and outcomes (George & Leidner, 2019) or its social cycles (Tarafdar & Kajal Ray, 2021). Another difference would be whether organizations are born-digital (e.g., digital start-ups) or predigital (e.g., traditional, established) (Chanias, et al., 2019; Engesmo & Panteli, 2021), which again may indicate unique mechanisms. Social actors also differ in power and access to digital technologies. Accordingly, several mechanisms are likely to be related to the exercise of or reaction to power differentials and inequity, and discovering them would be important. Likewise, by scrutinizing the concept of locus of transformation, researchers can reach a deeper understanding of the extent to which the objectives of a transformation can truly remain open and how the DT evolves in such cases, e.g., moving towards coherence, diversity, or both (Miranda et al., 2022).

Another limitation is that we did not examine trajectories among ideal types. This would be an important avenue for future work. For instance, M-PESA, a money transfer platform (McBride & Liyala, 2023; Nan et al., 2021; Oborn et al., 2019) that initially experienced a prescribed transformation (with predetermined objectives set by Safaricom and Vodacom) but shifted into connective transformation processes, could potentially experience further systemic transformation through the spontaneous extended use of the platform in the future. Similar trajectories are also occurring in open banking, which initially intended to allow customers and businesses to share data with other social actors (banks and third parties), as directed by the EU’s Payment Services Directive (European Banking Authority, 2015), thus following a predeterminate objective (Gozman et al., 2018). However, more recently, open banking has challenged established norms and business models (Hedman & Kalling, 2003), with banks beginning to offer third-party services or acting as connectors between third parties and their customers. We suggest that future longitudinal studies examine these trajectories among and between the ideal types we propose.

Finally, we limited our typology to just one industry sector—financial services. Future studies should test the generalizability of the typology to other sectors (both service and nonservice) and explore the extent to which common or distinct mechanisms exist across sectors. Another opportunity would be to focus on the measurement aspects of the typology. For instance, researchers could devise a DT maturity scale to complement research using our typology.

## 4.3 Implications for Practitioners

Our typology could help practitioners understand the landscape of DT options and how to engage in DT, depending on their aims. We feel that stakeholders in established organizations (i.e., firms), are most in need of guidance on how to foster and ultimately benefit from collective social action as it pertains to digital transformation. For example, established organizations focusing on prescribed transformation could use the typology to identify mechanisms to focus on. Some of these companies may wish to expand their transformations (e.g., towards connective, constructive, or even systemic transformations), and the typology shows the mechanisms they should then consider in each case. Other companies may be more interested in protecting their own transformations rather than allowing them to merge with broader transformations outside their control. Our typology would urge such firms to focus on the borders between the ideal types, e.g., strengthen their organization’s boundaries and the clarity of their DT objectives. While such actions may only succeed temporarily until the technology and its meanings slip beyond their control, this may still provide firms with a temporary advantage from the DT; at this point, a temporary advantage is all that most firms can gain (Dagnino et al., 2021). Beyond individual firms, other social actors such as regulators, investors, and entrepreneurs could also use our typology to understand the DT landscape and how they can take part.

## 5 Conclusion

Our work responds to calls for IS scholarship to expand theories of DT (Markus & Rowe, 2020) by presenting and explaining a typology of DT as collective social action. Using the financial services sector as our context and typological theorizing as our underlying approach, we delineate four distinct types of DT (prescribed, constructive, connective, and systemic) that collectively offer an original theoretical contribution. The proposed typology helps explain what digital transformation as collective social action is, thus providing a complementary viewpoint to prior work on DT that expands the focus from an organization as the unit of analysis to social actors, their actions, and their impact. The typology can be refined and extended further, and we hope that future researchers will do so.

## About the Authors

Silviana Tana is a lecturer in information systems at The Australian National University with a particular interest in digital transformation and innovation, specifically related to the usage of emerging digital technologies. She recently completed her PhD in information systems at the UQ Business School, University of Queensland and, before that, a master’s in information systems from the University of Melbourne, School of Computing and Information Systems. Prior to joining academia, she worked professionally as a business process/enterprise systems functional specialist and project manager. Her research to date has been published in the Journal of Business Research and presented at proceedings at the European Conference on Information Systems.

Christoph F. Breidbach is an associate professor of business information systems at The University of Queensland. His empirical and conceptual research addresses fundamental questions of digital transformation in service settings. His recent work appeared in leading journals such as Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Journal of Service Research, Organizational Research Methods, and MIS Quarterly Executive, as well as in ICIS, ECIS, and PACIS conference proceedings. He serves on the editorial boards of Information Systems Journal, Journal of Service Research, Journal of Service Theory and Practice, and Journal of Business Research and led the AIS Special Interest Group “Services” as elected president from 2018-2021.

Andrew Burton-Jones is a professor of business information systems at the UQ Business School, University of Queensland, Australia. Andrew undertakes research on how effectively organizations use IT, how to improve the methods organizations use to analyze and design IT systems, and how to improve theories and methods used in IS research. Currently, he is engaged in a multiyear study of the digital transformation of healthcare. Prior to his academic career, he was a senior consultant in a Big-4 accounting/consulting firm. He is a Fellow and president-elect of the Association for Information Systems and the current editor-in-chief of MIS Quarterly.
