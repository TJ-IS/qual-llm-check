# Family Preferences Concerning Online Privacy, Data Mining, and Targeted Ads: Regulatory Implications

ERIC K. CLEMONS AND JOSHUA S. WILSON

ERIC K. CLEMONS is professor of operations and information management at the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania. His education includes an S.B. in physics from MIT, and an M.S. and a Ph.D. in operations research from Cornell University. His research for the past 30 years has involved the systematic study of the transformational effects of information on the strategy and practice of business. He was among the first scholars to study online global securities trading, business process outsourcing, channel conflict and e-commerce, and the effect of information on product proliferation and the transformation of consumer behavior in these new marketplaces. More recently, he has begun studying privacy and the challenges of applying current antitrust law to online business models. He is the founder and project director for the Wharton School’s Sponsored Research Project on Information: Strategy and Economics within the Program for Global Strategy and Knowledge Intensive Organizations. He has also held appointments at the Harvard Business School, the Johnson School of Management at Cornell, the Engineering College at Cornell, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Peking University Law School, and the Desautels Centre at the Rotman School of the University of Toronto. He has published over 100 scholarly papers and regularly publishes online in Huffington Post, Business Insider, and Tech Crunch.

JOSHUA S. WILSON is research coordinator for Eric Clemons in the Department of Operations and Information Management at the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania. His research and teaching interests include strategic uses of information systems, information economics, online privacy, and the analysis of mandatory participation third-party payer markets such as search. He received his B.A. in philosophy, politics, and economics from the University of Pennsylvania.

ABSTRACT: Young Internet users engage in risky or inappropriate behavior online that could either be embarrassing or harmful to their future. As importantly, young Internet users engage in online activities that reveal a great deal about the cost to serve them and their willingness to pay for goods and services, which could be used against them by well-informed sellers. Educational applications that collect users information are becoming ubiquitous in the classroom, presenting the opportunity for students’ data to be mined. We are not aware of prior studies that examine parental or students’ attitudes and preferences toward data mining of educational application accounts, and how these attitudes differ across several countries. We used three survey instruments to measure parents’ and students’ attitudes toward data mining of educational applications. Parents in all countries studied prefer far less data mining of students’ online activities than seems to be the current practice. Most importantly, aversion to data mining does not seem to be correlated with awareness of current practices of data mining of teens’ activities. This study highlights regulatory alternatives and suggests future research and future data requirements for designing appropriate regulatory interventions. The nature of the intervention will be guided by the nature of the causes of inappropriate online behavior and inappropriate selection of educational software. Intervention could range from no regulation needed, through providing greater transparency, to new and detailed legal requirements that software providers must meet.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: data mining, educational software, online behavior, online teen behavior, privacy, privacy regulation.

This paper examines families’ attitudes toward teen privacy around the world, as a preliminary input to shape and to inform the creation of an appropriate regulatory policy regarding the data mining of students and the use of data mining to send targeted ads to students. Parents in all of the countries we studied seem to want privacy practices for their children that are very different from those followed by most providers of educational software and online educational services. Survey data unambiguously indicate what parents and families say they want. However, what students actually do online suggests that the survey data may not conclusively tell us what families do want. Either families (1) do not actually want what they say they want, or (2) families’ decision processes may be seriously flawed. This is important for the formulation of regulatory processes because the first suggests that no change in regulation may be necessary, while the implications of the second are complex and require further study.

This paper seeks to use findings on parental attitudes to guide regulatory policy. Parents’ and students’ stated preferences do not appear consistent with students’ actions. Are students’ decisions rational and consistent with their true preferences, in which case the expressed preferences revealed in our surveys should have no regulatory impact? Are students’ decisions rational, but based on insufficient information? In that case regulatory intervention that forced full transparency on providers on educational software and online services might be fully sufficient to achieve consistency between beliefs and actions. Or is multi-period, multi-attribute decision-making inherently so complex that students are not capable of making decisions now that are consistent with their longterm best interests? In that case, regulatory intervention may indeed be necessary.

It is well-known that teenagers and younger children engage in risky activities online [50, 51]. It is well-known that these groups often do not take adequate care to protect themselves online [16, 44, 47]. Data-mining privileges that are now being granted to some providers of educational applications and services create new risks to preteen and teen privacy. Recent revelations by Google and a study by law professors at Fordham University demonstrate that these practices ignore the explicit preferences of parents and students [32, 64]. Recent legislation about the right to be forgotten offer long-term protection about unwanted information being found through search, but offer no protection against unwanted information being stored, analyzed, and used for ads by service providers [86].

The online privacy of teenagers and younger children is an increasingly important issue and an increasingly contentious one [67, 69, 75]. The very public failure of inBloom, the host of new laws addressing student and teen privacy, and the lawsuits alleging privacy violations of educational software companies all suggest that the future of online privacy is as yet unresolved [77, 78, 82]. Parents worry about the privacy of young Internet users, yet a significant fraction of young users cheerfully embrace the idea that “You have zero privacy. Get over it!” [63]. The use of social media technology and e-mail among teenagers is pervasive [52], and the use of some technologies, such as texting, is nearly universal [43], and all of these increase the number of opportunities for a teen to share personal information, and in turn, increase the amount of information for applications and services to mine.

This paper addresses what parents believe their children are doing online, and what these students are actually doing. We determine what parental concerns are and what students’ concerns are, in order to assess what parents and students want from best practice in K–12 educational software. Our research is based on surveys of parents and students, conducted online in the United States, Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Germany, Japan, and Mexico.

The intent of this work is to set a firm foundation for future research on public policy and regulatory policy for teenagers’ online privacy. We set out to learn what we could about contentious issues within the contentious topic of online privacy of students. This includes learning the extent to which individual users were aware of current online practices, and the extent to which they approved of them. We examined differences and similarities among users’ online privacy preferences across different countries. We did not set out with a specific set of attitudes we wished to demonstrate, hypotheses we wished to test, or theories we wished to validate. We did intend to establish the bases needed to develop hypotheses and theories in future research.

Since we did not begin with specific hypotheses, we did not begin with causal models of online behavior that we wished to explore. However, we did observe data on parental awareness of students’ online behavior and companies’ online practices, and we did explore parental attitudes toward them. We were able to test the extent to which awareness and attitudes were correlated. Likewise, we did observe data on students’ engaging in risky practices and their attitudes toward their being tracked, and were able to test the extent to which engaging in specific risky online practices were correlated with concern for tracking these practices.

Problems Associated with Online Behavior of Young Internet Users

Teenagers are spending more time online and texting [43, 52]. Using this technology, teenagers engage in risky behaviors such as cyberbullying [33, 45], sexting,<sup>1</sup> piracy [31], and bragging about or describing inappropriate behaviors with drugs, alcohol, or sexual partners [15]. Stories are frequently published about inappropriate behavior that can destroy students’ reputations or those of their friends and acquaintances [11, 57].

Teenagers are vulnerable and they do not appear to protect themselves in their online activities [56]. This is relevant to our research because it creates a research question: are teenagers aware of the risk associated with their online behaviors and do they take precautions to protect themselves from this risk? We explore this research question in later sections. If students and their families are demanding privacy, but taking no actions to protect their privacy, there are two possible explanations. First, students and their families may not actually care about their online privacy, and the preferences they express in their survey responses may not reflect their deep beliefs. Alternatively, students and their families may care deeply about privacy, and their online actions may be the result of a failure to understand the implications of those actions.

Teenagers do appear to be less likely to understand the future ramifications of disclosing personal information, and the future implications of their actions on their careers or their social lives [23, 58, 79, 94]. Likewise, they appear to be less likely to understand the implications of their online actions for the lives of others [36, 95]. In addition, they appear to be less likely to understand the risks of cyberstalkers, or the risks of disclosing their identities to strangers online [16, 29]. Something once posted online can remain online forever [54, 65]. Students obviously appreciate the immediate gains they get from their online activities, but for a variety of reasons they appear to be less able to make fully informed, fully rational long-term decisions about the costs and risks of different actions. We discuss experiments that would enable us to verify these statements in the section that addresses the complexities of the privacy decision. We address possible future regulatory implications in the section that points the way forward for regulators.

Parents are concerned about their children’s online activities [7, 47, 73], both because of the press surrounding the activities of young users and because of their suspicions about the online activities of their own children. Parents worry about their children’s safety. They may also worry about the legal implications of their children’s abuse of others. Increasingly, parents are concerned about what advertisers and online applications can learn about their children and their children’s activities, and they worry about how the information on their children may be stored, accessed, and used [39, 67].

Schools increasingly use e-mail to communicate with their students and increasingly use online apps, personal computers, and tablets for instruction [10, 19]. These apps often track users and amass large amounts of data [66, 74, 75], as do some providers of schools’ e-mail systems. These vendors frequently obtain data related to schools and school activities, then combine it with other information and use it for commercial purposes that are in no way related to the provision of academic services [25, 81]. A recent New York Times article points out that educational software providers sometimes may inadvertently violate U.S. law on student privacy [78]. However, violations that may have begun as the result of unintentional neglect can easily turn into intentional underinvestment in protecting student privacy. This was the case when a software engineer detected serious, and basic, security flaws in the educational apps that a teacher required his students to use to submit their homework. Two years after the flaws were singled out, the educational software firm had done nothing to rectify the situation [78]. Such violations have become more public and more frequent. Google recently admitted that Google Applications for Education has been scanning the content of students’ e-mails, acknowledged that this may violate federal law, and pledged to stop the practice [3, 28, 70]. A lawsuit over the issue alleges that Google has been using the content obtained from Google Apps for Education in order to build profiles of users that are used for targeting advertising across Google’s other products [25, 32]. Another vendor, inBloom, a \$100 million Gates-funded student information aggregator, failed because parents believed the information being collected was not being adequately secured [76, 82]. We do know that U.S. providers of educational software and services are violating U.S. law. We do not yet know how families feel about these violations, which certainly should be relevant to regulators seeking to influence future behavior of vendors. Outside the United States, U.S. providers of educational software may believe themselves to be subject to little or no oversight, since not all jurisdictions have exact equivalents of U.S. Law [85]. Increasingly, governmental bodies outside the United States will address issues of teen privacy, data mining of student’s educational accounts, and protection of teens from predatory advertising based on data mining. Consequently, regulators outside the United States will be facing greater parental concerns about these issues, and parental demands for additional oversight and additional protections.

## Problems with Data Mining of Students

We examine how parental concerns differ across countries, irrespective of national laws and regulations restricting or permitting online data mining of children. It is useful to start with some potential bases for objection to data mining:

● Price discrimination and the myth of anonymization—targeted ads are not safe because they are not anonymous, at least not after the users click on them [13].

● Targeting to create impulse buying—ads targeted to children are not safe, which is why we regulate ads on children’s morning TV [38, 49, 80], and well-aimed, well-targeted ads may be particularly unsafe.

● Recommendations to create impulse buying—when their closest friends or a celebrity idol pitches directly to young consumers, impulse buying may be even more dangerous to children [8, 30, 53, 95].

However, invasion of privacy can have the result that merchants are better informed and better positioned to set prices that command more of the buyer’s surplus [60, 88]. That is, with more information, merchants can charge some of their customers higher prices, increasing their profits at the expense of their customers. For a variety of reasons, customers differ in their willingness to pay for the same goods and services. Some can afford more, either because they are wealthier or because their employers are paying for their purchases. Some customers value a particular service more than others. A casual vacation travelermay choose to fly to London only if the flight is cheap enough. A business traveler might be willing to pay for a sleeper seat if he needs to engage in sensitive and time-critical negotiations in the morning after he lands. Someone else with a medical condition that makes it dangerous to remain upright for long periods of time in an airplane, might choose first-class seating almost irrespective of the cost. Customers can also differ in their willingness to pay because of differences in the expected cost of serving them. Someone with a history of smoking might be willing to pay more for medical insurance because he knows he has a higher risk profile than a nonsmoker. Service providers have always wanted to be able to make more informed decisions about customers’ willingness to pay. Airlines and insurance companies have explored legal ways to infer customers willingness to pay.

Historically, economists have made distinctions among first-degree, seconddegree, and third-degree price discrimination. The differences among them are based on how much information is available for the seller to use when setting prices for individual customers [4]. It is now more accurate to refer to perfect or complete price discrimination [71], as opposed to incomplete, or less-thanperfect price discrimination. With perfect price discrimination the seller can charge each buyer exactly what that buyer is willing to pay. Obviously, one of the most powerful potential uses of data mining is in the accurate setting of prices for individual customers. This is also one of the most profitable uses of data mining.

Without complete price discrimination, sellers have to use rules and heuristics to estimate each buyer’s willingness to pay. For example, sellers make distinctions based on each customer’s expected type, in order to obtain the maximum revenue from each customer. In the travel industry, they might seek to design screening mechanisms that help them to determine whether or not a specific customer is a business traveler, whom they can charge more, or a leisure traveler, whom they will charge less. The assumption is that business travelers are willing to pay more because they have to travel and because they can often get someone else to pay for the cost of their ticket. And business travelers frequently require flexibility in travel dates, including the right to change their reservations or cancel their flights entirely, because meetings get rescheduled or canceled. In contrast, holidays are rarely canceled, so leisure travelers are less likely to need the ability to reschedule or cancel their flights. As a result, airlines offer low prices for advanced purchase tickets with cancelation penalties, since these are usually purchased by leisure travelers. Airlines usually have higher prices for last-minute tickets with no penalties because these are more often purchased by business travelers.

With more information, sellers can approximate complete price discrimination. The additional information available to sellers is not just limited to better information about the customer’s type. It includes information about the customer’s intent as well. When airlines knew only travelers’ types, they were guessing about what travelers were actually willing to pay. This left travelers with significant opportunity for consumer surplus, the difference between their willingness to pay and their actual price. The less the airlines knew, the greater the resulting consumer surplus. When sellers know why customers want to travel and how urgently they want or even need to travel, they can price more accurately. With complete price discrimination, sellers know exactly what each buyer is willing to pay, and no longer need to price their goods or services based on their estimates of the buyer’s type. With complete price discrimination, airlines can set prices based on each passenger’s need to travel on each specific flight, and on each passenger’s exact willingness to pay for that flight at that time. Consumer surplus is inevitably reduced.<sup>2</sup>

In a recent study, Ben Shiller demonstrates that the use of big data can lead to firstdegree price discrimination, an older term for complete price discrimination [72]. That is, airlines, insurance companies, and many other services are beginning to charge some consumers more than others, and to capture more and more consumer surplus. Clearly, firms do not yet get enough information for complete price discrimination, but Schiller does demonstrate movement in that direction. Sometimes complete price discrimination is illegal. But even where it is legal, why should anyone want it to happen to himself or to his children?

In order for a company to engage in effective price discrimination the company must be informed about the buyer and able to assess the buyer’s willingness to pay. There are companies whose entire business model is to create profiles and assess the prices that individual companies can charge [39, 87]. But the best and easiest way to do this is to integrate data obtained from search history, e-mail history, texting, and other online activities. That is, the best and easiest way to do this is to allow Google to data mine consumers’ activities and to integrate data from various sources.

The widespread acceptance of routine corporate invasions of privacy relies in part upon their promulgation of the “myth of anonymization” [13]. Search-engine vendors often claim that no human reads a user’s texts, e-mails, and search history, or a user’s history of GPS locations, and no human sees exactly why users were added to a particular list of email addresses used for targeted advertising [1]. They then argue that since no human sees a user’s history or the reason a user was added to a list, a user must be perfectly safe. Many Americans accept the idea that the information that is captured about them is used only for ads, that they are anonymous to the advertisers, and that anonymous ads are safe [59]. It is often argued that welltargeted ads must be better for users [93]. A single example should be sufficient to disprove this dangerous misconception. Clicking on a targeted ad can destroy consumers’ privacy, and give the seller the ability to implement complete price discrimination. Moreover, this is true even if, as promised by search-engine operators, no advertiser is ever given a customer’s name or access to his e-mail or search history.

As sellers approach complete price discrimination, their pricing policies can reduce or even eliminate all consumer surplus in air travel. Imagine that the following have occurred:

● Someone texts a friend and he tells him that he desperately needs to get to New York tomorrow morning for an interview, for a senior position at a corporation based there.

● He just received an e-mail to his Gmail account from the executive recruiter assisting in the corporate job search, confirming that the starting salary will be significantly higher than his current job’s salary.

● He asks a friend if he is free for dinner tomorrow after his interview.

Suppose he now starts getting ads for flights to New York. Should he click on any ticket offers that he gets? Definitely not! The airline that sent him the ad may have requested that Google send ads to a list of potential buyers without knowing who the customers are, but they certainly know how badly the customers want to travel. Once he clicks on an ad he loses his anonymity and he immediately identifies himself as someone who desperately needs to get to New York.

Google ads, supported by Big Data Analytics, are valuable to advertisers because they allow companies to know not only who wants what, but how badly they want it, and by inference, how much they are willing to pay. Under these circumstances, clicking on ads can have the result that consumers pay much higher prices.

There are two parts to the problem that all consumers face. First, sellers buy the right to send ads to a list of e-mail addresses with known characteristics, and not simply the right to send ads to a list of e-mail addresses. Consumers may have been anonymous to the seller when the seller sent them the ad, but once consumers click on the ad the seller knows who they are. The seller can then associate the consumer with a specific and detailed set of characteristics, and the seller can determine how much to charge the consumer. Second, consumers rarely understand this, and teen consumers may be even more likely to accept the idea that privacy is dead. Should consumers ever click and identify themselves as desperately needing to get to New York? More generally, should they ever click on a targeted ad and identify themselves? Targeted advertising is not better for consumers, and the more accurate targeting becomes the more harmful it can potentially become.

Big Data Analytics is not restricted solely to identifying individuals for targeted ads. What if the list on which a person is placed is not used just for ads, but is also used to identify people with dangerous behavior? What if this behavior would induce a service provider to charge higher prices or to deny service entirely? Suppose someone has been engaging in unprotected casual sex with strangers in New York, which can definitely be dangerous behavior. Suppose this person starts searching for information on HIV/AIDS infection, symptoms, and the period in which an infected person can briefly avoid detection when undergoing a blood test. This person and someone he or she knows has now become a person of interest to insurance companies. This person is still unknown and anonymous to them. But the company may buy the right to send this person a targeted ad, selecting them for the ad precisely because of the person’s recent online activity, which is being used as a surrogate for dangerous behavior in the physical world. Once again, as soon as the consumer clicks on the ad, he or she begins the process of identifying him/herself to the sender of the ad. It should be clear why consumers should think twice before clicking on ads and identifying themselves.

Data mining is profitable because it is used to send targeted ads. Targeted ads are profitable because they allow companies to know far more about consumers than those consumers would like. Data mining is not safe for consumers.

## Targeted Social Network Ads and Impulse Buying

Google+, Google’s social networking site, and Facebook have both launched similar ad capabilities that leverage user’s content and reviews for the purpose of targeting ads to members in their respective networks [5, 27]. For example, if a local grocery store wants to advertise through Google, they have the option of using a Google+ member’s positive review of the store. The ad will contain the member’s recommendation, word for word or abridged, and the member’s likeness, or picture [26]. This ad is not restricted to Google+; that is, the ad can show up during search queries involving Google search and Google Maps. While consumers may be able to prevent Google from using their likeness for ads, they cannot opt out of being targeted by Google with ads involving their friends’ likenesses. This creates another source of harm, since teens and children are often more prone to impulse purchases, especially if perceived peer pressure is involved [8, 53].

The effects of targeting ads toward teens and children have been studied in depth: children and teens are more susceptible to influence and manipulation by ads [2, 21, 22]. Ads have been misinterpreted by youths as public service announcements, and others have been incorrectly seen as commercials for an athlete when they are in fact commercials for sporting equipment [24]. Initial studies of online advertising demonstrated that teens and children have difficulties distinguishing banner and display ads from the content belonging to the website [56]. And several studies have shown that ads targeted at youths lead to the purchase, adoption, and lifelong use of products, sometimes even harmful products like alcohol and tobacco [2, 22]. Moreover, teen purchasing habits are influenced by peer pressure and the desire to fit in with the norms and styles of the group [30, 53]. The new types of ads developed by Google and Facebook, which look like spontaneous endorsements from friends, can be misinterpreted as personal recommendations. They may not appear to be ads at all [17, 18, 20].

## The Study of Students’ and Parents’ Attitudes Toward Privacy

The survey questions were developed from interviews with policy and legal experts who work at large technology firms. An anthropologist was consulted on the creation of each of the surveys. We tested the validity of the survey design and questions by conducting a pretest, with a small number (n = 40) of subjects in each of the countries. Using descriptive statistics, we determined that the subjects understood the questions being asked of them and that survey instruments were measuring the variables for which they were designed. The pretest data are included in the full data set because the survey instrument did not need to be changed between the pretest and the full launch.

Our study included three separate surveys:

● Our first survey assessed parents’ attitudes toward a wide range of data mining of their children’s e-mail accounts, including forms of detailed tracking and integration with search, texting, and other online activities. Parents were asked to estimate the percentage of students who engaged in a range of risky online activities.

● Our second survey assessed what young Internet users actually do online. Teenagers were asked for their awareness of other students’ online activities. They were also asked questions concerning their attitudes and their preferences toward online data mining of their school e-mail accounts, data mining of other e-mail accounts, and the linking of information from various online sources.

● Our third survey was a repeat of the first survey, except that the parents in the third experiment were briefed about the percentages of students who do actually engage in a range of risky activities. Parents in this survey were asked the same questions as were the parents in the first survey about their attitudes toward data mining of their children’s e-mail accounts. This study was designed to determine whether parents’ attitudes toward the data mining of school-provided e-mail would change when they were informed of the prevalence of risky behavior engaged in by children online.

All three surveys were conducted using Qualtrics, an online survey software company, and their associated panel companies in each of the surveyed countries. The first and second surveys were run in the United States during May 2013; in August 2013, we followed up with the third survey in the United States. In July and August 2013, we ran all the surveys in Japan, and in November 2013, we conducted all the surveys in Germany. We conducted only the first survey in the Latin American countries; all of these surveys were launched and completed during April and May 2014. In order to ensure that the surveys included only relevant populations, screens were put in place to secure a sample of adults that had one or more children under the age of 17. Young adults were screened to include only subjects between 13 and 17 years old who use e-mail and texting. The number of parents surveyed for the United States is 200; Germany, 254; Japan, 246; Argentina, 340; Brazil, 327; Chile, 107; Colombia, 347; and Mexico, 332. The number of teens surveyed in the second study in the United States is 467; Germany, 356; and Japan, 241. Qualtrics operates a panel company or coordinates with panel companies in foreign countries that meticulously proportion their survey base in order to make representative of the general population. For the studies in the United States, Germany, and Japan, Qualtrics determined the representativeness of the sample based on the needs specified by the studies. As a consequence, the demographic data for these select studies was not released. This represents a limitation of these studies, insofar as the generalizability cannot be judged using propensity scores. However, for the studies conducted in the Latin American countries, creating a demographic questionnaire was necessary to monitor representativeness. The results from this questionnaire are described in Tables 1a and 1b.The survey was conducted online until the quota was filled, while simultaneously maintaining the predetermined percentage distributions of the populations.

Of course, given the survey methodology, the concept of response rate has less meaning; the survey remains online until the desired total number of responses is received, while maintaining the appropriate demographic proportions to match the desired population. For example, if there is an insufficient number of people older than 55, then the survey remains online until this part of the sample meets its designated quota. However, many people who are younger than 55 might attempt the survey and get dropped when they reach the question concerning age.<sup>3</sup> The population that responds to online surveys is likely to be a bit skewed; obviously, only technology users respond to these surveys. The panel companies in Latin America controlled the population in accordance with the instructions, with breakdowns on age and educational level; the samples from these surveys are likely to be far more representative, given the research goals, than samples in which subject selection bias often occurs, such as in samples from universities’ behavior research labs.

## Parental Attitudes Toward Teen Privacy

A summary of the findings is presented first, followed by detailed data in the tables. Most parents in the United States, Germany, and the Latin America countries generally do know that search engines track and retain individual users’ search history, but parents in Japan are mostly unaware that search engines track usage. Parents in the United States and Germany are more aware of tracking than their counterparts in Latin America countries, which all have similar degrees of awareness; this suggests that there may be a pattern in parental awareness. For several questions in the survey, independent sample t-tests with unequal variance were performed in order to assess whether parental attitudes differed across countries. This statistical test appropriately addresses the nature of the data, since data from each country were collected independently of each other and, although the distributions for each country may be the same, an inspection of bar graphs suggested unequal variances. In general, the results show that the United States and Germany rarely deviate from each other across many of the questions. The Latin American countries were nearly indistinguishable from each other, but often statistically different from the United States, Germany, and Japan. Japan was statistically different from all other countries, across most of the questions. We performed many pairwise comparisons between countries to determine whether there were any statistical differences. As more pairwise comparisons are tested, the likelihood of obtaining false positives increases beyond the threshold level at which we are testing (e.g., 5 percent level). In order to ensure that we are not admitting false positives among all of the hypotheses we are testing (i.e., the family-wise error rate), the Holm– Bonferroni method adjusts the threshold downward to account for the multiple pairwise comparisons [34]. When we apply the Holm–Bonferroni method, we find that our results generally hold; this method was applied to each question on which t-tests were performed to measure cross-national similarities and differences. Table $^ { 6 , }$ which is a sample of these results, shows that when e-mail services are free, Japan is statistically different from all other countries except Chile when we adjust $p { \mathrm { - v a l u e s } }$ according to the Holm–Bonferroni method; the Latin American countries are statistically similar to each other but statistically different from the non–Latin American countries; and the United States and Germany are statistically similar to each other. ## Teen Attitudes Toward Teen Privacy

The teen attitudes toward privacy study was performed only in the United States, Germany, and Japan. In every country we surveyed, the data show that teenagers care about their own privacy: less than 25 percent of teenagers care “A little” or “Not At All” if their searches, e-mails, or texts are tracked. A comparison of teenagers in each country shows that all teens care most strongly about texts being tracked versus searches and e-mails, when using “Greatly” and “A lot” as the measure. Even though teenagers in each country care about whether their communications are being tracked, the data show that they do not care to the same degree. A comparatively large increase in the number of German teenagers who care “Greatly” (in Tables 8 and 9 below) shows that German teenagers are much less willing to accept tracking of their e-mails and texts than their U.S. and Japanese counterparts. For the data on tracking of online searches contained in Table 7, independent t-tests with unequal variance reveal that Japan’s mean is statistically different from the U.S. mean $( t = 2 . 7 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and Germany’s mean $( t = 3 . 9 6 , p < . 0 0 1 )$ , but that the respective means for the United States and Germany are not statistically different $( t = 1 . 3 1 , p = 0 . 1 9 )$ . However, using the same statistical test for the data contained in Tables 8 and 9, we find that the respective means for the United States and Germany are statistically different $( t = 6 . 7 1 , p < . 0 0 1$ , and $t = 4 . 9 3 , \ \mathsf { p } < . 0 0 1 )$ , while the respective means for the United States and Japan are no longer different $\left( t = 0 . 6 7 8 \right.$ 2 $p = 0 . 5 0 ;$ , and t = 1.41, $p = 0 . 1 6 )$

Teenagers in the United States, Japan, and Germany mostly object to linking their private e-mail to their texts, their school e-mail to their texts, or any other combination of information sources that were explored. Of the teenagers in these three countries, U.S. teenagers are the least concerned with linking between their text messages and other forms of their online communications. The data in Table 10 show that German teenagers have the greatest overall objection to linking of their e-mail accounts and texts, even if the U.S. teenagers are more likely to care “Greatly.” In the United States a small portion of teenagers has no objection to linking of any kind, whereas in Germany and Japan a dearth of teenagers feel the same way. And yet, the independent sample t-tests show that all of the countries have respective means that are not statistically different from each other (United States and Germany, t = 0.87, $p = 0 . 3 8$ ; United States and Japan, $t = 0 . 5 5 , p = 0 . 5 8 ;$ Germany and Japan, t = 1.23, $p = 0 . 2 2 )$ . It is quite possible that the distributions for each country average out to similar numbers, while simultaneously supporting very different interpretations of how much teenagers in each country are concerned about linking.

Additional analyses were performed to determine whether there was any relationship between awareness of, and attitudes toward, performing data mining. The results are shown in Tables 12 and 13. ## Understanding Students’ Privacy Decisions

Critics of any and all governmental attempts to regulate the privacy policy of individual school districts might argue that the privacy risks that schools create for students are comparable to the risks that students routinely create for themselves. They might argue therefore that if parents and students actually made policy decisions for themselves within individual school districts, the actual decisions they would reach would not be dramatically different from those made by the school districts. This does not obviate the need for a rational privacy policy. Indeed, it suggests that students’ privacy decisions need to be understood far more completely. Depending on what is learned about students’ privacy decisions, it may also be argued that a far more comprehensive policy decision is required, extended beyond the domain of school-based privacy policies that we consider here.

The decision to ignore personal safety in the future and to accept free service now is indeed a decision, and therefore needs to be analyzed as a decision. If students’ behavior and family decisions do not appear to be consistent with the preferences expressed in surveys, this is worthy of further decision. Among students and other young populations, the decision to ignore personal privacy and act in ways that are inconsistent with expressed preferences is almost universal. Students’ expressed preferences regarding online privacy—what they say they believe about privacy— show surprisingly little effort at self-protection. However, students’ revealed preferences regarding privacy—what they actually do to protect themselves online—are even more open, and even less defensive, than their expressed preferences. Since privacy decisions are indeed decisions, and students’ actual decisions indeed differ significantly from their stated beliefs, one is forced to examine the gap. Surely regulators need to know far more than they do at present about the source of this gap before they can take any appropriate action or develop any appropriate regulatory policy.

If the privacy decision-making processes of students and their families are fully informed and fully rational, then perhaps regulators should accept these decisions. If students and their families make fully rational decisions to sacrifice privacy today, with all of the long-term implications of those decisions, in exchange for short-term financial gain, regulators should be aware of this. If students and their families make decisions to sacrifice privacy today, with all of the long-term implications of those decisions, in exchange for no financial gain, regulators should be aware of this as well.

It is possible that decisions are not fully informed, but require only the most modest of intervention for an optimal solution. For example, it is possible that the privacy decisions of students and their parents are not yet fully informed, but that full transparency could rectify this. It is possible that full transparency of searchengine power and search-engine behavior and the behavior of all other educational software vendors would result in privacy decisions that are fully informed and fully rational. In that case, regulators do not have to do much except insist on full and honest disclosure of behavior. The market would decide. Firms with software privacy policies that violated the wishes of students and their families would fail in the market for educational software and educational services. School administrators who violated parental wishes would be disciplined by the electoral marketplace; they would lose their positions on school boards, or be forced out of their positions by elected overseers who did not want to lose their own positions.

However, there are several ways in which decisions regarding students’ privacy may be less than fully rational:

● As decision makers, students may use far too high a discount rate when assessing future impacts of their current decisions [12, 40]. When the decision maker is a corporation, especially a corporation with decades of history that plans to exist for decades into the future, financial decisions generally use realistic discount rates to assess the current value of future financial asset positions resulting from current decisions. When decision makers are 16 years old, long-term discounting and the current value of financial positions decades into the future may be almost incomprehensible, since they represent time scales that the individuals have never experienced. This problem is exacerbated when decision makers need to assign financial values to nonfinancial outcomes. It is difficult to place a financial value on the future implications of a current action today when that action leaves a permanent, searchable, online public record of inappropriate behavior. This problem is not unique, and is not unknown to decision scientists or regulators. Similar problems arise when individuals try to make rational decisions about the current value of the future implications of smoking, and the potential for developing lung cancer decades later. Societies face similar issues when they try to relate the current value of the future costs of uncertain environmental damage against the current value of the current rewards of air conditioning, powerful automobiles, or any of the other activities that improve current quality of life, at the potential cost of future declines in quality of life. These problems in decision making are important, difficult, and real.

● As decision makers, students may suffer from false consensus bias [6, 90]. A simple way to view false consensus bias is seen in the thinking that “If I do this, all my friends must do this too. If my friends do this now, everyone must do this now. If everyone does this now, there cannot be a penalty in the future for having done this now. If everyone does it, it will have to be seen as OK in the future, right?” However, it is extremely difficult to predict societal attitudes 25 years or 35 years into the future. Thirty-five years ago homosexuality was taboo, being caught possessing small amounts of drugs could end someone’s career, and everyone smoked tobacco. Thirty-five years is a long time, and at 35th class reunions, discussions of homosexuality and possession of small amounts of drugs may now be the norm, while even outdoor smoking of tobacco may now be taboo. It is possible that students behave the way they do because they believe that any and indeed all of their current activities will appear quite acceptable in the future.

● As decision makers, students may be unable to perform multi-attribute utility calculations. Multi-attribute utility problems are notoriously difficult [37, 61]. Consider a relatively simple problem, a two-dimensional knapsack problem, in which one has to load a container with items to achieve the best total value,<sup>6</sup> but the knapsack has two constraints, volume and weight. The onedimensional problem is already NP-hard, which means that for any reasonably large problem it is not amenable to exact solution. With two dimensions it becomes even less tractable. Now consider increasing the number of objective functions, not the number of constraints. Early work in environmental planning gives some indication of the complexity of the problem, as one considers transportation, energy, food supplies, and all the desirable objective functions that should be increased in any ideal solution. One must consider global warming, more extreme weather conditions, rising sea levels, air pollution, and all the undesirable objective functions that need to be reduced in any ideal solution. One realizes the need to solve the problem for some long-term horizon, with some concept of multi-period optimization that does not involve financial discounting. For most people, survival as individuals, as families, and as a species five years from now is most definitely not reduced by compound application of the cost of funds for five years. The environmental engineering community has been unable to develop a mechanism for analyzing these trade-offs in the decades in which environmental issues have been studied [42, 89]. This gives some indication of just how complicated it must be for individuals to make multi-attribute, multi-period optimization decisions in their own lives. Imagine a student estimating the impact of future embarrassment, which is not measured in financial terms. Then imagine the student estimating the current sense of being socially excluded if he or she does not use some software app that all of his or her friends use, which again is not measured in financial terms. Now imagine the student trying to find the optimal balance between the two, while figuring in numerous other privacy decisions at the same time. It is no wonder that none of us has a fully consistent personal privacy policy, and that none of us is fully consistent in our personal behavior regarding our online privacy.

It is also possible that students may do no analysis at all when they make their privacy decisions. They may use the simplest possible heuristics, and decide that it is cool to use Gmail, or some other service that their friends use, or that it is uncool to use a service that none of their friends use. Peer pressure, combined with effective public relations campaigns, may replace rational decision making.

## The Way Forward for Regulators

The most fundamental question for regulators to address is whether or not there actually is a problem. At least one commissioner on the Federal Trade Commission, Joshua Wright, has argued that there can be no harm to consumers from Google’s activities, since consumers have adopted Google freely and fully informed. He has argued that those who seek to limit Google’s activities are seeking to overrule consumers’ own wishes [91]. We agree that regulator’s most basic objective should be to do no harm,<sup>7</sup> and that regulators should not intervene unless it is necessary to fix problems that cannot be addressed through market mechanisms.

Regulatory policy therefore must first decide whether there is a problem. In the case of privacy, that means regulators must first determine whether current practices of educational software vendors actually violate the actual preferences of students and parents, not merely the expressed preferences. Since expressed preferences differ from preferences revealed by online activities, there is as yet no unambiguous indication of what true preferences are. If current practices actually do violate true preferences of students and parents, which we believe to be the case, then regulators need to know why, in order to develop the minimal possible intervention. If better information would resolve many problems, then requiring full and honest disclosure may be sufficient. If decision makers are encumbered by more fundamental cognitive limitations, more protection may be required. The argument that “kids like to drink” or “kids want to smoke” and “you have no right to restrict their behavior when they know what they want” would not be sufficient to get most lawmakers to reconsider restrictions on teen drinking or on selling tobacco products to minors. The argument that “kids don’t care what they accidentally disclose, despite the harm it may do them in the future” should not be sufficient to avoid any and all restrictions on using data mining to gather information on minors.

Much has been written lately about the “digital right to be forgotten” or the “right to be forgotten online” [35, 68]. It may appear that the right to be forgotten solves all outstanding problems with violation of students’ online privacy. It does not. The right to be forgotten provides the ability to remove links to content that an individual finds offensive, so that the content cannot be found via search. The right to be forgotten does not remove content from the Web. As importantly, it does not remove content from private marketing databases, and it does not remove content from

Google’s internal databases. Consequently, the right to be forgotten has very little impact on targeted ads.

## Conclusions and Directions for Future Research

The paper indicates that the behavior of educational software and service providers is not consistent with either parental attitudes toward privacy or with the attitudes of students themselves. This is consistent with prior studies in countries other than the United States [9, 62]. In all the surveyed countries, parents object to the data mining of their children’s school-provided e-mail accounts. And these objections remain regardless of whether the parents are aware of the extent to which educational software and service providers actually do track their children’s online activity. Moreover, parents in all countries almost universally believe that responsibility for giving consent to data mining does not rest with school district’s data-processing directors, but with the parents and the children. That is, in all countries surveyed, current search-engine practices violate the stated preferences of a vast majority of the adult population. Teens in the United States and Germany engage in risky online behavior more so than their counterparts in Japan. However, teens in each country feel strongly about being tracked by service providers and having their accounts linked by service providers. That is, in all countries surveyed, current search-engine practices violate the preferences of a vast majority of the student population.

A significant body of research should be done as follow-up work. Some is a natural outgrowth of our work. Other aspects follow from the work of other investigators:

1. We need to develop appropriate policies for the use of data mining in educational settings. This is a subset of work proposed at a panel discussion at the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS) in Auckland in 2014, in which Lynn Markus and Heikki Topi proposed that understanding the social implications of Big Data should be a fundamental responsibility of all members of the Association for Information Systems (AIS), the sponsor of ICIS [14]. A coherent regulatory policy needs to be informed in part by wellconsidered societal objectives, developed by impartial professionals who understand what is and is not possible, terms of both threats and threat mitigation.

2. We need greater understanding of the extent to which individual providers of software and services do or do not actually violate the preferences of students and their families. A coherent regulatory policy needs to be informed in part by whether or not there are abuses, and what those abuses are.

3. We need to develop a deeper understanding of teen behavior and of why teens’ actual behavior toward protecting themselves online differs so greatly from their stated preferences toward privacy. Is the gap between expressed and revealed preferences because students truly have little concern for privacy? Or is students’ behavior indicative of the perceived difficulty of being able to protect themselves online? A coherent regulatory policy needs to be informed in part by what students themselves want, and by whether or not they are getting what they actually want.

4. We need to develop a deeper understanding of teen behavior and their actual decision processes when analyzing risks and rewards of their online activities and their attitudes toward privacy [48, 92]. Are their decisions informed and rational? Would their decisions be rational if fully informed? Are their decisions systematically biased by the immediate rewards of their unprotected online activities, or by uncertainties concerning the real costs of future problems? Or are their decisions unduly influenced by peer pressure? That is, are the analyses of students and their families already achieving what students want, in a manner that is fully informed and fully rational? Are decisions falling short of the decision makers’ objectives because of limitations in the data that are available to them, in which case full, honest, transparent disclosure of privacy policies might by fully sufficient, requiring only minimal regulatory intervention. Are decisions falling short because of the complexity of multi-period, multi-attribute decision making? Are decisions falling short because of peer pressure or other factors that replace analytical decision analysis? We do not trust teens to decide for themselves when they can drink, because we do not trust their ability to balance the rewards of driving home against the risks of driving home after drinking. We do not trust teens to decide for themselves when they can drink, because we do not trust them to resist peer pressure. We simply ban teen drinking in the United States. Thus, we ask, are there fundamental cognitive limitations that require more activist regulatory protection, just as society reserves the right to tell teens that they cannot drink? A coherent regulatory policy needs to be informed in part by understanding the problem that is being solved, and by a search for the minimal intervention that would achieve the students’ own long-term objectives from a privacy policy.

5. We need to have a deeper understanding of the differences between countries and their attitudes toward teen privacy. Just as nations differ on their policies towards teen drinking, there may be profound differences in national objectives for policies regarding teen privacy.

6. We do not yet have an estimate of the current wealth transfer to companies that profit from privacy intrusions. That is, we do not know the revenues of Google and other data aggregators from the sale of information that leads to better-targeted ads and to more complete price discrimination. This would help in estimating the cost to consumers from privacy intrusions. We do not yet have estimates of trend lines and the future wealth transfer that will be created by future privacy intrusions. We need to know the size of at least this portion of the problem.

7. We do not yet have an estimate of the wealth transfer back to consumers in exchange for their privacy information. Google gets a great deal of its information from tracking searches, tracking the use of Android devices, and tracking the use of educational software. Gmail, the Android platform, search, and mobile search are provided to consumers either without charge or with subsidized prices. Of course this is true of many more applications, and of many more providers of systems and services. Many applications are provided to users without explicit charge, and many companies provide value to users in exchange for activities that we classify as privacy intrusions. We do not know the value that consumers place on these services, and therefore we do not know the net social value of privacy intrusions. We need to know not only the aggregate wealth transfer to companies that profit from privacy intrusions; we also need to know the aggregate wealth transfer back to consumers.

In brief, we know what consumers say about how they feel about abusing their children’s privacy. We do not know whether expressed preferences accurately reflect their true preferences, since behavior is not consistent with these expressed preferences. We do not know how they feel about price discrimination, or about the higher prices they or their children may pay for goods and services as a result of privacy intrusions. Until we know more, we cannot know whether significant regulatory intervention is needed to address privacy concerns, or whether a regulatory requirement for full transparency would allow market forces to solve privacy problems.

## NOTES

1. Several studies have found that nearly 20 percent or more of teenagers have sent sexually suggestive messages [83]. Other studies focus on whether teenagers are sending sexually explicit photographs; the results vary between 1 percent and 28 percent [46, 55]. 2. Regulators need to make complex trade-offs. Consumer surplus is generally seen as good because it represents consumers’ value above the prices they pay. In some markets, maximizing consumer surplus is the principal concern of regulators. Regulators generally view deadweight loss as bad because it represents a market inefficiency. Interestingly, complete and perfect price discrimination also reduce or eliminate deadweight loss. When sellers can charge only a limited number of prices, there will always be some consumers who are willing to pay more than the seller’s cost but less than the seller’s lowest price. As the seller becomes better informed, he may offer lower prices to consumers with the lowest true willingness to pay. Balancing the desire to maximize consumer surplus and the desire to reduce deadweight loss can be complicated. In some industries, such as air travel, with most aircraft largely filled to capacity, we might expect perfect price discrimination to increase airline revenues and reduce consumer surplus. Likewise, prices might increase for other services that consumers view as essential. It is probable that most faculty members would pay more for all of their telecommunications services than they now do, if service providers knew how much they valued the services and therefore could price these consumers more accurately. But we do not actually know what privacy policies would maximize total social or societal welfare. We are raising issues of fairness, which are explosively difficult for regulatory economists. Is it fair to force people with genetic predispositions to diseases to pay more for insurance? Is it fair not to do so, and to force the rest of us to subsidize them? The answer depends on which individuals you ask, and indeed, it depends on their ages when you ask them. Is it fair to charge some people more for the same product just because they announced that they really wanted it and Google overheard that? We surmise that most Americans would agree that this is not fair, even if it increases producer surplus by the exact amount that it decreases consumer surplus, and even if it leaves social welfare unchanged. This is a subject for further surveys.
