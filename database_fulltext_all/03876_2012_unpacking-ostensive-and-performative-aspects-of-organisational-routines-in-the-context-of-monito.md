# Unpacking ostensive and performative aspects of organisational routines in the context of monitoring systems: A critical realist approach

Federico Iannacci <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Kyriakos S. Hatzaras 1

<sup>a</sup> Faculty of Business & Management, Canterbury Christ Church University, United Kingdom <sup>b</sup> Healthcare Management Group, Imperial College Business School, United Kingdom

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 10 September 2010 Received in revised form 27 September 2011 Accepted 4 October 2011 Available online 6 November 2011

## a b s t r a c t

Drawing on the theory of organisational routines as generative systems, we deploy a critical realist approach based on Searle's philosophy of language to analyse the generative mechanisms that specify the preconditions for recognisable, repetitive patterns of interdependent activities. Using the example of the organisational routines implemented in Germany to monitor the allocation and disbursement of the European Social Fund, we contend that constitutive rules of the type “X counts as Y in context C” are at the very centre of organisational routines. Such rules consist of generative mechanisms that account for the emergence of the ostensive aspects of organisational routines out of artefacts and/or procedures in a social structure of power relations. We further claim that, far from being proxies for the ostensive aspects of routines, artefacts whether tangible or intangible are instantiations of such ostensive aspects on a par with any other performative aspects of routines. On this basis, a re-conceptualisation and a re-labelling of the ostensive and performative aspects of routines are proposed. The former are the result of the activation of systems of constitutive rules, i.e. actual routines in critical realist terminology, the latter are patterns of interdependent activities instantiating the ostensive aspects of routines, i.e. empirical routines in critical realist terminology. Implications for theory and practice are discussed by developing a model of organisational routines that interweaves extant research streams.

## 1. Introduction<sup>1</sup>

Organisational routines may be conceived as generative systems that produce recognisable, repetitive patterns of interdependent actions, carried out by multiple actors (Feldman & Pentland, 2003) within a preexisting social context. They occupy “the crucial nexus between structure and action, between the organization as an object and organizing as a process” (Pentland & Reuter, 1994: 484). They are ubiquitous in organisations and an integral part of organisations (Becker, 2008). In spite of the progress made to remove de<sup>fi</sup>nitional ambiguities surrounding this concept (Becker, 2008), extant literature has overlooked the idea of generative mechanisms, to the point of con<sup>fl</sup>ating the levels of action and representation (Becker, 2005: 251). In this paper, we set out to remedy this imbalance by casting generative mechanisms at the very centre of our analysis.

Although few scholars have taken account of the fact that the distinction between action and representation is a very important difference (Becker, 2005), Feldman and Pentland (2003) have made a remarkable contribution in this regard as they have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two aspects of organisational routines, the ostensive and the performative. The former embodies what we typically think of as structure. The latter exempli<sup>fi</sup>es speci<sup>fi</sup>c actions, by speci<sup>fi</sup>c people, at speci<sup>fi</sup>c times and places. While Feldman and Pentland's path-breaking work has shed light on these two levels, we submit that their work does not perform a full analytical separation between them. This is because it draws on structuration theory (Giddens, 1984) which conceives of structure, i.e. representation or “memory traces orienting the conduct of knowledgeable human agents” (Giddens, 1984: 17), as virtual that only becomes real in its instantiations (Dobson, 2001). Rather than assuming agents, i.e. action. and structures. i.e. representations. to be different aspects of the same thing, i.e, organisational routines, we endeavour to draw on the philosophy of science of critical realism that acknowledges that agents and structures are different entities (Archer, 1998; Sayer, 2000), and perform a full analytical separation between action and structure (or representation).

To this end, we take the monitoring systems used in Germany to monitor programmes and projects funded by the European Social Fund (ESF) as an illustrative example of monitoring routines. We argue that monitoring systems may be regarded as socio-technical ensembles of actors, artefacts, activities and interactions. Far from exclusively involving the allocation and disbursement of funding based on interlocked activities such as data entry, validation and processing, we maintain that monitoring systems also encompass abstract ideas that are embedded in a broad and heterogeneous array of artefacts and/or procedures. On a par with linguistic symbols, abstract ideas evoke mental representations that go beyond the observable domain of actors, artefacts, activities and interactions by virtue of collective agreement, whether tacit or explicit (Searle, 1995). Thus, we set out to reframe Feldman and Pentland's (2003) distinction between ostensive and perfor mative aspects of routines in a Searlian fashion (Searle, 1995) by using critical realist terminology.<sup>2</sup>

On the one hand, monitoring routines are the result of the activation of systems of constitutive rules of the type “X counts as Y in context C” whereby actual routines in the form of abstract ideas are generated that guide various participants' activities, as well as their underlying cognitive understandings (Iannacci, 2010). On the other hand, monitoring routines consist of empirical routines instantiating such abstract ideas in and over time in a recurrent fashion. As well as being inscribed within a broad array of artefacts, i.e. synchronic instantiations, abstract ideas can also consist of observed performances by speci<sup>fi</sup>c people in speci<sup>fi</sup>c places, i.e. diachronic instantiations. For example, data entry, a typical monitoring routine, involves a plethora of actors who interact by using hardware and software artefacts over time to ensure that data have been entered in appropriate forms orderly.

Not only do we propound to reframe Feldman and Pentland's (2003) terms, i.e. ostensive and performative aspects of routines, in a critical realist fashion but we also endeavour to take the concept of generative system forward by explaining (i) the manner in which such abstract ideas are created, (ii) the process of objecti<sup>fi</sup>cation that underpins them, and (iii) the role of symbolisation in this process. To this end, we claim that abstract ideas, i.e. actual routines, are predicated upon a deeper background of status functions that are acquired via the activation of constitutive rules. For example, within the European Union (EU) employment strategy context (C), a contractor (X) counts as, i.e. has the status of, a Service or Project Provider (Y) thanks to her right (and duty) to deliver training sessions in exchange for funding. Within this background, systems of interlocked constitutive rules are activated to bring about the of<sup>fi</sup>cial assignment of a collectivelyrecognised status to a physical artefact (e.g., a EU treaty, a regulation, a project template, etc.) and/or a procedure (e.g., a data entry procedure, a data validation procedure, etc.) to which a function is attached that could not be performed by virtue of their sheer physical properties.

Thus, we propose that the process of objecti<sup>fi</sup>cation (such and such is “the Service or Project Provider”, such and such is “the of<sup>fi</sup>cial template”, such and such is the “data entry routine”, etc.) is based on a system of interlocked constitutive rules (Searle, 1995), whereby something (X) is taken to stand for objective knowledge (Y) within a speci<sup>fi</sup>c socio-cultural context (C). Although physical or material artefacts are frequently used to activate these constitutive rules (e.g., a textual artefact, a digital artefact, etc.), such artefacts should not be mistaken for the actual routines; rather, they are practical instantiations of abstract ideas (Leonardi, 2010) and as such they fall in the domain of empirical routines.

The remainder of this paper unfolds as follows. The next section reviews the way organisational routines have been conceptualised across different strands of research, focusing on the distinction between structure and action. Section 3 introduces critical realism as the philosophy of science that underlies this work. Section 4 elaborates our research methodology. Section 5 provides an in-depth description of the monitoring routines performed in Germany, focusing on the data collection templates that are instrumental for data entry, storage and processing. Section 6 discusses our key <sup>fi</sup>ndings and proposed model while highlighting the role of language and constitutive rules as generative mechanisms of actual and, indirectly, empirical routines. Section 7, concludes with theoretical and practical insights deriving from our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 2. Background: conceptualising organisational routines

Organisational routines are the building blocks of organisations and organising. Despite their importance, there is very little consensus around this concept (Becker, 2005; Feldman & Pentland, 2003; Cohen et al. 1996). For instance, some scholars de<sup>fi</sup>ne routines as recurrent action patterns (Nelson & Winter, 1982) while others see them as equivalent to rules and procedures (Egidi & Narduzzo, 1997). Yet other scholars conceive of routines as dispositions that energise conditional patterns of behaviour within an organised group of individuals by involving sequential responses to cues (Hodgson, 2008; Knudsen, 2008). This variety of de<sup>fi</sup>nitions has triggered different research foci. Within the recurrent action patterns camp, for instance Nelson and Winter (1982) introduced a wide range of metaphors for routines by conceiving of them as genes, as memory, as truce, as targets for control, replication and imitation. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Winter (1986: 165), by expanding on March and Simon's (1958) notion of a <sup>fi</sup>xed response, de<sup>fi</sup>ned an organisational routine as a “relatively complex pattern of behaviour (or the theoretical representation of such a pattern) trig gered by a relatively small number of initiating signals or choices and functioning as a recognizable unit in a relatively automatic fashion”. Along the same lines, Cohen et al. (1996) distinguished between routines and other recurrent action patterns, the former being recurrent, selectable and set in an organisational context, the latter referring to deliberative choices.

Among those scholars regarding routines as equivalent to rules and procedures, some have viewed routines as the outcome of the execution of sets of condition-action rules stored in individuals' long-term memories (Egidi & Narduzzo, 1997). Others have resorted to grammatical models to analyse the often antithetical attributes of organisational routines (Pentland, 1995; Pentland & Reuter, 1994). By analysing the sequential structure of work, Pentland and Reuter (1994) conceived of routines as functionally-similar patterns, whereby organisational members enact speci<sup>fi</sup>c performances from among a constrained but potentially-large set of possibilities. In the same way that the English grammar allows speakers to produce a variety of sentences, an organisational routine allows members to produce a variety of performances.

Lastly, those scholars who view routines as dispositions have drawn an interesting difference between habits and routines, the former being individual propensities to behave in a particular way in particular situations, the latter being organisational or collective analogues of individual habits (Birnholtz, Cohen, & Hoch, 2007; Hodgson, 2008; Knudsen, 2008). According to these scholars, routines relate to organisations and habits to individuals. However, “routines are not themselves habits. They are organisational metahabits existing on a substrate of habituated individuals in a social structure. Thus, routines are one ontological layer above habits” (Hodgson, 2008: 18).

## 2.1. On the distinction between structure and action

On the backdrop of this variety of de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and research foci, certain overarching themes have started to emerge. For example, Becker (2008) has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed some promising signs of convergence by drawing a distinction between potentialities and actualities, the former encompassing rules, procedures and dispositions, the latter referring to recurrent action patterns. From this vantage point, he argues, by citing Hodgson (2008), organisational routines should refer to potential behaviour, i.e. causes of stable behaviour patterns, rather than to actual behaviour that has been expressed because “the essence of what an entity is cannot be entirely appraised in terms of what an entity does” (Hodgson, 2008: 19; his italics). The distinction between potentialities and actualities evokes major themes in the routines literature. Examples include routines as grammars of action versus routines as speci<sup>fi</sup>c performances (Pentland & Reuter, 1994), representations and expressions (Cohen et al., 1996), ostensive and performative aspects of routines (Feldman & Pentland, 2003), espoused theory versus theory in use (Reynaud, 2005), formal (artefactembedded) routines, i.e. rules, versus actual performances (D'Adderio, 2008), representations of routines versus representations from routines (Hales & Tidd, 2009) and routines as generative systems versus routines as <sup>fi</sup>xed patterns (Pentland, Haerem, & Hillison, 2010; Vromen, 2011).

The renewed interest for this particular type of scholarship has allowed for major improvements in the conceptualisation of organisational routines to the point that D'Adderio (2011: 198) has recently maintained “that the characterisation of routines as generative systems (Feldman & Pentland, 2003, Pentland & Feldman, 2005, Pentland & Reuter, 1994) has been <sup>fi</sup>rmly (and, hopefully irreversibly) established”. If routines scholars have <sup>fi</sup>nally agreed that organisational routines are the equivalent to generative systems, one should not be hard pressed to qualify what these generative systems consist of. This means that a convergent train of thought should be emerging from such scholars as Feldman, Pentland and Reuter with regard to the issue at hand. However, our careful reading of this remarkable stream of literature reveals that this convergent theme revolves around structuration theory as the scholars in question endeavoured to show that routines can be sources of flexibility and change by emphasising their processual character at the expense of their structural traits. Indeed several passages from Feldman and Pentland's (2003) groundbreaking work make explicit reference to structuration theory. “We offer a new ontology of organizational routines that adopts a perspective based on new understandings of the relation between structure and action. We adopt language proposed by Latour (1986) in his analysis of power, in which he pointed out that power exists both in principle and in practice. He referred to the former as the ostensive aspect of power and the latter as the performative aspect. We propose that organisational routines also consist of ostensive and performative aspects, which are closely related to the concepts of structure and agency, as found in structuration theory (Giddens, 1984)” (Feldman & Pentland, 2003: 100).

We trace this “structurationist turn” in routines theory to the quest to transcend the dualism between structure and agency.<sup>3</sup> Early approaches to issues of social ontology began from an uncompromising position that either emphasised that agency is moulded by society or conversely that social structure is a mere aggregate consequence of individual activities. Whether couched in terms of anti-reductionism v. reductionism, holism v. individualism, determinism v. voluntarism, macro v. micro, each approach about the nature of social reality effectively rendered either agency or structure “dependent, inert and, therefore, causally unin<sup>fl</sup>uential” (Archer, 1995: 14). By stressing that structure and agency are mutually constitutive, the “structurationist turn" breaks with earlier approaches and produces a “social ontology of praxis" that casts social practices at the very centre of social analysis. Though this approach overcomes the dualism between structure and agency, it fails to acknowledge that “the people in society and the parts of society are not different aspects of the same thing but are radically different in kind” (Archer, 1995: 15). By enjoining the exclusive examination of social practices, this approach “stresses the necessary involvement of the entirety of structure in even the most trivial act” (Archer, 1995: 95). Hence, it fails to provide an in-depth account of the social context where social practices necessarily unfold

Accordingly, while justi<sup>fi</sup>able from a conceptual perspective, Feldman and Pentland's reliance on structuration theory is unfortunate because it represents a “<sup>fl</sup>at” or “horizontal” ontology that collapses agency and structure into localised or micro-level social practices (Dobson, 2001). In addition, it downplays the fact that structure, i.e. the ostensive aspect of routines, has a layered ontology with emergent properties that are “neither reducible to, nor predictable from. properties of entities found at a lower level" (Hodgson 2007: 103). Not only does Feldman and Pentland's theoretical approach entail a rejection of a pre-existing social context with its own emergent properties but it also leads to an “analytical neglect of the natural and physical world as the essential substratum and context of human activity” (Hodgson, 2007: 103). While it is easy to conceptualise structure as virtual rules and resources that guide people's understandings of who they should communicate with and what they should communicate about, it is not easy to conceptualise physical artefacts as having a virtual character because they have a form and substance that exists in space and time (Leonardi, 2010). Indeed as Leonardi (2010) remarks, by quoting Sewell (1992), it is “only in particular times, places, and quantities that such material (in the sense of physical) objects can serve as resources”.

Therefore, it is not surprising that, in their more recent work, Pentland and Feldman (2005, 2008) have introduced artefacts in their model of organisational routines. Ostensive and performative aspects of routines may be codi<sup>fi</sup>ed or prescribed in artefacts, as well as enabled and constrained by them (Pentland & Feldman, 2005: 795). Even though artefacts <sup>fi</sup>gure conspicuously in Pentland and Feldman's more recent work, they seem to be only implicated in their instrumental roles, that is “in their possible codifying, prescribing, enabling and constraining roles” (Runde, Jones, Munir, & Nikolychuk, 2009: 7). As well as being instrumental to codifying, prescribing, enabling and constraining, artefacts serve a symbolic role because they are markers for the collective assignment of status functions, whereby they acquire an identity, i.e. a certain “for-ness”, purpose or function (Kroes & Meijers, 2006), that goes beyond their physical features. For example, as we will go on to discuss below, the “of<sup>fi</sup>cial templates” serve, to a large degree, the function of authorising Service or Project Providers' submission of claims for funding regardless of their physical properties. Our argument calls for a revised ontology of organisational routines; an ontology that is sensitive to the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed structure of social reality, whereby emergent units, including collectively-recognised status functions, are not mere epiphenomena of their component units (Hodgson, 2007).

## 2.2. Summary

The argument developed so far shows that Feldman and Pentland's in<sup>fl</sup>uential work has a major shortcoming. It forces scholars to accept a single level of reality which is “<sup>fl</sup>at” and “horizontal”, thus dismissing the existence of emergent properties that separate one layer of (social) reality from another layer of (social) reality in a strati<sup>fi</sup>ed fashion. Feldman and Pentland's argument also neglects the natural and physical world by suggesting a mvopic focus on situated social interactions and their “syntactic constituents" (Pentland & Reuter, 1994). Even though artefacts have conspicuously <sup>fi</sup>gured in their more recent work, Pentland and Feldman (2005, 2008) have overlooked their social identity as being a property of the organisational routine as a whole rather than its participants (Runde et al., 2009). In short, collective assignments of status functions and the identities they inform are as much part of social structure as social rules, relations and positions (Faulkner & Runde, 2009). This implies that the social rules that contribute to constituting such identities and the social practices that are bound up with them are different “kinds of things” (Faulkner & Runde, 2009: 444), thus calling for a revised ontology of organisational routines. To purport such a revised ontology we turn to the philosophy of science of critical realism (Archer, 1998; Bhaskar, 1978; Sayer, 2000) and adopt critical realist terminology to break with Feldman and Pentland's conception of structure and agency. This, in turn, requires a theoretical approach that matches its underlying ontology and epistemology (Cf. Volkoff, Strong, & Elmes, 2007: 835). We submit that Searle's theory of social reality is one such theory that maps onto critical realism stratified ontology. because it accounts for the emergent properties of social reality, thus casting the structural, i.e. ostensive, and agentic, i.e. performative, aspects of organisational routines across different domains. In this article we ask, what is about organisational routines that enables them to have a generative property? By propounding a revised ontology of organisational routines we set out to shed new light on the generative mechanisms that organisational routines are equivalent to.

## 3. Critical realism: a Searlian approach

Searle (1995) has argued against the anti-realist thesis suggesting that the only reality that can be meaningfully observed is the reality of perceptual experience. This confuses the real intransitive dimension with the observers' transitive perceptions of it (Dobson, 2001) and identi<sup>fi</sup>es the axial principle whereupon critical realism is based (Sayer, 2000). Critical realism is a philosophy of science that claims the existence of a reality totally independent of individual observation and representation of it, the former operating in the intransitive ontological domain, the latter in the transitive epistemological realm (Dobson, 2001). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, critical realism posits that reality is both intransitive, i.e. existing independently of human beings, and strati<sup>fi</sup>ed, i.e. decomposable in multiple layers or domains of the real, the actual and the empirical (Mingers, 2004). The real domain includes mechanisms, events and experiences, i.e. enduring entities be they physical (e.g., atoms, organisms, etc.), social (e.g., markets, families, etc.) or conceptual (e.g., categories, ideas, etc.) that have tendencies to act in particular ways, whether observable or not. The actual domain is a subset of the real and consists of events that do (or do not) occur. The empirical domain is a subset of the actual and encompasses those events that are observed and/or experienced (Mingers, 2004). Fig. 1 illustrates these three domains.

Critical realism holds enormous promise for several disciplines and has the potential to overcome theory-practice inconsistencies in social science research (Fleetwood, 2001; Longshore Smith, 2006; Ron, 2002). Examples encompass inconsistencies emerging when scholars theorise about power, somehow neglecting that, in practice, various forms of power may be conceptualised as Boolean operations insofar as “to have an obligation is to have a negative power” (Searle, 2005: 17) and to have a right is to have a positive power. It can also enable scholars to move beyond structuration theory because it conceives of structure as an independent social object with emergent and irreducible properties, rather than a virtual object that is only made real through instantiations (Dobson, 2001). This can be seen clearly in the natural sciences, where it is possible to advocate the existence of a physical reality independently of us, but also, and crucially, in the social sciences. Though “social structures do not exist independently of the agents' conceptions of what they are doing” (Mingers, 2004: 96), within the domain of social reality there is a particular class of events that require human institutions for their existence. Searle labels such facts as institutional facts because they exist only by human agreement. According to Searle (1999), institutional facts and the institutional reality they presuppose are ontologically intersubjective because they are constituted by shared feelings and attitudes hinging on tacit and/or explicit human agreements. But knowledge about such facts is an objective phenomenon because “the facts in the world that make them true or false are independent of anybody's attitudes or feelings about them” (Searle, 1995: 8). Thus, institutional facts are epistemologically objective because, for example, the fact that a standard operating procedure is the routine in principle is not a matter of opinion but can be ascertained as true or false independently of the attitudes and feelings of observers (and participants alike). Hence, institutional facts are epistemologically objective, even though subjective at titudes and feelings are part of their mode of existence.

According to Searle, fundamental to the understanding of institutional facts is the notion of constitutive rules. Searle uses the term “constitutive rule” for rules that de<sup>fi</sup>ne institutional activities. He draws a contrast between regulative and constitutive rules, the former regulating antecedently or independently existing activities, the latter creating the possibility of institutional forms of behaviour. “For example, the rule ‘drive on the right-hand side of the road’ regulates driving; but driving can exist prior to the existence of that rule. However, some rules do not merely regulate, they also create the very possibility of certain activities. Thus the rules of chess do not regulate an antecedently existing activity… the rules of chess create the very possibility of playing chess. The rules are constitutive of chess in the sense that playing chess is constituted in part by acting in accord with the rules” (Searle, 1995: 27–28; his italics). Constitutive rules take the form “X counts as Y in context C”. Searle refers to this form as the “count-as-locution”. A constitutive rule entails the assignment of a function (Y) to an object, person and/or procedure (X) in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context (C), even though the object, person and/or procedure in question does not have the intrinsic properties to perform the function under investigation.

Searle argues that constitutive rules are the building blocks of social institutions and, therefore, the glue that holds human societies together, because they reveal the underlying structural features of institutions such as money, property, government, marriage, etc. Though in his in<sup>fl</sup>uential work, i.e. The Construction of Social Reality, Searle's examples are based on money, his insights can be transferred to the domain of organisational routines because organisational routines stem, among other things, from institutional rules and norms (Pentland, 2011: 285). Searle claims that the assignment of the function (Y) to an object, person and/or procedure (X) is performed by virtue of collective acceptance or recognition. Indeed, it is thanks to such collective acceptance or recognition that power relations can be explained within a formal system of rights, duties, and obligations. The very existence of such power relations presupposes their collective acceptance or recognition. According to Searle, power is to be understood in a very general sense of shaping the area of one's possibilities. It “covers any form of what one is able, entitled, expected or reguired to do within a certain framework de<sup>fi</sup>ned by collective acceptance” (Moural, 2002: 274).

Three crucial insights from Searle's work are particularly relevant to the study of organisational routines. First, constitutive rules predicated upon collective acceptance or recognition form the basis for the emergence of actual routines out of artefacts and/or procedures. Second, social institutions are above all enabling (Cf. Searle, 2005: 10; his italics) because they rely on a system of interlocked constitutive rules that create rather than determine the (deontic) powers, i.e. rights, duties and obligations, at people's disposal. Third, Searle's conceptual framework applies not only to social objects and technical artefacts, but also to social processes and events.

In Section 2 we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed areas of convergence in the organisational routines literature. Routines should refer to potential behaviour, i.e. rules, procedures and dispositions, as opposed to actual behaviour, i.e. recurrent action patterns (Becker, 2008). We have also maintained that organisational routines refer to organisations, whilst habits to individuals (Hodgson, 2008; Knudsen, 2008). Knudsen (2008), in particular, argues that organisational routines are organisational-level dispositions, whilst habits are individual-level dispositions and that, thanks to the execution of individual habits, a group of people can develop a common disposition to behave or think in a particular way in a particular class of situations. According to this line of scholarship, individual habits provide the substrate whereupon organisational routines are cast. Knudsen mentions several examples ranging from football games through job interviews to assembly lines. In every instance, there seems to be a clear sequence of individual habits where the execution of each habit triggers the next habit thanks to sequential responses to cues. Our contention is that these examples boil down to instances of collective behaviour (Searle, 2005), where participants act individually but their individual actions are parts of their collective behaviour. However, the shift from individual dispositions to collective dispositions or, put differently, from individual behaviours to collective behaviours revolves around language and, more broadly, symbolisation. In the context of a football game (C) for instance (Cf. Knudsen, 2008:

131), kicking the ball high (X) counts as rushing ahead to favourable target positions (Y). Thus, symbolisation is the crucial bridge between the individual and collective levels.

Furthermore, beyond a reality of physical movements, there is a reality of roles, positions, touchdowns, wins, defeats, etc. Searle attempts to capture the emergent properties of social structure by arguing that constitutive rules have an iterative feature, in that they can iterate upwards from a lower level, downwards from a higher level, as well as laterally within the same level (Cf. Searle, 2005: 9; Searle, 2008). This is because the status function or $\ " \mathrm { Y } ^ { \prime }$ term of one level can become the $\ " { } \mathbf { X } ^ { \prime }$ term or the $" C '$ term of the next level up. To illustrate this point, Searle makes some very simple but illuminating examples. “When I make certain sounds through my mouth $\left( \mathsf { X } _ { 1 } \right)$ , making these sounds counts as uttering sentences of English $\left( \mathsf { Y } _ { 1 } \right)$ ; but uttering those sentences of English $\left( \mathsf { Y } _ { 1 } = \mathsf { X } _ { 2 } \right)$ counts as making a promise $\left( \Upsilon _ { 2 } \right)$ ; and, in that context, making a promise $( \Upsilon _ { 2 } = \Upsilon _ { 3 } )$ counts as undertaking a contract $\left( \Upsilon _ { 3 } \right)$ . Making that kind of contract $( \Upsilon _ { 3 } = \Upsilon _ { 4 } )$ in that context counts as getting married $\mathrm { ( Y _ { 4 } ) }$ , and so on upwards” (Cf. Searle, 2005: 8). Locked into this institutional structure is a complex set of power relations that Searle labels as ‘deontic powers’, i.e. rights, duties, obligations, etc. considering that “status functions are the vehicles of power in society” (Searle, 2006: 59). For example, getting married counts as acquiring the “status” of husband and wife with its associated rights and duties. Crucially, these rights and duties presuppose their collective acceptance or recognition as “the creation of the status function, which is the same thing as the creation of the institutional fact, only works to the extent that it is collectively accepted” (Searle, 2008: 453). Ultimately, Searle argues that language, and more broadly symbolisation, is the underlying mechanism that warrants the creation of institutional reality, according to the $\ " \mathrm { x }$ counts as $\Upsilon ^ { \dag }$ locution because it ensures a double direction of <sup>fi</sup>t, i.e. from word (or mind) to world and from world to word (or mind).

## 4. Methodology

Against this backdrop, we deploy a qualitative research approach aimed at unpacking the ontological and epistemological intricacies of monitoring routines. Our qualitative approach could be labelled as constructionist though falling within the “weak” rather than “strong” camp (Schwandt, 2000), considering that in its weak form constructionism is compatible with critical realism (Cf. Sayer, 2000: 63).<sup>4</sup> Although a large portion of social facts is negotiated, institutional facts are epistemologically objective because they are independent of subjective interpretations. Institutional reality “infects our perceptual and other forms of cognitive apparatus” (Searle, 2006: 61) so much so “that the immediate processing of perceptual inputs is already done at the institutional level” (Searle, 2006: 61). Indeed, one could argue that such facts as money, language, roles, property rights, etc. are ontologically intersubjective and epistemologically objective (Searle, 1999), with language being the fundamental social institution. The logical structure of other social institutions depends on language or some form of symbolism and, therefore, representation (Searle, 2005).

Though some scholars have purported the qualitative testing of the effects triggered by generative structures through ethnographies (Dobson, 2001), our research strategy revolves around a rigorous dialogue between Searle's (1995) theory of social reality and the empirical data obtained through structured interviews, focus groups and observations. Though an ethnographic study is better suited to the analysis of culture from an “emic” (internal) perspective, our goal is to analyse the structural features of culture (Cf. Searle, 1995: 3) from an “etic” (external) perspective and explain the notion of generative systems in a highly-fragmented context of monitoring systems and indicators.

Our study was part of a broader research project funded by the European Commission to evaluate the monitoring systems deployed within ten EU Member States during the programming period 2000–2006. During the course of the investigation our analysis looked at the interplay between structure–process– outcome constructs (Cornford, Doukidis, & Forster, 1994), which was judged as being fundamental to the analysis of structure–agency interactions over time and, ultimately, the study of organisational routines as the crucial nexus between structure and agency (Pentland & Reuter, 1994). Our research design followed the canons of critical realism in general (Sayer, 2000) and realistic evaluation (Pawson & Tilly, 1997) in particular. We thus challenged the perceptual criterion for ascertaining the existence of events (Mingers, 2004; Sayer, 2000) and espoused a causal criterion for existence according to which “a plausible case for the existence of unobservable entities can be made by reference to observable effects which can only be explained as the products of such entities” (Sayer, 2000: 12).

Consistent with our research strategy, our data collection and analysis occurred iteratively along three phases, namely phase one: desk research; phase two: <sup>fi</sup>eldwork at federal government level; phase three: <sup>fi</sup>eldwork at state government level. Once <sup>fi</sup>eld notes and interviews were transcribed, they were coded along three axes, i.e. structures, processes and outcomes. Before the coding process took place, preliminary meetings were held to ensure a consistent use of our axial labels of structure, process and outcome. Based on these preliminary meetings, it was agreed that structure refers to the formal institutional structure of the Member State under investigation including its embedded rules, roles and positions. It was also agreed that process refers to how the main institutional bodies relate to one another; how relevant actors exchange information and how their technical artefacts work together as a system. Lastly it was agreed that outcomes refer to the intended or unintended consequences of the activities undertaken by the actors under study from different levels of analysis, namely the social/institutional, organisational and technical level.

We subsequently selected Germany to explore the structural context where monitoring routines unfold due to the wealth of data collected across two tiers of government, federal and state. Germany features a high degree of institutionalisation in the management and implementation of programmes co-funded by the ESF. Programme management is partitioned between federal and state levels of government and, in both cases, implementation is deemed to proceed along a highly-regulated set of functions and requirements de<sup>fi</sup>ning each institutional actor's role and activities. Our respondents suggested that, “the roles and mandates that actors have and ful<sup>fi</sup>l as part of the monitoring process… are regarded as the most important factors among those aspects that shape interactions among stakeholders” (ESF Policy and Programme Of<sup>fi</sup>cer). Yet Germany has a highly fragmented system of indicators as each state has developed its own set of indicators. While there is some liaison among states concerning monitoring indicators, there is no formal agreement to use the same indicators or software (Taylor, Bachtler, & Polverari, 2001). At the time of the investigation, several states were trying to build monitoring requirements (e.g., the completion of standardised monitoring sheets as a condition of assistance) into the implementation framework. We deemed these efforts toward heightened standardisation worthy of investigation because they could reveal the structural substrate that underpins the emergence of shared monitoring requirements.

Our approach to data analysis was exploratory and iterative. Several themes emerged, however we deemed the theme of organisational routines to be the most conspicuous as our informants were clearly implementing patterned sequences of activities to monitor the allocation and disbursement of the European Social Fund (ESF). Thus, we conceptualised our unit of analysis, the ESF monitoring process, as a set of interlocked organisational routines and attempted to understand this complex phenomenon by using existing theoretical concepts (e.g., performative/ostensive aspects drawn from Feldman & Pentland, 2003). However, the monitoring process under inyestigation was complex and we noticed that Feldman and Pentland's (2003) theory was an inadequate theoretical lens for examining the structural substrate that underpins the unfolding of monitoring routines. Though we were able to identify sequential patterns of activity carried out by multiple participants, i.e. what Pentland and Reuter (1994) call “syntactic constituents”, it was not entirely clear to us why, for instance, the monitoring routines participants were regarding the templates in use as their “of<sup>fi</sup>cial templates”. Our informants seemed to be following a bundled set of syntactic and semantic rules because the templates in question acquired a social meaning that literally de<sup>fi</sup>ned their identity. As well as performing an instrumental function. we noticed that such templates performed a symbolic or social function that went beyond their physical properties.

This raised the intriguing possibility of investigating a rare episode of institutionalisation in the making as such templates symbolised something beyond themselves by human agreement, whether tacit or explicit, in a public way, hence featuring the three essential traits of linguistic symbols (Cf, Searle. 1995: 66). Through a process of retroduction (Cf. Mingers, 2004: 94–95) we proposed hypothetical mechanisms, i.e. Searle's constitutive rules, that, if they existed, would explain the phenomena under investigation. On this basis we set out to revise Feldman and Pentland's (2003) theory of organisational routines by turning to Searle's notion of the “Background”: a reservoir of non-intentional “capacities, abilities, tendencies, habits, dispositions, taken-for-granted presuppositions, and ‘know-how’ generally” (Searle, 1999: 107–108) that enabled the monitoring routines participants to perceive the templates as their “of<sup>fi</sup>cial templates”. By interweaving theory with data iteratively we arrived at a revised ontology of organisational routines that sheds light on the notion of routines as generative systems in general and the symbolic role of artefacts in particular.

In addition to our triangulation of data collection methods through interviews, focus groups and observations, our qualitative data were elicited through meetings with informants representing three actor organisations, namely members of staff working within federal agencies, employees within state agencies, as well as Systems Managers coordinating training sessions on the use of the templates. The number and typology of informants was determined in collaboration with the European Commission, in order to achieve theoretical saturation with regard to the analysis of structure–agency interactions over time. These programmes come under speci<sup>fi</sup>c policy priorities (e.g., matching labour market supply and demand, creating “new economy” jobs, etc.), and consist of individual projects grouped in separate measures (or types of projects) for each programme.

A regulation central to the ESF is the EU Regulation 1260/99 that has set out a new management framework of cohesion programmes, requiring the introduction of new actors, rules and procedures for managing, monitoring and implementing ESF programmes by EU member states. New actors include the programme Managing Authority, the Paying Authority and the Monitoring Committee. The Managing Authority is the body designated to manage a speci<sup>fi</sup>c ESF programme; it can be a public or private authority at national, regional or local level. The Paying Authority is a public agency entrusted with the power of disbursing ESF funds to the so-called Bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary Organisations which, in turn, are private or public <sup>fi</sup>rms responsible for commissioning programme operations in the form of projects, carried out by individual Service or Project Providers. The Monitoring Committee oversees programme implementation. Monitoring is a key procedure in this context. The <sup>fl</sup>ow of EU funding to the national government and ultimately to Bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary Organisations and Providers depends on the approval of yearly <sup>fi</sup>nancial and implementation reports submitted to the European Commission. These reports are based on monitoring data collected on individual projects and Providers.

Regulation 1260/99 has stipulated different types of indicators for monitoring programme, project and Provider performance such as inputs, outputs, results and impacts. ESF programmes must be monitored by means of <sup>fi</sup>nancial and physical indicators, the former referring to <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources needed to implement individual projects (e.g., payments, eligible costs, etc.), the latter demonstrating progress made in implementing these projects (e.g., number of training places, number of trainees successfully completing their training sessions, number of training hours, etc.). Managing Authorities need to work in partnership with other actors to track the amount of <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources expended in individual projects (inputs), measure the number of training hours delivered (outputs), capture the number of successful trainees (results), and assess the longterm effect of projects (impacts). In turn, these indicators are used in the evaluation and subsequent adapta tion of programmes, a process in which EU and national actors participate.

In Germany, Regulation 1260/99 has been enacted through its transposition in domestic legislation in a manner that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the German institutional structures, laws and policies relevant to the labour market. The German federal government has created federal and state Managing Authorities and Monitoring Committees whose operation has followed the EU stipulations and domestic governance rules and norms. It has codi<sup>fi</sup>ed the institutional principle of partnership among policy stakeholder organisations in purposely-crafted templates that have become instrumental for data collection, storage and processing in the context of best practices embedded in the Template Process handbook. These templates are standardised forms (Star & Griesemer, 1989) that enable a large number of monitoring routines participants to coordinate their activities across federal and state levels. They include standardised indexes and indicators in accordance with the ESF and EU terminology.

Moreover, the use of these templates by federal and state organisations and their staff has been phased in through a programme of training workshops, whose aim has been to align users' understandings of concepts and terminology and thus support the activation and use of such templates in a standardised fashion. As a Systems Manager noted:

“We devoted a lot of time and effort at the start of the implementation of the templates to prepare all users [including Service or Project Providers and Bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary Organisations]. What we wanted [to do] with the templates, what the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the templates were, what was necessary [to do] in the framework of our collaboration with them, what was necessary [to do] vis-à-vis the Commission, and also to advise on the meaning and importance of the data in the ESF framework of implementation in Germany. This is why we have invested so much time in these training sessions. We have run them together with the Managing Authority and the Monitoring Committee and partly also with the commissioned company that has developed the template system, to prepare and train the users in many different groups. We wanted to avoid that the physical and <sup>fi</sup>nancial measures were not followed properly, or the data [items] were not entered properly.”

The use of data collection templates has been integrated in the German ESF management structure as follows. At federal level, monitoring starts with the activation of a project record in the purpose-built system, ESF-Online, by staff of the federal Managing Authority, a division of the federal Ministry of Labour. Initially, the record includes the project's basic data, namely programme name, priority (or goal) and measure (or type of project). Then, a “Project” template is generated together with a Service or Project Provider user name and password. Service or Project Provider staff are required to key in physical data through the use of “Participant” (e.g., trainees' data) and “Enterprise” (e.g., companies that have been sub-contracted for providing training sessions) templates, while staff of Bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary Organisations enter the aggregate <sup>fi</sup>nancial data of their programme(s). Service or Project Providers are formally advised at the start, through their grant awarding decision, that the ful<sup>fi</sup>lment of monitoring tasks is a formal responsibility undertaken by them, that data need to be keyed in orderly, and that this is tied with subsequent funding disbursement to them. As noted by one of the ESF Programme Of<sup>fi</sup>cers:

“We make it clear that, if data are not entered in the templates, then there is no money, after project closure.”

The formal requirement for data entry by means of the templates has essentially encoded the partnership principle in such templates as they bridge the information requirements of heterogeneous social worlds, namely Managing Authorities and other government agencies on the one hand and Bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary Organisations and Service or Project Providers on the other. By virtue of the aforementioned training sessions and thanks to legal requirements, Service or Project Providers have developed a tendency, or disposition, to perceive such templates as their “of<sup>fi</sup>cial templates”. As observed during <sup>fi</sup>eldwork:

“The templates represent the of<sup>fi</sup>cial route to ESF funding.” (ISG Staff Member)

Furthermore, from an exchange with a Service or Project Provider:

“By keying in physical data in the templates we are authorised to submit claims for funding at project closure”. (Service or Project Provider)

Thus, the templates have acquired a particular status, i.e. the status of “of<sup>fi</sup>cial templates”, to which a function has been attached, i.e. the function to authorise Service or Project Providers to submit claims for funding. In other words, Service or Project Providers have developed a legitimate expectation during their training sessions that, by entering physical data in the templates orderly, they are entitled to ESF funding. While being individually-held, such a disposition is also a social phenomenon in that it re<sup>fl</sup>ects a common understanding of concepts, terminology and information requirements that undergirds monitoring routines participants' enactment and re-enactment of their activities:

“Service or Project Providers are required to ful<sup>fi</sup>l distinct tasks and their understanding of concepts and terminology has converged towards meeting monitoring requirements. There is the requirement of the grant agreement to report on the physical dimension of their projects by making use of the Project, Participant and Enterprise templates and indicators included in those [templates]. [Physical] data are required at the start and the end of a project. If a project spans several years, physical data need to be made available once a year for the preparation of the annual report.” (Federal ESF Programme Of<sup>fi</sup>cer)

To be sure, the convergence around a common understanding of information requirements, concepts and terminology has also been enhanced by the participation of Service or Project Providers in a consultation process aimed at boosting their commitment to the template process:

“Service or Project Providers took part in a consultation process. The result of these deliberations has been a plan to shorten the Participants' template primarily, but also the Enterprise Template. In conjunction with other stakeholders, the Managing Authority has examined the process as it has run until now. On the basis of past experience, it has been agreed that it is possible to reduce the [data in the] templates and still acquire the data necessary for successful programme management and implementation. This effort is now also based on estimations of the workload imposed on them and on the ongoing deliberation on which indicators are those that bring the main bene<sup>fi</sup>ts through monitoring.” (ESF Programme Manager)

Not only has the enactment and re-enactment of these legally-binding procedures reinforced the under lying assumptions, expectations and knowledge that Service or Project Providers have about the templates, i.e. the expectation to exchange physical data for ESF funding but it has also buttressed Managing Authorities and Monitoring Committees' previously-held assumptions about the role of the ESF as a funding instrument. As noted by one of our interviewees:

“The ESF has had a traditional focus on people. Monitoring and evaluation based on the use of the “of<sup>fi</sup>cial templates” gives us the opportunity to make a real difference in the lives of those people who are experiencing dif<sup>fi</sup>culties.” (ESF Monitoring and Evaluation Of<sup>fi</sup>cer)

The identity associated with the templates, therefore, has become a composite of various identities. What is more, the collective disposition to enter physical (and <sup>fi</sup>nancial) data in the templates in exchange for funding has become the main source of patterned activities and interactions over time as data entry in the “of<sup>fi</sup>cial templates” has prompted a process of data validation and processing aimed at ascertaining that there are no implausible entries in the templates. To automate this process, federal and state actors have adopted a system by means of which Service or Project Providers are given feedback whenever, for example, there are errors or miscalculations in the aggregate data. This feedback mechanism is based on a colour code system:

“If the Service or Project Provider has correctly and plausibly entered physical data, a green light appears in the web user interface window. A yellow light indicates that template data have not been fully entered up to that point in time. The data sections that have not been <sup>fi</sup>lled in as yet are also shown. A red light is indicative of errors in the data” (Systems Manager)

In addition to automated checks, the Managing Authority cross-checks that Service or Project Providers' claims for funding match their physical data. To be sure, such checks cover not only physical but also <sup>fi</sup>nancial data as aggregate eligible costs need to match their corresponding funding allocations. Through the use of the ESF-Online and the associated templates, <sup>fi</sup>nancial and physical data are wholly registered and aggregated on a commonly accessible platform and a unique recording format. Data entries subsequently go through various <sup>fi</sup>lters and plausibility controls. In the words of one of our interviewees:

“The Managing Authority in conjunction with the Monitoring Committee is the organisation that ultimately assumes this responsibility [of data validation/processing]. But there are many in-between controls, because in the systems there are plausibility and other controls, and also locally-run controls aimed at ensuring that responsibilities are being kept up with” (ESF Programme Manager)

The instantiation of organisational routines is based on social (e.g., regulations, policy statements, handbooks, job descriptions, words, utterances, etc.) and technical artefacts (e.g., standardised forms. electronic systems. etc.) mediating interactions among monitoring routines participants. For example, a colour code system is used to verify data accuracy whenever Service or Project Providers feed physical data in the electronic systems at hand. By the same token, data collection templates are actively used to keep track of projects from their very start to their closure. Thus, one could argue that a broad and heterogeneous array of artefacts has been deployed in Germany to perform the monitoring routines under investigation. However, a myopic focus on artefacts and interlocked action patterns (or activities) may neglect the deeper structural features at play. For example, the data collection templates are regarded as the “of<sup>fi</sup>cial templates” by monitoring routines participants who are legally required to enter <sup>fi</sup>nancial and physical data in such templates. The repeated execution of individual tasks coupled with training workshops aimed at aligning monitoring routines participants' understandings of concepts and terminology has created a background of shared premises, assumptions and dispositions (Hodgson, 2008; Knudsen, 2008; Searle, 1995; Vaast & Walsham, 2005). Against this background the “status” of “of<sup>fi</sup>cial templates” has collectively been assigned to the data collection templates in use, thus generating an institutional fact, i.e. the “of<sup>fi</sup>cial template” or “Y” term, which is not a mere epiphenomenon of its corresponding brute fact, i.e. the data collection sheet or the “X” term. Similarly, the complex set of interdependent activities monitoring routines consist of, i.e. data entry, data validation/processing, disbursement of funding, is predicated upon a role system whereby institutional actors (and their employees) are granted abstract operational requirements (Kallinikos, 2004) enacted on a recurrent basis.

In short, a <sup>fi</sup>gure-ground reversal of the monitoring routines in question reveals the existence of deeper structural features. We submit that a <sup>fi</sup>gure-ground reversal can shed new light on the very notion of organisational routines as generative systems by distinguishing between potentialities and actualities. Our claim is that, by casting in the foreground that which lies in the background, we can propose a revised ontology of organisational routines that identi<sup>fi</sup>es three distinct ontological levels, the real, the actual and the empirical. This revised ontology can take Feldman and Pentland's (2003) analytical separation between the levels of action and representation (or structure) forward by addressing the ontological and epistemological issues surrounding the level of action and representation. For example, it is telling that in Feldman and Pentland's account of ostensive aspects of routines, after conceding that the ostensive and performative aspects of organisational routines are “closely related to the concepts of structure and agency, as found in structuration theory (Giddens, 1984)”, Feldman and Pentland (2003: 100) propound that the “ostensive aspect is the ideal or schematic form of a routine”, as well as the “subjective understandings of diverse participants”. If the ostensive aspect of a routine is simultaneously “the abstract, generalized idea of the routine, or the routine in principle” (Feldman and Pentland, 2003: 101), as well as the “embodied and cognitive understandings that guide actions taken in the enactment of routines” (Pentland & Feldman, 2008: 242), somewhere there must be a conceptual slippage because we are moving from an epistemologically-objective statement to an epistemologically-subjective one.<sup>5</sup>

Another way to address this conceptual issue is to recall that, according to critical realism, there exists a three-fold ontological strati<sup>fi</sup>cation between the domains of the real (what exists), actual (events), and empirical (observable events). The crucial feature of this strati<sup>fi</sup>cation being “that structures and their generative mechanisms are ontologically decoupled from the events that they produce” (Longshore Smith, 2006: 201). This implies that structures, i.e. ostensive aspects of routines, and agency, i.e. performative aspects of routines, are ontologically decoupled because they fall within different domains, the real/actual and the empirical respectively. Since “the abstract, generalized idea” and “abstract regularities and expectations” (Pentland & Feldman, 2008: 241) are ontologically decoupled, treating them as substitutes would con<sup>fl</sup>ate distinct ontological domains but this is exactly what these two scholars do when they refer to the ostensive aspects of routines as consisting of “abstract regularities and expectations that enable participants to guide, account for, and refer to speci<sup>fi</sup>c performances of a routine” (Pentland & Feldman, 2008: 241). On the contrary, we propound that “the abstract, generalized idea, or the routine in principle” has a different ontology and epistemology altogether, because it may be conceived as an institutional fact that is ontologically intersubjective and epistemologically objective (Searle, 1999). In set-theoretic terms (Ragin, 2008), “the abstract, generalized idea, or the routine in principle” could be conceived as an abstract superset among several subsets representing the individuals' embodied and cognitive understandings. Thus, the collective representation or understanding, i.e. “we understand”, could be portrayed as the union of a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient number of individual understandings where “portions of the envisaged system are known to all, but all is known to none” (Weick & Roberts, 1993: 365). However, the “of<sup>fi</sup>cial SOP” $\left( \mathsf { Y } _ { 1 } = \mathsf { C } _ { 2 } \right)$ and its corresponding artefact $( \mathsf { X } _ { 1 } = \mathsf { t h e }$ Template Process handbook) is part of a deeper background $\left( \mathsf { C } _ { 1 } \right)$ of deontic powers predicated upon collective acceptance or recognition considering that monitoring routines participants have signed grant-awarding decisions whereby they have collectively accepted or recognised the emergence of new status functions, i.e. roles, with their interlocked set of deontic powers.<sup>6</sup> Thus, whether instantiated in laws, regulations, programming documents, data collection templates, SOPs or otherwise, these artefacts are markers for the amount of deontic powers that the agents have (Searle, 2005).

To break with Feldman and Pentland's terminology we adopt critical realist language to capture the socio-cognitive, cognitive, material and human aspects of routines in a strati<sup>fi</sup>ed fashion. The sociocognitive aspects of routines may be labelled as actual routines because they denote abstract ideas that are the result of the activation of an underlying mechanism, i.e. the constitutive rule. Viewed from an ontological perspective, actual routines may be conceived as intersubjective facts within a social structure of power relations. Viewed from an epistemological perspective, actual routines correspond to objective knowledge that is available within a particular socio-cultural context as a “stable and socially-shared set of common knowledge and ideas” (Vaast & Walsham, 2005: 69). The cognitive, material and human aspects instead may be labelled as empirical routines. They consist of orderly or systematic arrangements of processes, i.e. social practices, and products, i.e. artefacts (Hevner, March, Park, & Ram, 2004), or, put differently, verbs, i.e. diachronic instantiations, and nouns, i.e. synchronic instantiations. Whereas material aspects designate instantiations of abstract ideas in the form of artefacts at speci<sup>fi</sup>c points in time, cognitive and human aspects are embedded within social practices that unfold over time. While instantiations are realisations, concretisations or materialisations of abstract ideas, performances bring into existence abstract ideas “through the actual doing of them” (Pollock & Williams, 2007: 133). Although instantiations and performances are embedded within social practices, this is not necessarily the case for (technological) artefacts. Our re-conceptualisation of organisational routines is depicted in Fig. 4.

According to this logic, actual routines may be instantiated at a point in time (by inscribing them into speci<sup>fi</sup>c artefacts whether tangible or intangible) but they may also consist of diachronic performances (e.g., social interactions) enacted by speci<sup>fi</sup>c people in speci<sup>fi</sup>c places and unfolding over a speci<sup>fi</sup>c period of time. They may also be stored within electronic systems (a particular type of digital artefacts) as, for example, work logs and databases can provide an archival trace of social interactions (Pentland & Reuter, 1994). Accordingly, diachronic performances create, maintain or modify artefacts while being enabled constrained and/or represented by them. However, they fall within the domain of empirical routines because they correspond to events, i.e. diachronic performances and/or artefacts, which are observed and/or experi enced. Empirical routines, in turn, are a subset of actual routines, the latter being ontologicallyintersubjective facts or epistemologically-objective ideas (e.g., the “data entry” routine) generated by language or more broadly by a system of constitutive rules of the type “X counts as Y in context C” (Searle, 1995). Lastly, language, as a symbolic system of constitutive rules, has a logical structure with its own gener ative properties because language, and consequently representation, is the fundamental building block of social reality (Searle, 2005).

We submit that our revised ontology of organisational routines sheds new light on the notion of routines as generative systems. In our model, the building blocks of organisational routines are neither human nor material agencies, but the interlocked system of constitutive rules whose activation grants a particular “status” to an object (e.g., a textual, verbal, digital artefact, etc.), person (e.g., a quali<sup>fi</sup>ed trainer, a contractor, etc.) and/or procedure (e.g., a process, a practice, etc.), thanks to its collective acceptance or recognition. This implies that constitutive rules are the key mechanism for the emergence of actual routines because they grant empirical events the ability to symbolise something beyond themselves. Indeed, in our case study the templates came to symbolise something beyond themselves, i.e. the “of<sup>fi</sup>cial templates”, by virtue of a collective agreement. The same can be said for the Template Process handbook which became the “of<sup>fi</sup>cial SOP”, i.e. a checklist of best practices.

Not only are constitutive rules the generative mechanisms of actual routines but the conceptualisation of organisational routines as interlocked systems of constitutive rules can also interweave the distinct but interrelated streams emerging from the routines literature. By casting structure and agency across separate but interpenetrated ontological domains, one can identify three strata. On the empirical level, there are events that are observed and/or experienced which correspond to the empirical aspects of organisational routines as observers and participants alike respectively observe and/or experience the unfolding of the organisational routine, i.e. the routine in practice, regardless of the type of artefact that is being used. Such socio-technical practices encompass actors, artefacts, activities and interactions that are enacted in a recurrent fashion, thus creating recognisable, repetitive patterns of interdependent activities. Indeed, the recurrent enactment of empirical routines is such that their referent, i.e. the routine in principle, exists regardless of what the participants themselves think, believe or feel when enacting it. On the level of the actual, we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the routine in principle or the abstract idea, that emerges from the activation (or not) of systems of constitutive rules of the type “X counts as Y in context C” because the ontological level of the actual consists of events that do (or do not) occur. Lastly, on the level of the real, there are underlying mechanisms that correspond to the aforementioned constitutive rules that have the generative property to create actual routines out of artefacts and/or procedures in a pre-existing context of power relations informed by an ensemble of individual and collective dispositions.

What is the implication of this re-conceptualisation of organisational routines? By identifying three distinct and irreducible ontological levels, and by casting structural and agentic aspects of routines across these levels, we submit that scholars can shed new light on the intransitivity (or not) of social artefacts in general and codi<sup>fi</sup>ed rules in particular (Cf. Becker, 2005: 251). But what are artefacts? Far from being proxies for the ostensive aspects of routines, artefacts may be conceptualised as material instantiations of abstract ideas (Leonardi, 2010) and, therefore, “as one of the multiple possible expressions or codi<sup>fi</sup>cations that an actual routine can assume” (D'Adderio, 2011: 223). In other words, artefacts may be conceived of as entities “containing selective instantiations or selective configurations of ostensive views” (D'Adderio, 2011: 223; her italics). It is worth noting that our conceptualisation of artefacts does not deny that they may have some kind of structure. However, this is a physical structure rather than a system of interlocked constitutive rules that generate institutional facts. Artefacts may be either social or technical (Kroes & Meijers, 2006). Social (or symbolic) artefacts serve a function to a large degree independently of their physical properties (e.g., laws, money, codi<sup>fi</sup>ed rules, etc.). Technical artefacts are different from social artefacts in that the realisation of their (instrumental) function crucially depends on their physical structure (e.g., a “project template” serves as a data collection, storage, retrieval and sharing tool in virtue of its form). Nevertheless, social or symbolic functions that are not intrinsic to their physical properties are collectively assigned to such artefacts too (Runde et al., 2009; Searle, 1995).

Having clari<sup>fi</sup>ed the difference between social and technical artefacts, we are now in a position to address the issue concerning the intransitivity (or not) of social artefacts in general and codi<sup>fi</sup>ed rules in particular. As institutional facts (e.g., the “of<sup>fi</sup>cial Template Process SOP”), codi<sup>fi</sup>ed rules are placeholders for patterns of activities that generate standing possibilities (e.g., the “of<sup>fi</sup>cial SOP” is a standing possibility of following a programme of action). As forms of representation of the agentic aspects of routines (e.g., socio-technical practices), codi<sup>fi</sup>ed rules clearly encompass regulative aspects because they either prescribe or describe pre-existing socio-technical practices (e.g., the exchange of data for monitoring purposes existed prior to the deployment of the Template Process handbook).<sup>7</sup> Although embedded within the same artefact, codi<sup>fi</sup>ed rules play both a constitutive and a regulative role. Viewed as constitutive rules, their referent is the abstract idea that exists at least in part independently of any knowledge claims of which it is the referent. Viewed as regulative rules. their referent is a pre-existing socio-technical practice rather than the abstract idea, It follows that one and the same social artefact is analytically separable in two different types of rules, regulative rules being differentiated from, but not exterior to the constitutive rules. Even though regulative and constitutive rules are entangled (Iannacci, 2010), they are two different types of rules, the former being sanctions, norms or even regulative technological regimes (Kallinikos, 2005; Van de Poel, 2003) that regulate pre-existing activities, the latter being drawn upon in the production and reproduction of empirical routines. Accordingly, constitutive rules should be located in the domain of the real, their regulative aspects instead falling in the domain of the empirical considering that “constitutive rules not only regulate but rather consti tute the very behaviour they regulate, because acting in accordance with a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient number of the rules is constitutive of the behaviour in question” (Searle, 2005: 9).

Our study addresses the issue of the intransitivity (or not) of codi<sup>fi</sup>ed rules, and brings the notion of power back in the organisational routines literature. Whether re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the capacity to mobilise material or human resources (Howard-Grenville, 2005), we contend that this capacity presupposes collective acceptance of rights and corresponding duties. Locked into this institutional web of material and human relations, and in a way constituting the very substrate where patterns of activities unfold, monitoring routines participants have interlocked rights and duties. For example, “Service or Project Providers” have the duty to enter physical data in the “of<sup>fi</sup>cial templates” in an orderly fashion but, pending the ful<sup>fi</sup>lment of this task, the “Bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary Organisation” has the obligation to provide them with <sup>fi</sup>nancial support, thus mobilising <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources.

## 7. Conclusion

Admittedly, our revised ontology of organisational routines has focused mostly on the ostensive aspects of organisational routines and has neglected the performative aspects to a degree, Writing about new studies of organisational routines, Pentland (2011: 288) has maintained that “in each of these studies, the main source of data consists of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c actions taken by participants as they do their work. This is what Feldman and Pentland (2003) call the ‘performative’ aspect of the routine”. Rather than providing an in-depth study of the performative aspects of organisational routines on a par with new and extant research (Becker & Lazaric, 2009), our study aims to redress this imbalance by focusing on the ostensive aspects of organisational routines. To be sure, our revised ontology of organisational routines is compatible with these new studies. For instance, Leonardi (2011) has persuasively argued that human and material agencies are the building blocks of organisational routines and technologies. If one were to study the empirical domain in the model outlined above (see Fig. 4), she would <sup>fi</sup>nd out that the complex interaction between human agencies, i.e. diachronic performances, and material agencies, i.e. artefactual performances, may provide rich empirical insights with regard to organisational routines. Pentland et al. (2010), on their part, studied invoice processing in four small Norvegian organisations. They discovered that the percentage of actions taken by the humans ranged from 15% to 89%, whilst the computerised work<sup>fl</sup>ow system used for invoice processing performed the rest of the action. Again, this insight is consistent with our conceptualisation of artefacts that puts them on an equal footing with the human aspects of routines. Indeed, in terms of our model, Pentland et al. (2010) <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that technological artefacts, compared with other types of technical artefacts, are not merely instrumental to human action, but they can also replace human action (or agency), thus acquiring a more performative/agentic role.<sup>8</sup>

Another limitation of this work is that. in line with critical realism. we have focused on how social structures are produced (and reproduced) rather than their transformation (Mingers, 2004). For instance, we have acknowledged that institutional facts can be created de novo when we argued that the data collection templates became the “of<sup>fi</sup>cial templates” in Germany thanks to their collective acceptance or recognition and that such acceptance or recognition has to be symbolic and, therefore, linguistic in the most general sense (Searle, 2006). An additional limitation is that, in our quest to explain the emergence of structure and regularities, we have somehow neglected the role of constitutive rules as “guiding principles” for ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and <sup>fl</sup>exibility, as well as standardisation and coordination (Van de Poel, 2003). However, within the model of organisational routines developed in this paper, episodes of endogenous change may be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. For example, Pentland and Feldman (2005) point to an interesting case in which employment ads are designed and simultaneously disparaged as not really being instances of the hiring routine in principle within a speci<sup>fi</sup>c setting. According to our re-conceptualisation of organisational routines, borderline cases such as this one are clearly characterised by mismatches between the actual routines performed through social interactions, and those routines inscribed into employment ads (which are instantiations of extant legislation and, therefore, the legislator's abstract idea rather than the abstract idea that is shared in the employment context under investigation).<sup>9</sup> Such cases could clearly trigger instances of endogenous change and subsequent alignment between employment ads and social interactions or, alternatively, employment ads could be seen as being merely descriptive rather than prescriptive (D'Adderio, 2008), notwithstanding current legislation. Further research is required to see what types of routines dynamics prevail in what types of socio-cultural settings through diachronic studies that look at the unfolding of routines over time.

Moving from a critical realist perspective, this paper unpacks the concept of ostensive/performative aspects of routines. It argues that, although artefacts play a central role in the unfolding of organisational routines (D'Adderio, 2011; Kallinikos, 2010; Rafaeli & Vilnai-Yavetz, 2004) they are simply instantiations of actual routines (Leonardi, 2010) not proxies for the ostensive aspects of routines. In addition, arguments that propound that the ostensive aspects of routines consist “of the understandings (embodied as well as cognitive) of the participants” (Pentland & Feldman, 2008: 241) commit a “category mistake” (Bateson, 1972) because they con<sup>fl</sup>ate ostensive aspects with performative aspects. On the contrary, the ostensive aspects of routines should be conceived as dualities that consist of intersubjective facts when viewed from an ontological angle and objective knowledge, i.e. the routine in principle, when analysed with epistemic lenses. While the ostensive aspects of routines so conceived fall within the realm of the actual, the performative and, more in general, agentic aspects fall in the empirical domain. In every walk of life whether within the domain of monitoring routines or hiring procedures, social reality is a complex edi<sup>fi</sup>ce that revolves around the institution of language as a system of representation. We submit that a movement from surface to depth (Longshore Smith, 2006) is of the utmost importance if one is to understand the ontology and epistemology of organisational routines and, as a consequence, the role of language and, more broadly, of constitutive rules as the “glue” that holds organisational routines together.

In addition to its theoretical implications, this paper has implications for practice as it shows that, although the enactment of monitoring routines is based on a broad and heterogeneous array of artefacts, participants operating in the German federal setting had to resort to the creation of a new artefact, the data collection template, to ensure coordination of a fragmented monitoring system. Though templates operate as boundary objects (Star & Griesemer, 1989) across different states within a federal institutional setting, it is worth stressing that successful coordination rests on the management of such templates, not simply their creation. In our case study, resources were invested to train monitoring participants how to use such templates, and a review of these templates took place over time through a consultation process to reduce the number of indicators used and boost commitment to the template process.

To put a <sup>fi</sup>nal seal to this paper, although sources of ambiguities regarding the very conceptualisation of routines as cognitive vs. behavioural regularities are still looming large in the routines literature (Becker, 2004), this paper helps to overcome these ambiguities by casting both cognitive and behavioural regularities within the empirical domain which, in turn, is part of a broader space of possibilities that is delimited by the actual realm, i.e. the institutional realm. Against this backdrop, constitutive rules work as the cognitive principles that underpin the emergence of institutional facts and, indirectly, their associated regularities, thus closing the double gap identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Becker's (2004) insightful literature review. Further research is needed on this subject based on a re-conceptualisation of organisational routines as systems of interlocked constitutive rules.
