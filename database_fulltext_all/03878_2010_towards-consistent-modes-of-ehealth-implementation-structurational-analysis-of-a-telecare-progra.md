# Towards consistent modes of e-health implementation: structurational analysis of a telecare programme’s limited success

Albert Boonstra & Marjolein van Offenbeek

University of Groningen, Faculty of Economics & Business, PO Box 800, 9700 AV Groningen, The Netherlands, email: albert.boonstra@rug.nl; email: m.a.g.van.offenbeek@rug.nl

Abstract. Telecare is the use of information and communication systems to facilitate care delivery to individuals in their homes. Although the expectations of telecare are high, its implementation has proved complex. This case study demonstrates this complexity through a structurational analysis of a telecare implementation process. The paper shows how structuration concepts enable a combined analysis of actors’ interactions with a technology and of the interaction among these actors from different institutional contexts. In this example, frag mented multi-actor agency induced an inconsistent implementation mode, leading to unsuccessful telecare appropriation. This paper concludes with a preliminary proposal for more consistent telecare implementation modes. These modes may better support the actors’ reflexive monitoring and dialogue and inform further research.

Keywords: telecare, e-health, structurational model, stakeholders, institutiona context, implementation

## INTRODUCTION

Most Western countries are facing a future with an aging population, and as a consequence, rising health care costs (Poisal et al., 2007; OECD, 2009). A situation of increasing demands for health care coupled with limited budgets encourages health care providers and insurers to develop efficient ways to expand their current services. In achieving equal or even better services at lower costs, information and communication technologies are portrayed as part of the solution (Sauer & Willcocks, 2007). As a result, an e-health field has emerged that includes health-related applications such as telemedicine, shared medical records, e-cure and domot ics. This paper offers a structurational analysis of the complexity inherent in implementing one e-health application, telecare.

Telecare is the use of information and communication technology to facilitate health and social care delivery to individuals in their homes (Barlow et al., 2006). The telecare technology consists of a videophone system, which works through the client’s TV that includes a camera, a set-top box and a remote control, allowing the client to have videophone conversations with a carer. Telecare services can involve care-related information (such as health or diet advice), monitoring of health conditions (including advice about drug use) and safety issues (such as warning about an overflowing bath or an unlocked door) and social care (such as offering morning and/or evening chats). Services can be directed at prevention and daily care or at an acute need, for example if a client falls (Barlow et al., 2006). A telecare connection generally indicates the existence of a corresponding service centre that receives and handles client calls, sometimes on a round-the-clock basis. In many situations, telecare services are provided as part of integrated care arrangements or in combination with other care means. Institutions providing homecare generally tend to use telecare as a substitute for other means of care provision, on the basis that it enables them to maintain or even improve the quality of care a a lower cost.

Policymakers have high expectations of telecare with many policy reports on telecare adopting an optimistic and deterministic view (Department of Health, 2005; Scheepbouwer, 2006; Sauer & Willcocks, 2007; European Information Society, 2008). These reports emphasize the medical, economic, social and political advantages by suggesting that telecare will, in itself, improve the lives of people needing care, while at the same time saving public money. Following these expectations, governments and health insurers seem eager to financially support telecare initiatives (Urtubey & Petrich, 2002; Fisk, 2003, p. 169; Department of Health, 2005; Scheepbouwer, 2006).

Two reviews show that telecare evaluation studies within the homecare sector are actually rare (Koch. 2006: Bensink et al., 2007). A meta-analysis within the homecare sector finds support for the expectation of improved care, but no convincing evidence of a decrease in costs (DelliFraine & Dansky, 2008). Moreover, translating small-scale trials with positive care outcomes into large-scale practices has proven difficult (Barlow et al., 2005; Hebert et al., 2006). The high expectations compared with the disappointing outcomes raise questions about the complexities involved in telecare implementation. Earlier studies only focus on the content and effects of telecare programmes, rather than on their implementation process and context (Barlow et al., 2006), a view confirmed in Broesamle’s review (2007).

This paper addresses this gap by providing a structurational analysis of a large-scale telecare programme. Through combining specific structurational concepts, this paper also contributes to the information system (IS) structuration literature. The analysis unravels the complexities inherent to telecare implementation, and shows how these have led to inconsistencies in the implementation choices which, in turn, have limited successful appropriation. In this way, the study addresses the following research question: how do the interactions among the diverse institutional contexts, stakeholders and technology during telecare implementation determine telecare’s appropriation?

In the following section we present our research model. The model reflects the view that a structurational perspective is required because of the complexities inherent to e-health implementation. Once we have described our method and case, this model is used to analyse the interactions that evolved during this particular telecare implementation. Following this analysis, we then posit that the stakeholders’ underestimation of telecare’s interpretative flexibility – viewing it is a proven technology – made them less aware of the inconsistencies in and between their implementation choices and the ongoing development of the technology as it was used. These inconsistencies stemmed from the different sets of rules and resources that were enlisted in the telecare implementation process. Starting with the inconsistencies found during the implementation, the discussion section works towards establishing four more consistent telecare implementation modes that acknowledge the various institutional contexts possible. This preliminary proposal for more consistent telecare implementation modes is the paper’s contribution to IS practice.

## THEORETICAL BACKGROUNDS

Properly addressing the complex nature of e-health implementation requires a theory that offers a simultaneous analysis of the interaction between the actors and the technology as well as the interplay among the stakeholders, with their different institutional backgrounds, involved. Such a theory also needs to acknowledge that these two types of interaction are interwoven. This paper therefore draws on structurationism, which offers a user-friendly set of sensitizing concepts for social analysis (Layder, 1994; Weaver & Gioia, 1994). A central concept in structurationism is ‘duality of structure’ (Giddens, 1984; 1993), meaning that the structural properties of institutional systems are considered as both the medium and as the outcome of human interaction. These structural properties consist of the rules and resources that govern action. Human actors can modify their circumstances, while the existing rules and resources both enable and constrain their actions. At the same time, people can recreate structural properties by acting according to, and thereby endorsing, the values of existing cognition, norms and resources. Technology can be seen as a carrier of the institutional system’s structural properties (Orlikowski, 1992), or as a social institution in itself (Van der Blonk, 2002). Two concepts that build on Giddens’ duality of structure are ‘technology appropriation’ and ‘social multidimensionality’. These are discussed below and then combined in a model that guides our analysis.

## Technology appropriation

Since the 1990s, studies have been published on the two-way interaction between IS tech nology and organizational actors (Barley, 1986; 1990; Walsham & Han, 1991; Orlikowski, 1992; 2000; Orlikowski & Gash, 1994; Sahay, 1997; Yates et al., 1999; Orlikowski & Barley, 2001; Van der Blonk, 2002; Chu & Smithson, 2007). This soft-deterministic view acknowledges that technology influences human behaviour. However, actors have interpretative flexibility: they develop their own reasons and motivations for their actions. On the one hand, a technology’s properties do define the rules (techniques, norms, procedures), while the resources (allocative and authoritative) empower action (Giddens, 1984). However, on the other hand, people use and develop the technology, producing and reproducing its structural properties and social practices through their activities. Designers construct information systems according to their priorities and expectations. Other actors, such as users and managers, may select, specify, reject or adapt these systems during their various life cycle stages. In so doing, they socially construct the technology in the sense that their responses may become features of the technology-in-use.

Studies into the ‘duality of technology’ show that information systems are not fixed, given pieces of technology (Jones & Karsten, 2008, p. 142): actors may interpret and use the same technology in fundamentally different ways, and modify a system through their interaction with it. Consequently, the outcome of a technology-enabled change can best be regarded as a product of the interaction between technology and people over an extended period of time. Such studies view ‘technologies as embodying structures (built in by designers during technology development), which are then appropriated by users during their use of the technology (Orlikowski, 2000, p. 405). Jones and Karsten’s review (2008) of IS structuration studies shows that the focus is generally on development (n = 46) or use (n = 21), but seldom on implementation (n = 3). This suggests that people perceive there to be a hard distinction between development and use, something which is, however, not always tenable. Pettigrew & Whipp (1991) emphasize that the implementation of innovative change is an iterative, cumulative and reformulation-in-use process. By focussing on implementation, and conceptualizing this process as the interaction between development and use that results in technology-in-use. this paper adds to the body of IS structuration knowledge.

## Social multidimensionality

To date, empirical IS structuration studies have paid little attention to the notion that technology appropriation takes place within a certain institutional context with its own structural features (Orlikowski, 1992; Orlikowsky & Barley, 2001; Jones & Karsten, 2008). e-Health projects can involve many stakeholders, such as technicians, patients and their families, carers, entrepreneurs wanting to tap new business through health service centres, infrastructure suppliers, insurers and health care managers. These stakeholders come from distinctly diverse institutional contexts. In this respect, e-health implementation differs from the appropriation processes described in many previous studies (Barlow et al., 2006). The question is, what are the impacts of this multidimensional social reality during the implementation process?

The institutional context determines the cognitions, norms and resources that stakeholders can draw upon. The contexts of the various stakeholders may overlap or differ substantially. As explained below, such intersections and tensions can create multi-actor agency directed at change (Giddens, 1984; Whittington, 1992). As such, in explaining e-health implementation outcomes, the concept of a multidimensional social reality therefore seems crucial.

Even in a single institutional context, the internal ambiguity and plurality of the rules governing the reproduction of social structures require actors to make choices, which may lead to structural change. Actors have to exercise discretion in interpreting their immediate roles: for example, when there is an incoming video call in a service centre, the telenurse has to decide whether to immediately answer it or give priority to an ongoing activity. A personal habit may become common practice and subsequently be accepted as a rule, which may even be formalized in a protocol or in routing software. Thus agency, in the sense of using one’s discretion, enables change.

Moreover, given the diverse stakeholders, e-health implementation takes place within a multidimensional institutional context. As a result, actors may be confronted with contradictions between their own institution’s structural properties and the rules and resources of other activity systems in which they now participate, and that cross-cut and overlap the project’s boundaries. For example, in answering video calls, telenurses may try to adhere to the ethical codes of their nursing association, to the existing policies of the homecare organization and to the directions provided by the telecommunications supplier. In having to decide how to combine and deal with the different values, pieces of knowledge and rules of these three institutional contexts, a teleservice centre may develop its own structural properties. This is viewed as a second and stronger form of agency (Whittington, 1992) because the multidimen sionality offers actors greater choice among rules and resources for inspiring and empowering their decisions and actions.

However, the space for choice depends on whether those who actors wish to influence acknowledge the legitimacy of the enlisted rules and resources (Whittington, 1992; Fondas & Stewart, 1994). Thus, the interaction among technology, institutional context and actors also encompasses the interplay among stakeholders (Sahay, 1997). From this perspective, the issue with respect to managing e-health implementation becomes whether stakeholders in their interaction show agency when enlisting and legitimizing different sets of rules and resources since this will determine whether or not they bring about change.

## METHODOLOGY

The previous section presented the structurational model (Figure 1) applied to unravel the complexities in a telecare implementation process. A single case-study approach fits this perspective well and permits an in-depth analysis of the evolving interaction processes and their outcomes, and allows us to compare these with the stakeholders’ voiced intentions (Gerring, 2004; Yin, 2009). Since these issues were initially unclear, they needed to be studied in their natural setting in order to understand their nature and the complexities involved (Myers, 2009, p. 78). The research poses ‘how’ and ‘why’ questions, exploring actors’ appropriation of the technology and clarifies this by taking into account the interactions among the various stakeholders with their diverse knowledge, norms and resources (Gerring, 2004; Yin, 2009).

The research was conducted in two phases.

## Phase 1: Familiarization and data collection

During this phase, we familiarized ourselves with the project, the major stakeholders and the telecare implementation debate. Questions surrounding the process and context of telecare implementation were identified and a preliminary model developed. Following this, qualitative and quantitative data were collected. The emphasis was on the qualitative data, with the quantitative data helping to validate and frame the interview and document data. The data collection took place over 18 months (October 2006–March 2008), enabling us to capture the emerging implementation mode and the evolving appropriation process.

To ensure construct validity and triangulate the data, several data sources were used including interviews, workshops, written reports, policy plans, minutes from meetings and observations (Dubé & Paré, 2003). The interviewees (28) represented the major stakeholders as follows: six clients, six care coordinators, four carers, three telenurses, three service centre managers, two homecare managers, two insurance managers, one telecom manager and one telecom technician. Some of the interviewees were visited on multiple occasions.

The interview schedules were based on the structurational model of e-health implementation (Figure 1). The interviews lasted between 60 and 90 minutes and started with open-ended questions about the telecare system, its goals, its organization and the implementation process. The open-ended questions encouraged respondents to give their views on the telecare system. Following this, specific questions were asked about the system’s appropriation and how this affected work and care processes. With two researchers present, most of the notes taken were handwritten, and individual interview reports were subsequently made. These were presented to the interviewees for feedback. In some cases, the interviews were taped and then transcribed

In addition, semi-structured telephone interviews were conducted with 26 clients to gain insight into their expectations of the system (in May 2007) and of their use of it and attitude towards it (in February 2008). Quantitative data were also collected to obtain information on the number of connections. the time of day and the length of the calls. Further, the medical service centre was observed for approximately 2 hours a week to register the nature of the calls and the routines being developed by the telenurses. The telenurses were also asked to register the nature of the calls being made. In July 2007 and in March 2008, workshops were held to discuss the findings and interpretations with the co-researchers and the responsible representatives from the joint venture partners. The first workshop was used to identify and address goal diversity among the stakeholders. In the second workshop, we discussed the results of the initial data analysis with the focal stakeholders. In addition, we presented some provisional results on the modes of telecare implementation identified during the data analysis. Through this workshop, we learned that additional data were needed to substantiate and refine the implementation modes identified.

## Phase 2 Data analysis

As explained in the previous paragraph, our longitudinal approach allowed both real-time and retrospective analyses. The data were first interpreted to reconstruct the sequence of events, develop a chronological view of the implementation choices made and analyse telecare appropriation during the implementation process. The data were then grouped around the elements of the structurational model (institutional context, telecare technology, stakeholders) to see how they were related in the implementation process. This resulted in an analysis of the evolving interactions among these elements, thus addressing our research questions.

The resulting structurational analysis pointed towards an inconsistent implementation mode. The mainstream ‘alignment’ literature suggests that these inconsistencies could explain the disappointing outcomes in this particular project. However, this finding also raises the question as to what would have been entailed in achieving a consistent mode. In seeking an answer to this question we searched the data for common patterns, dominant themes and explanations. Further, we asked the respondents to comment on our interpretations (Ryan & Bernard, 2003; Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007). Then we started to define initial modes, such as ‘telecare as substitution’ and ‘telecare as a complementary tool’, which were based on the goals as initially voiced. Next, we looked for traces of these modes in the results of the structurational analysis. We then checked the initial modes for underlying dimensions and contrasted them with these traces while looking for other possible modes and related dimensions. As qualitative data analysis is an iterative process (Patton, 2002, pp. 268–269), we strengthened, refined or rejected early modes and identified new modes throughout the course of the data collection and analysis. A major objective in the second workshop (March 2008) was to present our initia results and discover whether the respondents recognized the tensions we had found among the various goals of telecare implementation. This helped us develop a more extensive description of the modes from the perspective of the structurational concepts. The second workshop provided some supporting recognition but also critical comments, which enabled us to refine and redefine certain modes.

The telecare case study section describes the institutional context, stakeholders and telecare technology elements, and the evolving implementation process. Following this, the structurational analysis section provides answers to the research questions, after which the discussion section interprets these findings and develops preliminary modes for more effective telecare implementation.

## TELECARE CASE STUDY

## Institutional context

The case study examined a telecare programme developed jointly by a homecare provider, a national health care insurer and a large international telecommunications firm. The telecare programme was aimed at promoting information technology-enabled forms of care delivery to people’s private homes. One of the programme’s objectives was the substitution of traditiona forms of homecare by telecare in 1500 households. A central issue was how care could be provided to more people using the same finite resources. In an early business plan, the consortium expressed the ambition to profitably integrate a range of services in the fields of social care, health care, entertainment and communication through one electronic channel. The project was partly funded through an innovation programme of the Ministries of Health and Economic Affairs as part of the Dutch government’s policy of realizing large-scale telecare provision.

The homecare organization studied offers conventional homecare by carers who provide a variety of services to approximately 16 000 clients spread over a province. These carers, many of whom are female part-timers, and some nurses who provide more specialized care, are supervised by care coordinators who plan and schedule the work for the health care teams, each consisting of 30–70 carers. The care coordinators are responsible for the individual clients’ care plans based on an indicative plan made after an initial formal assessment. With telecare, clients receive care through two channels (individual carers and the service centre) from a number of telenurses and carers who deliver components of the indicated care. The care coordinators remain responsible for this care throughout the whole process. Figure 3 depicts the care provided by the carers and the service centre (telecare).

## Telecare technology

This telecare initiative included a video connection and a camera in the homes of volunteering clients, enabling them to contact a service centre based in the main office of the homecare provider. This service centre had the facility to receive calls around the clock and observe the clients on a monitor during calls. A software programme provided access to the client’s personal and medical data during the call, enabling the telenurse to add call-based information to the record. Only the telenurses had access to the patients’ records; they could not be shared electronically with the care coordinators or the carers. Telenurses would fax or email the care coordinator if they thought they had new information that was relevant to them.

## Stakeholders

This telecare project was implemented within a web of stakeholders from various organizations with a diverse range of influence levels and interests. Figure 4 shows the three major groups of stakeholders identified.

The first group consists of representatives of the three participating organizations who formed the project team. This group’s joint interest was to profile the project by realizing a large-scale telecare programme with a high exposure. The telecom firm’s underlying goal was to achieve a market position in health care provision and develop a knowledge base in this field. The insurance firm wanted to be seen as an innovative corporate brand and sought, in the longer term, to improve the transparency and cost-effectiveness of its services. It believed that telecare could contribute to these objectives. Similarly, the homecare organization’s management expected that it could either improve its cost-effectiveness by replacing home care with telecare or, if this proved untrue, that they had at least tried to build a business case.

The second group, consisting of service centre managers and telenurses, shared an interest in delivering effective telecare services. The service centre managers were responsible for the functioning of the centre and, as such, decided on the necessary resources in terms of telenurses and connections, as well as the software and hardware to achieve adequate and cost-effective forms of telecare. Telenurses were responsible for the actual delivery of telecare

The third group was the network of people involved in the daily organization of care provision and the clients themselves, as indicated in Figure 4. They had a shared interest in the effective delivery and control of homecare, irrespective of the mode of delivery. The care coordinators were mainly concerned with the quality of the care and, since they were the ones responsible, they also wanted to be in control of the care. The carers visited the clients in their homes and provided the required care according to the prepared plan. Further, in order to identify possible problems, they were also expected to observe the clients. The clients on their part expected an appropriate level of care, both from quantitative and qualitative viewpoints. In addition, many clients expected their carers to play some role in meeting their social and safety needs.

## Implementation process

In June 2005, a consultancy presented a business plan to the consortium formed by the three companies. It was argued that a large-scale implementation of telecare would offer significant cost savings to the providers and be attractive and beneficial for the clients. The business plan portrayed telecare as part of a range of teleservices covering the fields of care, security, socia contacts and entertainment. It would partially replace traditional care provision and comple ment this with a round-the-clock ‘total programme’. It was also assumed that clients would be prepared to pay for one or more modules in these offered services. Based on a business case, the three participating organizations agreed to start a joint venture in March 2006, called Koala. The joint venture would be managed by a board of managers, made up of members from the three companies. One of Koala’s initial goals was to realize telecare in the northern region of the country.

In September 2006, a homecare project team selected potential telecare clients based on their database of 11 000 regular customers. The following criteria were used: (1) a relevant part of the client’s current homecare could be replaced by telecare; (2) the care would be required for more than 3 months, since this was the minimum time that would be needed to justify the costs and install a connection; and (3) the client’s social and mental conditions allowed care delivery by telecare. In terms of this last condition, people suffering from addictions, Alzheimer’s disease and certain psychiatric illnesses were considered inappropriate at this stage. The selection process resulted in a list of only 463 potential telecare clients. This number was significantly lower than the initial estimation of 1500 likely candidates.

In October 2006, the homecare organization established its in-house service centre for dealing with client calls. Workplaces were designed, software installed and telenurses recruited. During this period, the telecom companies involved ordered cameras, set-top boxes and the other necessary facilities to be placed in the clients' homes. In December 2006. the Koala Telecare initiative was launched during a health congress that received national press coverage through the presence of the Prime Minister and a number of cabinet ministers. The telecare programme was presented with great optimism as a promising innovation in the national health care infrastructure.

At that time (January 2007), the project managers in the homecare organization started to more intensively inform the care coordinators about the project, particularly about the urgent need to attract clients. The homecare organization intended to recruit at least 400 suitable clients who were interested in homecare. However, it proved difficult to motivate the care coordinators over the project and get them to actually approach their clients. Although many had been informed about the telecare possibilities, they had not been participants at any stage of the development process. Moreover, most of them found it difficult to determine when and how telecare would be useful for their clients or for themselves. Further, some of them were worried about a possible loss of control when delegating some of their services to the service centre. One of them said: ‘With telecare, I am not only responsible for my own team of carers, but also for the care provided by this centre. This involves delegating that responsibility to the manager of the service centre whom I hardly know’.

The indifference among the care coordinators, and the lack of awareness among the carers over the telecare option, resulted in few telecare connections. In response to this, the project management increased the pressure on the care coordinators to reach their customer target and relaxed the initial criteria. In March 2007, a special acquisition team started to visit clients to persuade them to accept a telecare connection. They were relatively successful, but this upset the care coordinators who felt they had been bypassed. Some carers even advised their clients not to accept an offered connection.

The carers, the people who actually visit the clients in their homes to deliver care, had not been involved in the telecare implementation process. During an evaluation workshop, the director of the homecare organization said: ‘Many carers have part-time low income jobs. Many of them experience telecare as a technological threat. Since the issue has not been discussed with them they may be unaware of it, indifferent, or even opposed to it. Only a very few have a positive attitude. Those opposed to it communicate this attitude to their clients. We need to deal with this problem.

The number of registrations developed slower than initially expected, while the cancellations were higher than anticipated, resulting in only 200 effective connections as of January 2008.

The majority, 82% of those connected, never, or hardly ever, used the system for care purposes. Many were indifferent: the traditional homecare approach had been satisfactory and telecare was seen as a free additional offer, something which did not add any significant extra value other than providing them with an ‘increased feeling of safety’ as some described it. The take-up numbers indicate that this telecare project had only a limited overall impact on how care was provided by this homecare organization. Despite the implementation efforts, in terms of time, finance and other resources, telecare only replaced traditional homecare for a very limited number of clients. Telephone interviews before and after the implementation suggest that those clients who had received telecare did not feel the quality of their care had been changed by the implementation. The next section explains the project’s limited success through a structurational analysis.

## STRUCTURATIONAL ANALYSIS

In this section, the structurational model of e-health implementation (Figure 1) is used to analyse the case study situation by addressing the four questions derived earlier from this model.

Question 1: In what ways do the technology’s structural properties enable and constrain the stakeholders’ decisions during the implementation process?

The telecare technology constrained its users in a number of ways. First, the physical infrastructure necessary limited the potential target group since it took 4–6 weeks to install the system at a client’s home. Consequently, it was not feasible to include clients needing short-term care, such as palliative care or monitoring while recovering from an operation. Further, some clients cancelled their subscription because they disliked ‘all the cables installed in their homes.

A second issue pertained to interconnectivity. At the service centre, the system was not connected with the homecare organization’s other applications and databases, preventing its integration with the regular homecare services and thus its utilization by the care teams. For the clients, there was a video connection with the service centre, but not with their family, friends and other care professionals as some had anticipated. Clients indicated that this made the system less attractive. Some time later, the telecom company tried to offer this functionality, but the consortium vetoed the idea.

Moreover, the application appeared to be programmed in such a way that clients had to take the initiative for a call by pushing the connect button on their equipment. This procedure had been set up for privacy reasons in the military, where the application had originally been developed. Initially, this prevented the telenurses from proactively checking up on their clients. It was only after an installer from a subcontractor happened to show a telenurse how she could tune in the client’s remote control that a procedure was developed that allowed telenurses to initiate calls for specific clients after receiving written consent.

Further, the equipment was fixed in one place in the clients’ homes. Many clients assumed, or were told by the carers, that telecare was only for emergency calls. As a result, it was seen as inflexible. Moreover, the television and the associated equipment were not easy to reach in the event of an accident in another room. In response, the project managers argued tha telecare was not meant to replace the existing alarm system, ‘. . . but some care coordinators felt inclined to present it to their clients as a free alarm system . . . so that less well-to-do clients would at least have something’ (a project manager).

These four constraints limited telecare’s usefulness. There were also clients who appropriated the system in initially unforeseen ways. One client who objected to being seen in her private environment covered the camera with a tissue. She simply appreciated a visual one-way contact with the telenurse. Sometimes, such unexpected appropriations would lead to technological improvements. For example, telenurses noticed that some clients were unsure about their appearance on the screen, so a small frame was included in the client screen showing the nurse’s view.

Although the technology allowed such incremental improvements, these were limited due to the initial lack of communication about the system among the carers, between the carers and the clients and between the carers and the telenurses. One blind client would push the cal button to ask the telenurse to help her look for mislaid items (such as her glasses). Her unexpected appropriation was very useful in this situation. However, another client cancelled his video connection because of his impaired sight. This example illustrates that individua client experiences were, at least initially, not systematically monitored and communicated, leading to fragmented learning. This partly explains the limited number of connections and the low usage frequencies.

To summarize, the data show that there was room for interpretative flexibility in the interaction between the technology and the actors, but that shared sense-making lagged behind. Further, the technology could not always be appropriated in line with the clients’ expectations. This was partly due to technology inherent limitations and partly to the diverging values, rules and cognitions of the various stakeholder groups, which leads to the second question.

## Question 2: To what extent and in which respects were the stakeholders confronted with distinctly different or even colliding social realities?

The data show that the stakeholders’ different social realities constrained the technology’s appropriation. When addressing the added value of telecare services, politicians, government officials and telecom managers all emphasized concepts such as wellness, comfort and helping the lonely. However, these are complementary services that are not refundable within the health care insurance system. A tension can also be observed between these voices and those that were pursuing cheaper delivery of the existing services as a response to scarce resources, namely the difficulties in recruiting suitable staff. One homecare manager said: ‘Homecare is a very physical activity that primarily takes place behind the front doors of our clients. These clients are quite often old, poor, vulnerable and have serious health conditions. It was not easy to find 5% that qualified to have part of thei regular care replaced by telecare’.

Moreover, the time available for the project was limited and the pressure to achieve connections was high: ‘We were used [in previous IS projects] to first having a development phase and then the implementation . . . here, the telecare development and implementation almost coincided’. The project manager and the telecom managers were mainly focussed on realizing technical issues and, from an economic perspective, stressed the importance of generating volume to build the business case. In the rush to realize volume, they did not sufficiently reflect on the specific roles of telecare within the care delivery chains. The homecare organization’s line managers were not represented in the project organization and ‘. . . they did not actively monitor the number of applications’. Although the board and support staff were in the lead, they were dependent on operational levels for client connections and use.

Located at the homecare organization’s headquarters, the service centre was far from the 47 geographically dispersed teams that took on clients and coordinated care delivery. Given its central but remote position, the local care coordinators and the carers remained relatively unaware of the opportunities for integrating telecare into their own care processes. One care coordinator commented about the service centre: ‘. . . I don’t know who they are and I don’t care. My job is to organise and deliver homecare. The telecare operation is headquarters responsibility’. Although care coordinators are responsible for all the channels through which homecare is provided, telecare’s central position constrained them in appropriating this channel. Consequently, care coordinators felt little commitment to encouraging either carers or clients to appropriate telecare: ‘Since we don’t share an electronic client record, I don’t know how often clients use telecare and what care the service centre delivers. For that reason, I see it as a separate service and not really as my responsibility.

On their part, the local carers did not see it as in their own interests to stimulate the replacement of home visits by telecare Some clients said that carers had told them that ‘it is only to be used in emergency cases, not on a day-to-day basis’. A canvasser recalled that as he was presenting information about the telecare service at a client’s home, the carer said to the client: ‘Oh no, telecare is not for you’. The two canvassers interviewed said this happened more often and that elderly and otherwise vulnerable patients tended to rely on the opinions of carers who visited them at home and with whom they were familiar. Moreover, the top-down way in which the goal of replacing home visits by telecare was communicated conflicted with the need to develop telecare services through close cooperation between carers and clients. A manager admitted that ‘an important lesson is that we only informed the care coordinators and were not in touch with the teams and the carers. It is, however, very important to listen to their ideas and find ways to make telecare fit with the current ways of care delivery’.

Even the telenurses did not always stimulate frequent use, although they did encourage the clients to call if ‘something’s wrong’. Before the telecare introduction, the telenurses predominantly had high-tech and cure-oriented tasks at the centre. When the pilot started, they were expected to answer the clients' calls in addition to their other duties. Some telenurses perceived the provision of telecare as an ‘interruption’ to their ‘real’ work and as less relevant because of the ‘soft’ nature of the calls. Frequently, calls were for testing purposes, to report technical problems, or to demonstrate the system to neighbours and relatives, and this reinforced this argument (source: call registration). From the telenurses’ professional perspective, receiving and analysing medical data were considered to be more urgent tasks.

Finally, the clients themselves were well aware that they were participating in a pilot. Although the insurance company refunded their homecare costs, many clients feared that they would have to start paying for telecare once the pilot phase was finished. A letter and remarks by the telecom personnel had given this incorrect impression. The telecom firm’s information (style and content) was not always in line with that of the homecare agency, and this also led to misunderstandings and, according to the secretary, some cancellations. As a project manager observed: ‘Many clients are very vulnerable and fearful of the technology and its provider. The slightest confusion may lead to panic and requests to cancel telecare.

## Question 3: To what extent did the stakeholders show agency through displaying ambiguity and tapping diverse contexts for rules and resources?

We traced a few instances of agency whereby advantage was taken of the different sets of rules and resources to be found within the project. These are discussed in the following paragraphs. However, this agency rarely led to a sustainable project-wide developmental step in terms of the technology-in-use. It was simply too difficult to overcome the tensions ensuing from the diverse contexts (as addressed in Question 2). Nevertheless, the need to exploit one another’s knowledge, norms and resources was recognized. As a homecare manager said: ‘There are differences in culture between the telecom and the homecare providers . . . we can also learn something from their culture. Such differences in value-sets can be used in the joint venture . . . they can be discussed’.

The homecare project’s managers were focussed on selecting clients who were (1) capable of using the system and (2) appropriate for what it offered. Cooperation was sought with the care coordinators in selecting and approaching potential users. This selection was to be based on their professional knowledge and legitimized by their value system. However, there were far fewer subscriptions than envisaged in the targets set in the agreement with the business partners and the government bodies funding the project. A project manager said: ‘Care coordinators are not used to such targets, and this leads to a lack of commitment and resistance’. When the programme manager stressed the high volume required to realize economies of scale, homecare’s project managers resorted to easing the selection criteria and hiring canvassers who directly approached clients. In opting for this acquisition means, the homecare manager was inspired by telecom sales practices rather than by homecare routines. As a result, a number of care coordinators objected as it went against their professional norms. This centralization of acquisition seemed to reinforce the idea that it was ‘headquarters’ responsibility’, and this undermined still further telecare’s appropriation by local care teams and their clients.

In addition, since the telecare service was optional, clients had room for choice. Some agreed to a connection to support the homecare pilot project. They were satisfied about their homecare, and willing to participate in the agency’s innovative pilot. Although they felt no particular need for telecare and had no specific expectations, they regarded the offering as a free trial and as a legitimate ‘experiment’. This benevolent, non-committal attitude by a small minority of clients was in sharp contrast with the steering committee’s belief that there was a huge market for telecare. After the equipment had been installed, many clients reported on the lack of information provided by the carers about how to use the system. Consequently, they became indifferent or reluctant in their participation. This seems particularly the case when the commercial tone used by the telecom company contrasted with their idea of wanting to help their trusted homecare agency.

Fifteen per cent of the clients saw telecare as a free, additional and flexible service. They used it from time to time for purposes varying from social interaction, technology checks (does it still work?), to obtaining answers to pressing medical questions (‘Can I use a certain drug?’, ‘My partner fell; what should I do?’). Sometimes these uses had not been unforeseen. For example, some clients who could not afford a personal alarm system now used telecare as a sort of substitute (see Question 1). Although such use was legitimate from the viewpoint of learning and experimenting, it did not contribute to replacing homecare visits. Further, telecare was never intended as a complementary alarm system, and alarm services were already provided on a commercial basis.

Only 3% used telecare for a specific and intended service (namely, the daily monitoring of medication) and as an integrated part of their care arrangements, and this did indeed significantly reduce the number of home visits. These clients, who appropriated the system together with the local carer and the service centre, mostly used it in line with the project team’s substitution intentions. According to the client interview data, these users were very satisfied. It increased their sense of safety, and for some, their independence and emotional well-being. The common element linking these clients was that they had chronic health and/or social conditions requiring continuous monitoring of their medication and/or well-being.

## Question 4: How does the emerging implementation mode (arrows A to D) help to understand the outcomes in terms of appropriation?

Eighty-two per cent of the connected clients used the system only infrequently, or not at all, or stopped using it after a while or even cancelled their subscription. That is, clients could not always appropriate the technology in line with their expectations, and some could find no need for it.

This was only partly due to technology inherent limitations (see Question 1). The answers to Questions 2 and 3 tell us that the challenge of bridging the different social realities turned out to be a major and often insurmountable obstacle, even though the project leader was characterized as ‘a clever boy . . . who knew how to get things done . . . listened well . . . who had caught up with the practices of homecare in no time’. In hindsight, a project manager admitted that ‘the implementation process was not balanced’. For example, he observed that prior to the implementation they had neither investigated the clients’ needs nor used marketing communication tools such as showcases. In his view, if a project was especially aimed at experimenting and learning, then such a marketing approach was not required but, when the aim was substitution, it was. In the discussion section, it is argued that managerial agency is especially difficult under conditions of social multidimensionality, a situation indicated by this persisting diffuseness in projec goals.

## DISCUSSION: TOWARDS DIFFERENT MODES OF TELECARE IMPLEMENTATION

The challenge in telecare implementation is to reconcile the technical possibilities and economic imperatives with the needs and interests of clients, health care professionals and their managers within their regulatory frameworks. Earlier, in the Theoretical Backgrounds section, we explained how telecare appropriation, as an innovative form of technology enabled change in a multidimensional social system, was dependent on managerial agency.

In terms of Question 1, and consistent with previous implementation studies (e.g. Barley, 1990), our research shows that there is room for interpretative flexibility in the interaction between the chosen technology and the actors. However, in this case, telecare’s appropriation by the clients and their carers was constrained by inherent limitations of the technology.

In answering Question 2, we have shown how the technology’s appropriation was also hindered by the different social realities of the stakeholder groups. The challenge of bridging and exploiting various sets of rules and resources proved to be a major bottleneck for the managers involved. Divergent sense-making (Weick & Westley, 1996) was visible in the diffuse project goals, and also in the various views on the process of implementation.

Concerning the third question, we reported that instances of managerial agency did occur, but without resulting in a coherent implementation approach – in fact rather the opposite. The interviews provided evidence of different views on telecare, something that was recognized by the stakeholders’ representatives in a feedback meeting. Actors acknowledged the existence of different goals and recognized that these originated from the different institutional contexts. In interviews they openly acknowledged the need for one another’s input, but we believe that they underestimated the implications of the choices made during implementation. These choices were enabled by the various sets of rules and resources (see Questions 2 and 3). Moreover, the project team selected what they saw as a proven technology, but this then turned out to need further adaptation after initial use. As a consequence, implementation involved both development and user activities and these overlapped considerably. Given this situation, they were only confronted with the implications of the emerging choices during the appropriation phase. Their different sets of rules and realities then clashed, and the incompatibilities in goals became apparent.

The consensus that the actors had reached about the ‘hard, seemingly simple technology (Orlikowski, 1992, p. 401) diverted their attention from the degrees of freedom remaining in the actual development of the technology-in-use. As a consequence, when the technology’s implementation and use, as well as the subsequent development activities, became intertwined, the managerial actors, rather than consciously exploiting the technology’s interpretative flexibility, did not know how to deal with the alternatives (Question 1). This is in line with the ideas of soft determinism (Barley, 1990). In terms of Question 3, we did find instances of individual managers who answered to and consciously integrated different sets of rules and resources, but such managerial agency was insufficiently consistent over time.

In elaborating on our findings related to the fourth question, we address the identification o more consistent telecare implementation modes, an aspect which can be derived from the structurational analysis. A consistent telecare implementation mode involves achieving an alignment of the goals, the technological change and the related changes in the health care services offered, their delivery and their organization. With this aim, we started from the goals voiced in the first interview round, and worked towards a preliminary proposal for more consistent implementation approaches. As a first step, we labelled the partially contradicting and counteracting means and ends voiced during the project. Next, we categorized these into what turned out to be four distinct modes of telecare implementation, which were then acknowledged, discussed and refined by the stakeholders’ representatives during a feedback meeting. Table 1 indicates how the statements of the various stakeholders were interpreted and categorized into the four modes.

The four modes identified are as follows: (1) telecare as a substitute for specified, limited services; (2) telecare for more general substitution purposes; (3) telecare as complementary services; and (4) telecare for learning and innovation purposes. Below, we discuss examples of these usage modes that were found during the research. In the early selection phase, the homecare coordinators sought out clients with ‘specific needs that can be met through telecare’, which aligns with mode 1: the substitution of specified, limited services. This aligns with the wish expressed by the homecare management to see if they could establish a business case by investigating whether they could deliver some existing services more efficiently through telecare substitution within the budgetary constraints prescribed by government agencies. Mode 2, telecare for more general substitution purposes, was promoted by the managers of the insurer and the homecare organization. They expected that the costs of homecare could be contained by installing the homecare technology in the homes of their clients. During the preparation phase, the consortium claimed that this programme would bring ‘added value and new services to the clients’, which reflects mode 3: telecare as complementary services. Throughout the project, some care coordinators indicated that ‘this is an experiment, intended to learn from’, a statement which reflects mode 4. This indicates that telecare was communicated to them, to their team members and to their clients as a pilot. Categorized quotations in Table 1 from the actors involved show some examples of the evidence supporting these four modes.

None of these modes became dominant during the process. This may be explained by the technological and institutional constraints associated with each of the various goals. Modes 2 and 3, for example, do not align with the application’s stand-alone character, and neither with the government’s policy of only reimbursing hands-on care. Despite some stakeholders desires to conquer a new market, restrictions and colliding realities pushed them towards viewing the project as an experiment (mode 4). Clearly, this emphasis does not fit with the top-down, volume-oriented project management approach adopted at the start of the project in response to government requirements. The next four paragraphs give a more generic, outline description of the modes of telecare implementation as they emerged during the project.

The telecare as a substitute for specified, limited services mode is based on the view that care can be divided into separate parts, which can be delivered by different suppliers through different channels at different costs. This mode is based on the idea that certain services, such as assistance with drug regimes, can be delivered at a lower cost through telecare, while other services, such as washing and cleaning, are delivered by homecare visits. This is a rational and professional imperative approach in that it seeks to divide care into different units provided by various professionals and other specialized providers. With this mode, the care coordinators remain the ones who direct and coordinate the opera tional care delivery. Its aim is cost-effectiveness. The care suppliers can be contracted to deliver predetermined care ‘products’ at specified prices. Critics argue that this mode will lead to fragmentation of the delivery of care, resulting in a depersonalized health care sector.

The telecare for general substitution purposes mode argues that telecare technology can replace homecare for a large percentage of clients. It emphasizes the ubiquitous and omnipotent nature of technology that makes it possible to support people in increasing and often unexpected ways. In this view, the service centre will direct and coordinate the operational homecare delivery. In time, cheap sensors, robots, cameras and other advanced technology will become available and can also be deployed to help people meet their psychosocial needs. This mode reflects an economic imperative approach, based on the assumption that personalized care can be cost-effectively replaced by telecare, thereby achieving economic rationality. It reflects a deterministic and optimistic view on technology. Critics can argue that many homecare elements cannot be replaced by technology, and that this perspective is over optimistic and heavily supply-driven.

The telecare for complementary services mode is based on the view that homecare cannot be easily replaced by telecare. Homecare is physical and social in nature, whereas telecare is informational and remote. However, it also assumes that the telecare technology can meet certain client needs not addressed by carers. Telecare can serve as a tool enabling clients to receive helpful information and to connect to various care providers and/or other services. The technology can conceptually be integrated into a variety of information, communication and entertainment systems. From a marketing perspective, this mode is essentially demanddriven: it seeks to create new markets and thus demand. Critics may argue that rather than helping to control the costs of care. this mode will increase them. Further. there are no viable business models available that would support this mode.

The telecare for learning and experimentation mode is based on the view that it is not clear how and when such technology would contribute to the quality and cost-effectiveness of care. Only by pilot studies, in which motivated clients, carers and telenurses participate, can an accurate view be obtained of how technology can improve care. Intensive communication among these three groups coupled with a willingness to use technology in unusua and unexpected ways could result in a form of telecare which could either substitute or complement the existing modes of care delivery. Critics may argue that experimentation is difficult in this field because of the inherent high costs. Further, it may be difficult to scale up the potential benefits to mainstream care provision because of the limited appropriateness.

## CONCLUSIONS

This paper has provided a structurational analysis of a telecare project. The specific structurational concepts applied – technology appropriation and social multidimensionality – help in understanding how the implementation mode that emerged from the interaction among actors, technology and institutional contexts resulted in limited appropriation. The questions posed in this paper have revealed diverging sense-making among the various stakeholders with regard to the telecare initiative’s means and ends, and this led to inconsistencies which were difficul to resolve, especially in a situation where implementation coincided with ongoing developmen and use of the technology.

The analysis shows how the inconsistencies in the choices made during the implementation phase were the result of the interactions among actors who had limited awareness of both the extent to which the different interpretations of the given technology informed people’s choices and the interdependencies among these choices. With these interdependent choices and actors who do not sufficiently recognize the different interpretations underlying them, the resul is an inconsistent telecare implementation mode. As such, our study supports the growing body of literature on technology’s ‘interpretive flexibility’. However, in a given time and place, it is not possible for all the actors involved to appropriate it in different ways if they are interdependent in their use of the system.

Häkkinen & Hilmola (2008) stressed the need for communication between organizational levels in an implementation process. Our study adds to this by suggesting that social multidimensionality complicates communication between stakeholders. It follows that deterministic models cannot offer an adequate understanding of telecare implementation. In contrast, by adopting structuration theory (Giddens, 1984; Chu & Smithson, 2007), the case shows that telecare implementation requires profound changes in structures of domination (allocative and authoritative resources), legitimation (norms and values) and signification (meaning and interpretation). From the domination perspective, telecare is faced with a multilevel and multidimensional network in which the stakeholders’ access to resources is conditional and based on their cooperation. Telecare implementation requires the network relationships to be reconfigured and a modification to the power positions among the parties involved. The legitimation perspective shows that telecare questions existing norms and values by starting from a socia system that is often unfamiliar to clients, carers and care coordinators. Delivering ‘soft’ care from a distance through hard technologies can easily conflict with established views on how care should be provided, especially when clients are vulnerable and unassertive (Hedström, 2007). From the signification perspective, telecare is inherently difficult because the work and its coordination has to be meaningfully redivided among the providers of homecare and telecare, and a balance has to be found between the human elements and the non-human aspects (Nicolini, 2007). This implies a need to reframe both the delivery mode and the care content, including the emergence of new artefacts and renewed identities (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994).

The four modes proposed: (1) telecare as a substitute for specified, limited services; (2) telecare for more general substitution services; (3) telecare for complementary services; and (4) telecare as experimentation, are suggestions for achieving an alignment between technological change and the coupled changes in the health care services offered, their delivery and their organization. Further research is required to develop, refine and validate these modes.

Our strongest recommendation for policymakers would be to reject simplistic and overoptimistic views on e-health technology applications. Given the complexities of telecare and related e-health services, the telecare implementation modes identified in this study may help practitioners grasp the significance of the ways in which implementation can vary. Moreover, they may stimulate and help clarify the debate among the various stakeholders who collectively need to make sense of and develop a consistent implementation mode.

By means of reflective monitoring (Giddens, 1984), the stakeholders involved need to acknowledge not only the specific health care context, but also how this contrasts with the structural contexts of other stakeholders. As with e-business (Chu & Smithson, 2007), e-health services involve major organizational change processes which draw on and affect the health care sector’s domination, signification and legitimation structures, and possibly also those of other domains. It has been claimed that telecare technology’s availability does not guarantee its sustained use (Barlow et al., 2005; Hebert et al., 2006). We have shown that between a telecare application and its successful appropriation there is a grey area of interaction between a ‘seemingly straightforward’ technology and diverse actors whose sense-making may diverge, without them even being aware of this. In order to take concerted, purposeful action, that is managerial agency (Giddens, 1984; Whittington, 1992), these actors have to develop a common, thorough understanding of the care services and the target groups involved so that they can align their visions and arrive at a consistent implementation mode where development and use activities coincide.

A limitation of this study is associated with the inherent weakness of manually interpreting case study data. Subjectivity and the involvement of the researchers with a project, or with a particular group of stakeholders, may influence the analysis and conclusions. On two occa sions, we obtained a degree of intersubjectivity by discussing our interpretations in meetings with the focal stakeholders and researchers from other disciplines. Another issue is the study’s limited generalizability to other telecare implementations in particular, and to e-health in general. Nevertheless, we would expect the need for consistency in the implementation approach, and in interactions with a system’s appropriation, to be an issue in other multi stakeholder environments.

## Biographies

Albert Boonstra is a Professor of Management in the Faculty of Economics and Business at the University of Groningen, the Netherlands, where he is director of the graduate programmes in Business Administration. His research focuses on organizational and social aspects of information systems, especially in health care environments. Albert’s work has appeared in journals such as Journal of Strategic Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, International Journal o Information Management, New Technology, Work & Employment and International Journal of Project Man agement. His most recent co-authored book is Managing Information Systems. Strategy and Organisation (2009), with Financial Times/Prentice Hall. He earned his doctorate in social sciences from the University of Glasgow.

Mariolein van Offenbeek is Assistant Professor of Organization Studies in the Faculty of Economics and Business at the University of Groningen. She studied work and organizational psychology at the Free University of Amsterdam, where she gained her PhD for her study entitled ‘From Method to Scenarios: Matching Context and Strategy in Information Systems Develop ment’. She has published on team learning and informa tion systems development, Her present research focuses on changing contexts, work and occupational structures and professional roles in health care. She has developed government-funded research projects together with the University Medical Centre of Groningen and other insti tutions. Her work has appeared in journals such as Organization Studies, Behaviour & Information Technology, Journal of Health Organization and Management and European Journal of Work & Organizational Psychology.
