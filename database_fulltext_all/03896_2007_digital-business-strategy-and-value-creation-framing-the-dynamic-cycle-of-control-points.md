# DIGITAL BUSINESS STRATEGY AND VALUE CREATION: FRAMING THE DYNAMIC CYCLE OF CONTROL POINTS<sup>1</sup>

Margherita Pagani

Marketing Department, Bocconi University, Via Roentgen, 1,

20136 Milan ITALY {margherita.pagani@unibocconi.it}

Within changing value networks, the profits and competitive advantages of participation reside dynamically at control points that are the positions of greatest value and/or power. The enterprises that hold these positions have a great deal of control over how the network operates, how the benefits are redistributed, and how this influences the execution of a digital business strategy. This article is based on a field study that provides preliminary, yet promising, empirical evidence that sheds light on the dynamic cycle of value creation and value capture points in digitally enabled networks in response to triggers related to technology and business strategy. The context used is that of the European and U.S. broadcasting industry. Specifically, the paper illustrates how incremental innovations may shift value networks from static, vertically integrated networks to more loosely coupled networks, and how cross-boundary industry disruptions may then, in turn, shift those to two-sided markets. Based on the analysis, insights and implications for digital business strategy research and practice are then provided.

Keywords: Control points, incremental innovation, disruptive innovation, digital business strategy, value network

## The Emergence of a Digital Ecosystem<sup>1</sup>

In the last few decades, companies have moved away from hierarchical integrated supply chains in favor of fragmented networks of strategic partnership with external entities (Bitran et al. 2007). This pattern, already visible in many industries such as automotive, apparel, textiles, electronic manufacturing, and service, is being repeated in digitally enabled networks where digital technologies are fundamentally reshaping traditional business processes as modular, distributed, crossfunctional, and global processes enable work to be carried out across boundaries of time, distance, and function (Sambamurthy et al. 2003; Straub and Watson 2001; Wheeler 2002).

The multichannel digital revolution coupled with the development of interactive digital technology, virtualization, peer-topeer networks, cloud computing, Internet of services, and other IT developments is changing the rules of the game in many industries through disruptions of business models. The result is the emergence of a much more complex and dynamic ecosystem for growth and innovation (Iansiti and Levien 2004). The profits and competitive advantages of participation in a given value network reside dynamically within the chains, accumulating at the positions of greatest value and/or power (control points). The enterprises that hold these positions have a great deal of control over how the network operates and how the benefits are redistributed (Rülke et al. 2003).

Structural contingency theorists (Burns and Stalker 1961; Lawrence and Lorsch 1967) have long postulated that the environment is an important factor in defining the organizations within it. In particular, they have pointed to rates of change of key environmental factors like technology (Dowling and McGee 1994) and marketing choices (Claycomb et al. 2000) as driving the optimum structure of organizations operating within these environments. For them, however, the environment is considered as a static exogenous variable moderating organizational structure and successful performance. Technology and innovation theorists (Abernathy and Utterback 1978) and affiliated organizational theorists (Anderson and Tushman 1990; Henderson and Clark 1990) have taken steps to advance structural contingency theory by endogenizing technological evolution and its effect on organizational evolution. Despite the increasing interest in the topic, there does not appear to be a common understanding of the effect of technological evolution on the structure and dynamics of control points in a digitally enabled value network.

The present paper provides preliminary, yet promising, empirical evidence that sheds light on the dynamic cycle of value creation and value capture points in digitally-enabled networks as a result of the emergence of incremental innovation and cross-boundary industry disruptions. We consider in this paper the shifts within the broadcasting industry as a result of the ICT revolution. It is worthwhile to mention that other variables such as regulation, bargaining power of stakeholder, or new players from outside the traditional ecosystem may affect the evolution into one of the three types of network structure.

In this paper, we analyze three value networks—a vertically integrated one, a loosely coupled one, and one based on a multisided platform—and break them down into their functional components to examine the ownership and organization of these components, which are critical in controlling the dynamics of core and edge competencies of players. We then analyze how components change in response to triggers related to a digital business strategy. To accomplish this task, we construct a view of the value network as a configuration of control points, which comprises the various service transactions involved in implementing the functional components required to deliver a service offering. We call these configurations control point constellations (CPCs). Control points are analyzed in this paper in terms of how, and to what extent, they create and capture value, and in what forms.

Below, we focus on how industry, firm, and resource-related variables interact and affect firm outcomes in term of value creation and value appropriation. In doing so, the paper (1) focuses on the properties of real-world networks, (2) takes the view that networks are not static but evolve in time according to various dynamical rules, and (3) aims to understand networks not just as topological objects, but also as the framework upon which distributed dynamic systems are built.

The plan of this paper is as follows. We begin by summarizing the results of a literature search for value network and value creation articles published in leading IS, strategic management, and operations research journals. Next, we offer opinions and supportive arguments on how digital ecosystems evolve and what their dynamics are in order to provide insights into defining the control points that influence the execution of a digital business strategy within changing value networks. We conclude by summarizing findings and describing the implications of these findings and insights to research and practice.

## Reconfiguring for Value Creation

Within the strategic management literature, several scholars have studied the concept of value and the sustainable competitive advantage (Brandenburger and Stuart 1996; Mol et al. 2005; Porter 1985). Bowman and Ambrosini (2000) define value creation as the contribution to the utility of the final good or service to end users and distinguish it from value capture defined as the difference between revenue and cost retained by the firm. Mol et al. (2005) conceptualize a passage through the multiple value-adding stages, which eventually accumulates into an overall value proposition. In their model, each stage contributes a particular proportion of the overall value created, but value capture by each contributor depends on the participants’ relative bargaining power (Brandenburger and Nalebuff 1997; Brandenburger and Stuart 1996; Porter 1980; Teece 1986). Of course, this process may not always be as sequential as this description suggests, but no viable product or service will materialize unless a collection of these value-contributing parties can settle on a division of spoils that keeps each party willing to participate.

In this paper, we agree with the new logic of value presented by Normann and Ramirez (1993), which breaks down the distinction between products and services and combines them into activity-based “offerings” from which customers can create value for themselves. According to the authors, as potential offerings grow more complex, so do the relationships necessary to create them. As a result, a company’s strategic task becomes the ongoing reconfiguration and integration of its competencies and customers. This literature starts with value creation by coalitions of different economic actors. Subsequent work in this strand has tended to focus on the conditions that govern the ability of different parties to capture value (Lippman and Rumelt 2003; MacDonald and Ryall 2004) and the drivers of value creation (Adner 2006; Adner and Zemsky 2006).

We find it useful to frame the challenge as the need to engineer a value chain simultaneously with the engineering of the products/services and processes for providing value. In this respect, we build on the “three-dimensional concurrent engineering” framework (Fine 1998), which adds value chain engineering to augment the traditional two-dimensional concurrent engineering of products and processes (Fleischer and Liker 1997; Nevins and Whitney 1989; Ulrich and Eppinger 1994). We define a value network as one in which a cluster of economic actors collaborates to deliver value to the end consumer and where each actor takes some responsibility for the success or failure of the network (Barnes 2002; Bitran et al. 2003; Pigneur 2000; Sabat 2002). We distinguish five types of actors: consumers, service providers, tier 1 and 2 enablers, and auxiliary enablers (Basole and Rouse 2008). Their key strategic task is the reconfiguration of roles and relationships among this constellation (Normann and Ramirez 1993) of actors in order to mobilize the creation of value in new forms and by new players. Partnership management capabilities (Dyer and Singh 1998) will have to be a core competence that new business actors must possess (Pigneur 2000).

When an incremental innovation appears improving upon something that already exists or reconfiguring an existing form or technology to serve some other purposes (Christensen 1997), significant value can be created assessing the value of relevant knowledge residing at different points in the network and arranging its transfer to other points in the network where it is needed (Doz 1996; Gulati 1999). This implies exploiting resources that are made available through the network relationships (Gulati and Singh 1998; Inkpen and Dinur 1998; Kale et al. 2000; Khanna et al. 1998).

As products and services increasingly have embedded digital technologies, it is becoming more difficult to disentangle business processes from their underlying IT infrastructures (e.g., El Sawy 2003). Digital platforms are enabling crossboundary industry disruptions (Christensen 1997), thus inducing new forms of business strategies (e.g., Burgelman and Grove 2007). Consequently, theoretical structures for strategy making in nonlinear dynamic environments are also emerging (Pavlou and El Sawy 2010). Digital business strategies call for coordination across firms along product, process, and service domains, thereby creating more complex and dynamic ecosystems (Adner 2006; Iansiti and Levien 2004; Moore 1996) for growth and innovation. The whole value network is underpinned by a particular value creating logic and its application results in particular strategic postures. Adopting a network perspective (Burt 2004; Gulati 1995; Kogut and Walker 2001; Marsden and Podolny 1990) provides an alternative perspective that is more suited to “new economy” organizations, particularly those where both the supply and demand chain are digitized (Peppard and Rylander 2006).

Scholars working in a parallel stream have studied in engineering terms the concepts of architectural modularity in order to define more precisely the conditions under which suppliers and customers of products and services might need to engage a in structured versus unstructured technological dialogue (Baldwin and Clark 2000; Chesbrough and Kusunoki 2001; Christensen 1997; Henderson and Clark 1990; Ulrich and Eppinger 1994).

Despite increasing interest in the topic, there does not appear to be a common understanding of the effect of technological evolution on the structure and dynamics of control points in a digitally enabled value network. We depart from this literature by explicitly linking the dynamics of value creation and their implications for value capture to the structure of interdependence in a firm’s ecosystem following the emergence of an incremental and disruptive innovation. We find it useful to frame the dynamic cycle of value creation and value capture points, defined in this manuscript as control point constellations, with the business double helix model (Fine 1998, 2000). This model asserts that when the industry structure is vertical and the product architecture is integral, the forces of disintegration push toward a horizontal and modular configuration. These forces include (1) the relentless entry of niche competitors, (2) the challenge of keeping ahead of the competition across the many dimensions of technology and markets required by an integral system, and (3) the bureaucratic and organizational rigidities. These forces typically weaken the vertical giant and create pressure toward disintegration to a more horizontal, modular structure (Staudenmayer et al. 2005).

On the other hand, when an industry has a horizontal structure, another set of forces push toward more vertical integration and integral product architectures. These forces include (1) technical advances in one subsystem that can give market power to its owner, (2) market power in one subsystem that encourages bundling with other subsystems to increase control and add more value, and (3) market power in one subsystem that encourages engineering integration with other subsystems to develop proprietary integral solutions.

## Premise of this Study

This study seeks to illustrate how incremental innovations may shift value networks from static, vertically integrated networks, to more loosely coupled networks, and how “disruptive” innovations may then in turn shift them to two-sided markets. The study intends to provide insights into defining the control points that influence the execution of a digital business strategy within changing value networks.

Our opinion is that, in order to succeed over the long haul, firms within value networks have to periodically reorient themselves by adopting new strategies and structures that are necessary to accommodate changing environmental conditions. These shifts, occurring through incremental or discontinuous changes, have different impacts on the structure of control points in the value networks. Our propositions, built inductively from empirical analysis of digitally enabled networks, are as follows:

1. A closed, vertically integrated model of CPC structure emerges in tiers of the market where customers are underserved by the functionality or performance available from products in the market.

2. When the market is less demanding of functionality and an incremental innovation appears that improves upon something that already exists or reconfigures an existing form or technology to serve some other purposes, a horizontally stratified or disintegrated CPC structure is the dominant business model.

3. When the dominant business model in a tier of the market shifts from vertical integration to horizontal stratification, the ability to achieve above-average profitability tends to transfer to companies that build an infrastructure (platform) that creates value by reducing distribution, transaction, and search costs incurred when different subsystems interact with one another.

4. When performance gaps emerge in markets due to cross boundary industry disruptions, the pendulum of CPC structure is likely to swing back toward vertically integrated CPCs structures.

Our primary purpose in offering this paper is to invite other scholars to empirically test these hypotheses and thereby continue to build deeper understanding of the dynamics of value creation and value capture in digital business networks, thus providing a better understanding of digital business strategy.

## Field Study and Context of the Broadcasting Industry

The methodology adopted in this study includes a system identification process called black-box modeling (Norgaard et al. 2000) and follows a three-phase process: (1) experiment (empirical results emerging from case studies), (2) selection of the model structure, and (3) model estimation and validation. We use as an empirical setting the broadcasting industry in Europe and the United States in the period 2000–2008. We selected this industry as it transitioned through three distinct technology generations (analog TV, digital TV, and multisided digital platforms) resulting from the emergence of an incremental innovation and cross-boundary industry disruptions.

We used both primary and secondary data sources for this study. We began by developing a detailed historical understanding of the broadcasting industry during a 10-month field study. We conducted multiple interviews with 45 industry experts from 15 leading companies in Europe and the United States. The interviewees came from a variety of positions within the ecosystem: content providers, semiconductor and electronic suppliers, broadcasters, software, terminal providers, platform aggregators, and telecommunications.

The interviews were semi-structured and lasted two hours on average. We used the information from the interviews to develop an understanding of the ecosystem challenges that governed the emergence of the different technology generations. We consolidated this information in a document and discussed it during a meeting attended by all the interviewees. We incorporated all comments emerging from the first meeting and discussed the final document in a second meeting six months later. All of the experts agreed with our final characterization.

We searched every issue of New TV Strategies and Broadcast and Production (leading industry journals whose mission is to cover the key trends and issues that confront the industry) from 2000 to 2008 for articles relating to broadcasting innovation challenges. Using this source, we created measures (discussed below) that characterize the extent of challenges in the core and edge capabilities for each of the technology generations. Finally, longitudinal data were collected on a sample of 792 firms across the supply chain of broadcasting industry (15% content providers, 15% semiconductor and electronic suppliers, 13% broadcasters, 17% software, 14% terminal providers, 12% platform aggregators, 14% telecommunications) in Europe and the United States between April 2000 and May 2008. We constructed a unique data set to test our arguments. Using an observational method (Bernard and Killworth 1979; Bernard et al. 1980, 1982; Killworth and Bernard 1976), we collected detailed archival market data from websites, newspapers, and the leading industry consulting firms. The information recorded for each company (every year) was the specific horizontal functions controlled and secondary capabilities acquired in other functions along the value chain.

The content validity was proven by consulting multiple sources (interviews and documents). Internal validity was tested by constructing a detailed research framework ahead of time. External validity was limited, since it was an exploratory study. Reliability was based on a detailed case study protocol that documented the selection procedures, analysis, and summary database.

We controlled for a number of firm- and industry-level effects. Henderson and Clark (1990) identified important differences between incumbents and entrants during technology transitions in the broadcasting industry. We control for this effect using the variable incumbent, which takes a value of one if a firm also was present in an earlier technology generation. We included one industry-level control represented by the number of firms, which controls for the competitive density in the generation in a given year.

## Three Types of Control Point Constellations

In this section, we first examine three types of CPCs emerging from the case studies analyzed and the longitudinal data (Figure 1): (1) closed vertically integrated model, which defines the short-term static properties of industry structure; (2) loosely coupled coalition model, which implies the emergence of an incremental innovation; and (3) multisided platforms which define the midterm dynamic—but nonevolutionary—process of competition characterized by the emergence of cross-boundary industry disruptions.

We then illustrate transition from one CPC type to another by examining the changes experienced by control points in response to triggers related to technology and business strategy. We model the mechanisms through which incremental and cross-boundary industry disruptions change the structure of value networks and provide support for the double helix model (Fine 1998) while adapting it to a digital business environment.

## Type 1: Closed Vertically Integrated Model

Prior to the disintegration and fragmentation of supply chains, media companies were responsible for coordinating their own value chains, and the control of core activities was strictly centralized through vertical alliances determined by the need of the single company to develop along the vertical dimension of the value chain (Figure 1a).

Media companies in the United States and Europe (1980– 1985 phase) adopted strategies aimed at obtaining scale economies within mutual horizontal core businesses (focalization strategies) and expanded their activities to adjacent markets through cooperation strategies and alliances (vertical and horizontal strategies). Based on the case studies analyzed, the following forces were found to drive vertical and horizontal alliances:

1. Risks linked to the uncertain demand and the rapid market change. (For example, the short-lived alliances of TCI with Microsoft or Kirch, Bertelsmann with BSkyB for the creation of a digital satellite platform in Germany.)

2. Market positioning and access to new capabilities (For example, the alliance of Bertelsmann with America Online to form AOL Europe enabled the group to increase network technical capability and supply Internet services. BBC developed Internet services and online information with ICL, which supplies the informatics capabilities required to run the service. The merger of Bell Cablemedia, NYNEX CableComms, and Videotron with Mercury Communications Ltd. originated Cable and Wireless Communications and the expansion of service providers Internet UUNet through acquisition of Unipalm PIPEX in the United Kingdom and France.)

3. User control possibilities and the increase in the share of profits deriving from the sale of products and services. (For example, Disney Corporation obtained access to many distribution channels by taking over ABC and Capital Cities in 1995, thereby accessing terrestrial and cable TV channels. BSkyB developed new opportunities of distributing new interactive services with BT, potentially gaining access to the BT installation base. Similar advantages drove Bertelsmann to cooperate with Deutsche Telekom in Germany.)

4. Shifting to value chain areas with a higher added value. (Mobile service providers such as Cellnet and Orange in the United Kingdom tended to shift in the supply of Internet services with UUNET. Microsoft and NBC created the joint venture MSNBC, which enables NBC to add value to its basic TV activities through Internet and Microsoft to become a content supplier.)

5. Facing the competition of companies in connected markets. (For example, the investments by U.S. Regional Bell Operating Companies in cable TV aimed at preventing competition in cable companies entering the telecommunication market.)

Value creation. For this value system configuration model we distinguish four main sequential primary activity categories: (1) content creation, (2) multimedia packaging and services, (3) network and infrastructure equipment provision, and (4) telecommunication service provision (Figure 2). The tendency to integrate these main activities is driven by the need to achieve independence, control the content access in order to guarantee access to the most interesting and innovative contents (i.e., contractual agreements, primarily between TV channels and film production companies), and acquire access control on the target user.

Value capture. As the industry structure is vertical and tend to integrate the main functions the value capture is controlled by key players (mainly media companies) integrated in all of the four main sequential primary activity categories.

## Type 2: Loosely Coupled Coalition Model

We examine next the emergence of digital broadcasting systems as an incremental innovation. Digital broadcasting systems enable the delivery of more TV channels and the high definition (HDTV) format, as well as interactive services such as EPG, VOD, and interactive advertising. Previous studies show that the value network changes as a function of a technological shock (Knoben et al. 2006).

Figure 2. Value Network of the Analog Broadcasting Offering (Closed Vertically Integrated Model)

In this phase of evolution of the industry, the emerging interactive digital television marketplace is more complex, with competing platforms and technologies (satellite, cable, terrestrial, IP) providing different capabilities and opportunities. The TV value chain is increasingly being deconstructed, leading to the development of a complex and rapidly evolving value network (Pagani 2003). We look in depth at the changes occurring in the TV value chain, that is, the digital terrestrial, cable, and satellite systems in the United States and 10 countries in Europe. For each country, we mapped the main players. Figure 3 uses the example of Italy.

In this changed context, we can distinguish the emergence of firms (digital delivery platforms) that possess more prominence and power gained through a central position in the value network structure, and that use their prominence to perform a leadership role in pulling together the dispersed resources and capabilities of network members (digital TV channels, application service providers, network providers). These firms can be labeled as hub firms (Brass and Burkhardt 1993; Wasserman and Galaskiewicz 1994) and are represented by digital delivery platforms.

We define network orchestration as the set of deliberate, purposeful actions undertaken by the hub firm (digital delivery platform) as it seeks to create value and capture value from the network. This type of value network may be viewed as “loosely coupled coalitions” (Provan 1983), where loose coupling is a situation in which elements are responsive, but retain evidence of separateness and identity (Weick 1976).

The supply chain now appears more disintegrated and open. Traditional broadcasters, who in the previous stage developed primary skills in the phases of packaging and signal transmission (network provision), now develop contractual links with other players in the market (application service providers, network providers etc.). TV channels and delivery platforms compete to capture the attention of the viewer and the value is provided by the most compelling content and useful interactive service. Figure 4 illustrates the location of the biggest players along the value chain and the relations among them.

Digital TV offers attractive opportunities to players who by tradition were foreign to the broadcasting world, particularly to companies operating in the field of telecommunications (i.e., France Télécom, Telecom Italia, Telefònica, BT, AT&T) and informatics (i.e., Microsoft). Such companies are interested in exploiting digital TV primarily as it pertains to interactive services and the interconnection, software, and hardware demands arising from them—either alone or in combination with traditional television contents.

Value creation. Once the digital TV market matures, the retention of viewers will be determined by the quality and range of content and services provided by the operator. Service providers that secure attractive content services at an early stage will have an advantage in attracting and retaining subscribers. As the number of TV channels increase due to digital technology, value creation control points tend to increase. Network providers (i.e., traditional delivery platforms such as cable, satellite, OTA or newer telco IPTV and wireless services) offer a walled garden of content acquired and aggregated into various consumer packages.

Value capture. With reference to the flow of revenues among the different operators we found that network operators have an advantage in gaining revenue. Their condition of “bottleneck,” through which all of the content that appears on television must pass, provides a steady revenue stream for pay-TV services while interactive-TV applications are being developed. Service providers collect their revenues directly from final users through their services. Revenues are then distributed among the other upstream stages of the value chain. Broadcasters gather relevant additional revenues from indirect sources, such as advertising, which represents a fundamental part of the revenue model. Within telecoms, the percentage of call revenues funded by advertisers (through free phone, national call rate, and local rate numbers) continues to rise. As advertising is the central revenue stream in the broadcasting and telecom business model, it is extremely important for capturing the value generated in the ecosystem.

## Type 3: Multisided Platforms

Next, we examine the collision of multiple disruptions that influence computing (as devices are more personal), networks (connectivity is ubiquitous), value chain clockspeed (value capture is fleeting), content control, regulatory, consumer and IP protection, consumer experience, software distribution, social networking, storage and time shifting, and content production (webcams). These cross-boundary industry disruptions lead to the emergence of new business models (Google TV, Brightcove, Hulu, Joost), new interactive services, and radical changes in the traditional viewing habits, meeting new needs. We call this model (depicted in Figure 1c) the multisided platform.

The emergence of the multisided platform (Eisenmann et al. 2006; Geoffrey and Van Alstyne 2005; Parker and Van Alstyne 2005) represents a new form of market structure and can be seen as a consequence of the disruptive innovation related to the emergence of new services and new business models. It exists wherever a company brings together two or more distinct groups of customers (sides) that need each other in some way, and where the company builds an infrastructure (platform) that creates value by reducing distribution, transaction, and search costs incurred when these groups interact with one another (Eisenmann et al. 2006).

Value creation. In this model we distinguish three main players: (1) end user peers, (2) transport peers (network), and (3) platforms/aggregators.

End user peers are able to interact (real-time communication or asymmetric), be part of a social network (i.e., Facebook, some gaming environments), consume content (push versus pull—i.e., downloads, broadcast, on demand), publish content (as individual end users or for-profit), and advertise. The transport peers (ISPs, carriers) provide the “pipes” that enable utility/consumption and are concerned with ROI.

These two players are linked by platforms/aggregators, which provide environments that support end-user utility, usually using transport networks. Examples analyzed are eBay (or Yahoo! Auctions, etc.) bringing together buyers and sellers; Google with content providers, consumers, and advertisers;

Visa, American Express, or other payment systems bringing together merchants and consumers; Sony PlayStation with users, game developers, and middleware providers; Apple iTunes/iPod with music labels (content publishers) and consumers; and DoCoMo i-mode bringing together online content providers, application developers, handset makers, and consumers.

Multisided platforms (MSPs) can vary from having two sides (eBay, Visa), three sides (Windows, Google), four sides (imode, Brightcove), or potentially even more. However, the more sides, the higher the degree of complexity and the greater the strategic challenge of balancing the interests of all sides (other things being equal). The choice of which sides to target can have an immense impact on the structure and the success of an MSP business.

MSPs create value by generating indirect network effects between the multiple sides they serve. iTunes becomes more valuable to music labels as more and more consumers buy into that platform by purchasing iPods. As more merchants opt to take Visa, the Visa card becomes more valuable for consumers and vice versa.

Governance patterns vary from MSP to MSP. Some platforms retain a high degree of control over actions by all sides of their platform, because they conclude that centralized control is necessary for the platform to function and because they have the market power to do so. Apple, for example, in almost all cases mandated a single, across-the-board pricing structure on the iTunes music store. Other MSP companies allow participants broad latitude in their platform activity, setting minimum rules that will enable and encourage the platform to function smoothly and profitably. Thus, eBay lets sellers decide minimum bid amounts, auction duration, and so on. Microsoft places no restrictions over who can sell applications for Windows and how.

Value capture. In multisided networks, cost and revenue are on each side. The platform incurs costs in serving all of the groups and can collect revenue from each, although one side is often subsidized. In cases like eBay or Visa, one side pays to play (sellers, merchants), and the other doesn’t (buyers, consumers)—although the latter may pay indirectly. Craigslist is open on both the sides. With Windows, Microsoft derives its revenues from licensing fees charged to users, whereas application developers do not pay anything and are in fact subsidized through various forms of development support. By contrast, with Xbox, Microsoft derives the bulk of its revenues from royalties charged to game developers, while selling the consoles to users at or below cost. Their business model can be not-for-profit (i.e., Gnutella, BitTorrent) or for profit (Google, eBay, Facebook, Akamai, Skype, or corporations, universities, etc.).

## Framing the Dynamic Cycle of CPCs

Finally, we analyze the changes experienced by control points in response to triggers related to technology and business strategy and compare the three evolving value network structures in terms of value creation logic, primary activity categories, main interactivity relationship logic, primary activity interdependence, and key cost and value drivers. Table 1 summarizes our analysis and description.

What we find most critical is the change in the value creation and value capture logic. The closed vertically integrated model is characterized by the presence of giant, strongly connected components that create and capture value. The business value system structure is characterized by interlinked chains. In the loosely coupled coalitions, resulting from the emergence of an incremental innovation, different players along the value chain create value through a pooled, sequential, and reciprocal primary activity interdependence. Value is captured by referred hubs and redistributed. In the multisided platform type, resulting from cross-boundary industry disruptions, a main infrastructure brings together two or more groups and creates value by reducing distribution, transaction, and search costs.

Findings support the business double helix model (Fine 1998) in the digital environment and show that when the industry structure is vertical and the product architecture is integral, the forces of disintegration and the emergence of an incremental innovation push toward a horizontal and modular configuration. On the other hand, when an industry has a horizontal structure, another set of forces, named crossboundary industry disruptions, push toward more vertical integration and integral product architectures.

## Implications and Insights for Digital Business Strategy and Research

The study analyzes the structure and dynamics of value configuration in a technology-enabled industry in order to provide insights into identifying and defining the control points that influence the execution of a digital business strategy within changing value networks. Our study makes a number of contributions. First, it illustrates how incremental innovations may shift value networks from static, vertically integrated networks to more loosely coupled networks, and how cross-boundary industry “disruptions” may in turn shift them to multisided markets.

We use the business double helix (Fine 1998) to explain how digital ecosystems evolve and their dynamics. We identify three main types of network structures. Two of them are linear systems, while the third is characterized by nonlinearity.

In this evolving pattern, the parts can display complex nonlinear dynamics, and their implications can be quite stunning, as they often imply an effective disconnection between the well-defined behavior of the components and the global outcome that one observes. What this means is that the precise knowledge about the plans and strategies of individuals in the market would not suffice to understand the behavior of the market.

We have drawn a number of insights for the evolution of digital ecosystems and showed how digital business strategy shifts. These insights also provide two implications for both research and practice.

## Transform Organizational Intelligence into a New Relational Intelligence

This study shows that in order to survive in an increasingly uncertain and complex environment, the firm has to transform its organizational intelligence into a new relational intelligence, enacting an open communication process with its stakeholders.

The closed vertically integrated model (type 1) is no longer the best organizational architecture because it is designed to centralize the organizational intelligence, not to maximize it. In the loosely coupled coalition (type 2), it is much more valuable to play in an orchestra than to be an outstanding soloist as part of a value creation ecosystem (Adner 2006; Adner and Kapoor 2010). Value creation is the output of a process that encourages creativity and diversity, yet does not allow the players to go out of tune completely. In the third type of digital networked world, where everyone and everything is connected (multisided platforms), economic value behaves very differently than it does in the traditional, bounded world. These three types of value networks show different ownership and organization of functional components, which are critical in controlling the dynamics of core and edge competencies of players. Understanding these three types and their transition allows companies to better recognize how components change in response to triggers related to a digital business strategy.

## From Content Gatekeeper to Customer Gatekeeper

By comparing the three value constellation models, we observe a gradual shift in the critical role of a firm in a value network from being a content gatekeeper to becoming a customer gatekeeper. If we want to understand who controls the network, we need to explore in depth this evolving path through which value is created and who captures this value. We have been able to distinguish five layers of value.

Value in Customer Access: This revolves around becoming a customer gatekeeper. Most economic value will be created at the periphery/ends of networks. At the core, the end most distant from users that possesses only generic scale-intensive functions will consolidate. At the

## Concluding Thoughts

This paper broaches a key, unexplored issue in value network management, with implications for researchers in network theory, IS, strategy, knowledge management, alliances, and international business. Our main contribution is a novel way to study the control point constellations (value creation and value capture points) topology and the influence of incremental innovations and cross-boundary industry disruptions on them. The proposed conceptual framework captures concisely the highly skewed distributions of the value configuration models. We support the double helix model and discover three models that characterize the control point constellations of the multimedia industry.

Finally, what distinguishes the research described in this article from previous work is that traditional approaches to networks have tended to overlook or oversimplify the relationship between the structural properties of a network system and its behavior. Which outcomes occur, how frequently they occur, and with what consequences, are all questions that can only be resolved by thinking jointly about structure and dynamics and the relationship between the two. That is how we can clearly identify and define the control points that influence the execution of a digital business strategy within changing value networks.

## Data Analysis

We used panel data for our empirical analysis. Each panel corresponds to a firm in a given technology generation. The use of panel data help to control for potential sources of unobserved heterogeneity and enables us to test how the benefits of vertical integration change over time during the course of a given generation. In our analysis, core and edge capabilities are the key explanatory variables, but they do not exhibit intertemporal variation for a firm within a given generation. For this reason, we used a random-effects model (Baltagi 2005). We controlled for unobserved differences across firms by including firm dummies in a firm-generation panel (Wooldridge 2002). We also included year dummies to control for unobserved year-specific effects. We estimated the following equation:

$$
\mathrm{y} _ {i g t} = \beta_ {0} + \mathrm{X} _ {i g t} \mathrm{b} + \mathrm{D} _ {i} \mathrm{l} + \mathrm{T} _ {t} \mathrm{d} + \mathrm{a} _ {i g} + \mathrm{u} _ {i g t}\tag{1}
$$

where ${ \mathrm { y } } _ { i g t }$ is firm i’s market share in a generation g in year $t \mathrm { ; X } _ { i g t }$ is the vector of independent and control variables; D is the vector of dummies for each firm; $\mathrm { T } _ { t }$ is the vector of dummies for each year; $\mathbf { a } _ { i g }$ is the unobserved heterogeneity for a firm in a generation that is assumed to be uncorrelated with the explanatory variables; and $\mathbf { u } _ { i g t }$ is the error term.

Selection of the Model Structure. In phase 2 (selection of the model structure), we introduced a graph-generating model aimed at representing the evolution of control point constellations using a node-and-arc representation. In this approach, from graph theoretic notation, nodes represent actors (companies) while arcs represent relationships, or ties, between actors in the network (Moody et al. 2005; Newman 2003). This approach has been shown to be particularly effective in describing the structure and dynamics of socioeconomic networks (Brandes et al. 2001) and we adopted it to visualize our ideas. Following Botafogo and Schneiderman (1991) and Pirolli et al. (1996), we augmented graph theoretic analysis to include document content resulting in a rich understanding of domain structure and a taxonomy of the roles played. We then incorporated an element of time evolution in the network structure and offered a plausible explanation in terms of growth dynamics of the digital-enabled network.

Model Estimation and Validation. In order to estimate the model (phase 3), we discussed the emerging structure during two meetings with practitioners and academics.
