# Herding and Software Adoption: A Re-Examination Based on Post-Adoption Software Discontinuance

Xia Zhao<sup>a</sup>, Jing Tian<sup>b</sup>, and Ling Xue<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management Information Systems, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA, USA; <sup>b</sup>Center for Digital Innovation, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA, USA; <sup>c</sup>Department of Computer Information Systems, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA, USA

## ABSTRACT

Informational cascades are theorized as an underlying mechanism of herding. That is, an individual, having observed the actions of those ahead of him/her, chooses to follow the behavior of the preceding individuals even though his/her private information suggests other options. Empirical identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of informational cascades is challenging because individual users’ private information is unobservable. Our study utilizes a unique data set on post-adoption discontinuance of app usage to revisit herding and informational cascades in software adoption. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that with the download of apps being controlled, a higher software ranking is associated with more post-adoption discontinuance of app usage, which empirically illustrates the decision de<sup>fi</sup>ciency of following others’ observed behavior in adopting popular software apps and supports the theoretical perspective of informational cascades. We further show that the association between app ranking and post-adoption discontinuance is stronger for apps with higher ratings and with higher complexity levels. Moreover, as apps become more complex in the app life cycle, updated app versions with a higher level of complexity are associated with a weaker relationship between app ranking and postadoption discontinuance. Our study contributes to the literature by con<sup>fi</sup>rming the informational cascades e<sup>f</sup>ect and its interaction with other informational mechanisms (e.g., user rating) and software internal feature (e.g., product complexity) in software adoption. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings help software vendors gain insights in users’ herding behavior in software adoption and optimize their software releasing strategies and promotional e<sup>f</sup>ort allocation.

## KEYWORDS

Herding; software adoption; software discontinuance; informational cascades; software ranking; online rating; software complexity; behavioral herding

## Introduction

Herding refers to the phenomenon that “everyone is doing what everyone else is doing” [4]. It has been examined in a variety of social and economic contexts, such as online peer-to-peer lending [20, 59], IT investment [50], stock investment [9], product introduction and market entry [31], and website registration [28]. A key feature of herding is that people converge on similar social behavior or even uniform behavior. The underlying mechanisms examined by prior studies include positive network externalities, sanction on deviants, conformity preference, communication, social norm and informational cascades [7, 46].

Herding has also been observed in the context of software adoption [15, 46]. Prior research has found that the download number of a software application jumps up as its ranking increases and concludes that informational cascades could be a key driving mechanism of herding [15]. Informational cascades refer to a situation in which an individual chooses to follow the observed behavior of predecessors even though his/her private information suggests other options [7, 8]. The informational cascades perspective rationalizes the herding behavior based on the theory of observational learning [1, 2]. When an individual is uncertain about the quality of an item, such as a software application, the adoption behavior of proceeding users becomes a key source of information. A user can infer others’ private information based on his/her observation of their adoption behavior. The informational cascades perspective suggests that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of such observation can be strong enough to motivate the user to follow others in adoption even when his/her own private information suggests other options [4, 7, 8].

From an empirical perspective, the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of informational cascades using observational data is challenging. It is mainly because users’ own private information is typically unobservable. Even though the adoption data show that users follow the predecessors and converge on uniform adoption behavior, it is still inconclusive whether users indeed suppress their own private information and follow others. In this regard, the data of post-adoption outcomes can be useful in complementing the data on adoption behavior to better identify informational cascades.

Considering post-adoption outcomes, informational cascades can result in social ine<sup>fi</sup>- ciency [8]. When an individual follows the crowd and chooses against his/her own private information, the adoption may not <sup>fi</sup>t the user’s actual preferences and local use contexts [46]. Consequently, ex post regret or dissatisfaction could arise and users may subsequently discontinue adoption afterward [46]. In the context of software usage, we refer to this phenomenon as post-adoption discontinuance of usage. Observation of users’ post-adoption discontinuance can provide a better opportunity to identify and con<sup>fi</sup>rm the role of informational cascades in herding behavior. Prior research has used survey data about users’ intentions on software adoption and discontinuation to examine the mechanism of informational cascades in driving herding behavior [46]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, two subjective constructs, discounting own information and imitating others, are used to capture informational cascades in herding. In this study, we leverage observational data on post-adoption discontinuance to empirically investigate informational cascades in herding on a large-sample basis.

Our <sup>fi</sup>rst objective is to complement the extant literature by using observational data on post-adoption discontinuance to empirically identify informational cascades in software adoption herding. Prior research has concluded the existence of informational cascades in herding by establishing the relationship between software popularity and initial product adoption decisions, such as software download [15]. Our study advances this stream of research by characterizing the relationship between software popularity (in particular, software ranking) and post-adoption discontinuance of software usage, which provides additional empirical evidence on the role of informational cascades in herding. Our second objective is to explore the interaction between di<sup>f</sup>erent informational mechanisms, in particular informational cascades and user rating, in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing software adoption herding. User rating is also a source from which users can learn previous adopters’ opinions. This study speci<sup>fi</sup>cally focuses on how user ratings may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the relationship between software ranking and post-adoption discontinuance. Such examination would help reveal the nuanced e<sup>f</sup>ect of software rating in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing software adoption decisions.

Our third objective is to consider the interaction between software complexity and informational cascades in herding. Such examination helps advance the understanding on the interplay between software inherent features and external information in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing software adoption. We take into consideration con<sup>fl</sup>icting theoretical perspectives regarding the roles of software complexity. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, some prior research suggests that software complexity can exacerbate information uncertainty in technology adoption [55], and therefore aggravate informational cascades and subsequent post-adoption discontinuance. However, an alternative view suggests that software complexity can motivate in-depth assessment and processing of information by users [41], and therefore mitigate the extent of informational cascades and subsequent post-adoption discontinuance. We intend to reconcile these con<sup>fl</sup>icting views by examining the variations of software complexity along di<sup>f</sup>erent dimensions and their corresponding impacts.

Our empirical context is a major Web browser platform, Mozilla’s Firefox. As an opensource system, Firefox provides core functionalities of the Web browser and allows thirdparty developers to build complementary applications (which are called “add-on” apps). The Firefox Add-ons Store lists available add-on apps and o<sup>f</sup>ers multiple features such as app ranking, app search, and app review to facilitate users. Our empirical analysis is based on a panel dataset of 1,338 unique add-on apps with a time span from January 2012 to December 2014 (156 weeks). We collected all the observable app information available in the Firefox Add-ons Store including app source code for the analysis.

Our study generates several important <sup>fi</sup>ndings that contribute to the existing literature. First, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that with the adoption behavior (i.e., app downloads) being controlled, high-ranking software apps (i.e., those with smaller ranking values) are associated with more post-adoption discontinuance of usage. This result suggests that popular apps are more likely to be abandoned in the post-adoption stage than unpopular apps, and the magnitude of discontinuance of popular apps cannot be completely explained by their high adoption volumes. This <sup>fi</sup>nding con<sup>fi</sup>rms the existence of informational cascades in software adoption (i.e., download), and therefore complements the existing view of herding on software adoption [e.g., 15].

Second, our results show that the relationship between app ranking and post-adoption discontinuance is stronger for apps with higher ratings. In other words, higher ratings amplify the informational cascades e<sup>f</sup>ect in app adoption. This insight is in accordance with the theory of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence bias in online ratings [e.g., 32, 35], which suggests that the online rating may potentially serve as a facilitator of herding and unreliability of online rating further aggravates the informational cascades e<sup>f</sup>ect.

Third, our study reveals the nuanced e<sup>f</sup>ects of software complexity in informational cascades. In particular, when examining between-app variation, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the association between app ranking and post-adoption discontinuance is stronger for apps with higher complexity, suggesting that app complexity aggravates informational cascades. When examining within-app variation (i.e., across app updates), we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the association between app ranking and post-adoption discontinuance is weaker for app versions with higher complexity, suggesting that increased complexity of updated app versions mitigates informational cascades in app adoption over time. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings help reconcile contradicting theoretical views regarding the e<sup>f</sup>ects of software complexity, and add to the existing understanding of the relationship between software internal features and external information.

## Theoretical Background and Hypothesis Development

The literature has examined herding and informational cascades in a variety of context. Shen et al. [43] focus on online review information adoption and study both the herd factors and their key determinants using a survey data set. Lee et al. [25] identify both a herding e<sup>f</sup>ect and a di<sup>f</sup>erentiation e<sup>f</sup>ect in the online ratings generation process. Liu et al. [32] compare the impact of product ranking on consumers’ online purchase behavior regarding search products and experience products and <sup>fi</sup>nd that informational cascades e<sup>f</sup>ect is more prominent for experience goods than for search goods. Prior studies also show that informational cascades are a vital driver for decision-making in the Internet <sup>fi</sup>nancial markets such as P2P lending [20, 26, 59].

The body of literature examining the herding phenomenon in software adoption has remained limited. Li [29] adopts the lens of informational cascades to conceptualize the role of herding in managers’ IT adoption decision-making, in conjecture with other forces such as network externalities and word-of-mouth. Walden and Browne [55] simulate the herding phenomenon using a model of sequential adoption of novel technologies. Recent IS literature has empirically examined the role of informational cascades in the herding behavior in technology adoption [15, 46]. The informational cascades theory explains individuals’ behavior based on the theory of observational learning. The underlying rationale is that when users are uncertain about product values, they may infer other users’ private information (about the products) from the observation of the latter’s behavior and update their own beliefs. During the information aggregation process, the inferred information may overweigh users’ own private information, and motivate them to follow the predecessors’ adoption decisions [4, 7, 8]. This is how informational cascades drive users to herd (i.e., imitate the behavior of predecessors). Duan et al. [15] empirically examine the relationship between users’ initial software adoption decisions (i.e., downloads) and software rankings and <sup>fi</sup>nd that the download number of software increases when software ranking rises, and decreases when the ranking declines. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent with the prediction of herding from the theoretical perspective of informational cascades.

Informational cascades theory has important implications for users’ post-adoption behavior as well. A notable adverse consequence of informational cascades is resource allocation ine<sup>fi</sup>ciency [1, 7]. At the individual level, a user may adopt a software application which is quite di<sup>f</sup>erent from what his/her own information suggests [4, 7]. The user may later <sup>fi</sup>nd that the adopted application does not <sup>fi</sup>t in his/her own needs or local use contexts [42, 46]. In this case, ex post discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation and dissatisfaction arise. The literature <sup>fi</sup>nds that discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation and dissatisfaction in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ post-use attitudes and in turn their intentions of continued usage [6, 56]. In particular, discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation and dissatisfactory could result in product abandonment or discontinuance [46, 47], and adoption driven by herding and informational cascades will not be sustainable. At the aggregate level, a more e<sup>fi</sup>cient technology may be rejected by the majority of users and eventually exit the market [1].

## Software Ranking and Post-Adoption Discontinuance

Software ranking is an important source of information that software platforms or websites (e.g., app stores) provide to users. Ranking is often based on the number of active users or downloads, which signals the market popularity of software [32, 43]. As an aggregate measure of user adoption, ranking information helps potential users observe the preceding users’ behavior and infer their private information about the software [32, 59]. So prospective users can synthesize their own information and other users’ information, and make informed decisions. However, in this process, informational cascades may occur and result in herding and suboptimal adoption decisions [42, 46].

In software adoption, when users take software ranking as an indicator of predecessors adoption actions, and mainly follow this indicator to make their own adoption decisions, informational cascades essentially arise and can cause two types of ine<sup>fi</sup>ciency for such decisions. First, when users’ own information is dominated and not e<sup>f</sup>ectively used in decision making, users may end up with adopting software apps not <sup>fi</sup>tting their own needs. Therefore, such popularity-driven adoption is likely to result in post-adoption regret and discontinuance of usage. Second, software ranking may not aggregate all the appropriate information of the predecessors. When the predecessors engaged in herding, their private information was dominated too. In this case, the high rankings may drive users to overestimate the quality of software. When users use the overly high quality expectations to guide their adoption decisions, these decisions are also likely to result in post-adoption regret and discontinuance.

For low-ranking software apps, the low-ranking positions signal relatively inferior quality and popularity, or simply become unimportant as signals. For adopters of these apps, their adoption decisions are more likely to be guided by their own preferences rather than ranking [2, 5, 48]. Evaluation based on users’ own information should weigh more in such decisionmaking, and therefore the adoption is more likely to meet users’ own needs. As a result, lowranking software apps should be subject to less informational cascades, and therefore are associated with less post-adoption discontinuance of usage. We hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): With initial adoption decisions (i.e., download) being controlled for, higher-ranking apps (with lower rank values) are associated with greater post-adoption discontinuance of usage.

## User Rating and Post-Adoption Discontinuance

The literature has shown that online rating systems can help address information uncertainty and bolster online transactions [e.g., 27, 40, 57]. For example, Chevalier and Mayzlin [11] <sup>fi</sup>nd that a higher rating score for a book increases the book’s sales. Zhang [58] <sup>fi</sup>nds that online sellers’ reputation scores increase both closing prices and probability of sales in online auctions. However, it is noteworthy that recent studies have also recognized the downside of online rating. For example, <sup>fi</sup>rms have incentives to manipulate user ratings by posting faking reviews [24, 33]. Another issue with online rating is social influence bias [35]. Muchnik et al. [35] <sup>fi</sup>nd that positive rating can elicit more positive ratings from users than a negative rating, resulting in a biased accumulated rating toward the positive side. Lee et al. [25] also show that for highly popular movies, a positive trend of prior ratings leads subsequent reviewers to provide more positive ratings. Even negative reviews combined with a negative user experience may lead to more positive user’s review sentiments than the case without any exposure to past reviews [22]. In this regard, high ratings are likely to contain bias by overrating the product quality and in<sup>fl</sup>ating users’ expectations.

While a higher software rating often suggests a higher quality and leads to more downloads, the rating may be subject to social in<sup>fl</sup>uence bias and exaggerate the quality [25, 35]. The bias of online rating is less likely to be recognized by users at the stage of transactions or adoption decision-making. But it is more likely to manifest itself in the post-adoption stage because users may <sup>fi</sup>nd that their decisions subject to such in<sup>fl</sup>uence eventually lead to undesirable choices. Regret or dissatisfaction can arise and make users discontinue using the adopted products [46]. Therefore, post-adoption discontinuance can also help reveal social in<sup>fl</sup>uence bias.

User rating is a source of information for users. User ratings re<sup>fl</sup>ect previous adopters’ postadoption evaluation of products [27, 40, 57]. Both software rankings and user ratings can inform subsequent users and impact their decision making. The literature shows that social media wordof-mouth and past product adoption are complements in promoting sales [30]. In this regard, high app rating can reinforce the signaling e<sup>f</sup>ect of high app ranking and strengthen the relationship between app ranking and post-adoption app discontinuance. High ratings can con<sup>fi</sup>rm users about the popularity that high app ranking signals. It can therefore make ranking information more dominant in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing users’ adoption decisions, which leads to stronger informational cascades e<sup>f</sup>ect. Moreover, high app ratings also make users form high expectations about the quality of high-ranking apps, because ratings re<sup>fl</sup>ect prior users’ post-adoption evaluation of apps. The excessively positive ratings can therefore mislead users to form overlypositive quality perception that further aggravates the informational cascades e<sup>f</sup>ect. Therefore, we expect app rating strengthens the relationship between app ranking and discontinuance and hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): The relationship between software ranking and post-adoption discontinuance of usage is stronger for apps with higher user ratings.

## Software Complexity and Post-Adoption Discontinuance

Software complexity, as an inherent feature of software applications, has indispensable in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the evaluations and adoption decisions of individual users [51, 54]. Previous studies suggest that complexity can in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ information search and processing [14, 37]. Complex products can exacerbate information uncertainty and complicate individual users’ adoption decision-making [14, 37]. Users therefore have to bear high risks in the adoption decisions-making, and post-adoption regret and dissatisfaction are likely to occur. In this regard, we expect that the level of post-adoption discontinuance is higher for more complex software applications.

Inferring information from others’ behavior is an e<sup>f</sup>ective approach for users to overcome challenges in product evaluation [55]. Software ranking, as an extrinsic cue that captures other users’ adoption decisions, helps users save signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cognitive e<sup>f</sup>orts in evaluating the software [54]. Darban and Amirkhiz [12] <sup>fi</sup>nds that herding can overcome uncertainty and save the cost of information search. When adopting complex software apps, the adoption decision-making is more challenging and software ranking weighs more in users’ decision-making process. Therefore, we expect that, for software apps with higher levels of complexity, there should be a stronger relationship between software ranking and post-adoption discontinuance. In other words, when distinct software apps are compared, informational cascades should be more intense for those apps with higher levels of complexity. We therefore hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3a (H3a): When comparing between distinct software apps, the relationship between software ranking and post-adoption discontinuance of usage is stronger for apps with higher levels of complexity.

The literature suggests that an improved knowledge base of users leads to a better understanding of the value of technology [3]. Technology-related knowledge enables users to better utilize product-related information in making adoption decisions [36], which reduces the dependency on other informational sources. As a software app evolves, its complexity typically increases [17, 53]. Users gain experiences and obtain more information from using earlier versions. The adopters of later versions are more likely to have accumulated substantial productrelated knowledge and become more technical savvy. These users are also more mindful when evaluating technological features of late updated versions and comparing them with other alternative choices [47]. Moreover, for experienced users with relevant technical knowledge, increased complexity in recent versions of a software app can arouse their curiosity and diligence in evaluation [14, 23]. As a result, they are able to place more weights on their own knowledge and less on the observation of other users’ behavior, weakening informational cascades. Their adoption decisions, in turn, should better <sup>fi</sup>t their speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs, and are less likely to result in regret and post-adoption discontinuance of usage [39]. Therefore, compared to the early and usually less complex versions of an app, informational cascades should be mitigated for the late updated versions which are usually more complex. Hence, the relationship between app ranking and post-adoption discontinuance of usage is weaker for late updated app versions with increased complexity. In this regard, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3b (H3b): When comparing between distinct versions of the same app, the relationship between software ranking and post-adoption discontinuance of usage is weaker for versions with higher levels of complexity (which are typically more recent versions).

## Research Methodology

## Data and Measurement

We test our hypotheses using a data set of Firefox apps from January 2012 to December 2014 (156 weeks). We collect the data from the o<sup>fi</sup>cial website for Firefox apps, that is, Firefox Add-ons Store (https://addons.mazilla.org/en-us/<sup>fi</sup>refox). The website provides over 20,000 free apps for the Firefox browser.

On the Firefox Add-ons Store website, apps are categorized into <sup>fi</sup>fteen categories to facilitate users’ search and download. For each app, the site publishes information such as the number of weekly downloads, total number of users, user reviews and ratings, release time, updating history (i.e., when and how many times), category, the developer’s name and source code. Firefox keeps track of the number of users who disabled the apps and publishes this information on the app’s webpage. In addition, the source codes of apps are available in the Firefox Add-ons Store. We collect the app information and source code of 1,338 apps over 156 weeks from January 2012 to December 2014.

The dependent variable in our empirical analysis is discontinuance of software use, which is measured as the number of users whose app is in the disabled status in a week. Our key independent variables are software ranking, user rating and software complexity. Software ranking re<sup>fl</sup>ects the predecessors’ adoption decisions. In the Firefox Add-ons Store, the apps can be sorted by either “most users” (i.e., daily user base averaged in the previous week) or “weekly downloads” (i.e., the total number of downloads in the past week).<sup>1</sup> Please convert note numbers to start with 1. Both most user ranking and weekly download ranking capture the popularity of the apps in the market. We use most user ranking in the main analysis and weekly download ranking in the robustness checks. User rating is measured by the cumulative app ratings published on the store website.

Regarding software complexity, we adopt text analytic approaches in the software engineering literature to analyze the content of app source code. In product design, product complexity refers to the number of components or sub-systems speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in the product and the coupling extent among them [21, 38]. From the perspective of users, a product is a bundle of functional features and attributes [19] and complex products are characterized as comprising a larger number of components or attributes [49]. The software engineering literature has developed a series of measures to capture software complexity, such as the binary size of installation <sup>fi</sup>le [52]; the lines of non-empty, noncomment statements in code [18, 45]; the number of classes [52], and the number of methods de<sup>fi</sup>ned in classes [18, 34, 45].

Following the software engineer literature [18, 34, 45], we apply text analytics to identify the procedure de<sup>fi</sup>nitions in the source code. The complexity variable is measured by the total count of procedure de<sup>fi</sup>nition statements.<sup>2</sup> Since the Add-ons Store publishes all historical versions of an app, we are able to track an app’s complexity evolution by evaluating the complexity of each version of this app. In the robustness tests, we use the installation <sup>fi</sup>le size and the version number to represent the level of software complexity.

Network e<sup>f</sup>ect is also an important driver of software adoption and di<sup>f</sup>usion [10, 13, 16, 29]. In addition to the number of users in the disabled status, Firefox also publishes the number of new downloads in the current week and the number of active users. To control network e<sup>f</sup>ect, we include an app’s cumulative downloads, which is the sum of the number of downloads in the current week and the number of active users [15]. This measure also re<sup>fl</sup>ects the valid total downloads (excluding users disabling the app). We also control for seven other factors: (1) the number of user reviews; (2) the total number of versions for each app; (3) the age of an app, which is measured by the number of days elapsed since it was initially released; (4) the age of the most recent app version, which is measured by the number of days elapsed since the last app update; (5) a dummy variable which indicates whether the app was updated in the week; (6) the total number of apps listed in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c category; and (7) app’s category. To test H1, we include the variable $R a n k _ { i , t - I }$ which measures the ranking of app i based on its average daily user base in week t-1. Since the value of the variable Rank decreases as the software ranking rises, its coe<sup>fi</sup>cient, $\beta _ { I } ,$ , is expected to be negative as higher-ranking apps (i.e., smaller Rank values) are expected to be associated with more post-adoption discontinuance. To examine the impact of user rating, we include the variable $R a t i n g _ { i , t - 1 }$ and its interaction with app ranking, $R a n k _ { i , t - 1 } { \times } R a t i n g _ { i , t - 1 }$ . The coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of the interaction term $R a n k _ { i , t - 1 } { \times } R a t i n g _ { i , t - 1 }$ is expected to be negative because user ratings are expected to strengthen the association between post-adoption discontinuance and app ranking.

To investigate the impact of app complexity on herding, the variable Complexity<sub>i,t-1</sub> and its interaction with app ranking $R a n k _ { i , t - 1 } \times C o m p l e x i t y _ { i , t - 1 }$ are included. Following Soysal and Krishnamurthi [44], we <sup>fi</sup>rst apply ordinary least squares (OLS) estimation to explore the total moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect (including both between-app and within-app e<sup>f</sup>ects) of app complexity on the relationship between app ranking and the discontinuance number. We then use the app-<sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ect (FE) estimation to examine how the impact of app ranking on post-adoption discontinuance changes when a particular app becomes more complex or less complex across di<sup>f</sup>erent versions. The FE model speci<sup>fi</sup>cation enables us to focus on the within-app e<sup>f</sup>ects. Users who try out high-ranking apps may be more interested in frequently trying out new choices, and users who like trying out new choices may <sup>fi</sup>rst try out high-ranking apps. The FE speci<sup>fi</sup>cation also helps alleviate this issue. For the FE estimation, we expect that app complexity will weaken the association between app ranking and post-adoption discontinuance so the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of $R a n k _ { i , t - 1 } { \times } C o m p l e x i t y _ { i , t - 1 } ,$ $\beta _ { 5 }$ is expected to be positive. By comparing the coe<sup>fi</sup>cients of the interaction term $R a n k _ { i , t - I }$ $\times C o m p l e x i t y _ { i , t - 1 }$ in the OLS estimates and the FE estimates, we can infer the between-app moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of $C o m p l e x i t y _ { i , t - 1 }$ . We expect the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of $R a n k _ { i , t - 1 } { \times } C o m p l e x i t y _ { i , t - 1 } ,$ $\beta _ { 5 }$ to be negative in the OLS estimates. The change of coe<sup>fi</sup>cient signs from positive in the FE estimates to negative in the OLS estimates would suggest that the between-app e<sup>f</sup>ect is negative.

## Results

Figure 1. shows that the distributions of Download and Discontinuance are both highly skewed and long tailed. Therefore we use logarithm transform of these two variables in the analysis. Figure 2a and bFirst mention of Figure 2. show the relationship between the numbers of downloads and discontinuance versus app ranking which is determined by the average daily user base in the previous week. In particular, the <sup>fi</sup>gures show that both Download and Discontinuance increase exponentially as app ranking increases (i.e., smaller Rank values). Since an app with more downloads are likely to have a larger number of discontinuance, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne a new variable, DiscontinuanceRatio, as the ratio of Discontinuance to Download to control the impact of Download on Discontinuance. Figure 2c shows that the discontinuance ratio increases too as the app ranking increases (i.e., smaller Rank values). In another word, the discontinuance number increases to a larger extent than the download number, which suggests the existence of herding and informational cascades in app adoption.

Table 4. reports our empirical results regarding the e<sup>f</sup>ect of app ranking and user rating on app discontinuance<sup>3</sup>. In Table 4, columns (1) through (4) show the OLS estimations, and columns (5) through (8) show the <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects estimations. The coe<sup>fi</sup>cients of $R a n k _ { i , t - I }$ are negative and statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ ) across all equations. So H1 is supported. The coe<sup>fi</sup>cient estimation suggests that if the ranking of an app drops by ten positions (i.e., the Rank value increases by ten), the discontinuance number decreases by 1.3% overall.

Table 4 also shows that the coe<sup>fi</sup>cients of the interaction term between $R a n k _ { i , t - I }$ and $R a t i n g _ { i , t - 1 }$ are negative and statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $\left( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 1 \right)$ . So H2 is supported, suggesting that user rating strengthens the negative association between rank value and postadoption discontinuance (i.e., make the negative association between the rank value and post-adoption more negative). For an app with a 3.0 rating, the discontinuance number drops by 1.27% if the app ranking drops by ten (the Rank value increases by ten). But when the app’s rating is 4.0, the discontinuance number will drop by 1.4% if the app ranking drops by ten.

## Robustness Analysis

We conduct several additional analyses to verify the robustness of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the existence of herding, we have followed the literature and estimated a model with the download number as the dependent variable and software ranking and its square term as the independent variables [15]. Our results show that the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of the software ranking, $R a n k _ { i } ,$ is negative and the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of square term of $R a n k _ { i , t - I }$ is positive, which are both consistent with the literature (Appendix Table A1).

Second, we also capture user discontinuance behavior using DiscontinuanceRatio, which is the ratio of Discontinuance to Download. The results based on this new discontinuance measure (Appendix Table A2, Columns 1 and 2) are consistent with our main results. App ranking in our main analysis is based on the number of app users, which is the default ranking method for Firefox apps on the Firefox Add-ons Store site. In Firefox Add-ons Store, users can also sort apps by the number of weekly downloads. Therefore, we conduct a robustness check using an alternative ranking measure, weekly download ranking. The results (summarized in Appendix Table A2, Columns 3 and 4) are qualitatively consistent with the main results. Firefox publishes the number of active users for each app, which is the di<sup>f</sup>erence between the download number and the number of disabled users. We use the number of active users as an alternative to control the network e<sup>f</sup>ect. The results (summarized in Appendix Table A2, Columns 5 and 6) are consistent with our main results qualitatively. In addition, users could also view Firefox apps by category so an app is only compared with other apps in the same category. We therefore employ another new ranking measure — app ranking within a category. The results (summarized in Appendix Table A2, Columns 7 and 8) are qualitatively consistent with the main results.

Third, app complexity in our main analysis is measured by the number of procedures in a Firefox add-on’s source codes. To further verify the robustness of our analysis, we also choose another widely used metric, i.e. the installation <sup>fi</sup>le size [52] as an alternative measure for app complexity. Table A2 Columns 9 and 10 summarize the results, which are qualitatively consistent with the main results. We also use app version as a proxy of users’ perceived app complexity to verify the robustness of our analysis. Software’s later versions often come with bug-<sup>fi</sup>x and new features [17, 53], which results in an increase in complexity. We therefore use app version number as an alternative measure of app complexity. The number of functions (NumofFunctions) is still included as a control variable. The results (summarized in Appendix Table A3 Column 1 and 2) are qualitatively consistent with the main results.

Fourth, Firefox Add-ons Store reports the aggregate number of users whose apps are in the disabled status. ## Discussion

## Theoretical Implications

This study provides important <sup>fi</sup>ndings that contribute to multiple streams of literature. First, this study contributes to the literature on product adoption by examining the postadoption discontinuance of usage. The IS literature on technology adoption and usage has well studied users’ initial adoption decisions [15, 29, 55]. However, the understanding of the post-adoption behavior and the underlying mechanisms of herding are still limited. Prior studies on informational cascades that have focused on the initial adoption decision are not su<sup>fi</sup>cient to exclude spurious herding [46], i.e., the situation in which that users similar adoption behavior as the predecessors is mainly because they receive similar information. We use post-adoption discontinuance data to better reveal how herding may be driven by informational cascades. Our study shows that the software products with higher ranking have greater post-adoption discontinuance of usage, when software downloads and other factors are controlled. This <sup>fi</sup>nding con<sup>fi</sup>rms the mechanism of informational cascades by showing that the following of others’ actions can result in undesirable adoption outcomes such as ex post regret.

Second, this study extends the understanding of user rating by demonstrating how user rating may interact with informational cascades. The results show that high-rating apps have a stronger relationship between app ranking and post-adoption discontinuance (that is, a greater extent of informational cascades). In this regard, higher rating may be harmful for the di<sup>f</sup>usion of more popular software because it escalates the herding behavior in software adoption and subsequently leads to more regrets and post-adoption discontinuance of usage. Such theoretical implications not only contribute to the literature on herding in software adoption, but also add to the research on the downside of online user rating.

Third, we examine the dual role of software complexity in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing informational cascades in software adoption. Software complexity is an important factor that users process and evaluate in the decision-making of product adoption [e.g. 41, 46, 55]. This study empirically explores its e<sup>f</sup>ects by distinguishing between within-app variation and betweenapp variation of complexity. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings illustrate the nuanced e<sup>f</sup>ects of software complexity. While increase of between-app complexity reinforces informational cascades by strengthening the relationship between software ranking and post-adoption discontinuance, the increase of within-app complexity mitigates such e<sup>f</sup>ect. An important fact is that software apps are frequently updated to improve quality. The adopters of newer versions of a particular software are likely to have adopted the previous versions and are well informed about the software through prior experience. Herding is less likely to occur on these users and as a result, the impact of software ranking is weaker. Our study reconciles the theoretical tensions about the role of software complexity in herding at the post-adoption stage.

## Practical Implications

Our study generates important practical implications. First, ranking is widely regarded as a trustworthy quality signal and a higher ranking can help attract more users. In practice, software application providers strive to achieve top ranking positions. However, our study demonstrates a downside of having top ranking positions — downloads driven by high rankings may also induce herding which a<sup>f</sup>ects long term usage. In this regard, our study suggests cautions with the myopic seeking of top-ranking positions.

Second, in promoting their products, software providers should be aware of intricate interdependent relationships between various factors such as software ranking, user rating and software complexity. When there are multiple informational cues available for users to evaluate software quality, the e<sup>f</sup>ects of these factors may not be additive. Promoting user rating and software ranking simultaneously may also escalate informational cascades e<sup>f</sup>ect that results in undesirable high ex post abandon rate. The implication is especially useful for the software application providers with constrained resources. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study will help the providers allocate their promotional e<sup>f</sup>orts and resources wisely.

Third, software promotions by escalating ranking may have heterogeneous e<sup>f</sup>ect for software with di<sup>f</sup>erent complexity levels. It is important to consider the product complexity level before choosing the ranking promotion e<sup>f</sup>ort. If a new software application is very complex, high ranking is a double-edge sword, leading to more adoptions as well as a higher abandon rate. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings have important implications on new product release. When releasing a new software application, the providers could choose to <sup>fi</sup>rst introduce core functions in initial versions, which serve as a knowledge base for users. New features and functions can be added gradually by releasing updates. This strategy could lead to a more stable user base and long-term success.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study is subject to several limitations and can be extended in several ways. First, our <sup>fi</sup>eld data is about Firefox add-on apps, which are relatively light apps and less costly to download and remove. These features actually make it less expensive for users to experiment di<sup>f</sup>erent apps. Others’ information is not as in<sup>fl</sup>uential as that for expensive and risky decision-making. This e<sup>f</sup>ect may curb the e<sup>f</sup>ect of informational cascades and alleviate the potential overestimation to some extent. However, caution should still be used in generalizing the <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other products. Extending this study to other paid products would help verify the robustness of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Second, our empirical analysis cannot fully distinguish rational and irrational herding. Herding that arises from informational cascades could be rational under certain circumstances when decision makers properly incorporate the observation of others’ actions into their own decision-making. However, the same type of herd behavior can also be generated from irrational behavior when naive decision makers simply cannot interpret or process the available information [59, 60]. Future research may consider separating rational and irrational herding to generate more interesting insights.

Third, we measure Firefox add-ons’ ratings using the cumulative rating posted on the Firefox Add-ons Store site. The majority of add-ons raters did not provide review comments along with their ratings. Review comments could provide rich information about users’ expectations and usage experiences. In addition, other information sources, such as third-party review sites, social media sites and software magazines may provide useful product and usage information about Firefox add-ons. Future research may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from taking into account such information to develop more insights.

Finally, Firefox Add-ons Store only reports the aggregated download and discontinuance numbers at the app level. We are not able to observe individual-level app installation and uninstallation behavior. An extension to the current research would be valuable to examine the herding behavior and decision-making process at the individual level. Information such as users’ knowledge about computers and apps, Internet browsing habits, and users’ app usage history could be very helpful for understanding individuallevel decision-making process of app adoption and discontinuance.

## Conclusions

This study examines herding and the role of informational cascades in software adoption based on users’ software discontinuance behavior at the post-adoption stage. The results show that with the download number being controlled, a software application with a higher ranking is associated with more post-adoption discontinuance of usage. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings add to the literature by empirically con<sup>fi</sup>rming the existence of informational cascade e<sup>f</sup>ect in herding. We also delve into the interaction between software ranking and another widely used informational mechanism, user rating, and <sup>fi</sup>nd that user rating strengthens the relationship between software ranking and post-adoption discontinuance. The strengthening e<sup>f</sup>ect supports the existence of social bias in app ratings. Further analysis shows that the ranking e<sup>f</sup>ect on users’ discontinuance of software usage is also impacted by software internal feature, such as product complexity. Across di<sup>f</sup>erent software applications, a higher level of software complexity strengthens the ranking e<sup>f</sup>ect. But regarding a particular software application, the ranking e<sup>f</sup>ect is weaker as the complexity levels up. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings help software vendors gain insights in users’ herding behavior in software adoption and optimize their software releasing strategies and promotional e<sup>f</sup>ort allocation.
