# Economic metaphors for solving intrafirm allocation problems: What does a market buy us?<sup>☆</sup>

Michael Brydon

Faculty of Business Administration, Simon Fraser University, Burnaby, BC, Canada

Received 25 October 2004; received in revised form 20 August 2005; accepted 8 February 2006 Available online 6 March 2006

## Abstract

The primary advantage of using simulated internal markets to solve complex resource allocation problems is that markets permit much of the computation of a solution to be distributed over a large number independent agents running on separate processors. The difficulty that arises in the context of NP-hard resource allocation problems is that the market for resources inevitably takes the form of a combinatorial auction, which induces a different type of NP-hard problem. We examine an important class of stochastic, intrafirm resource allocation problems and ask whether economic constructs, such as agents, markets, and prices, provide a useful foundation for structuring decentralized heuristic solution techniques. We show how complex exchange protocols can help market based search techniques avoid the local maxima problems associated with other greedy search heuristics and converge on good equilibrium solutions.

## 1. Introduction

Firms such as Wal-Mart, UPS, and Dell, have demonstrated that the ability to tackle complex intrafirm combinatorial optimization problems can be a source of operational efficiency and competitive advantage. Accordingly, finding new and better ways of deploying computational resources to address the largest and most unruly real-world resource allocation problems is an important area of decision support research. One such approach that is generating considerable interest from within multiple research communities involves the use of economic metaphors – agents, markets, and prices – as the inspiration for large scale systems for distributed optimization. There is a large and growing collection of market-based systems to address resource allocation problems such as the provision of network services [26], train scheduling [16], the allocation of R&D resources [1], the assignment of trucking contracts [10], factory scheduling [27] and airport runway allocation [20], just to mention a few.

Although the formulation and implementation of these systems differ in important ways, two core principles of the market based approach can be identified: the necessity for agent self-interest and the importance of “gains from trade”. Agent self-interest is necessary because it permits a large, complex resource allocation problem to be decomposed into many smaller independent problems that can be tackled simultaneously by computer-based agents. Gains from trade accrue whenever an exchange between agents creates economic surplus that can be divided in such a way that at least one agent is better off and no agent is worse off. Given that the agents are exclusively self-interested, it is clear that such Pareto-improving exchanges are the only type of reallocations that can occur in a strict market-based system. The First Fundamental Theorem of Welfare Economics guarantees that such self-interested exchanges within a properly functioning market will propel the market towards a solution in which no additional economic surplus can be created [12].

The market-based approach thus provides a blueprint for practical distributed computation based on a wellestablished foundation of economic theory. The problem that arises in practice, however, is that resource allocation problems are characterized by significant complementarity and substitutability between resources that make a “properly functioning market” very difficulty to achieve. The interdependencies between resources mean that agents must ultimately consider arbitrary bundles of resources in a combinatorial auction (CA). The “winner determination problem” for a CA is known to be NP-hard [15,24] and thus market-based decomposition invariably transforms an NP-hard resource allocation problem into an NP-hard winner determination problem. Although several researchers have developed techniques for solving very large instances of the winner determination problem for CAs to optimality (e.g., [25]), the assumption at the core of this research is that large, industrial-scale problems in industries such as automobile manufacturing and retail distribution ultimately entail the development of heuristic solution techniques [9,8].

Accordingly, the question address in this research is whether the market-based approach and its underlying economic metaphors provide a good foundation for distributed heuristic problem solving. At first glance, it appears that the myopic, self-interested agents at the core of the approach constitute a liability since their greediness routinely leads to premature equilibrium at suboptimal solutions. Indeed, a very simple example in which agents use a straightforward and familiar exchange protocol is often used to illustrate the apparent inevitability of local maxima in problems with resource complementarity (e.g., [27]). We explore the underlying causes of this local maxima problem in market-based systems and show how increasingly complex exchange protocols can achieve a more favorable tradeoff between solution quality and computation time. To do this, we situate the winner determination problem within the broader context of local search in which “moves” correspond to the transactions supported by the market's exchange protocol. Framing the market-based approach as local search permits a direct comparison with other popular search metaheuristics such as tabu search [8,18].

The paper proceeds as follows. The essential features of an intrafirm resource allocation problem are identified in Section 2 and a three-phase process for decomposing an intrafirm resource allocation problem into agent-level problems is presented. The computational challenges posed by the agent-level price determination problem are briefly discussed whereas the computational challenges of the market-level CA are described in detail using the “contract graph” [24] formulation. An incomplete “SUB-LIST” exchange protocol is introduced in Section 3 and is used throughout the remainder of the paper as a representative instance of a market-based metaheuristic for local search. The randomly generated test set used for empirical analysis of the SUBLIST protocol and the results of the empirical analysis are presented in Section 4. Section 5 concludes by summarizing the main findings of this research and enumerating some of the remaining questions for future research.

## 2. Transforming intrafirm resource allocation problems

The focus of this research is on the use of marketbased systems to solve large intrafirm resource allocation problems such as production scheduling, inventory allocation, and transportation planning. The distinguishing characteristic of an intrafirm problem is the existence of a single well-defined objective function, which typically involves maximizing the firm's profit. Such problems can be tackled in principle using conventional monolithic optimization techniques; however, the size and complexity of industrial-scale problems mean that heuristic search techniques (including those embedded in products from vendors such as i2, ILOG, and Manugistics) are increasingly used in practice [13]. The rationale for introducing economic constructs into the intrafirm context is not to model the problem's natural decentralization, but rather to impose artificial decentralization via a simulated “internal market” so that massively distributed computation can be applied to very large problem instances [2].

Agents and markets have also been used to address interfirm resource allocation problems such as supply chain management (e.g., [7]). The critical difference between the intra- and interfirm contexts is that agents in the interfirm problems represent the interest of real stakeholders (such as customers and suppliers) and each stakeholder has its own idiosyncratic utility function. The notion of “global” optimality is meaningless in the interfirm context since there is no legitimate way to compare utilities across the stakeholders in the underlying resource allocation problem. Instead, non-additive social welfare criteria such as “fairness” and “minimum harm” are typically employed [21].

Interfirm problems also pose challenges for the designers of market-based systems since the designers typically have little control over the capabilities and behaviors of the agents within the market. The agents representing stakeholders external to the firm may lie, speculate, and behave opportunistically in an effort to appropriate surplus. As a consequence, much of the market design research for interfirm multi-agent systems is directed at mechanisms to encourage truthful revelation and discourage opportunistic behavior [7]. The design of market-based systems for intrafirm problems is comparatively much simpler. Designers have complete control over the agents that interact within a closed market and can therefore choose to not endow the agents with capabilities and behaviors (such as misrepresenting their willingness to pay) that are potential sources of market failure. The high degree of agent-level control means that the requirement for incentive-compatible exchange mechanisms (which are so important in the interfirm context) can be designed out of intrafirm market-based systems.

The process of transforming a stochastic, intrafirm resource allocation problem into a market-based winner determination problem can be conceptualized in terms of three distinct phases: decomposition, agent-level price determination, and market-based recomposition [4]. Each of these phases is described below.

## 2.1. Decomposition

The purpose of a social welfare function is to define global utility (the utility of society as a whole) in terms of the individual agents that constitute the society. However, global utility in an intrafirm resource allocation problem is defined exogenously by the firm's objective (or profit) function, π. The task facing the designer of the market is therefore to decompose the given social welfare function to establish a straightforward relationship between agent utility, $u _ { j } ( \cdot )$ for each agen $\mathrm { t } j ,$ and global utility. The simplest approach is to use a Benthamite (or utilitarian) social welfare function in which the global utility function is additively separable:

$$
\pi = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} u _ {j} (\cdot), \text {   where   } u _ {j} (\cdot) \in \mathfrak {R} ^ {+}.\tag{1}
$$

To illustrate how agent-level utility functions that satisfy Eq. (1) can be achieved, consider a scheduling problem in which the objective is to allocate $M = \{ 1 , 2 , . . . ,$ $m \}$ distinct resources to $N = \{ 1 , 2 , . . . , n \}$ jobs such that total value of the allocation is maximized. First, a computer based agent is created to represent the interests and make decisions on behalf of each job, $j \in N .$ Then, the costs and rewards routinely allocated to each job are used to parameterize the agent's utility function. For example, jobs in a manufacturing environment typically earn a “terminal reward” on completion – such as a wholesale price or a transfer price – that is determined exogenously and is known in advance, as are any dependencies on completion time such as lateness penalties. On the cost side of the equation, a job typically incurs a per-unit-time holding cost while it is considered work-in-process and may also incur setup costs for machines. The agents' utility functions capture all relevant externally defined costs and benefits so that the profit of the firm is equal to the sum of profits earned by each job executed within the firm.

In some cases, satisfying Eq. (1) may require additional modeling effort. For example, the property of additive utility does not hold when parts are mated together during final assembly. The joint utility function of the mating parts creates an economic externality that must be priced, or internalized, in order for an additive relationship between agent and global utility to exist. Such externalities can be internalized in a straightforward manner by introducing additional agents to provide coordinating incentives for others in much the same way as contractors coordinate the behaviors of subcontractors. Similarly, agents must be created to represent resources whenever the costs of processing depend on the sequence of the jobs through the resource.<sup>1</sup> These types of externalities are common in practice; however, they are not considered in this paper in order to simplify presentation of the core research findings regarding market-based recomposition.

## 2.2. Agent-level price determination

We assume here that all resources are modeled as discrete; thus, a unit of processing time on Milling Machine #5 at time t might be designated as resource i whereas the unit of processing time on the same machine starting at $t + 1$ might be designated as resource $i + 1$ . Owing such resources affords an agent some degree of control over the costs and rewards the job it represents encounters during its progression through the production system: An agent that owns time on the milling machine can move its job closer to completion and thereby minimize its holding and lateness costs. However, buying and selling resources in a market require each market participant to know its own private valuations for the resources. As a consequence, price determination for the resources in M is the primary task facing the agents.

At its simplest, the price an agent is willing to pay for a resource equals the agent's total utility when it owns the resource minus its total utility when it does not own the resource. Since scheduling environments typically involve numerous sources of uncertainty such as the amount of processing time required for a particular operation and the frequency of machine breakdowns, the bid price revealed to the market is the agent's expected marginal benefit of owning the resource. Endowing agents with the ability to reveal prices other than their true expected marginal benefit makes little sense in the intrafirm context since doing so could permit agents to opportunistically increase their surplus

There are two features of the agent-level price determination problem are that are attractive from a computational point of view. First, the agent-level price determination problem is exponentially smaller than the original firm-level resource allocation problem since only the needs of a single job need be considered by each agent. Second, each agent can determine its willingness to pay independent of all other agents so the agent-level problem is easily distributed. On the negative side, each agent may be required to generate prices for a large number of contingencies. One source of contingencies is that an agent's willingness to pay for a resource typically depends on the physical state of the job in the production system, $s { \in } S _ { j } .$ . For example, agent j's willingness to pay for resource i (as it is defined in the example above) is zero if the operation on job j for which time on Milling Machine #5 is required has already been completed. Agent $j ^ { \circ } \mathbf { s }$ valuation of a resource “good” i in state s is thus written $\phi _ { j } ( i , s ) { \in } \Re ^ { + }$ in order to reflect this dependency on <sup>ð Þ</sup>state. A second source of contingencies is that goods in a resource allocation problem are characterized by varying degrees of complementarity and substitutability.

Two goods $( i \neq k ) \in M$ are considered economic complements whenever $\phi _ { j } ( \{ i , k \} , s ) > \phi _ { j } ( i , s ) + \phi _ { j } ( k , s )$ and economic substitutes whenever $\phi _ { j } ( \{ i , k \} , s ) < \phi _ { j } ( i , s ) + \phi _ { j }$ $( k , s )$ . Fig. 1 illustrates the two types of dependencies for an agent: in the tree on the left, the agent requires resource 1 or 2, but has no need for both. The values on the leaf nodes of the tree show that the agent has a slight preference for resource 1 (\$10) over 2 (\$8) so the goods are imperfect substitutes. A complementary relationship between the goods is shown in the tree on the right. Both goods are required in order for the agent to realize a nonzero payoff.

Such interdependencies are common in manufacturing contexts. For example, an agent that requires multiple units of processing time has little incentive to acquire only a subset of the necessary units. On the other hand, one unit of processing on a machine is typically interchangeable with another; the only difference between the two may be that the timing and sequencing of the processing entail differences in costs and rewards. The valuation function is thus rewritten as a non-linear function of resource bundles, $\phi _ { j } ( r , s ) { \in } \Re$ where $r \subseteq M$ and $\phi _ { j } ( r , s ) { \neq } \sum _ { i \in r } \phi _ { j } ( i , s )$ <sup>ð Þ</sup>. The implication is that an agent's price determination problem in each state s may involve up to $2 ^ { m }$ distinct resource combinations.

The output from the agent-level price determination phase can be conceptualized as a partially ordered set of expected valuations, $V _ { j } ( s ) = \{ V _ { j 1 } ( s ) , V _ { j 2 } ( s ) , . . . , V _ { j \nu } ( s ) \}$ in which each valuation is a tuple $V _ { j k } ( s ) = \langle r , \phi _ { j } ( r , s ) \rangle$ . Since the intention here is to examine the computational characteristics of the market rather than the agents, a discussion of the precise manner in which the agentlevel price determination problem is solved to yield $V _ { j } ( s )$ is beyond the scope of this paper. However, is worthwhile to identify the core challenges inherent in the agent-level price determination problem and to briefly outline the approach used in this research. Unfortunately, an obvious barrier to using an MDP formulation and dynamic programming to determine prices is that the resulting state space is unmanageably large, even for relatively simple agents.

There are, however, three factors that can help reduce the effective size of the price determination problem's state space in practice. First, the actual number of resources under consideration by an individual agent representing an individual job is typically a subset of the total number of production resources in the entire system, m. A particular job may not have to visit each machine on the factory floor or may have upper and lower bounds on start and finish times. Second, as the tree representation in Fig. 1 illustrates, some combinations of resources are dominated or infeasible and need not be included in the valuation set. For example, the bundle $r = \{ 1 , 2 \}$ is not included in the tree on the left because the two goods are substitutes and {2} adds no additional value if {1} is already owned. Conversely, the ownership status of {2} is not considered in the tree on the right when {1} is not owned because both resources are required and the value of either resource by itself is the same as having no resources. Additional, problemspecific constraints may exist that make certain resource combinations clearly infeasible or dominated. For example, the array of precedence constraints embedded in multiple machine scheduling problems makes many sequences of allocations technically infeasible. Finally, much of the information embedded in a conventional MDP representation of a problem's state space is irrelevant to the choice of an action and the value of being in that state. “Structured” techniques for representing and solving large MDPs can be used to dramatically reduce the effective size of this state space without impacting optimality [3,6]. Indeed, the effective size of the agent-level price determination problem using such techniques has been shown empirically to be much smaller than the conventional MDP formulation [5].

Ultimately, the requirement to determine prices over an exponentially large number of state and resource contingencies means that an exact, precomputed list of valuations may be unrealistic. Techniques for approximating agent-level valuations or computing them ondemand are likely necessary for industrial-scale marketbased systems to be feasible. However, since the focus of this research is on the quality of solutions enabled by incomplete exchange protocols at the market level, exact values of $V _ { j } ( s )$ are precomputed using dynamic programming so that any losses of optimality can be attributed exclusively to the various market heuristics.

## 2.3. Market-based recomposition

The decomposition and price determination phases described in the preceding sections can be used to transform an interfirm stochastic resource allocation problem into a deterministic combinatorial allocation problem (CAP). If the variable $x _ { j } ( r )$ is defined to equal 1 whenever the bundle of resource r is owned by agent j and zero otherwise, the problem can be written [15]:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max \sum_ {r} \sum_ {j} x _ {j} (r) \phi_ {j} (r, s) \\ \text {s.t.} \sum_ {r} x _ {j} (r) \leq 1, \forall j \\ \sum_ {r: i \in r} \sum_ {j} x _ {j} (r) \leq 1, \forall i \\ x _ {j} (r) \in \{0, 1 \}, \forall i, r \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

The objective function maps back to the Benthamite social welfare function in Eq. (1) so that solving Eq. (2) maximizes firm profit, π. The first two constraints in Eq. (2) ensure that each agent owns at most one bundle of resources and that each resource is contained in at most one bundle. Since CAP is known to be NP-hard [15], the question facing the market designer is how to get selfinterested agents to voluntarily exchange resources in a CA so that a “good” (though not necessarily optimal) allocation is achieved.

One means of visualizing the complexity issues that arise in a CA is to construct a contract graph [24] showing the possible reallocations that might occur as a result of interagent exchanges of resources in a pure exchange [12] economy. The nodes in the contract graph represent allocations of resources to agents in the form of an mtuple in which element i is the label of the agent that currently owns resource i. To illustrate, a small portion<sup>3</sup> of the contract graph for a three-job, one-machine scheduling problem is shown in Fig. 2. The decision horizon of the problem is nine time units so the nine units of processing time on the machine can be allocated to the three agents or left unallocated, as indicated by a dash in the m-tuple. The arcs between the nodes of the contract graph represent reallocations that can occur within the scope of a single transaction. An arc is Pareto improving – and thus feasible in a market consisting of autonomous, self-interested agents – if all agents involved in the transaction are at least as well off after the transaction as before the transaction and at least one agent is strictly better off. Although the firm-level value of the allocation at any node can quickly be determined by summing the utilities of the individual agents, the difficulty that arises in the context of a CA is that a fully connected contract graph has each node connected to $( n + 1 ) ^ { m } - 1$ other nodes, any one of which could be optimal.

Numerous complete and incomplete algorithms for solving CAs have been proposed (e.g., [22,29,15,9]). Most algorithms described in the literature invoke the metaphor of an auctioneer and implement a highly centralized algorithm using well-established optimization or search techniques to select winning bids. For example, Zurel and Nisan [29] generate approximate solutions by formulating the winner determination problem as a packing problem, solving a linear program (LP) relaxation of the problem based on all the agents bids, and use the results of the LP relaxation to direct the progress of greedy local search algorithm. Hoos and Boutilier [9] use a centralized heuristic local search algorithm that selects bids for insertion into the solution based on the average revenue per good that the bid contributes. The approach used in this research is different in that it retains a more familiar form of exchange for the recomposition phase by implementing a continuous auction in which the agent themselves initiate and execute all reallocations of resources.

Execution of mutually beneficial exchanges between agents is facilitated by the introduction of a numeraire commodity. Such a commodity has no intrinsic value to the agents but is universally recognized within the economy as a store of value. Recognition can be achieved by endowing the agents with a quasi-linear utility function [28],

$$
u _ {j} (r, s) = \phi_ {j} (r, s) + \mu_ {j}\tag{3}
$$

in which $\mu _ { j }$ is the amount of the numeraire commodity held by the agent. There are three advantages to the formulation in Eq. (3): First, an agent's intrinsic value for of a bundle of resources is independent of $\mu _ { j }$ and is thus remains undistorted by wealth effects. Second, combining the agent-level utility function with Eq. (1) makes it clear that $\sum _ { j } \mu _ { j }$ is a constant quantity that is independent of the allocation of resources to agents. The implication, which is discussed in greater detail below, is that transaction prices (which determine the precise division of economic surplus between agents) have no impact on the ultimate allocation of resources nor the value of the firm-level objective function. Finally, the quasi-linear utility function permits agents to express marginal differences in private preferences relative to quantities of numeraire. To illustrate, consider the conditions under which agent j is indifferent between two resource bundles, r and $r ^ { \prime } { : }$

$$
u _ {j} (r, s) = u _ {j} \left(r ^ {\prime}, s\right) = \phi_ {j} (r, s) + \mu_ {j} = \phi_ {j} \left(r ^ {\prime}, s\right) + \mu_ {j} ^ {\prime}
$$

so that:

$$
\phi_ {j} (r, s) - \phi_ {j} (r ^ {\prime}, s) = \mu_ {j} ^ {\prime} - \mu_ {j} = \Delta \mu_ {j}
$$

Agent j will voluntarily relinquish control of r in exchange for $r ^ { \prime }$ if it receives a side payment of at least $\Delta \mu _ { j }$ . The quantity $\Delta \mu _ { j }$ is called j's indifference price for the transaction and a Pareto-improving exchange is possible whenever some other agent (or collection of agents) is willing to pay j its indifference price as compensation for any loss of utility arising from the exchange. Conversely, j is in a position to initiate an exchange whenever the value of $\Delta \mu _ { j }$ is negative, indicating that j is willing to pay some agent (or collection of agents) up to $\Delta \mu _ { j }$ to own $r ^ { \prime }$ instead of r.

## 3. An incomplete exchange protocol

An exchange protocol specifies the types and scope of the transactions that can be executed within a market. A complete “OCSM” exchange protocol that permits multiple goods, multiple buyers, and multiple sellers within the scope of a single transaction has been shown to be necessary and sufficient for economic efficiency in a CA [24]. A market based on such a protocol has more in common with the complex, multiple-player, multiple team deals one occasionally sees in professional sports than a continuous double auction like the NASDAQ. An incomplete exchange protocol, in contrast, implements only a subset of the arcs emanating from a node in a fully connected contract graph. Most of the auctions we encounter in real economies use simple exchange protocols that only admit transactions involving two agents (a seller and a winning bidder) and a single good. These restrictions mean that the connectivity of each node in the contract graph is limited to the nm − 1 nodes that differ from the current allocation in the ownership of one good.

The downside of reducing search by using an incomplete exchange protocol is an increased risk of both small hops and local maxima. The problem of small hops occurs whenever the arc between two nodes is not supported by the incomplete exchange protocol and moving from one node to the other requires transitions to one or more intermediate nodes. Since each arc on the indirect path requires a self-contained Pareto-improving transaction, the total amount of computation required by market participants increases in proportion to the number of hops. Conversely, the local maxima problem arises whenever an optimal node is unreachable from the current node — that is, no path exists between the current and optimal nodes for which each arc in the path is Pareto-improving. The market thus attains equilibrium at an economically inefficient allocation.

The impact of the market's exchange protocol on both the small hops and local maxima problems can be illustrated with an example. Recall the partial contract graph for the three-job, one-machine scheduling problem shown in Fig. 2. Assume that each agent receives a terminal reward of \$50 on completion of its job but incurs a constant holding cost of \$5 per unit of time in the production system. Assume as well that the deterministic processing requirements for each job are (4, 3, 2) for j = (1, 2, 3), respectively. The initial allocation of resources to jobs shown in node A permits each job to attain its terminal reward before the end of the decision horizon. However, we know that the optimal schedule in this very simple deterministic example is provided by the shortest processing time (SPT) dispatch rule and corresponds to node D. Since the optimal solution has agent 3 owning the first two resources, we start by considering the case shown by the left-most arc in which agent 3 bids for the bundle $r = \{ 1 , 2 \}$ . On the sellers side, agent 1 requires compensation of at least \$60 to relinquish {1} and agent 2 requires at least \$75 to relinquish {2} for a total ask price of \$135. Accordingly, no transition from A to B can occur since agent 3's willingness to pay for the bundle {1,2} is only \$35.

The large discrepancy between the bid price and the combined ask price along the arc A–B occurs because the loss of a single unit of processing time for both sellers prevents them from exiting the system and earning their terminal reward. More generally, the problem is that the simple exchange protocol implied by A–B does not permit agents to acquire substitute resources. The combined ask price thus compensates the sellers for their worst-case losses. A more complex bidding protocol permits each seller to relinquish some goods and acquire substitutes within the scope of a single transaction. To illustrate, consider the arc A–D, which represents a single transaction consisting of three separate exchanges: agent 3 buys {1,2} from agents 1 and 2; agent 2 buys {5} from agent 1; and agent 1 buys {8,9} from agent 3. The indifference price calculations for each agent are shown in Table 1. Terms of the form p(r) correspond to the transaction prices of other goods exchanged within the scope of the transaction. In this example, agent 3 is willing to pay up to \$35 plus whatever it earns from the sale of {8,9} to acquire {1,2}.

A Pareto improving transaction can occur along arc A–D since the price agent 3 is willing to pay for {1, 2} exceeds the combined indifference prices of the sellers. That is, $3 5 + p ( 8 , 9 ) > 1 5 + p ( 8 , 9 )$ for all values of p(8, 9). This outcome is not surprising since the arc A–D represents an unrestricted search for substitute resources. Unrestricted search permits the market to move to the optimal allocation in a single transaction; however, the cost is recursive bidding in which a single bid may involve every good and agent in the economy. In this example, agent 2 cannot quote an ask price for {2} until it finds a substitute and must therefore initiate its own bid for a resource, in this case {5}. But agent 1 cannot respond to the bid for {5} until it determines the price of a substitute and thus the chain of bids continues until all the possibilities are exhausted. In the worst case, the agents in a complete exchange protocol will evaluate a number of allocations bounded by $O ( \overline { { \mathbf { v } } } ^ { n } )$ , where $\overline { { \mathbf { v } } }$ is the average size of the agents' valuation list, before the optimal transaction is found.

As an aside, it is worthwhile to consider the role of $p ( 5 )$ and $p ( 8 , 9 )$ in the indifference calculations shown in Table 1. Although the specific transaction prices ultimately affect the absolute wealth of the agents, we can see that the $p ( 5 )$ and $p ( 8 , 9 )$ have no impact on the economic efficiency of the transaction or the ultimate allocation of resources to agents. The ask price used in this research, for example, is set by convention to the honestly reported indifference price of the seller. Similarly, the price of all goods in the SUBLIST (described below) is set to zero.

## 3.1. A restricted search for substitutes: the SUBLIST

The arc A–C in Fig. 2 represents a compromise exchange protocol in which a restricted search for substitute resources is permitted. Specifically, potential sellers are permitted to acquire substitute resources from a “substitutes list” of resources. This SUBLIST contains resources which are either unowned or which have been discarded by other agents participating in the transaction. Returning to the example in Fig. 2, resources {8, 9} are made redundant and are added to the SUBLIST when agent 3 bids on {1, 2}. Potential sellers are permitted to check the SUBLIST for potential substitutes before responding to bids. In this case, agents 1 and 2 can use resources 8 and 9 to complete their processing requirements within the deadline imposed by the decision horizon and thereby avoid the heavy penalty associated with arc A–B. The assumption in Fig. 2 is that agent 1 chooses first and pulls {8} from the SUBLIST although, as discussed below, the sequence does not impact the final solution in this particular example. The transaction represented by A–C is Pareto-improving since the sum of indifference prices for the two sellers, $\$ 5+525=530$ , is less than the \$35 agent 3 is willing to pay for the transaction to occur. As it turns out, C is not an equilibrium outcome since the transaction represented by C–D is also Pareto-improving: agent 2 is willing to pay up to \$20 to acquire {5} whereas agent 1 is willing to accept any amount over \$5 in exchange for {9}. The path $\{ { \bf A } { - } { \bf C } , \ { \bf C } { - } { \bf D } \}$ illustrates a problem instance in which two short hops can be used to attain a global maximum even though the contract graph is only partially connected (arc A–D is not supported by the SUBLIST exchange protocol).

The search for economic surplus in the contract graph is thus driven by individual agents seeking to increase the utility of their own resource allocations. The market for resources is structured as a continuous reverse auction in which agent j identifies a desirable allocation and then issue a “request for quotation” (RFQ) to the agent or agents who own the resources missing from agent $j ^ { \circ } \mathbf { s }$ current endowment. The reason a reverse auction is used is because a double auction requires bid and ask prices for every good in the economy. In a CA setting, individual markets would have to be made for an exponential number of resource bundles and the auctioneer would have to cope with a complete set of bids from each agent, $n ( 2 ^ { m } - 1 )$ bids in the worst case.

The partially ordered list of valuations, $V _ { j } ( s ) _ { i }$ , plays an important role in a reverse auction since each agent must be able to identify its most valuable allocations in order to initiate exchanges. In each iteration, each agent starts at the top of its valuation list and works its way down the list submitting RFQs until either a feasible exchange is discovered, an allocation with the same value as the current allocation is reached, or some other stopping criterion is satisfied. Given the size of $V _ { j } ( s )$ and the declining desirability of the allocations as the agent moves down its valuation list, it may be advantageous to limit the number of allocations that an agent considers in each iteration. For example, stopping criteria can be defined in which the agent submits $\mathrm { R F Q s }$ for only its top b allocations. Not only does such a criterion reduce search, it creates the possibility of eliminating the need to completely precompute $V _ { j } ( s )$ and rely instead on heuristics to guess the agent's top b allocations. The value of less-promising allocations, which are needed in response to RFQs from other agents, could be computed on-demand based on the heuristic selection of promising substitutes. The time and solution quality implications of imposing a b-limit on RFQs are examined in Section 4.

A serialized version of the SUBLIST exchange protocol is summarized in Fig. 3. The worst-case complexity of the algorithm is $O ( n ^ { 2 } b ^ { 2 } { \overline { { \mathbf { v } } } } )$ , where $b \in [ 1 , \overline { { \mathrm { v } } } ]$ can either vary with the average number of tuples in the agents' valuation lists or be set to a constant value in a blimit variant of the SUBLIST protocol. Even if b is permitted to vary with v¯ and is therefore potentially exponentially large in the number of resources, it is independent of the number of agents (jobs) in the system and can be controlled to a large extent by modeling decisions such as the granularity of time, the length of the relevant decision horizon, and so on. Moreover, as discussed above, the actual number of relevant tuples in each agent's list may be much smaller in practice than the worst case. The magnitude of the reduction in the effective size of the agents' valuation lists due to dominance and infeasibility is examined empirically in Section 4.

## 3.2. Local search formulation

Formulating the market-based approach as a search over a partially connected contract graph permits a direct comparison with existing heuristic optimization techniques based on local search. To illustrate, consider the standard formulation of a local search problem in which the objective is to maximize a function f(x) subject to $x \in X .$ Assuming an initial solution $x ,$ the search for an improved solution is limited to some neighborhood $N ( x ) \subset X ,$ which includes all alternative solutions $x ^ { \prime }$ that can be reached from x in a single move. If an improved solution $f ( x ^ { \prime } ) { > } f ( x )$ is found, $x ^ { \prime }$ becomes the new solution, a new neighborhood is generated, and the process continues. Otherwise, the search terminates.

An important methodological issue that arises in local search concerns the definition a move used to generate the neighborhood for a given solution. A second issue concerns the problem of local maxima. Steepest ascent (or greedy) methods by themselves seldom provide good solutions [8,23] and thus many search techniques permit the search algorithm to violate the steepest ascent criterion so that a larger part of the search space may be explored. For example, random restart, simulated annealing and genetic algorithms include an element of randomness that permits nonimproving solutions to be selected [18]. The Casanova algorithm for CAs [9] includes several sources of randomness for selecting bids and includes a provision for random restarts. Tabu search [8] deemphasizes randomness but violates the steepest ascent termination condition by forcing selection of a move even if all solutions in the neighborhood are inferior to the current solution. A tabu list of forbidden solutions is then used to prevent cycling on subsequent iterations.

In market-based search, a node in the contract graph corresponds to a solution x and the neighborhood corresponds to all the nodes (allocations) that are reachable using a single valid transaction. The set of valid transactions is determined by the contents of the initiating RFQ and the transaction scope supported by the exchange protocol. Market-based search thus represents a departure from the most popular strategies for local search since core elements of the market approach—agent autonomy and self-interest—preclude any possibility of non-improving moves (only Paretoimproving moves are acceptable to agents) and obviate the need for “adaptive memory” schemes such as tabu lists to prevent cycling (a Pareto-improving move cannot be reversed with another Pareto-improving move within the same state, s). Market-based search is thus a pure steepest-ascent approach in which the only non-determinism is the sequence in which agents submit RFQs to the market.

## 4. Empirical results

Given the framework for market-based decomposition described in Section 2 and the SUBLIST exchange protocol introduced in Section 3, three problem-specific (and therefore empirical) questions arise: the first concerns the feasibility of the agent-level price determination problem and asks whether the actual number of tuples in the agents' valuation lists is small relative to exponentially large worst case. The second, which is the primary research question in this paper, asks whether search based on an incomplete market exchange protocol can overcome the problem of premature commitment to suboptimal local maxima. The third question addresses the effects of limiting the number of RFQs submitted by an agent in any iteration by setting parameter b. Before presenting the experimental results for these questions, the test set of resource allocation problems and the alternative solution techniques used in this research are described.

## 4.1. The test set

The underlying resource allocation context is an njob, one-machine stochastic scheduling problem in which all jobs receive a terminal reward if the job is completed before the end of the decision horizon, d. A job shop scheduling context is included in CATS, one of the most widely used standard combinatorial auction test sets [11]; however, the scheduling problem modeled by the CATS test sets lacks some of the complexity of real-world production scheduling problems and could, as a consequence, jeopardize the generalizability of the empirical results. To remedy this, a novel set of scheduling problems incorporating the additional sources of complexity summarized in Table 2 was developed. The purpose of including additional sources of complexity in the test set is to create more nuanced and realistic interdependence relationships between the resources, which are in turn reflected in the agent-level valuations. For example, stochastic processing times cause agents to place a small, but non-zero expected values on a large number of resources that might be required. This creates greater substitutability between resource bundles. Non-linear holding costs and setup costs, in contrast, strengthen sequence and complementarity relationships between resources.

## 4.2. Solution methods

The 2<sup>d</sup> “bids” in each agent's valuation lists were combined into a global solution using four solution methods: complete search (“Complete”), tabu search (“Tabu”), market-based search using the SUBLIST exchange protocol (“Market”), and market based search using the SUBLIST exchange protocol and a b-limit on the number of RFQs an agent can submit in any iteration (“Market(b-limit)”). The solutions generated by complete search provide a benchmark against which the quality of the solutions from the heuristic solution techniques is evaluated. However, the requirement to find optimal allocations imposes practical constraints on the size and complexity of the problems in this type of analysis. For this reason, tabu search is used as a benchmark for larger problem instances.

Tabu search is one of the most popular and successful techniques for heuristic search [8,18]. In order to provide a fair comparison, the amount of time required to achieve equilibrium was recorded for each problem instance and the tabu search algorithm was permitted to run for approximately the same amount of time.<sup>5</sup>

## 4.3. Size of the agent valuation lists

As discussed in the previous section, the unrestricted SUBLIST exchange protocol attains an equilibrium in an amount of time that is a polynomial function of ${ \overline { { \mathbf { v } } } } ,$ the average size of the valuation lists created during the agent-level price determination phase. The b-limit variant described above is also sensitive to v¯ since responding to an RFQ requires a search for substitutes within the agent's valuation list. The number of relevant tuples in each agent's valuation list depends on many characteristics of the job, such as the size of the potential profit margin, the amount of slack time before the deadline, the degree of substitutability between the resources, the existence of other constraints, and numerous modeling decisions. Dominance exists whenever the expected value of an allocation is less than the value to the agent of owning nothing. In this case, $\phi _ { j } ( \mathcal { O }$ s) = 0, ∀j, s so all allocations with negative values are omitted from $V _ { j } ( s )$ .

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

The primary objective of this research is to determine whether economic metaphors – specifically the notion of self-interested agents exchanging resources – provide a useful foundation for heuristic search in the context of intrafirm resource allocation problems. Despite the apparent efficiency of markets within the real economy, the deterministic and monotonically improving search path that results from strict adherence to basic microeconomic theory is at odds with the non-determinism and non-monotonicity that are employed by many popular and powerful heuristic search techniques. The results presented here suggest that the problem of local maxima that characterizes greedy search algorithms in the face of resource complementarities can be avoided if the exchange protocol is sufficiently complex to exploit the substitutability relationships that exist between bundles. The SUBLIST exchange protocol presented in this research enables a restricted search for substitutes that enables agents to find good allocations by executing a number of “small hops” to traverse the contract graph.

![](/api/attachments/G4M2HPXT/fulltext/images/e3a4a65205a6c7a3f54877de752e59b73ce00ad1a89ed0ba8dda335fc615a985.jpg)  
Fig. 8. Computation time versus number of agents when the decision horizon is held constant. The vertical axis for the Market algorithm is shown on the right.

The primary difference between the small hops executed by the agents within the market and the moves of a conventional local search algorithm is that the selection of moves in the market is informed by the agents' price information. The agents always submit the RFQ of their most preferred allocation first and, since no exchange occurs without a net increase in social welfare, the value of the global objective function increases rapidly and monotonically. The benefit of “search informed by self interest” is illustrated by the comparisons with tabu search in Fig. 6. Both the Market and the Market (b-limit) outperform the Tabu algorithm given the same amount of computation time or, in the case of Market (b-limit), much less computation time. The convergence of the algorithms for problem instances with a large number of agents is likely an artifact of the unrealistically high number of agents competing for a relatively small number of resources. In such a case, both the market and tabu algorithms are capable of finding a good solution among the many possible good solutions given sufficient time.

There are a number of advantages of the marketbased approach in addition to its favorable time-quality tradeoffs. McKay et al. [13] identify three “practicefocused” issues to be addressed by scheduling research:

flexibility, robustness, and support of multiple objectives. The flexibility of the market-based approach comes from the modularity inherent in agent-based decomposition and the clear demarcation between the agent-level price determination problem and the marketlevel recomposition problem. New production resources and changes in workflows can be accommodated by incrementally adding new agents or updating the parameters of those agents affected by changes. Market-based systems are robust because shocks, such as machine breakdowns and new orders, can be dealt with gracefully. Each shock causes a transition from state s to s′ (for which agents have, or can determine, new valuations) and initiates a new search for equilibrium. Support for multiple objectives and uncertainty is provided by the use of arbitrarily complex expected utility functions at the agent level and a common numeraire good at the market level.

Systems for resource allocation based on economic metaphors have additional advantages that are specific to markets. First, market-based systems benefit from similarities to our own economic system for allocating resources. Few practicing decision-makers appreciate the subtleties of an auctioneer that uses LP-relaxation to allocate goods to agents; however, the operation of a continuous reverse auction is familiar and relatively transparent. Second, short-term equilibrium prices can provide planners with useful information concerning long-term capacity and configuration changes. Although exact market prices for individual resources cannot be extracted directly due to resource interdependencies, “dummy” RFQs can be submitted in equilibrium to provide estimates (subject to incomplete search)

of the opportunity cost of specific resources or bundles of resources. Finally, continuous auctions are naturally distributable. Returning to the contract graph formulation in Fig. 2, most arcs implemented by a restricted search exchange protocol (such as the SUBLIST) involve only a subset of the agents in the economy. As a result, agents can submit RFQs simultaneously with a reduced probability of conflict between them. Moreover, the fact that most RFQs are not Pareto-improving (and are therefore not executed) further minimizes the probability of a collision between conflicting exchanges.

## 5.1. Limitations of the research

Since the objective of this research is to explore the broad issues in market-based search, a relatively simple exchange protocol is developed and presented as a representative example of the family of incomplete exchange protocols. Although the time-quality performance of the SUBLIST protocol is encouraging, very little effort has been made in this research to optimize the algorithm or explore the many possible extensions and enhancements to the field of market-based heuristics. The one extension that is explored (the b-limit variant) is meant only to provide an illustrative example of the use approximation to sidestep the combinatorial complexity created by resource interdependencies. Space constraints preclude a systematic analysis of, for example, different values of b or dynamic strategies for controlling the submission of RFQs.

The primary limitation of the market algorithm as it is presented in this paper is that the time required for winner determination can be an exponential function of the number of resources in the production system. Although the results in Section 4 suggest that agents will consider only a fraction of the possible combinations of resources, the size of the valuation lists can still be very large and may necessitate the use of approximations and restrictions such as the b-limit.

## 5.2. Areas for future research

Although there are many opportunities for significant refinement of the market-based approach at both the agent and the market levels, two areas for future work are especially important. First, the development of heuristics for identifying high-valued allocations and substitutes at the agent-level is critical if industrial-scale problems are to be addressed. Experiments with the blimit algorithm suggest that the problem of large valuation lists can be mitigated in practice by permitting agents to focus on only their most promising allocations.

Second, a distributed version of the market protocol should be investigated in order to confirm our intuition that simultaneous submission and evaluation of RFQs could provide significant performance benefits at the market level. These two enhancements combined with the framework for market-based transformation outlined in this paper might provide a local search technique that is general enough for use in a wide range of intrafirm resource allocation contexts but specific enough to exploit the problem-specific structure of industrial-scale problems.
