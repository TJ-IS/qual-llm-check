## 1. Introduction

## A B S T R A C T

In this paper, we investigate group maintenance behavior in community-based Free/Libre Open-Source Software (FLOSS) development teams. Adopting a sociolinguistic perspective, we conceptualize group maintenance behavior as interpersonal communication tactics—specifically, social presence and politeness tactics—that help maintain relationships among group members. Developer email messages were collected from two FLOSS projects with different development statuses, and their content was analyzed to identify frequently used group maintenance tactics. We then compared the group maintenance tactics used in the two projects, finding differences that reflect changes in the project work practices. Our work theoretically contributes to FLOSS research and has practical implications for FLOSS practitioners.

Because it is an alternative model for commercial knowledgebased collaboration work [1], Free/Libre Open-Source Software (FLOSS)<sup>1</sup> has attracted increasing interest during the past decade in both the commercial and academic worlds. Although FLOSSlicensed software may be developed in the same way as proprietary software (e.g., as in the case of the MySQL database), much of it is developed by teams of organizationally and geographically distributed developers in what has been described as community-based development [3]. FLOSS’s community-based form of development is the focus of this paper.

An important feature of the community-based FLOSS development process is that projects depend on voluntary contributions from developers [4]. Sustaining participation—that is, retaining developers’ continued contributions and recruiting new contributors—is a critical factor for the continued development of a FLOSS project [5]. However, it has been observed that 80% (or more) of FLOSS projects fade away due to insufficient long-term participation [6]. For example, on SourceForge (http://sourceforge.net), one of the most popular FLOSS project portals, more than 324,000 projects have been created by 3.4 million developers worldwide, but most of these projects are inactive. To sustain participation, it is important (but not sufficient) to sustain social interaction among the members of FLOSS projects [7,8]. Social interaction underlies the software development process and helps build relationships between participants in shared activities; thus, it has positive group-level outcomes [9].

Most prior research on social interaction in FLOSS focuses on the strategies and processes that members use to address particular group work functions such as decision making [10,11] and coordination [12,13]. However, the manner in which members interact on a daily basis to build and maintain relationships that contribute to project continuity remains largely unaddressed. We focus on this topic. The objective of this study is to theorize group maintenance behaviors in community-based FLOSS projects expressed through daily interpersonal communications. We define the phenomenon of group maintenance behavior as pro-social, discretionary and relation-building behavior among members that builds and maintains reciprocal trust and cooperation [14]. Group maintenance is targeted towards the development and maintenance of social relationships with others [15]. It demonstrates a concern for others and for the well being of the group or community [16].

Theories of group behavior, such as the input-process–output model proposed by Hackman and Morris [17], have emphasized the important role of social relations in effective group outcomes [15]. Group maintenance behavior is thus known to be important to group function because it keeps social relations pleasant, increases interdependence among members, facilitates resolution of conflicts, provides encouragement and enables minorities to be heard [18]. Prior group research has found that effective group maintenance increases long-term group performance by fostering various team activities, such as higher group cohesion, better information exchange, lower in-group conflict and greater commitment [19–21].

Furthermore, Stewart and Gosain [22] have found that because most FLOSS development and communication activities take place online and can be observed by outsiders, they provide an opportunity for potential members to observe others’ behaviors and the climate of the project before deciding to join. Thus, positive efforts to maintain good social relationships among FLOSS team members may help in recruiting new members, in addition to motivating the existing members to continue their participation. For both these reasons, it is critical to understand group maintenance behaviors in FLOSS teams.

Although prior research provides important insights into the nature of group maintenance, the differences between conventional face-to-face groups and virtual and voluntary FLOSS projects suggests that researchers should be cautious in directly applying those previous findings. Furthermore, reviews of previous research on FLOSS [e.g., 10,23] indicate that few studies have empirically examined topics related to group maintenance behaviors, further emphasizing the nascent state of research on this topic.

Two areas of research that are relevant to this topic are research on socialization (i.e., studying how new members join a project and sustain their participation) and on ideologies (i.e., studying norms shared among project members that regulate their interaction behaviors). These two streams of research are related because they examine how project members interact with each other to construct positive social relations and contribute to their teams.

Research on socialization in FLOSS projects examines the strategies and processes through which new members join an existing FLOSS development project and internalize processes and values that spur participation [6,24–26]. This body of literature treats socialization as a process and places emphasis on participants’ actions and willingness to understand both a project’s code base and its social structure. Although these studies provide a good understanding of how new project members interact with others and move from being non-participants to full-fledged FLOSS developers, they do not explore the specific tactics that established project members use daily to build and maintain social relations when interacting with others.

A small body of existing FLOSS research examines ideologies (i.e., norms, beliefs and values) shared among project members and their impact on relationship-building behaviors [27]. In general, this literature argues that shared ideologies help build and maintain social relationships among team members [22,28,29]. Most of these studies examine the ‘‘big picture’’ ideologies that are shared within the wider FLOSS community on issues related to software, technical and work processes. However, group maintenance behavior does not take place only through these large events. Rather, it is enabled and manifested in practical daily interactions [29]. Researchers have argued that individual interactions can lead to surprising group-level outcomes [30]. For example, Kozlowski and Chao [31] found that social interaction among team members increases team cohesion. In FLOSS projects, these everyday interactions among team members shape mem bers’ behavior while they are engaged in FLOSS development activities, which in turn impact group-level outcomes such as reciprocal trust and cooperation (i.e., group maintenance).

In summary, although studies have touched on issues related to group maintenance, researchers thus far have paid little attention to the seemingly mundane activities and everyday interactions that underlie FLOSS development [7,29]. Although the prior research provides useful insights into FLOSS projects, it remains unclear what specific tactics are used during everyday interactions to maintain group relations. To address this gap and to understand how the existing literature on group maintenance tactics can be applied to FLOSS projects, we first address the following research question:

RQ1: What group maintenance tactics do community-based FLOSS members use to maintain relations through everyday interaction?

Second, previous research has noted that many FLOSS projects fail due to insufficient volunteer participation [6,32,33]. Group maintenance behavior helps to build and maintain relationships among group members and thus plays an important role in sustaining participation and continuous development. Conversely, changes in exhibited group maintenance behaviors may be an indication of changing group dynamics. Therefore, we expect to observe different group maintenance behaviors in projects that sustain development versus projects that cease development, reflecting differences in the nature of participation and practice in those projects. More specifically, we expect to observe different group maintenance behaviors developing over time as projects either mature and prosper or wind down. Observing such differences will provide further evidence of the validity of the application of group maintenance concepts in this setting. Therefore, our second research question is as follows:

RQ2: What differences in group maintenance behavior exist in general and over time between projects that sustain development and those that eventually cease development?

We note that we are not proposing a casual relationship between group maintenance behaviors and project success (e.g., that a lack of group maintenance behaviors causes a project to fail). Rather, we expect that different group maintenance behaviors among projects reflect variations in group processes that also lead to different project outcomes. In this way, observable group maintenance behaviors can serve as a useful proxy for harder-toobserve group practices.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we introduce the two theories that provide the theoretical grounding for our study: social presence and politeness theory from computer-mediated communications. Then, we describe our research method, including project selection strategies, data collection techniques and data analysis methods. The results are presented in the following section. Finally, we discuss the implications and limitations of our study and make recommendations for future research.

## 2. Theoretical development: social presence and politeness theory

The central element of group maintenance behavior is the act of building and maintaining relations (e.g., reciprocal trust and cooperation) among group members through everyday interac tions. In FLOSS development, developers primarily interact through text-based communication tools such as developer email lists and discussion fora. Media richness theory has noted that text-based communication is less rich than face-to-face communication because it disables nonverbal cues, such as facial expressions, which are important in helping to build relationships [35,36]. As a result, participants adopt alternative strategies to provide paralinguistic and social cues [37]. Specifically, language plays a basic role because relational meaning is conveyed through specific language elements [34].

In this research, we adopt a linguistic analysis to develop deeper insights about group maintenance behavior in community-based FLOSS development. Specifically, we build on two theoretical bases that have been used to study relationship building via text-based communication: the theories of social presence [38,39] and politeness theory from computer-mediated communications (CMC) [34]. These two theories provide complementary theoretical insights into identifying interpersonal communication tactics that individuals perform to build and maintain relationships and collectively provide a basis for identifying potential group maintenance tactics applicable to FLOSS development teams.

## 2.1. Social presence

Social presence explains ‘‘the sense of being with another’’ [40]. This phenomenon has been studied extensively in CMC, and various definitions and categorizations have been provided to describe this phenomenon [See [40] for a review of different definitions]. A widely accepted definition has been proposed by Short et al. [41], who have defined social presence as ‘‘the degree of salience of the other person in a mediated interaction and the consequent salience of the interpersonal interaction’’ (p. 65).

Social presence is important because prior research has demonstrated the impact of social presence on outcomes such as group cohesion [e.g., 42], trust [e.g., 43], social identity [e.g., 44] and project participation [e.g., 45], which are the direct outcomes of group maintenance as we define it. In general, a positive relationship has been found between social presence and these outcomes [46]; that is, a high degree of social presence perceived by team members is a contributor to a cohesive and sustained community. These results suggest the importance of communication tactics that build social presence as an important component of group maintenance and, therefore, project outcomes. For example, Shen and Khalifa [45] found that online community users’ sense of social presence has strong, positive impacts not only on their motivation to participate but also on their level of contribution.

Social presence is also interesting particular features of the FLOSS setting. In a virtual environment such as community-based FLOSS development, members communicate mainly through lean media such as email and discussion fora, where text is often the only way to express a message. At first glance, it would seem difficult for team members to build a high degree of social presence in this setting because social presence is more easily established in the absence of ambiguous and equivocal informational cues (i.e., in richer media) [47]. To increase social presence, these participants must engage in communicative tactics such as the use of emoticons and paralanguage through their written communication to compensate for the lack of other cues in the lean medium [38].

Many researchers have studied the tactics that individuals use to build social presence in CMC settings. A majority of these studies focused on the online educational environment [e.g., 48,49]. For example, Rouke et al. [39] provided a template for assessing social presence in asynchronous text-based computer conferencing learning settings through content analysis of conferencing transcripts. Their study identifies linguistic symbols that indicate social presence, such as the use of emoticons, humor, inclusive pronouns and vocatives (i.e., referring to participants by name or addressing comments to a specific person). The insights gained from this literature review were used to develop our measures of social presence.

## 2.2. Politeness theory

A second theoretical base for our study is politeness theory. Politeness theory explains that in relational communication [50], people phrase communications in a way that takes the feelings of others into consideration, thus contributing to the development of social relations. Other CMC researchers studying relational communication have used politeness theory. For example, Morand and Ocker [34] state that ‘‘The specific tactics of politeness can be reliably observed and thus quantitatively measured; as such, they can be used in the assessment of relationalities within CMC, at a linguistic level of analysis’’ (p. 5). There are three main elements to politeness theory: face, face-threatening acts (FTAs) and politeness strategies [51]. The first two of these elements provide the theoretical argument for the importance of the final element, politeness strategies. Politeness theory thus provides a complementary theoretical base for identifying additional group maintenance behaviors.

Face is the central element in politeness theory and is defined as the positive value that individuals claim for the public self that they present [34]. Because face is emotionally charged and is inherently vulnerable when engaging in interaction with others, people strive to maintain face in social settings and communications [52]. However, one’s claimed identity can only be validated by others, and is, therefore, dependent on others. Thus, it is in everyone’s interest to maintain a group by maintaining the faces of those with whom they interact [53]. Face is therefore viewed as ‘‘a social rather than a psychological construct’’ [54], and it is within these social situations that people continuously interact in ways that preserve, bolster, or show consideration for the faces of others [52]. Thus, politeness theory emphasizes interactional support work directed toward others’ faces [34]. Face is constructed from two wants: autonomy of action (also known as negative face) and the need for validation (also known as positive face) [51]. Negative face is exemplified by wanting to be left alone, independence from others, self-direction, and freedom from restrictions created by others, whereas positive face includes the desire for respect, membership in a valued community, and a reputation for competence and fairness [55].

Despite the need to support both the negative and positive faces of others, there are situations in which one may need to ‘‘make requests, disagree, and offer advice or criticism to others’’ [55]. These instances are known as FTAs; they can be directed either toward the speaker or the listener and can threaten both types of face [51].

Finally, politeness strategies are linguistic acts that take the forms of positive tactics (to encourage positive face) and negative tactics (to encourage negative face) to redress or mitigate any threats to others’ faces engendered by an FTA [34]. Examples of positive politeness tactics include the use of colloquialisms or slang, vocatives, agreement, inclusive pronouns and sympathy that express positive face, e.g., membership in a project. Examples of negative politeness tactics include the use of hedges, indirect inquiries, subjunctives, honorifics, apologies, formal verbiage, passive voice, and rationales for FTAs that preserve negative face, e.g., not giving direct instructions to preserve self-direction [34,52]. By supporting, preserving or restoring face, both types of communication tactics help to maintain the relationships among group members. Collectively, these politeness strategies are therefore additional elements in the understanding of group maintenance behaviors.

In summary, these two bodies of theories suggest that individuals exhibit specific communication behaviors that serve to build and support relationships among group members. The setting of Rouke et al.’s study of social presence [39] is comparable to the setting of FLOSS projects, where text-based messaging is the major communication channel for distant learning. We therefore adapted Rouke et al.’s template to examine social presence as a type of group maintenance tactic in FLOSS teams. In online settings, politeness strategies are also visible in group discussions. We adapted Morand and Ocker’s [34] approach to examine politeness strategies as a type of group maintenance behavior in FLOSS teams.

## 3. Research method

As noted above, prior research has identified a set of possible group maintenance behaviors that are exhibited in conventional teams and for which evidence has been found in online settings. However, given the differences between those settings and FLOSS teams, it is not clear which, if any, of those behaviors apply to FLOSS teams. Given the current state of the research on this topic, we adopted an exploratory case study approach [56]. We studied group maintenance behavior through a content analysis of the email archives of two FLOSS teams. This strategy provides a deep understanding of the applicability of theory in the FLOSS setting, albeit one which comes at the expense of generalizability, a tradeoff to which we return in this paper’s conclusion. The following section describes our case selection strategy, data collection method, coding scheme development and coding process, and data analysis methods.

## 3.1. Case selection

Several criteria were established to assist in selecting ideal FLOSS projects for this study. First, to control unwanted variance from systematic factors such as different types of software development and different project ages, we wanted to focus on projects that were in the process of developing similar software. Second, to answer RQ2, we needed to explore both a project that continued to have active development activities and a project that lasted for a fairly long period but eventually ceased development activities, so that we could examine the existence of any differences in group maintenance behaviors between these two categories of projects. Needing to know the fate of the projects led us to a retrospective analysis, picking projects with known outcomes and examining the activities of the projects that led up to that point.Keeping these criteria in mind, we selected two projects from SourceForge.net: Gaim and Fire. These two projects met the first criterion because they both developed the same type of software: multi-platform Instant Messaging (IM) clients. A user of Gaim or Fire can run a single program and chat with users across multiple services. Therefore, the two projects were similar in terms of their project goals, task natures, and potential users. The two projects also met the second criterion: Gaim continues to operate as an active project (although it is now known as Pidgin), whereas Fire ceased development in early 2007. We should emphasize that we do not explore the reasons for the project’s demise nor attribute any blame for it, but focus instead on the developers’ behaviors through this process.

## 3.2. Data collection

Because most, if not all. FLOSS activities are archived. FLOSS projects provide a helpful setting in which to examine group maintenance behaviors over time. In this study, we analyzed email messages from the public mailing lists of the two projects. The fact that Fire ceased development in early 2007 limited us to gathering messages posted before early 2007 for both projects. As a result, we decided to select messages from Fire between June 2002 and December 2005, which allowed us to have enough messages to observe group maintenance behavior before it ceased development. Messages from Gaim were selected for the period between June 2002 and February 2006. Analyzing email messages over such extended periods provided us with a sampling frame to examine an essentially complete record of group maintenance behavior over time. Although software development has certainly evolved since the time our data were generated, our study examines everyday interaction that is a fundamental social process of how people interact with each other, a phenomenon that does not change rapidly over time. Furthermore, many FLOSS projects still use the same communication technologies of email and discussion fora. Therefore, analysis of these data should generalize to current FLOSS development.

Because thousands of messages were exchanged during the selected period for each project, it was not feasible to analyze all of them using the intensive manual coding method described below. Instead, we sampled messages from each project to study. We wanted a sample of at least 300 messages, large enough to provide sufficient power for our statistical tests, but small enough to be tractable for analysis. We started by dividing the messages from each list into 360 sequential ‘‘chunks’’ of messages. If all of the messages in a chunk were spam, then the chunk was removed from the sample. Using this process, 360 messages were selected from the Gaim mailing list. These messages were sent from June 2002 to February 2006, covering 45 months. Three hundred thirty-six messages were selected from the Fire mailing list from June 2002 to December 2005, covering 43 months; the other 24 chunks contained only spam or automated messages, reflecting the decreased level of activity in the project, and were dropped. ## 3.3. Analysis approach

Given the nature of our data—namely, textual email messages— we adopted a qualitative data analysis approach. It is sometimes assumed that the goal of qualitative analysis is to uncover latent or hidden meanings in a text, e.g., to understand individuals’ concepts of their social worlds from their communications. In other words, it is sometimes assumed that qualitative research is always interpretivist. However, qualitative research can in fact adopt any research perspective: positivist, interpretivist or critical [57].

We assumed that the social processes of the groups studied were accurately reflected in the texts that they produced as part of their work together. Rather than searching for hidden meanings, we searched for explicit evidence of particular behavioral patterns by the participants. Therefore, our approach was essentially positivist, despite its reliance on qualitative data.

Specifically, for this study, we identified the communicative tactics that individuals used to maintain team relations in the two projects though quantitative content analysis of their messages. Using this data analysis approach, we sought to identify units of text in the email messages that were examples of particular theoretical categories (in our case, of particular group maintenance behaviors) and labeled those text segments with category tags, a process referred to as coding.<sup>2</sup> Coding is guided by a coding scheme that provides definitions of theoretical categories of interest and guidance for how these categories are exhibited in a text. The result of the coding process is a text that is annotated (or tagged) with codes for the categories exhibited [58]. Quantitative content analysis allows researchers to determine the specific frequencies of relevant categories and examine the relationships involving these categories using statistical methods [59]. Specifically, we compared the frequencies of different group maintenance behaviors in two different projects.

As the unit of coding, we adopted the thematic unit, defined as ‘‘a single thought unit or idea unit that conveys a single item of information extracted from a segment of content’’ or the ‘‘unit of meaning’’ [60]. Such units vary in size, from an emoticon or punctuation to a word, a phrase, a part of a sentence, a sentence, or even a few sentences that capture meaning.

The initial analysis of the textual data was performed by two research assistants, referred to as the coders. Two or more coders were needed to compare the separately analyzed data and isolate mistakes or errors in judgment [61]. Although two coders is only the minimum requirement to determine reliability, for practical reasons two coders are commonly used [e.g., 62,63]. For example, to study citizen-driven information processing through Twitter services, Oh et al. [63] investigated three social crises using two coders for each crisis to code the studied variables. The two coders working on our project had a basic understanding of software development processes. Although they had not worked as software developers, these coders had two years of experience studying FLOSS development practices, which were the focus of the analysis. Because we were not focused on programming, but on the social interaction of the people, the coders’ knowledge of FLOSS development and the projects studied was sufficient for them to code the collected messages for the concepts of interest, even when technical terms were used.

## 3.4. Coding scheme development

We developed an initial coding scheme to both deductively and inductively identify and categorize group maintenance tactics from the messages to be analyzed. Deductively, an initial coding scheme for the exhibited group maintenance tactics was derived from the two theories discussed above: social presence theory and politeness theory. The coding scheme for tactics to establish social presence was adapted from Rourke et al. [39]. In their study, social presence in an asynchronous online learning setting is assessed according to three categories: affective, interactive and cohesive responses. The coding scheme for politeness tactics was adapted from Morand and Ocker [34] and included both positive and negative politeness tactics. After comparison, we found that the indicators for interactive and cohesive responses from Rourke et al.’s study were similar to the positive politeness tactics from Morand and Ocker’s study, both indicating group closeness. Therefore, our coding scheme combined these three sets of codes together in a category named ‘‘positive politeness’’. Affective responses were renamed ‘‘emotional expression’’. The negative politeness category’s name was adopted from Morand and Ocker [34].

Inductively, the initial coding scheme was further revised and refined by the two coders and the authors by pilot coding an independent sample of approximately 400 messages<sup>3</sup> from the Gaim and Fire mailing lists. The coders first independently coded a subset of the messages, and their results were discussed among the two coders and the authors to correct the coders’ misunderstandings and to identify problematic codes. The scheme was adjusted based on the difficulties encountered by the coders. The revised scheme was then used to code more messages, and the coding scheme was discussed and revised again. This iterative process was repeated until all disagreements were addressed and a reliable coding scheme was achieved, as described below.

We made the following changes to the coding scheme during this development and refinement process. First, fifteen of the indicators from prior work were removed. Twelve indicators were removed because they occurred very infrequently or not at all in the messages in our sample, an example of the need to customize a theory to this setting. Examples included ‘‘self-disclosure’’ from Rourke et al. [39] and ‘‘notice hearer’s admirable qualities or possessions, show interest, exaggerate’’, ‘‘ellipsis’’, ‘‘claim common view’’, ‘‘give reasons’’, and ‘‘assert reciprocal exchange or tit for tat’ from Morand and Ocker [34].

The other three indicators that were removed were ‘‘continuing a thread’’, ‘‘quoting from others’ messages’’ and ‘‘asking questions’ from Rourke et al. [39]. Although these indicators had high occurrences in the sampled messages, we believe that in this setting, they do not capture the desired behavior of maintaining relationships. Rather, ‘‘continuing a thread’’ and ‘‘quoting from others’’ are simply a matter of using asynchronous software (i.e., email) features to carry on a conversation, whereas ‘‘asking questions’’ is a common behavior in human communication. Inclusion of these indicators would inflate the apparent occurrence of group maintenance behaviors; therefore, they were removed from the coding scheme.

Second, two indicators were added based on our observation of the messages. Participants in both projects often used jargon (e.g., abbreviations of technical terms) or metaphors in their emails. Similar to the usage of colloquialisms and slang (codes already included in the scheme), these terms communicate commonalities among team members. People who understand and use groupspecific jargon or metaphors are perceived to be closer to other group members. In contrast, those who do not understand the jargon may feel distant from other members. Thus, the usage of ‘‘jargon/metaphor’’ indicates positive politeness among group members. This indicator was added to the positive politeness category in the coding scheme. The other added indicator was ‘‘participation’’, which indicates behaviors that encourage group members to participate in discussion. As we discussed earlier, member participation is important to the sustained development of FLOSS projects. However, participation in FLOSS development is voluntary. Accordingly, messages that encourage others to participate are perceived as trying to build relationships with others. This code was also added to the positive politeness category.

Third, four pairs of indicators were combined because they were found to be conceptually similar in nature or serve the same purposes, and practically speaking, these indicators often occurred together within a thematic unit and were difficult to distinguish during coding. For example, ‘‘using hedges’’ and ‘‘using subjunctive’’ [34] were combined into one indicator named ‘‘Hedges/ Subjunctives’’. ‘‘Vocatives’’ and ‘‘referring explicitly to others’ messages’’ [39] were combined into a single indicator named ‘‘vocatives’’.

Finally, ‘‘complimenting, expressing appreciation’’ [39] was split into two indicators, ‘‘complimenting’’ and ‘‘appreciation’’, because they were felt to convey different meanings. ‘‘Complimenting’’ refers to praising others or message content but does not necessarily express thankfulness for work. On the other hand, ‘‘Appreciation’’ refers to expressing gratefulness for a person’s effort but it does not necessarily indicate a compliment about his or her work.

Appendix A details the evolution of our coding scheme, showing the codes that were removed,added, combined and split. Appendix B shows the list of tactics used in group maintenance, including definitions and examples. The final coding scheme contained 15 indicators in three categories: emotional expression (2 codes), positive politeness (9 codes) and negative politeness (4 codes).

## 3.5. Coding reliability

We assessed inter-rater reliability between the two coders using a simple percentage agreement rate [64]. Cohen’s kappa is often used instead of simple agreement to correct for the effect of chance agreement. In our study, though, the codes were infrequent at the level of thematic units (that is, only a few words or sentences in a message were examples of group maintenance behaviors), so the probability of chance agreement was low, obviating the need for the correction. During the coding scheme refinement process, the inter-rater reliability between the two coders reached 80% in the second half of the pilot-coding process and 85% in the final fifth of the pilot-coding process. This level of agreement is generally considered acceptable [64]. Therefore, after the reliability of the coding scheme was established, each of the two coders independently coded half of the messages sampled for this study.

## 3.6. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed on the coded data to answer the research questions. The unit of analysis was the message. The measure of the group maintenance behavior that we analyzed was the frequency of code occurrence, which was defined as the count of a particular code per message. Messages contain multiple thematic units, and therefore, multiple instances of a particular code (i.e., multiple examples of a particular group maintenance behavior) can be included in a single message. To explore the possible effects of the differing lengths of messages, we ran the same tests using density, the count of a particular indicator in a message divided by the number of words in the message [39]. However, we did not find meaningful differences between the results of these two approaches; thus, for simplicity of presentation, we only present results for the frequency of indicator occurrence.

The analysis proceeded in several steps. First, we averaged the number of indicator occurrences per message at the project level for the two projects to assess the general patterns of communication tactics used for group maintenance in FLOSS projects (RQ1). Second, to answer RQ2, we conducted a series of Mann–Whitney

U test comparisons between Fire and Gaim to identify betweengroup similarities and differences in group maintenance behaviors. We used a non-parametric test because the code counts were not normally distributed. Because we were making multiple comparisons (15 comparisons, one for each indicator), it seemed possible that one or more comparisons might achieve significance by chance. To correct for this effect, we applied a Bonferroni correction to the usual cut-off alpha of 0.05, which resulted in a required alpha of 0.003 to declare statistical significance. This approach was conservative and reduced the power of the statistical tests, an issue that we discuss below.

To identify when differences in group maintenance behaviors arose between Gaim and Fire (RQ2), we compared the frequencies of group maintenance behaviors in messages from two periods for each project. Because the two projects initially were almost equally active, we expected Fire and Gaim to show similar group maintenance patterns at the beginning of the study period. However, we expected the projects to develop differences over time as Fire began to wind down and Gaim continued development. To examine this change, we separately compared the frequencies of group maintenance behaviors from Fire and Gaim in the first third of the messages (i.e., the first 112 messages from Fire and the first 120 messages from Gaim) and in the last third of the messages (i.e., the last 112 messages from Fire and the last 120 messages from Gaim), omitting the middle transitional period.

## 4. Findings

In this section, the data analysis results that address the two research questions are presented. We first report the general pattern of group maintenance behaviors in the two projects. Next, we compare the group maintenance behaviors in Fire and Gaim.

4.1. RQ1: What group maintenance tactics do community-based FLOSS members use to maintain relations through everyday interaction?

Our results indicate that messages from FLOSS projects exhibit a level of group maintenance behaviors, particularly a certain set of indicators. Table 2 displays the means, medians, ranges and percentages of the occurrence frequencies of the group maintenance indicators found during the selected periods for the Gaim and Fire projects. The 360 Gaim messages included a total of 3748 occurrences of group maintenance indicators, whereas the 336 Fire messages included 2,861 such occurrences. From the table, we can see that the use frequencies of the different tactics were diverse, with some used more often (when aggregated at the message level) and others used rarely. ## 5. Discussion

The goal of this study is to advance our understanding of the nature of group maintenance behavior in community-based FLOSS development teams. Our results suggest that FLOSS team members exhibit certain types of group maintenance tactics in their communication that are similar, but not identical, to those exhibited by other types of virtual teams. Additionally, differences in the frequencies of these behaviors are found between a project that continued development and one that ceased development, both overall and during later periods in the projects in particular. These differences reflect the projects’ outcomes and illuminate their teams’ underlying processes. In the following sections, we discuss our findings.

## 5.1. Major findings

RQ1: What group maintenance tactics do community-based FLOSS members use to maintain relations through everyday interaction?

By examining the occurrence frequencies of group maintenance tactics and the percentages of messages that used these tactics, our results suggest that FLOSS team members frequently use social presence and politeness tactics that serve to maintain team social relations.

Emotional expression. Intuitively, one might expect a low degree of emotional expression in FLOSS development discussions, for several reasons. First, interaction in FLOSS development is typically task-oriented [65]. Research has found that individuals express fewer emotions in task-oriented contexts than in socialemotional contexts when using CMC [e.g., 66], such as by using fewer emoticons. Second, members are geographically distributed, and some have never met, suggesting a low level of personal connection. Third, the communication medium is lean and not conducive to emotional expression.

However, our study contradicts this expectation. We find that FLOSS team members frequently use a number of communicative tactics that express emotions in their online discussion. The most common tactic is the use of emoticons, capitalization and punctuation, which are present in almost one-third of the sampled messages (30.5%). For example, one developer in Fire used an emoticon $" ; - ) "$ to simulate a smile (i.e., a facial emotional expression) when talking about his computer: ‘‘I will check if that crash also happens on my g4 powerbook, which is much slower (and of course has only one cpu;-)).’’. In another example, a developer from Gaim used punctuation (‘‘???’’) to strongly express his eagerness to know an answer: ‘‘Also, how do you send a message to someone??? I see a useful file, Conversation $I M . c ,$ which has likely looking calls, but I have no idea what the arguments $a r e . \ " { }$ By using these tactics, individuals can communicate via email in ways that resemble the use of body language, facial expressions or even speech tones used in face-to-face communication [67]. Such emotional tactics enable members to convey social information that mere words may not be able to communicate, which in turn can increase mutual understanding and maintain interpersonal relationships among members [67].

Positive and negative politeness tactics. Our results show that members of the FLOSS projects studied used both positive and negative politeness tactics, with an emphasis on the former. Positive and negative politeness tactics serve two different functions: positive politeness tactics are intended to bond people together, whereas negative politeness tactics reduce threats to face by respecting others’ autonomy and preventing others from getting too close. Our findings indicate that although members were somewhat careful to respect others’ autonomy (i.e., by using negative politeness tactics such as hedges/subjunctives to reduce the threat of their acts), they seemed more likely to attempt to build and sustain a sense of group commitment by using positive politeness tactics. Indeed, among the five most frequently used tactics, three were from the positive politeness category: jargon/ metaphor (found in 92% of messages), vocatives (35%) and inclusive pronouns (26%).

Jargon/metaphor, that is, abbreviations or use of technical terms, could be observed in almost every message in the sample. Here are a few examples (with bold type indicating jargon). ‘‘What should it be Arabic letter on my EUC-K\*R environment, even without utf8-aware?’’ ‘‘I cannot make the fix myself because I do not have cvs.’’ ‘‘I am working on a pure Gtk2.0/Gnome2.0 platform’’. We argued earlier that jargon/metaphor builds connections by communicating commonalities among team members. Team members seek common ground by expressing their shared background in knowledge and expertise, thus contributing to reducing social distance [68]. However, the high frequency of jargon/metaphor used in these two projects alerts us to another functional purpose of jargon/metaphor: it might reference the technical skill necessary for team members to join in the discussion of software development. Future research should therefore distinguish jargon as generally used programming terms from jargon as a project-specific language.

Vocatives means referring to participants by name or addressing a message to a specific person, as shown in the following examples: ‘‘Glen’s suggestion of I Seek You is alright’’ and ‘‘I trust Simon’s code far more than an unknown.’’ The use of vocatives again indicates familiarity among team members. FLOSS members also frequently used inclusive pronouns such as ‘‘we’’, ‘‘us’’ and ‘‘our’’, as in these messages: ‘‘We will certainly NOT be using ‘‘libyahoo2’’’’ and ‘‘when I get some spare time, I’m going to make a UI for it (and for MSN) more befitting to conferencing than our plain-ol chat UI.’’ Using these tactics creates intimacy among team members and helps build team solidarity. This observation is consistent with Bagozzi and Dholakia’s [9] argument about the importance of we-intention in Linux user groups, i.e., when individuals think of themselves as ‘‘us’’ or ‘‘we’’ and consequently attempt to act in a joint manner.

In the negative politeness category, hedges/subjunctives are the most frequently used tactic and were found in more than half of the messages (58%). Here are a few examples. ‘‘I would guess that means that. . .’’ ‘‘This likely needs to be changed as well.’’ ‘‘It would be really nice to ditch the old model of hundreds of #ifdef’’s and instead go with a layered approach.’’ By employing this and other negative politeness tactics, members redress the force of an FTA and keep a proper distance from others to minimize the chance of intrusion and offense.

Given the various discontinuities that characterize virtual collaboration environments [69], one might expect members to try to reduce the distances created by these discontinuities, that is, to use positive rather than negative politeness strategies. For example, in a study of politeness strategies in collaborative email exchanges between students, Vinagre [70] finds that only 3.6% of the examined messages use negative politeness tactics, whereas 94.3% use positive politeness tactics. Our observation of a relatively high use of negative politeness tactics contrasts with that study. One possible explanation is that membership in FLOSS development teams is fluid and voluntary, with new members joining and old members quitting at any time. Furthermore, these members might not have met before and most likely will not meet in person even after they join the team. Under these circumstances, people are reluctant to impinge on each other’s time. Therefore, people must engage not only in actions that can bring them together and foster closeness but also in actions that protect their independence and freedom from imposition. This observation adds support to Morand and Ocker [34], who proposed that the maintenance of harmonious social relations in CMC depends not only upon the exchange of positive messages but also on negative politeness.

RQ2: What differences in group maintenance behavior exist in general and over time between projects that sustain development and those that eventually cease development?

We compared communications from Fire and Gaim to identify differences in the use of group maintenance tactics. Our results suggest that the two projects do show differences in the frequency of use for a number of group maintenance tactics (Table 3). Considering the codes that were frequently used, as shown in Table 2, an interesting observation is that one might expect developers in Gaim, which continued development, to use more positive politeness tactics than those in Fire. However, our results indicate that, in general, the developers in Fire used more positive politeness tactics (i.e., complimenting and appreciation) and less negative politeness tactics (i.e., hedges/subjunctives) than the developers in Gaim. It seems that to maintain group cohesiveness, the developers in Fire used more tactics to create cohesive relationships, whereas those in Gaim used more tactics that distanced themselves from each other and protected members independence. Because Fire later ceased development, we suspect that this observation might primarily apply to the second period, when Fire and Gaim showed differences in development status.

A further across-period examination confirmed our expectations that Fire and Gaim were initially mostly similar in their use of tactics to build and maintain relationships among members, but over time, as Fire slowed its production, differences in group maintenance behaviors developed. In the first period we investigated, Gaim demonstrated more usage of only one type of group maintenance behavior compared to Fire: humor. However, in the second period, Gaim appeared to use more hedges/subjunctives and disclaimers and less appreciation and formal verbiage than Fire. This pattern of initial similarity and subsequent difference suggests that both group maintenance behaviors and team performance are reflective of the underlying group processes.

If we only examine frequently used tactics (those boldfaced in Table 2), this analysis shows the same trend as the results from the overall comparison between Gaim and Fire.<sup>5</sup> That is, it seems that the members in Gaim used more negative politeness tactics (i.e., hedges/subjunctive and disclaimers) and fewer positive politeness tactics (i.e., appreciation) than the members in Fire. These results suggest that as the projects developed, members in Gaim used more distancing tactics that also respected the members independence, whereas members in Fire seemed to use more bonding tactics.

One possible explanation might be that as Fire began to cease production, fewer and fewer people participated in its development (for reasons that we do not explore in this research). As a result, the remaining members were more familiar with each other and experienced higher interdependence [71]. Therefore, it became unnecessary for members to frequently use negative politeness tactics and keep their proper distance from one another. The higher use of appreciation in Fire may reflect members’ expressions of appreciation for the work that was still being contributed, perhaps in the hopes of encouraging additional contributions. However, in Gaim, the project was still growing and attracting new members. Therefore, members of Gaim used negative politeness tactics to avoid offending others and to show politeness, especially to unfamiliar newcomers. Thus, the use of these visible linguistic tactics provides insight into the processes within the projects. In particular, our findings suggest the important role of negative politeness tactics in maintaining relationships among FLOSS team members over time.

## 6. Implications, limitations and conclusions

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

This study begins to examine the important question of group maintenance behavior in FLOSS development projects. To our knowledge, there has been no empirical work to understand how project members maintain social relations with one another through everyday communication in FLOSS development. By conducting a content analysis of email messages from the public mailing lists of two FLOSS projects, this study provides initial insights into the specific nature of group maintenance behaviors in the FLOSS setting. Additionally, it demonstrates that members in projects of differing development status (i.e., continued production vs. ceased development) show different patterns of group maintenance tactics. For example, projects that continue production seem to maintain or increase their use of negative politeness tactics, whereas projects that cease development grow to use fewer of these tactics. Such in-depth examination enhances our understanding of social/emotional behaviors in FLOSS projects, in contrast to prior research that has mainly focused on task-related behaviors [e.g., 26,72,73].

## 6.2. Methodological implications

A majority of prior FLOSS research has used either narrative case study or quantitative survey methods [10]. In contrast, this study demonstrates a different approach, namely, a linguistic analysis of everyday online communications. The FLOSS setting is heavily reliant on text communication and, therefore, provides a unique opportunity to observe and identify different group behaviors though detailed linguistic analysis of what actually happens in everyday communication, the venue for the practice of FLOSS projects [29]. This approach thus provides a way to answer von Hippel and von Krogh’s call for more research attention to the interpretation of subtle matters in FLOSS project activities from contextual and behavioral perspectives [7].

## 6.3. Practical implications

This study also has practical implications for FLOSS practitioners. FLOSS practitioners should be aware of the importance of various communication tactics for maintaining interpersonal relationships as expressed in everyday online communication. Our results suggest that members frequently employ a number of tactics involving emotional expression, positive politeness and negative politeness. These tactics not only foster closeness, solidarity and cohesion in groups but also show respect for one another’s independence and freedom from imposition. Thus, the descriptive analysis of group maintenance tactics in this research provides guidelines for FLOSS practitioners for how to interact with others in a text-based environment.

The results further suggest that as a project begins to cease development, members seem to use different types of group maintenance tactics than those in a project that continues development. Therefore, FLOSS practitioners, particularly those in leadership roles, might want to monitor members’ communication styles and language, which might give them hints about the development status of the project from a social-emotional perspective that complements other indicators of project activity and success. Additionally, project leaders might want to encourage the use of particular tactics. For example, if leaders feel that their project needs more participants, they might encourage the use of more negative politeness tactics to make the project more attractive to potential volunteers.

## 6.4. Limitations and future work

A major limitation of our research is its small sample size. Fire and Gaim are only two out of hundreds or thousands of active FLOSS projects and represent a small sample from which to draw conclusions, leading to questions about the generalizability of our findings. Understanding two projects in detail was necessary at this initial stage of research to develop a reliable and valid coding scheme and to perform the necessary coding to provide initial evidence for our research questions. Still, the small sample suggests that our results should be interpreted cautiously.

To address this limitation, future research should apply the framework of this research to communications from a larger and more representative sample of FLOSS projects. Of course, increasing the sample size will require methodological innovations to reduce the cost of the necessary linguistic analysis. One particularly promising approach is the application of computerized analysis [e.g., 74,75–77]. For example, in a pilot case study, Crowston et al. [78] applied natural language processing technique to automate content analysis and achieved good performance on a number of codes. The current study provides a tested content analysis codebook and a sample of coded data, both of which are necessary prerequisites for future automated analyses.

A further limitation is that the present study focuses on FLOSS teams developing the same type of software (i.e., instant messaging clients) and using the same development platform (SourceForge). It does not consider the potential effects of projectlevel factors (e.g., project size, leadership types, member roles, software types or different technologies used) on group maintenance behaviors. Past research has found these factors have implications for FLOSS projects. For example, studies have found that shared leadership in FLOSS projects encourages voluntary participation and facilitates project growth and development [79,80], suggesting additional factors to consider when developing a research model. Research might also consider the impact of more detailed features of the project work on the exhibited behaviors, such as the proportion of new members participating in the project, the rate of code production or the timing of releases, all of which are significant milestones in the life of a project.

Finally, our study does not examine the consequences of group maintenance behaviors beyond their relation to project continuance. Prior research has hypothesized that these behaviors serve to increase group performance by increasing group cohesion [e.g., 42], trust [e.g., 43], social identity [e.g., 44] and project participation [e.g., 45]. The FLOSS setting provides an opportunity to explicitly test some of these connections. Although a number of the identified consequent factors (e.g., cohesion and trust) are difficult to identify from the email messages that we studied, other outcomes, such as the level of contribution, are more apparent. For a more fine-grained analysis, group maintenance behaviors can be linked to subsequent levels of contribution to the group. Additionally, future research should seek to develop new measures for factors such as social identity, which leave observable traces in developers’ communications, to test those theoretical linkages. Finally, because group maintenance behaviors can be assessed unobtrusively, they may be a useful addition to studies that primarily employ other methods, such as combining an assessment of group maintenance behaviors from content analysis with the assessments of trust or group cohesion obtained from interviews or surveys. Again, it will be necessary to reduce the cost of the required content analysis for this opportunity to be widely used.

Despite these limitations, this study provides the first exploration of group maintenance behaviors embedded in everyday communication in community-based FLOSS projects. The results reveal that group maintenance tactics are frequently used in members’ communications, and different tactics seem to reflect the projects’ development status (i.e., projects with continuous development vs. projects that have ceased development). It is hoped that this in-depth examination will provide insights into the distinctive nature of group maintenance behaviors in communitybased FLOSS development.
