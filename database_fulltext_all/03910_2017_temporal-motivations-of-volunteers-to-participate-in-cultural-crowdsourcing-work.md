# Temporal Motivations of Volunteers to Participate in Cultural Crowdsourcing Work

Sultana Lubna Alam,<sup>a</sup> John Campbell<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. Crowdsourcing (CS) by cultural and heritage institutions engage volunteers in online projects without monetary compensation. Uncertainty concerning online volunteer motivation has led to a growing body of academic research. This study contributes to that debate, by extending focus to CS volunteer work in nonprofit cultural institutions where no monetary benefit is ofered to volunteers. This study examines motivations of high performing volunteers in a newspaper digitisation CS project, initiated by the National Library of Australia. Volunteers are motivated by personal, collective, and external factors, and these motivations change over time. Volunteers initially show intrinsic motivations, though both intrinsic and extrinsic motivations play a critical role in their continued participation. Volunteer contributions range from data shaping (e.g., correcting digitised optical character recognition data) to knowledge shaping (e.g., shaping historical data through tagging and commenting, but also through development of norms and social roles). The locus of motivation (intrinsic or extrinsic) also changes with diferent kinds of contributions. The distinction between data and knowledge shaping contributions, and the locus and focus of motivation behind these activities, has implications for the design of CS systems. Design for improved usability through cognitive and physical system afordances and development of social mechanisms for ongoing participation is discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Crowdsourcing (CS) cases cited in the literature focus mainly on for-profit organisations (Afuah and Tucci 2012), but CS projects are increasingly used by nonprofit organisations (NPOs) for online activities previously deemed infeasible or carried out ofline by staf, volunteers or external contractors (Dutton 2011, Smith-Yoshimura and Shein 2011, Ridge 2013, Estelles-Arolas and Gonzalez-Ladron-de-Guevara 2012, Estermann 2014). New forms of digital volunteerism are neither regulated by contract, nor are participants ofered financial incentive (Benkler and Nissenbaum 2006, Smith-Yoshimura and Shein 2011, Dutton 2011, Alam and Campbell 2012, Ridge 2013, Owens 2013). Cultural CS is voluntary and initiated by NPOs to refine, enhance, and support cultural heritage collections, often aimed at achieving social benefit. Examples include correction and transcription (e.g., Transcribe Bentham), contextualisation (e.g., 1,001 Stories (Denmark)), complementing collections (e.g., Soundmap (UK)), classification (e.g., Mtagger, Historical Maps Pilot (British Library)), and cocuration (e.g., Open Call (Brooklyn Museum)) (Oomen and Aroyo 2011, Smith-Yoshimura and Shein 2011). There is a need to improve discovery and access. Cultural CS can create “a more open, connected and smart cultural heritage,” involving users of cultural data in production (Oomen and Aroyo 2011, p. 147). Volunteer organisations have found CS to facilitate new and highly valuable forms of digital volunteerism (Starbird and Palen 2011, Owens 2013, Rodell 2013, Sladowski et al. 2013, Cobb et al. 2014). As “these (new) models of online production emerge, questions arise as to how to motivate many independent, distributed, online participants to contribute information, updates, and commentary to a central site” (Budhathoki and Haythornthwaite 2013, p. 549). This paper addresses those questions.

Organizational volunteering is inherently contradictory and uncertain in nature (Pearce 1993). The absence of payment for organizational work results in facile explanations for volunteering motivation, ordinarily indeterminate and ideological in nature (Pearce 1993).

Uncertainty concerning volunteer motivation has led to a growing body of academic research and virulent debate around volunteering (Pearce 1993). This study contributes to that debate, by extending focus to CS volunteer work where no extrinsic monetary benefit is ofered to volunteers.

Related studies in open source software, peer production, and citizen science show participants contribute to CS for several reasons (Benkler and Nissenbaum 2006, von Krogh et al. 2012, Ridge 2013). These motivations change over time (Rotman et al. 2012, 2014). Recent research on virtual communities (Majchrzak et al. 2013) and organisational Wikis (Yates et al. 2010) suggest factors motivating personal knowledge contribution (i.e., adding content) difer from those for knowledge shaping contributions (i.e., rewriting, integrating, and restructuring). Conceptual development for why people contribute to CS projects must consider alternative forms of contribution (Yates et al. 2010). This applies particularly in volunteer-based CS contexts where data, knowledge, and other products contributed by volunteers, are supervised and managed by a formal custodian or caretaker (e.g., a cultural institute). Unlike peer production systems, such as Open Source Software (OSS) and Wikipedia, projects in a GLAM context (galleries, libraries, archives, and museums) often involve mundane and repetitive small-scale contributions that do not require formal coordination or administrative oversight (Oomen and Aroyo 2011, Owens 2013). Few studies have examined nonprofit contexts like government and cultural sectors (Oomen and Aroyo 2011, Alam and Campbell 2012, Ridge 2013, Owens 2013, Estermann 2014). However, growing evidence suggests motivation can vary greatly based on community type (Tausczik and Pennebaker 2012).

This research examines motivations of productive digital volunteers in one cultural CS project, by studying intrinsic and extrinsic motivations, initial and longterm participation, and motivations behind diferent contributions. It focuses on the Australian Newspaper Digitisation Program (ANDP) CS project, sponsored by the National Library of Australia (NLA). The ANDP seeks to improve the searchability of online Australian newspaper data by inviting the crowd to correct errors. This paper extends focus to nontraditional commercial organisations; specifically, organisations that contribute to society at large (Orlikowski and Robey 1991, Majchrzak et al. 2013). The next section (Section 2) positions the theory of participant motivation toward CS. A literature review follows to address diferent volunteer motivations. The research method and results sections (Sections 3–5) describe motivations of high performing volunteers for initial and long-term participation, as well as contributions to diferent data and knowledge shaping. The paper concludes with implications for research and practice, future research, and limitations (Sections 6 and 7).

## 2. Theorising Participant Motivation for Cultural Crowdsourcing

## 2.1. Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivations for Crowdsourcing

Motivation for online collaboration has been studied in peer production (Benkler and Nissenbaum 2006), participatory culture (Jenkins 2006), open source (Lakhani and Wolf 2005, von Krogh et al. 2012, Budhathoki and Haythornthwaite 2013), online community (Haythornthwaite 2009, 2012), volunteerism (Sladowski et al. 2013, Cobb et al. 2014), collective intelligence (Malone et al. 2010), online forums (Yates et al. 2010), CS (Brabham 2010, 2012), cultural CS (Holley 2009, 2010; Smith-Yoshimura and Shein 2011; Oomen and Aroyo 2011; Owens 2013), Wikipedia (Bryant et al. 2005, Nov 2007, Rafaeli and Ariel 2008, Hütter et al. 2008, Kittur et al. 2009), and citizen science (Nov et al. 2010; Rotman et al. 2012, 2014). Prior research suggests participants in voluntary CS (see Lakhani and Wolf 2005; Benkler and Nissenbaum 2006; von Krogh et al. 2012; Ridge 2013; Rotman et al. 2012, 2014) are motivated by a variety of factors that can be classified into intrinsic and extrinsic motivations, based on self-determination theory (SDT) (Ryan and Deci 2000). Intrinsic motivation emphasises inherent sources of satisfaction, not separable consequences of the act (e.g., acting just for fun); it is what makes a task interesting, or the satisfaction a person gains from doing something. Intrinsic motivations can be “volitional and accompanied by the experience of freedom and autonomy,” and “emanate from one’s sense of self” (Ryan and Deci 2000, p. 65). Extrinsic motivation seeks a desired outcome, compensation, or reward (e.g., acting for money or to avoid sanctions). Extrinsic motivations “are accompanied by the experience of pressure and control and are not representative of one’s self” (Ryan and Deci 2000, p. 65). They can vary by self-determination: the more one internalizes and assimilates the reasons for an action to self, the more extrinsic motivations can become self-determined. Collective motivations, such as altruism and kinship, are more autonomous, aligning with personal values and endorsements (the person identifies with the personal importance, accepting behavioural regulation as their own). This allows the person to experience intrinsically interesting properties of the action, coming from their sense of self rather than external reward/sanction. These motivations can have externally perceived loci of causality, enhancing or maintaining self-esteem and feelings of worth, and are perceived to be intrinsic (referred to as internalised extrinsic motivations). ## 2.2. Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Motivations

The relative value of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation is mixed, but most studies have established that both are important (Krishnamurthy 2006). However, their interaction has received less attention. The exception is crowding theory (Frey and Jengen 2001), “which predicts mutual adjustment between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation” (von Krogh et al. 2012, p. 13). The implication is that pecuniary incentives might not always increase participation for the common good. Successful CS projects take place without ex ante contracts when appropriately skilled individuals within the crowd are suficiently motivated to self-select and work on the project (Afuah and Tucci 2012). Monetary and nonmonetary (e.g., reputation-based incentives) can motivate efective participation (Leimeister et al. 2009).

## 2.3. Participant Motivation for Cultura Crowdsourcing

There is little CS participant motivation research where no monetary incentives are provided. Some studies have attempted to address this (e.g., Holley 2009, 2010; Oomen and Aroyo 2011; Smith-Yoshimura and Shein 2011; Estermann 2014), but have not attended to diferent theoretical perspectives on motivation. Oomen and Aroyo (2011) analysed intrinsic motivations for GLAM projects, finding connectedness, membership, sharing, and generosity to be important motivators for heritage institutions. Ridge (2013) reviewed cultural CS projects and found a mix of extrinsic and intrinsic motivations (e.g., altruism, fun, the pleasure in doing hobbies, enjoyment in learning, mastering a skill, practising an existing skill, recognition, community and passion for the subject). Ridge (2013) notes that motivation “is always strongly related to the project or activity’s subject area” (p. 441). Contextual factors bear on the eficacy of diferent motivators, so there is a need for better theory about CS project motivation.

## 2.4. Temporal Aspects of Motivation to Participate in Crowdsourcing

Few studies have considered the temporal aspects of motivation. Preliminary research shows citizen scientists often have diferent motivations for contributing, which can change over time, and can be salient at critical junctions of activity and decision making (Rotman et al. 2012, 2014). The Rotman et al. (2012) study of motivation in collaborative citizen science projects found temporality afected the participant’s initial decision to participate, and subsequent decision to continue participation. Citizen scientists are initially motivated by intrinsic factors—such as personal interest, self-promotion, self-eficacy, and social responsibility—but can have more complex and ostensibly diferent motives for long-term participation: trust, common goals, acknowledgment, mentorship, education and outreach, and a sense of belonging to a community. Continued participation is “strongly influenced by interpersonal relationships, particularly between citizen scientists and scientists, which were also influenced by culture” (Rotman et al. 2014, p. 232). Motivation dynamics can have important theoretical and design implications for cultural CS participation, and motivations are likely to change over time.

## 2.5. Motivation Behind Diferent Forms of Contribution Online

A deeper understanding of factors that motivate participation is needed. Contemporary online CS projects show characteristics of crowd-like and community-like forms of organisation (Budhathoki and Haythornthwaite 2013). In Wikipedia and OpenStreetMap diferent motivations can hold for those who contribute as crowd members, and those who contribute as community members (Haythornthwaite 2009). One-of contributors provide data, while repeat contributors serve longer term objectives, negotiate directions and standards (e.g., through “talk pages” in Wikipedia), and reorganise data (i.e., rewrite, edit, integrate, restructure). Haythornthwaite (2009) argues that crowd- and community-based participation falls on a continuum between lightweight and heavyweight forms of participation. Budhathoki and Haythornthwaite (2013) invite research that examines facilitation of crowdsourced operations, so motivations for lightweight and/or heavyweight contribution may be better understood.

Virtual communities of practice (CoPs), such as organisational wikis, show personal knowledge contribution (i.e., adding content to wikis) and knowledge shaping (i.e., rewriting, integrating, and restructuring wiki content) (Yates et al. 2010, Majchrzak et al. 2013). The term “shaping” connotes an activity that changes a knowledge asset, without adding domain knowledge. Shaping entails refactoring and reconstructing knowledge assets (e.g., wiki content) to increase understandability and insight, through removal of ambiguities and inconsistencies, or the extraction of higher level patterns. Frequency of contribution is diferent for those who shape and those who contribute only personal knowledge. Intellectual capital (e.g., task novelty, reputation, access to community) motivates shapers, while social exchange (e.g., personal costs, performance benefits, meeting core responsibilities) motivates personal knowledge contributors. Yates et al. (2010) and Majchrzak et al. (2013) invite detailed studies that examine diferent types of participant contributions. Their findings suggest incorporating alternative forms of participation.

Responding to this call, this study examines motivations behind diferent forms of cultural CS contributions. We examine aspects of motivation for alternative forms of participation and contribution by volunteers.

We propose to theorise the temporal nature of motivation, based on our examination of both the locus and focus of motivation across diferent forms of volunteer contributions.

## 3. Research Method

We conducted a qualitative interpretive study of an information-rich cultural CS case, to explore understandings, uncover novel insights, and to access intricate details, thought processes, and emotions (Orlikowski and Baroudi 1991). Such studies can produce deep insights (Klein and Myers 1999) and build theory (Eisenhardt 1989, Walsham 1995).

## 3.1. The Research Case

We examined motivations of text correctors in the ANDP. ANDP developed an online, full-text searchable, digitised newspaper delivery system of out-ofcopyright Australian newspapers from 1803 to 1954 (NDP 2013, NLA 2013). The searchability of entries was found to be adversely afected by errors made during the optical character recognition (OCR) process. The NLA did not have the resources to rectify these errors, so CS was a potential cost-efective solution. The ANDP CS system was implemented in three phases: (1) design and development; (2) beta launch; and (3) operationalisation into Trove (the NLA’s digitized resources system).

ANDP uses personal computers and laptops, plus forms of interaction (text editing, commenting, tagging, etc.) to enhance the digitised Australian newspaper’s collection. More than 8,000 registered users actively contribute to this collection each month (detailed statistics are available at http://trove.nla.gov .au/system/stats?env<sup></sup>prod). Contributions extend beyond simple text correction (i.e., fixing OCR errors), to include tagging, commenting, making lists, and participating in Trove forums. Volunteers engage in the further enhancement of the collection by adding local knowledge, posting comments, or volunteering to prepare text correction guidelines. From its beta launch in 2008 until August 2016, more than 203 million lines of text have been enhanced or corrected, with 4.4 million tags and 133,368 comments added by 197,436 registered users. The library estimates that digital volunteers contributed more than 425,000 volunteer hours, or 270 standard Australian work years, to the project in 2013 alone. At the NLA’s lowest rate of pay, this equates to more than \$17 million (AUD) of contributed efort (Ayres 2013). This is the world’s first library project to undertake CS on this scale (Holley 2009, Ayres 2013).

## 3.2. Volunteer Engagement: Patterns of Usage

A study of user satisfaction (Ayres 2013, 2014) found ANDP CS Trove (herein, Trove) users to be spread across Australia, with similar proportions in each state and territory. One percent identified as indigenous (compared with 2% representation in the Australian population), and 4% spoke a language other than English in their home (compared with 15% representation in the Australian population). Users who corrected text were more likely to be family historians, retired, and long-term users (more than one year) (Ayres 2013). Average users are “fifty plus, city dweller, female, well of, well educated, professional, employed or retired” (Ayres 2014, p. 17). The top 100 users account for 43% of all corrections (i.e., 41 million lines of text), and the top 1,000 account for 81% of the corrections (Hagon 2013). This distribution of activity is similar to other commons-based peer production systems, like Wikipedia.

## 3.3. Data Collection

Leading users were interviewed via Skype or telephone to acquire primary data (see Table 2 for interviewee details). Interviews <sup>(</sup>n <sup></sup> 12<sup>)</sup> were done with a cross-section of project stakeholders (i.e., NLA staf, partner libraries, and users who do not correct text) to provide other perspectives. Interviews lasted between one and two hours. Purposeful and snowball sampling approaches were used to select participants (Patton 2000). Five of the six volunteers interviewed were listed in the top 10 text correctors for the ANDP site. Interviews were informal and semistructured, and adapted to gather data on a variety of issues and perspectives, based on interviewee background and involvement with the Australian Newspapers site. Additional data included: motivations for participating, text correction practices, perspectives and sentiments toward CS, risks and concerns, support received, and views of the CS platform. Anecdotes and specific narratives were often shared by the interviewees to illustrate points of view and particular experiences. Where possible, the narratives were cross-checked with user statistics and usage patterns on the website.

Data and background material were also collected from other sources: NLA project documents and reports, user surveys undertaken by NLA, media articles, the Trove forum, and extant literature on the case study (e.g., publications by Holley 2009, 2010). The first researcher also registered as a text corrector on the site. A summary of the multiple data sources is provided in Table 3.

## 3.4. Data Analysis

Interviews were recorded and transcribed through a professional transcription service. NVivo was used for textual content analysis, using coding methods proposed by Saldaña (2009). A thematic data analysis technique was used to explore themes in a manner similar to that proposed in Straussian grounded theory (Strauss and Corbin 1990). Open coding was used to flag instances where interviewees communicated motivations; these instances were clustered into conceptual categories during second-order coding. This iterative process alternated with investigation of theories that fit the emerging interpretation (Saldaña 2009). Memoing recorded researcher reflections and connections between diferent forms of data, with findings validated by sending the transcriptions back to the interviewees for review (i.e., member checking). Interviewees usually concurred with our findings, with only minor adjustments needed.

Drawing on Archer’s (1995) and Orlikowski and Yates’ (2002) work, Peters et al. (2012) articulated two modes of time: temporal (linear and cyclical, past, present, and future) and relational (socially constructed time, experienced through culturally relative interpretations). We used both in this study. Relational time might include cultural time (the time consciousness inherent in each culture), organizational time (acquired through membership of social organizations such as family, school, or workplace), and individual time (the subjective experience of time). Our analysis of motivations identified socially constructed temporal structures, experienced through interpretations of interviewees for initial, continuing, and sustained participation (Peters et al. 2012). Motivations, social practices, and emergent work practices enacted by volunteers were analysed, based on diferent forms of contributions by crowd-like (individual/pair) and community-like (collective/community) interviewees. We used a practice perspective of time (Orlikowski and Yates 2002) to focus on text correction and related peripheral practices, such as tagging, commenting, and forum posts. These were performed at diferent levels of participation, including individual, pair, collective, or community.

## 4. Findings

We first present our findings on intrinsic and extrinsic motivations, and how they change over time. We then present findings on motivations for data and knowledge shaping participation, and factors that explain motivations behind digital volunteering. We conclude with how these might shape afordances of cultural CS.

## 4.1. Changes in the Motivation of Digital Volunteers Over Time

We divided motivation into intrinsic and extrinsic motivation (see Table 4). Changes in motivation over time were observed from initial participation to long-term participation.

Volunteers reported high but sustained levels of intrinsic motivation, with an initial interest for volunteering manifested because of personally oriented reasons. These included the volunteer’s own-use, afective (enjoyment-based) motivations, and identified (othersoriented) motivations, which included altruism and prosocial motivations relevant to the group, collective, or wider community. Most volunteers were interested in family history research or genealogy. Some were interested in researching historical events, such as famous crimes and trials, or public transport history. Newspapers are regarded as historical artifacts that individuals and groups relate to, and connect with on a personal level. As one volunteer explained:

I think people just like history, especially the sort of history that’s presented in newspapers. It’s something that they’re very familiar with, it’s not dry and dusty— it’s not threatening like getting a history book of the shelf.

Top volunteers reflected workloads similar to fulltime jobs (40–60 hours per week). Sue, the top corrector at the time of the interviews, admitted, “I suppose initially it was to fill in some of the family history gaps in my family and I can say it is very addictive once you get really involved.” Linda, among the top 100 volunteers, reported her concerns about the possible health risks of such addictive behaviour:

Doing this type of semi-repetitive work is indeed addictive, as much as compulsive gambling, over-training at sports, or indeed, to a certain extent, alcoholism, although it does not have such self-destructive physical or financial efects. However, prolonged intensive performance of this type definitely alters the wiring of the brain, and not for the better.

Volunteers participated because it was fun and enjoyable (“Just the enjoyment is my motivation and gain”). They used the site to pass time and to keep active. Many volunteers saw the work as an altruistic patriotic task to preserve Australian history and the nonprofit National Library. Volunteers increased welfare of specific groups to which they belonged (kinship/collectivism). Some were members of genealogy sites (e.g., RootsWeb), and others volunteered for local libraries and genealogy societies (e.g., Bendigo Historical Society).

The greater the level of freedom and trust given to volunteers, their contribution and feelings of loyalty increases and results in higher accuracy in text corrections. Volunteers expressed gratitude for the library’s provision of resources and in return, felt obligated to assist the library: (“I felt an obligation to repay the assistance that I have received from the existence of the site—to sing for my supper, so to speak”).

Volunteers were not paid, so extrinsic motivations were social rather than monetary. The library deployed three forms of nonmonetary social mechanisms: attribution recognition and reputation-based rewards, feedback, and advocacy. The NLA did not initially ofer acknowledgment or reward to volunteers, but later instigated several, following suggestions by the volunteers. Public recognition, ranking tables, user profiles, and the ability to communicate with other volunteers improved motivation (Holley 2009). Acknowledgments, attributions, and rewards recognised the contribution of volunteers and became ancillary motivators for their continued contribution. Feedback mechanisms initially expected to create an open communication channel between NLA and volunteers became prominent motivational factors. Newfound knowledge (e.g., as a family history resource) was an educational benefit that helped volunteers personally, and created a knowledge base for local and distributed communities and social networks. This is a form of advocacy, which, unlike education, emphasises the personal gains volunteers may receive from their participation in CS projects.

In summary, volunteers reported high intrinsic motivation and lower extrinsic motivation. Most presented intrinsic motivations for their initial engagement, while extrinsic motivations such as recognition and rewards afected long-term participation. Leading volunteers began slowly, but over time became heavily active, with participation transforming them from novices to leading volunteers. As their participation became more routine and frequent, they adopted new goals and leadership roles, even though there was no formal role structure within the site. Their motivations changed and broadened as social mechanisms (e.g., trust, recognition, and attribution) reinforced their extrinsic motivations.

## 4.2. Forms of Contribution: Data Shaping vs. Knowledge Shaping of Digitised Newspaper Data

Yates et al. (2010) and Majchrzak et al. (2013) emphasised the importance of shaping (i.e., rewriting, integrating, and restructuring wiki content). “Shaping” could change a knowledge asset without adding domain knowledge, by refactoring and reconstructing to increase understandability and insight, removing ambiguities and inconsistencies, or extracting higher level patterns, or it could add domain knowledge. In the ANDP case, we found volunteers were involved in both data shaping and knowledge shaping.

Data shaping involves self-contained actions for individuals to achieve personal goals, or goals of the project through transcription, translation, geo-coding, or other activities. In ANDP, data shaping is the act of correcting OCR errors through the application of human intelligence—assessing face value, or using guess work, local knowledge, and/or domain expertise. Data shaping can be carried out by individuals alone, in pairs, groups, or at a community level (Ayres 2014). Individuals might correct family history articles. Volunteers might form teams or taskforces, to ensure that local newspapers are fully corrected.

Knowledge shaping helps coordinate planning and execution of first-order activities, or adds contextual knowledge. In the latter, it can add meaning and structure to the community. Knowledge shaping involves peripheral tasks motivated by strategic reasons, enabled through technology afordances such as tagging, commenting, forums, and listserv groups. In ANDP, knowledge shaping influences data shaping. Historical data can be shaped in meaningful ways by adding value in addition to OCR correction. Knowledge shaping can be carried out by individuals alone or in pairs, in groups or at a community level, as when the LRRS tagged relevant newspaper articles to assist light railway researchers. Adding metadata to digitised newspaper content would add meaningful context to digitised historical data. This may be achieved through comments that would correct incorrect news, tag relevant articles to improve searchability, or to create subfolders and lists for educational purposes. Knowledge shaping can also stem from the work of others: original typos in newspapers, editorial mistakes, and rectifying of-topic text. Knowledge shaping contributions constitute strategic shaping of OCR through technological afordances, such as tagging, commenting, lists, and forum communication. Emergent normative roles might be undertaken by volunteers (e.g., monitoring and moderating quality of text correction) for knowledge shaping.

Data and knowledge shaping behaviours are elaborated below, with examples of motivational afordances from the ANDP CS platform.

4.2.1. Motivations for Data Shaping. Data shaping stemmed from self-oriented and collective-oriented motivations. Research interests, hedonic gains from enjoyment and recreation, freedom to choose tasks, opportunity to learn from others, altruism, and kinship all focused on instrumentality: first-order activities that individuals pursue to achieve personal or project goals. Leading volunteers reported initial motivation from self-serving interests (e.g., genealogy, Australian history, interest in famous crimes). They would correct text on newspaper articles of direct relevance to topics of personal interest:

I was alerted to the site through an email posted on a Jewish email site that I subscribe to. I straight away began to look for any family notices that I could add to my family tree, and I think one of the first articles I corrected was the death notice of my great great grandfather in 1922. I was determined to find and correct any articles that I could find that were of interest to me. I have been interested in family history for about 35 years, and the newspaper site was just another avenue to explore to garner more details.

Another would only correct articles containing references to his family name:

What’s in it for me? Okay, a strange thing. I’m an only child. I’m the last one of my family line. There are no more. So there are no other [surname] related to me. For posterity, I want to leave a mark in time and space and this is the basic reason.

Some corrected text in pairs, often sharing the same user profile. A retired couple used the same user profile to carry out combined text corrections of out-ofprint serialised fiction stories in newspapers to create online books and export the results to another site (i.e., the Guttenberg Project).

Volunteers were free to select articles to correct. Many pursued their own interests, but later accessed the website while correcting text. The transition from initial to active involvement involved broadening their initial research interest:

My personal interests are in family history but I don’t confine myself to correcting articles or family notices, as we call them. I often go of on tangents and I have corrected many things.

Individuals established identity profiles through user accounts, which allowed them to track their own contributions (e.g., text corrections, tags, and comments), and their progress and comparative ranking against others (e.g., monthly leader boards, Hall of Fame). Some set their own productivity targets as a form of personal challenge (e.g., how many text corrections to be done per month) to find out what they had accomplished within a set period of time (monthly, weekly, etc.). The ANDP project manager explained: “they wanted to be setting their own goals to do x amount in a period, which was quite interesting to us that they were motivating themselves.” In response to user demand, the project team introduced a “Hall of Fame” that listed top correctors via a monthly ranking of individuals who had corrected more than 1,500 lines. Individual profiles showed the entire league table and individual positions in comparison to other volunteers.

Volunteers contributed because they found it personally rewarding. They additionally wanted to help achieve the main project goal; the project manager recognized such motivations:

Disabled, sick, terminally ill, and recovering people are among the volunteers since working at home is convenient, gives purpose and structure to the day, and gives feelings of value and reward. I was indescribably moved when I discovered one of the “super” volunteers on the Australian Newspapers project had a terminal illness. She said helping gave a purpose to her remaining days and made her still feel useful now she had stopped work. It took her mind of her own situation for several hours each day.

Text correction became routine in conjunction with other daily chores:

Any correction done during the day may be done on and of through normal household interruptions.

Moving from an initial to long-term contributor meant the expansion of motivations, to include altruistic goals (improving a public good of historical significance to Australians), and the collective goal of servicing needs of special interests (e.g., genealogy societies, railway researchers). The ANDP project manager reflected: “The sense of making history, being a part of history and recording history is very important to most Australians. So Australian genealogists are perhaps even more community spirited than other nationalities and perhaps this is why text correction has been so successful.” A top volunteer confirmed: “I also voluntarily help a lot of people with their family history research.”

Volunteers formed many formal and informal interest groups in and outside the forum. A group located in Rockhampton (known as Rockhampton Trovers) corrected their local newspaper. As a project oficer explained: “The Rockhampton group actually arranged that as a meeting; as a chance to get together and have a chat and have a cofee as well as correct the text.”

Volunteers also reported they corrected text to increase the welfare of formal groups to which they belonged. Some were members of genealogy sites (e.g., RootsWeb), while others volunteered in local libraries and genealogy societies (e.g., Bendigo Historical Society). A local library helpdesk staf member noted:

There are groups who are using [the system]—like people who were indexing births, deaths, and marriages into another database—who actually go in and search Trove, put the data into another database like the Ryerson index, which is an index of births, deaths, and marriages, and correct at the same time. They see [the system] as a research tool and an extension of their service to online family search requests.

4.2.2. Motivations for Knowledge Shaping. Knowledge shaping is like meta-analysis of data shaping. Different knowledge shaping behaviours were observed within ANDP, depending on whether volunteers shaped the solution space so text correction quality was improved (e.g., preparing draft text correction guidelines), or contributed domain knowledge (e.g., correcting incorrect news in the digitised articles). Knowledge shaping behaviours were intrinsically or extrinsically motivated, and knowledge shapers were strategically motivated to achieve outcomes at both crowd (individual/pair) and community (group/collective) levels. Platform features afected knowledge shaping: tagging, commenting, lists, and forums were used to add value to the digitised OCR data.

Some volunteers contributed to discourse by correcting information incorrectly reported in newspapers, based on personal knowledge, or on further research on the topic. As one participant explained:

I tag most of my work, where it seems appropriate, and when considering a new tag, I check to see how any existing tag on the topic is entered. I enter comments only rarely, usually when there is some serious fault, either in the original article or in the computerisation, and occasionally if there is a gross factual error.

Volunteers reported they shaped newspaper data by using features like comments, and tags and lists to meet the specific group/collective’s aim(s). Collectives were more formal in nature; the LRRS tagged relevant newspaper articles to assist other light railway researchers, and the Climate Change Research Group used newspaper data as a secondary resource to collect weather data for Australian weather research.

4.2.3. Shaping Afordances to Support Data and Knowledge Shaping. Studies of peer production have noted the management of projects using a variety of social motivations (see, e.g., Hütter et al. 2008, Kittur et al. 2009). Technical and relational mechanisms might provide support for data and knowledge shaping in the ANDP. The NLA adopted support mechanisms both technical and relational in nature. They ofered a functionality closely aligned to the needs of volunteers, who could subsequently correct text from digitised originals for their own use that additionally facilitated routine text correction and aforded diferent forms of organising and participation. Most procedures were quite obvious. The NLA drew new users into text correction by allowing them to contribute anonymously without formal registration. As one put it, “I began by correcting anonymously all articles that I used, and I registered when I found that I could not carry out some necessary functions without being registered, for example, entering missing lines.” The interface achieved this by requiring only the completion of a captcha code— to deter spammers and bots—and the ability to correct text in multiple ways (e.g., line by line, by article, by download). Increased autonomy made corrections more intuitive; as an ANDP staf member commented:

We don’t discriminate between whether you’re a registered user or not. Absolutely anyone can go and correct text. Rather than saying “You must sign up, you must do this, you must follow these rules,” we’re saying “Here’s how to do it, have a go.” Because we still want to be able to do it anonymously, just the odd word as they come across it, and they might only ever do one word at that one time.

## 5. Discussion

Drawing on psychology (Toates 1986, Maehr and Braskamp 1986), volunteer motivation can be explained by locus of motivation (intrinsic/extrinsic), and focus of motivation (first-order instrumentality of data shaping, or second-order strategy of knowledge shaping). Data and knowledge shaping activities are carried out at diferent levels of participation and engagement: individual, pair, group, and community. Previous literature (see Haythornthwaite 2012) suggests engagement is either “crowd-like” (e.g., individual/pair) or “community-like” (e.g., group/community). We propose a CS motivation model across locus of motivation and focus of motivation (see Figure 1). These dimensions are continuums rather than dyadic extremes; at any point, a volunteer may be situated anywhere on the continuum, contributing to data and knowledge shaping activities.

Volunteer data shaping contributions were driven by instrumental motivations (i.e., correcting poor OCR), but their knowledge shaping contributions were driven by strategic motivations (e.g., facilitating the cocreation of value/social engagement). Temporality in motivation can be represented by a continuum from instrumental to strategic. Crowd-like participation (i.e., individual/pair) is motivated mostly by personal interests (i.e., intrinsic motivation), and community-like participation (i.e., groups/community) stemming from altruistic and collective motivations (i.e., extrinsic motivation). Individuals became more involved over time through external associations, and tended to volunteer in more strategic ways. They were extrinsically motivated, and some adopted mentoring roles within the wider volunteer community. They moved from lightweight CS work toward heavyweight CS work (as depicted in Figure 1 by the dotted line).

This model is time independent. Individual volunteers can be situated at any point along these dimensions. This practice perspective on time (Orlikowski and Yates 2002) focuses attention to diferent levels of participation and engagement: individual, pair, collective, or community macro and micro, implicit and explicit temporal structures. Subsequent design can align motivations of the crowd with the objectives of the cultural CS initiative. Central concepts and relationships are intimately related to the specific domains of cultural CS work from which they were derived. The model is general, suggesting potential applicability in a range of cultural CS applications. The model may serve as a basis for information systems practitioners and CS platform designers to better understand cultural CS work.

## 6. Implications for Practice

This paper suggests a multimotivational perspective for cultural CS work. Volunteerism can be encouraged by intrinsic and extrinsic motivations and crowd-like or collective-like participation. Diferent motivations may operate within a group of volunteers for distinct forms of contributions (i.e., data and knowledge shaping). Motivations can change over time in terms of locus and focus of motivation. The distinction between data and knowledge shaping contributions and the locus and focus of motivation behind these activities has implications for the design of CS systems. More specific recommendations follow.

## 6.1. Design for Data and Knowledge Shaping

It might be possible to classify cultural CS work into data shaping (e.g., carrying out text enhancement/ translation/transcription work) and knowledge shaping (e.g., adding metadata/shaping digitised data in meaningful ways, by commenting and tagging). Designing CS technology for volunteerism should enable and support both data and knowledge shaping. Our model suggests implications for the design of cultural CS tools that support shaping. One of these is through quality assurance. This is important for usergenerated contributions in GLAMs that have traditionally exercised control over the acquisition, organisation, and annotation of collected items (Oomen and

Aroyo 2011). Metrics to assess the quality of contributions produced in CS work would be useful for further research. ANDP community moderation could be assisted by such metrics as a rating system for corrections to provide an indication of correction quality at both the article and text corrector level. These ratings could work as indicators for both data and knowledge shaping. Indicators for incomplete corrections could be achieved through the inclusion of statistics measuring how many participants view an article in edit mode or look at a sentence but do not undertake corrections. Such indicators could assist both data and knowledge shaping.

Sending automatic notifications when articles are available for correction additionally could motivate and increase participation. Personalised topics preselected by volunteers could help as could synergetic ties between smaller communities that enable the discovery of required corrections. Local interests can be catalysts for continuous involvement as seen with the Rockhampton Trovers or the Bendigo Genealogy Society. Volunteers should be able to choose the location of interest (e.g., state or particular newspaper) and receive updates on articles and correction progress. The NLA could lead targeted campaigns, driving correction toward common topics, like articles related to Australia Day/International Women’s Day or a common cause such as cancer treatment. The CS platform could make data and knowledge shaping more explicit by encouraging comments, tags, lists, and forums to facilitate shaping activities among volunteers (Zheng et al. 2011). The use of tags, comments, and lists on the CS platform could show statistics on diferent forms of engagement, with the forum providing exemplar contribution types throughout the system.

## 6.2. Design for Motive Alignment

Appropriate motivation elements should be considered in project design. CS platforms and relational support systems should align with motivations to attract, incentivise, sustain, and guide the crowd to complete required tasks. Diferent periods in engagement call on diferent volunteer motivations. The NLA’s initial reluctance to ofer acknowledgment or rewards changed when simple and inexpensive acknowledgments and rewards were created, based on volunteer feedback (e.g., Hall of Fame, user profiles, website acknowledgment). These became efective motivators for continued and increased participation. Cultural CS organisations should communicate contribution of participants (e.g., monthly leader boards) to the community using day-to-day media. CS technology is an evolving artifact, with motive alignment evolving through the progressive release of CS features, which enable and support data and knowledge shaping.

## 6.3. Design for Community Building

Wikipedians view participation as membership in a community, adopting new goals and roles, and using diferent tools (Bryant et al. 2005, Kittur et al. 2009). There was no strong sense of community in ANDP, and no support strategies other than an online forum. Smaller groups formed in the site (e.g., LRRSA, climate change group, Rockhampton Trovers) as a response to this informal organisational structure. It might be useful to provide channels for social interaction, by facilitating the formation of subgroups within the wider volunteer cohort. Emergent role taking is encouraged by enabling flexible roles with better access to statistics on text correction, as with the evolution of Wikipedia leadership roles (Kittur et al. 2009).

## 7. Implications for Future Research and Limitations

CS is nascent (Marjanovic et al. 2012, Zhao and Zhu 2014, Pederson et al. 2013). This paper makes three contributions. It extends participant motivation research into cultural institutions. These are NPOs where volunteers are unpaid, yet are motivated by personal, collective, and external factors that change over time. Volunteers initially show intrinsic motivation, but over time both intrinsic and extrinsic motivations become important. Volunteers also engage in a continuum of activities, ranging from data shaping (e.g., correcting digitised OCR data) to knowledge shaping (e.g., shaping historical data through tagging and commenting). This continuum suggests both instrumental and strategic data and knowledge shaping at the individual, pair, collective, and community levels. Locus of motivation changes from intrinsic to extrinsic across different forms of participation. It is possible to design system afordances and develop social mechanisms for ongoing participation. Understanding and addressing motivations should lead to CS designs that facilitate collaboration among volunteers and cultural institutions.

CS is a social innovation that provides new thinking and changes in working or social practices, enabling things not possible before (Adams and Ramos 2010). CS can enable volunteer work that draws on community knowledge and changes stakeholders’ mindsets. This requires a reconceptualisation of the work performed by the crowd and the crowd’s motivations, decisions, agreements, and outcomes. CS technology, together with participatory organisational social mechanisms, facilitated cultural volunteering in an Australian newspaper CS site. This case helps us understand volunteer motivations in cultural CS work, facilitating further theoretical development, refinement, and testing.

This study has limitations. The research was conducted on one volunteer CS project, ANDP. Although “case studies are generalizable to theoretical propositions” (Yin 2003, p. 31), and “can take the form of concepts, theories, specific implications or rich insights” (Walsham 1995, p. 322), causal relationships should therefore be taken with caution. Our findings might not be generalizable across diferent types of cultural CS. Additional studies are required in diverse cultural settings to enrich our understanding of cultural CS, especially in GLAM. Such studies can explore, validate, and/or highlight issues and complexities with the phenomena.

Online community participation produces a longtail distribution. A comparatively small, active minority generates most of the content; most community members produce little (Halfaker et al. 2013, Budhathoki and Haythornthwaite 2013). This was observed in ANDP (Ayres 2013, Hagon 2013). Volunteer interviewees were chosen from a small pool of highperforming volunteers. Studies of Wikipedia volunteers and those of similar online platforms (e.g., Amazon Mechanical Turk) show top performers behave diferently from other volunteers (Halfaker et al. 2013). Other research must consider the motivations of lessproductive volunteers.

The antecedents of participation are not well understood, nor are subsequent behavioural influences on sustained involvement. Longitudinal studies might investigate changes in motivation, building on this study. Additional research is required to assess interdependence; not all constructs are monocausal. Similarly, the relationship between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, and especially “crowding-out” efects on intrinsic motivation need more examination. Furthermore, better understanding of which combinations of intrinsic and extrinsic motivations work best for diferent problem types (e.g., contest versus problem solving), and how they afect the composition of the crowd (Pederson et al. 2013) should be investigated.

Efective CS must align crowd motive with organisation motive, although both use diferent incentive mechanisms (Sharma 2010). “Technology is a crucial lever, but the challenge is ultimately one of governance and management” (Pederson et al. 2013, p. 584). More research is needed into how current and future technological capabilities interact with motivation and governance mechanisms to best support CS endeavours (Pederson et al. 2013).

Wikipedia shows the importance of smaller groups/ communities within the larger crowd in motivating participation (see Kittur et al. 2009, Budhathoki and Haythornthwaite 2013). Future research should examine peer feedback and emergent role-taking (e.g., community leadership) efects on setting goals and work practices across diferent forms of participation and organisation (Hütter et al. 2008, Zhu et al. 2012, Ridge 2013).

It is important to emphasise that reported volunteers’ motives were multiple, temporal, and even uncertain. Volunteers reported numerous nonaltruistic reasons for volunteering. They noted that they worked for self-oriented reasons, ofering not just one reason for participation. This implies that their motivations were “prosocial” acts—“those designed to produce and maintain the well-being of others without the restriction in other kinds of potential ‘payofs’ for the actors” (Pearce 1993, p. 91). Hence, it is recommended that successful volunteer CS platforms must cater for the rewards of unpaid work to meet volunteers’ needs.

This study of digital volunteerism for the cultural CS domain enhances our understanding of motivations for contribution to cultural CS work, and how motivations change over time. Future research should consider motivation in light of alternative forms of participant contributions, and strive for improved understanding of temporality.
