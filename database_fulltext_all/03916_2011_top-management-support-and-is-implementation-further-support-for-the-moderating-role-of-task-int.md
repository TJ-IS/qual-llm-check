# Top management support and IS implementation: further support for the moderating role of task interdependence

Rajeev Sharma<sup>1</sup> and Phillip Yetton<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Information Systems and Technology, University of Wollongong, Australia; <sup>2</sup>Australian School of Business, University of New South Wales, Australia

Correspondence: Rajeev Sharma, School of Information Systems and Technology, University of Wollongong, Northfields Avenue, Wollongong, NSW 2522, Australia. Tel.: þ 61 2 4221 3286; Fax: þ 61 2 4221 3286; E-mail: rajeev@uow.edu.au

## Abstract

Sharma and Yetton, in 2003, hypothesized and found empirical support for the moderating effect of task interdependence on the relationship between management support and implementation success. In this issue of EJIS, Hwang and Schmidt critique Sharma and Yetton’s meta-analysis protocol, arguing that a random-effects model should have been employed, rather than the fixed-effect model they used. Further, Hwang and Schmidt claim that the moderating effect hypothesized by Sharma and Yetton is not supported when a random-effects model is employed. We re-analyze Sharma and Yetton’s data by employing a dedicated, commercially available package for conducting random-effects meta-analysis. Our analysis finds no support for Hwang and Schmidt’s claim. The random-effects meta-analysis supports Sharma and Yetton’s original findings: task interdependence moderates the effect of management support on implementation success. European Journal of Information Systems (2011) 20, 703–712. doi:10.1057/ejis.2011.39; published online 4 October 2011

Keywords: management support; task interdependence; random-effects meta-analysis; meta-regression

## Introduction

Top management support has long been considered an important factor in the successful implementation of IS innovations. Extending this literature, Sharma & Yetton (2003) propose a model of the processes through which management support contributes to success. They hypothesize that task interdependence moderates the effect of management support on implementation success. Specifically, they argue that task contexts characterized by high levels of task interdependence require higher levels of management support for successful implementation than do task contexts characterized by low levels of task interdependence. A meta-analysis of the cumulative empirical evidence found support for the moderating effect of task interdependence on the relationship between top management support and implementation success.

Hwang & Schmidt (2011, this issue) re-analyze the data set reported by Sharma and Yetton and present the following critiques. First, they argue that Sharma and Yetton employ a fixed-effect meta-analysis when a random-effects model is appropriate. Second, they claim to show that, when a random-effects model is employed, the support for the moderating effect of task interdependence on the relationship between top management support and implementation success is weak. Finally, they argue that the specific functional form of the regression analysis employed by Sharma and Yetton to test for the moderator effect is inappropriate. Instead, they propose a general methodology for testing moderator variables by employing meta-analysis. A key component of their methodology is that moderator effects should not be tested using regression analysis; instead, they claim that the subgroup analysis protocol described by Hunter & Schmidt (1990, 2004) should be employed. In particular, they propose that continuous moderator variables should be dichotomized and the moderator effects tested via subgroup analysis.

We agree with Hwang and Schmidt that a randomeffects model, rather than a fixed-effect model, is the correct procedure. [In this paper, we use the first person plural to refer to Sharma and Yetton as authors of this paper. We use the third person plural to refer to Sharma and Yetton as authors of Sharma & Yetton (2003).] The use of random-effects models is now becoming the accepted practice in the literature (Schmidt et al, 2009; Table 1). For example, we use a random-effects model in our meta-analysis of the Technology Acceptance Model literature (see Sharma et al, 2009). Importantly, software packages for conducting random-effects meta-analysis have become commercially available, including, for example, the Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (CMA) package from Biostat (http://www.meta-analysis.com/).

However, we disagree with Hwang and Schmidt in that the results reported by Sharma and Yetton are weak when the data are re-analyzed by employing a randomeffects model. Instead, we show that the findings supporting the Sharma and Yetton hypothesis are replicated when a regression-based moderator analysis employing a random-effects model is employed. The findings are also replicated when the subgroup-based metaanalysis proposed by Hwang and Schmidt is employed. Finally, we comment on the subgroup analysis protocol proposed by Hwang and Schmidt.

## Re-analyzing Hwang and Schmidt’s findings

Sharma and Yetton propose a model for the effect of management support on implementation success, hypothesizing that task interdependence moderates the effect of management support on implementation success. They conduct a weighted least squares regression of the observed correlations between management support and implementation success on task interdependence and report that the slope for task interdependence is significantly greater than zero $( \beta _ { t a s k }$ ¼ 0.60, t ¼ 3.2, po0.05). We reproduce the graphical presentation of their findings in Figure 1.

Hwang and Schmidt argue that Sharma and Yetton should have conducted their meta-analysis employing a random-effects model rather than the fixed-effect model that they employed. As acknowledged above, we agree. A key difference between the two models is the assumption regarding the sources of variance in effect sizes observed across studies. The fixed-effect model assumes that the studies come from a homogeneous population: ‘There is one true effect size ... which underlies all the studies in the analysis, and that all differences in observed effects are due to sampling error’ (Borenstein et al, 2009, p. 61). Consequently, the sample size of each study is a good approximation of its precision and is employed to weight each study included in the analysis (Hunter & Schmidt, 1990, 2004; Schmidt et al, 2009). This is the protocol employed by Sharma and Yetton.

In contrast, the random-effects model assumes that studies come from a heterogeneous population and that the population parameters (effect size and/or variance) vary across studies (Schmidt et al, 2009). Consequently, the variance associated with each study is the sum of variance due to sampling error plus the variance due to heterogeneity across studies (Borenstein et al, 2009; Schmidt et al, 2009). The fixed-effect model is considered a special case of the random-effects model in which the heterogeneity across studies is assumed to be zero (Schmidt et al, 2009). A key implication of this difference is that the confidence intervals and credibility intervals estimated under the random-effects model are larger than those estimated under the assumptions of a fixed-effect model (Borenstein et al, 2009; Schmidt et al, 2009).

Hwang and Schmidt claim to show that, when a random-effects model is employed to analyze Sharma and Yetton’s data set, the higher variances associated with the random-effects model result in a weak or nonsignificant finding for the moderating effect of task interdependence on the relationship between top management support and implementation success. The results of their analysis are reproduced in Table 1.

Inspecting Hwang and Schmidt’s analysis, we identify four issues that raise questions about the validity of both their findings and their conclusions. Considering these issues together, we reaffirm our original conclusion that task interdependence moderates the effect of top management support on implementation success.

Against this, their analysis returns a p-value of 0.039 when a fixed-effect model is employed. Even if the analysis reported by Hwang and Schmidt (Table 1) were accepted, the validity of their conclusion is questionable. The hypothesis proposed by Sharma and Yetton is a directional hypothesis. Consequently, as recommended by Hunter & Schmidt (2004, p. 438), a single-tailed test should be employed to obtain the critical value for the test statistic. Second, the analysis by Hwang and Schmidt reported in Table 1 is conducted employing the weighted least squares (WLS) protocol in SPSS (personal communication from the authors to EJIS Special Issue Editors). We confirmed this by replicating their analysis and obtaining the same results as those presented in Table 1. However, the WLS protocol in SPSS is not directly appropriate for meta-regression (Lipsey & Wilson, 2001, pp. 138–142). Specifically, the WLS protocol in SPSS treats the weights as the number of subjects. However, in a meta-analysis the weights actually represent the variance associated with each study. As a result,

The standard errors of the regression coefficients y, the t-test values y, and the probability values for the t-test are incorrect y All the [SPSS] output related to statistical significance testing y is based on sample size assumptions that are incorrect in application to meta-analysis data. (Lipsey & Wilson, 2001, pp. 139–140; italics in original).

Lipsey & Wilson (2001, p. 140) present the formula for correcting the errors when the WLS protocol in SPSS is employed for meta-analysis:

The correct statistical significance of the regression coefficients can be determined by calculating a corrected standard error y This is done by first dividing the reported [standard error of the regression coefficient] by the square root of the mean-square residual.

Third, the results reported in Hwang and Schmidt are not directly comparable to those reported in Sharma and Yetton. Sharma and Yetton’s literature search identified 22 data points for inclusion in the meta-analysis. However, their results are based on 21 data points. One data point was identified as an outlier (see the original footnote from Sharma & Yetton (2003) reproduced in Figure 1). Excluding the outlier, Sharma and Yetton report a standardized regression coefficient for task interdependence of 0.60 (pp0.05). Hwang and Schmidt report a standardized regression coefficient of 0.442 based on the fixed-effect model employed by Sharma and Yetton.

Hwang and Schmidt do not report that, while their estimate is based on including all 22 data points in the data set, the Sharma and Yetton estimate is based on 21 data points, excluding the outlier. Excluding the outlier data point and replicating Hwang and Schmidt’s analysis, the standardized regression coefficient for task interdependence, employing a random-effects model and correcting the correlations for sampling error, is 0.532 (t ¼ 2.55, pp0.05, two-tailed).

If Hwang and Schmidt had excluded the outlier identified by Sharma and Yetton from their analysis, the results employing a random-effects model (see Table 3) would have been very similar to those reported by Sharma and Yetton employing a fixed-effect model. The difference in the magnitudes of the standardized regression coefficient for task interdependence between the Sharma and Yetton and the Hwang and Schmidt analyses is not on account of the difference between fixed-effect and random-effects meta-analysis. Rather, it is the effect of the outlier that is included in the Hwang and Schmidt analysis and excluded by Sharma and Yetton.

Finally, Hwang and Schmidt implement a restricted interpretation of the random-effects meta-analysis protocols. Specifically, they compute the weights associated with each study corresponding to the random-effects model. They then employ those weights in a conventional WLS regression analysis using SPSS.

However, the full implementation of the randomeffects model extends beyond the use of random-effects weights. First, as mentioned above, HS’s protocol is biased as a result of the method by which the standard errors for the regression coefficient are estimated (Lipsey & Wilson, 2001). In addition, as Borenstein et al (2009, p. 209) argue, ‘the rules for assigning degrees of freedom in the analysis of variance and meta-regression are different for meta-analysis than for primary studies, and so using the primary-study routines for a meta-analysis will yield incorrect standard errors and p-values’.

Consequently, a dedicated meta-analysis package is the appropriate tool to employ in order to draw valid conclusions from a meta-analysis and, in particular, from a random-effects meta-regression. Below, we report the analysis using the CMA package from Biostat (http://www .meta-analysis.com/). Dedicated packages for conducting meta-regression were neither commonly available nor commonly employed at the time when Sharma & Yetton (2003) was being prepared for publication.

## Re-analysis of the Sharma and Yetton data set using a random-effects meta-analysis

Table 4 presents the results of the full random-effects model based on two commonly employed protocols from Borenstein et al (2009) for estimating between-study variance – the DerSimonian–Laird method (also referred to as the method of moments) and the maximum likelihood method. Both analyses support the moderating effect of task interdependence. Further, consistent with Sharma and Yetton’s original finding, the main effect of management support on implementation success is non-significant: the intercept in the meta-regression, which estimates the main effect, is non-significant in both analyses.

Table 4 also reports the results of a fixed-effect metaanalysis. As speculated by Hwang and Schmidt, the p-values for the random-effects regression are more conservative than those estimated under a fixed-effect model. However, despite this, a random-effects metaanalysis reports support for the moderating effect of task interdependence.

## Critique of Hwang and Schmidt’s results of subgroup analysis for moderator detection

Hwang and Schmidt also test the moderating effect of task interdependence using subgroup analysis. They analyze the ‘Corrected Correlation’ between management support and implementation success as a function of task interdependence. Specifically, they claim that ‘Some correlations and sample sizes reported by Sharma and Yetton are incorrect and have been corrected here’. We address this criticism in the Appendix. More importantly, they claim that a subgroup analysis of their ‘corrected’ data set does not support the hypothesis that task interdependence moderates the relationship between management support and implementation success.

We begin by reviewing the results of the subgroup analysis reported by Hwang and Schmidt and reproduced in Table 5. To test for the moderating effect of task interdependence, Hwang and Schmidt partition the sample of 22 studies into two subgroups. They employ a cutoff score of 18 on Sharma and Yetton’s task interdependence ratings, which is based on the mean of the anchor points of 6 and 30 on the scale of task interdependence employed by Sharma and Yetton.

On the basis of this criterion, they classify 13 studies as low task interdependence and 9 studies as high task interdependence. They claim that a significance test, employing the protocol described by Hunter & Schmidt (1990), for the difference between the two subgroup means returns a z-value of 0.65 $( p = 0 . 5 2 )$ . They conclude that their findings do not support the moderating effect of task interdependence on the relationship between management support and implementation success.

Even if the methodology described and the analysis reported by Hwang and Schmidt were accepted, the significance test conducted by them and the conclusions drawn are flawed. ## Critique of Hwang and Schmidt’s hierarchical subgroup (HS)-based methodology for moderator detection

Hwang and Schmidt propose a general methodology for testing moderator effects based on the HS analysis rather than meta-regression. Here, we critique their general methodology for testing moderators based on an HS analysis (see Figure 3 in Hwang and Schmidt).

Hwang and Schmidt recommend that an HS-based methodology should be employed for moderator detection, even when the hypothesized moderators are continuous. However, they present no arguments in favor of their proposal, except to state that ‘Our preferred approach is the subgroup analysis described by Hunter & Schmidt (1990, 2004)’. Against this, Hunter & Schmidt (1990, pp. 463–466) present a critique of the HS method, concluding that ‘the analysis of subgroups exacts a terrible price’.

The arguments for rejecting a subgroup-based approach and selecting a regression approach when continuous moderators are available are extensive and are elaborated in many standard texts on the subject, including Hunter & Schmidt (1990, 2004). A key drawback of dichotomizing a naturally occurring continuous moderator is that it throws away information, losing relevant variance, and reducing the power of the test to detect moderator variables (Cohen et al, 2003). Further, moderator effects estimated using the dichotomization procedure underestimate the true moderating effect. Hunter & Schmidt (1990, pp. 46–47) estimate that the minimum attenuation of effect size due to dichotomization is 0.80. This occurs when the dichotomization is done employing a median split. In Hwang and Schmidt’s analysis, the number of studies in the low and high task interdependence subgroups is 13 and 9, respectively. Therefore, the attenuation in effect size is expected to be greater than 20%.

Another drawback of dichotomization is that it can capitalize on chance because of the specific criterion chosen for dichotomization. Typically, the criteria include the mid-point of the range, the mid-point of the scale, the mean and the median. The effect of choosing a specific criterion is particularly salient when the number of studies is low. This is the case here. Under this condition, outliers can disproportionately influence subgroup means and variances and reduce the power to detect moderators. For instance, in Hwang and Schmidt’s ‘corrected’ data set, the outlier in the high task interdependence subgroup increases the variance due to heterogeneity of studies from 0.653 to 0.953, an increase of 46%, and the total variance from 0.911 to 1.22, an increase of 34%.

Comparisons of the subgroup analysis and regressionbased protocols to test for the effect of continuous moderators support the use of regression-based protocols. For instance, Steel & Kammeyer-Mueller (2001) compare the effectiveness of four different metaanalytic techniques: bivariate correlations, ordinary least squares and WLS multiple regression, and HS analysis. The WLS procedure is identical to that employed by Sharma and Yetton, and the HS procedure is similar to that proposed by Hwang and Schmidt. Steel and Kammeyer-Mueller conclude that the WLS procedure typically provides the most accurate results and the HS procedure typically provides the least accurate (p. 96).

They report that the HS procedure underestimates moderator effects. The estimates, ‘under most conditions, asymptote toward some underestimate’ (p. 107). This bias is a major limitation of the procedure, as dichotomization of a continuous variable loses relevant variance (p. 108). Steel and Kammeyer-Mueller recommend against the use of the subgroup protocol with continuous moderators (p. 108).

In contrast, Hwang and Schmidt reject a regression-based approach on the grounds that regression suffers from a number of limitations, including multi-collinearity and capitalization on chance, when multiple moderators are analyzed. However, an HS analysis is algebraically equivalent to regression (Pedhazur, 1997; Aguinis et al, 2011). Hence, all the criticisms against regression apply equally to subgroup analysis. Further, subgroup analysis suffers from additional limitations discussed above.

Consistent with Steel & Kammeyer-Mueller’s (2001) conclusions, Aguinis et al (2011) stress the disadvantages of dichotomizing continuous moderators and recommend a regression-based protocol for continuous moderators:

Accordingly, the process for estimating possible moderating effects includes two steps. First, a meta-analyst assesses whether effect sizes vary across primary-level studies (i.e., degree of heterogeneity or dispersion of effect sizes). Second, if across-study heterogeneity is found, and it is not explained by artifactual sources such as sampling error, then follow-up analyses are conducted to assess the presence of moderator variables associated with this variance.

If there is heterogeneity and the moderator variable is categorical, the next step is to conduct a subgroup analysis in which each study is assigned a numerical value based on the moderator (e.g., gender: 1 ¼ female, 2 ¼ male) and grouped according to this coding scheme. Then, the withingroup effect sizes are computed and the presence of a moderator is confirmed if there is between-group heterogeneity. Although there are several types of subgroup analysis. they are all algebraically equivalent and vield the same p values (Borenstein et al, 2009, Chapter 19). If there is heterogeneity and the moderator variable is continuous, then the next step, instead of subgroup analysis, is to conduct a meta-regression analysis. Similar to multiple regression, meta-regression consists of a regression model involving predictors (i.e., potential moderators) and a criterion (i.e., weighted effect sizes). Just like multiple regression, meta-regression can also accommodate categorical predictors. (p. 318)

We agree with Steel & Kammeyer-Mueller (2001) and Aguinis et al (2011), and recommend against the HS-based approach advocated by Hwang and Schmidt. Researchers should employ a regression-based approach to metaanalysis when continuous moderators are available. While there is strong support in the literature for not dichotomizing continuous moderators, we found no support for Hwang and Schmidt’s recommendation to dichotomize continuous moderators.
