# A critical approach to human helping in information systems: Heteromation in the Brazilian correspondent banking system

## 1. Introduction

Information systems often fail after implementation. Sometimes they fail because users refuse to incorporate them into work. Other times they fail because they do not provide hoped-for benefits. Strategies to avoid failure include user training (so users might better operate the system), user input into design (so designers might better craft the system for use), and user alterations (so users might better <sup>fi</sup>t the system to use) (e.g., Jasperson, Carter, & Zmud, 2005; Majchrzak, Rice, Malhotra, King, & Ba, 2000; Markus & Mao, 2004). These strategies often rest on the implicit premise that a gap between how designers envisioned system use and how users experienced the system begets failure (Heeks. 2003: Suchman. 2002). Narrowing the gap between designers' conceptions and users' reality thus becomes the goal in many e<sup>f</sup>orts to achieve system success.

Information systems can nonetheless succeed if human users provide labor to bridge the gaps. Ekbia and Nardi (2014, 2017) coined the term “heteromation” (in contrast to automation, which removes humans) to describe this labor relation in which orga nizations or individuals appropriate the economic value generated when users provide the labor necessary to make an information system work. Because users' labor is typically thinly compensated, if at all, heteromated systems carry with them the risk of labor exploitation (Ekbia & Nardi, 2012, 2014, 2017). As such, these systems underscore van Dijck's (2009) concerns about an increasingly participatory culture that lauds unpaid contributions to the corporate good. Following critical scholars of digitalized labor such as Fuchs and Sevignani (2013), Andrejevic (2015), Berg and De Stefano (2017) and many others, it is imperative that we consider how heteromation can serve useful purposes while ensuring worker rights. Berg and De Stefano (2017) pointed out that computer: mediated labor can be managed to regulate work and protect workers, if the right protections are in place. We are a long way from such protections. We hope this paper raises awareness of the fact that heteromation is value-producing labor and as such, it should be part of conversations about worker rights.

Heteromated systems do have the potential for positive applications. Consider ICT for development (ICT4D) projects, which employ technology to address social problems. Examples of ICT4D projects include telemedicine systems to provide hospital consultation to doctors in rural health centers in Crete (Constantinides & Barrett. 2006) and the use of email programs for social inclusion of refugees in New Zealand (Andrade & Doolin, 2016). In many ICT4D projects, because designers do not know distant users or their context well, they cannot well account for user needs (Wyche & Murphy, 2013). As the literature on information systems would predict, and as the ICT4D literature documents, designs that leave such large design-reality gaps often fail (Heeks, 2003). Heteromated systems hold the promise of avoiding such failure: by relying on human labor for critical functions, these systems avoid tailoring the technology to <sup>fi</sup>t an imagined and uninformed use scenario. Whereas technical systems lean towards closure that renders customization di<sup>fi</sup>cult even for technically accomplished users (Kallinikos, 2004), human activity is, by contrast, a non-deterministic, open system. As such, human helping may adapt readily to <sup>fi</sup>ll unforeseen system gaps, and heteromation may prove a good approach in ICT4D projects as well as in many organizational systems.

In this paper, we examine heteromation's reliance on users for the helping necessary for system success to understand better the implications of this labor relation for system design and system users. Through an empirical study of heteromation in the case of the Brazilian correspondent banking system, an ICT4D project aimed at the <sup>fi</sup>nancial inclusion of remote and underserved people, we adopt a critical approach to the issue of reliance on user helping as we examine the type of heteromated labor expended and the amount of e<sup>f</sup>ort that labor required. We pay attention to how users interpreted their helping and to the contextual factors that shaped it.

Understanding heteromation as a labor relation and gaining insights for heteromated system design are important given the recent rise of heteromated systems. We see heteromation in knowledge repositories (Kankanhalli, Tan, & Wei, 2005), patient-centered health records (Davidson, Østerlund, & Flaherty, 2015), and product review and recommender systems (Barrett, Davidson, Prabhu, & Vargo, 2015; Xiao & Benbasat, 2007). In and beyond ICT4D projects, heteromated systems have potential whenever information systems must serve large numbers of users in varied settings and with diverse needs, such that tailoring systems to meet user requirements may prove impossible (Heeks, 2003; Janssen, van der Voort, & van Veenstra, 2015). Heteromation may also prove useful when users are not skilled in technology use; in such cases, user training may prove too expensive to be viable and user alterations may be unlikely (Lee, 2001). As these examples suggest, being cognizant of the labor demands of helping in heteromated systems should prove useful across a wide range of modern work settings

In the balance of this paper, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y situate critical theory in the information systems literature to explain how and why we will use a critical approach to examine heteromation as a labor relation. We then describe Ekbia and Nardi's (2014, 2017) concept of heteromation, with a focus on the types of user labor they identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Because these types remain undertheorized, particularly in terms of the amount of e<sup>f</sup>ort required and why users would willingly exert e<sup>f</sup>ort, we turn brie<sup>fl</sup>y to the literature on helping to gain insight into why users might provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant uncompensated labor. We detail how we explored heteromation in the context of the Brazilian correspondent banking system, employing critical case analyses to tease out the contextual factors that shaped the type of heteromated labor and related e<sup>f</sup>ort as well as the broader discursive factors that shaped people's interpretations of their own helping. We conclude by theorizing the implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings for heteromation as a labor relation, as a design strategy in ICT4D projects and beyond, and as a counterpoint to automation.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Critical theory in information systems research

Critical theory in information systems research aids scholars in addressing socio-political questions in the hopes of bringing about improvement in actors' lives (Gregor, 2006). Scholars who introduced critical theory to the <sup>fi</sup>eld adopted a Habermasian perspective that emphasized emancipation from alienating or distorting practices in technology and social relations to enhance freedom and justice (Lyytinen & Klein, 1985; Ngwenyama, 1991). For example, Lyytinen (1992) called for research to improve the human condition through (possibly, alternative) information systems. Later work drew upon Foucault, Giddens, and Bourdieu to complement attention to alienating or distorted practices with consideration of power relations, expanding the scope of critical theory to include theoretical traditions such as environmentalism, feminism, and Marxism (McGrath, 2005; Myers & Klein, 2011). With this expansion, Howcroft and Trauth (2005:3), drawing on Doolin (1998), argued that critical research questions and deconstructs the taken-forgranted assumptions inherent in the status quo, and interprets organizational activity (including information systems) by recourse to wider social, political, historical, economic, and ideological context.” Within the information systems literature, attention to this wider context has arguably been greatest among ICT4D studies (Avgerou, 2008).

Although scholars disagree to what extent critical research di<sup>f</sup>ers from interpretive research (see Gregor, 2006; Mingers, 2004; Myers & Klein, 2011), some scholars claim this emphasis on context is what di<sup>f</sup>erentiates them. As Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991: 20–21) noted when considering critical theory's attention to the totality of relationships around an information system:

...critical researchers depart from their interpretive colleagues, in that they believe interpretation of the social world is not enough. The material conditions of domination need also to be understood and critiqued, and these are typically not accessible by merely asking participants, who often are unable to perceive and penetrate the circumstances that shape and constrain them. Thus, researchers working in this tradition do not merely accept the self-understanding of participants, but also critically analyze it through the particular theoretical framework which they adopt to conduct their work.

Other scholars have noted the importance of context when arguing that a critical approach permits detailed causal explanations of events and phenomena that incorporate actors' interpretations and analysis of the structures that shape outcomes (Mingers, 2004; Wynn Jr & Williams. 2012)

Critical studies in information systems have emploved a variety of theoretical frameworks to guide their analysis of context (Myers & Klein, 2011). For example, Doolin (2004) employed Foucault's theory of disciplinary power to analyze responses to a new medical information system while Kvasny and Keil (2006) examined the digital divide in two cities by focusing on Bourdieu's concepts of habitus and forms of capital. Among ICT4D studies, Lin, Kuo, and Myers (2015) turned to postcolonial theory to uncover a narrative of marginalization within a digital divide project hailed as a success while Kanungo (2004) framed his analysis of the introduction in poor Indian villages of computer kiosks that provided information about agriculture, commerce, and health in terms of Habermas's theory of communicative action. Despite variety in theoretical frameworks, all critical studies maintain a concern with how context shapes outcomes. For example, Doolin (2004) found that di<sup>f</sup>erences among clinical specialties limited the hospital administration's ability to impose control with a new medical information system: control was easier in specialties in which types ot treatment, patient episodes, and clinical work were easily de<sup>fi</sup>ned and standardized than in those where they were not, and doctors argued that di<sup>f</sup>erences across specialties rendered problematic the system's comparison of doctors' performance. Analyzing context via a particular theoretical lens thus provides a background upon which to understand actors' interpretations

In our study of the Brazilian correspondent banking system, we analyze context through the theoretical framework provided by Ekbia and Nardi's (2014, 2017) heteromation labor relation. Heteromation will enable us to recognize helping acts as labor and to see the appropriation of that labor by others. Moreover, it will prompt us to consider di<sup>f</sup>erences in the type of labor, the amount of e<sup>f</sup>ort required by that labor, and actors' interpretations of their labor across banking contexts.

## 2.2. Heteromated systems and the labor that ensures their success

In Ekbia and Nardi's (2014:1.2) formulation, heteromated systems “push critical tasks to end users as indispensable mediators” [emphasis in original]. In the language of political economy that the authors employ, heteromation is a labor relation in which organizations or individuals extract economic value by relying on users to perform labor that is not (or is thinly) compensated <sup>fi</sup>nancially. For example, patient-centered electronic health records rely on patients to input and manage their own health data, e<sup>f</sup>ectively maintaining their own records and, in the process, saving healthcare organizations the cost of employing people to do this work (Davidson et al., 2015; Detmer, Bloomrosen, Raymond, & Tang, 2008). Similarly, video game creators deliver complex video games with little documentation, leaving players to educate each other through <sup>fi</sup>nancially uncompensated peer learning (Kow & Nardi, 2010; Nardi, 2010).

Ekbia and Nardi (2017) distinguished <sup>fi</sup>ve types of heteromated labor. Users provide communicative labor when, for example, uploading family photos, creating professional pro<sup>fi</sup>les, or posting videos on social media sites. Communicative labor often spawns appropriation through the analysis and selling of associated social data to marketers and advertisers, providing the business model for some of today's largest corporations, such as Facebook. Users provide cognitive labor when operating ATMs or grocery self-checkout stations; here, the appropriation of user labor is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in diminished labor force needs. Users also provide this labor when participating in crowdworking platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk, one of the rare examples in which the appropriating organization or individual compensates the user for that labor, albeit thinly. Creative labor is evidenced when users code modi<sup>fi</sup>cations to video games or submit designs to contests; in both cases, user e<sup>f</sup>ort goes uncompensated <sup>fi</sup>nancially (with the exception of contest winners) while organizations appropriate the coding and design efforts of many. When users interact with robots at work or handle interfaces for other people, they may put forth emotional labor. The case of “autonomous” robots that require human intervention to aid in caregiving demonstrates that emotional labor may be an additional task paid employees must perform, without additional compensation or recognition of improved professional and technical skills. Organizing labor contributes to endeavors such as citizen science or open source projects. Organizations or individuals reap the collective, free e<sup>f</sup>orts of users to accomplish, organize, and manage work; this highly skilled labor would normally be quite expensive.

Although Ekbia and Nardi distinguished types of heteromated labor, they did not discuss di<sup>f</sup>erences in the amount of e<sup>f</sup>ort each type of labor might require by type or context. One might infer from their descriptions and examples that many instances of communicative labor may re<sup>fl</sup>ect a seemingly inconsequential amount of e<sup>f</sup>ort (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>nding, uploading, and tagging a photo), whereas creative (e.g., crafting a logo design) and organizing (e.g., managing software code development) labor may incur signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ort. Moreover, because di<sup>f</sup>erent types of labor draw on di<sup>f</sup>erent human aptitudes, some types may prove more taxing than others (e.g., emotional versus cognitive labor) depending on the person or the context. Thus, although an objective measure such as time spent on the task might provide a reasonable assessment of e<sup>f</sup>ort for some types of heteromated labor, subjective assessments by users of their e<sup>f</sup>ort expenditure would likely be a necessary complement to it.

No matter the amount of e<sup>f</sup>ort, <sup>fi</sup>nancial compensation for heteromated labor is rare, although some scholars have begun to call for micropayments (Ekbia & Nardi, 2017; Lanier, 2013). When <sup>fi</sup>nancial rewards do exist, they are small, as with Amazon Mechanica Turk (Buhrmester, Kwang, & Gosling, 2011). Given, then, that this labor is typically comprised of a voluntary, unpaid act, one might presume that the amount of e<sup>f</sup>ort, whether subjectively or objectively measured, must be low enough to warrant user participation.

The literature on helping, however, suggests that other mechanisms may be at play beyond a simple calculus of e<sup>f</sup>ort. We explore these mechanisms brie<sup>fl</sup>y in the next section while noting that, from a critical approach, the existence of such mechanisms would not render less important the system's demand for heteromated labor or the appropriation of that labor by organizations or individuals. To dismiss a system's reliance on helping on the grounds that users willingly help ignores other possible complications of helping. For example, people who help can become burnt out or fatigued, especially if such helping is involuntary (Bolino, Turnley, Gilstrap, & Suazo, 2010; Grant, 2008). Thus, we pay attention to why users help not to rationalize heteromation, but to understand how the mechanisms that prompt helping may shade users' interpretations of helping.

## 2.3. If helping demands efort, why do people help?

Perhaps the most obvious explanation for helping, and one that organizational studies predominantly investigate (Bolino & Grant 2016), is that some people simply like to help. Thus, we might view the provision of some instances of heteromated labor as derivative of an individual personality trait that distinguishes people with prosocial values (Grant, 2008; McNeely & Meglino, 1994; Penner, Fritzsche, Craiger, & Freifeld, 1995). Studies of information systems have found some support for this proposition, showing that liking to help was predictive of knowledge contributions in electronic networks of practice and knowledge repositories (e.g., Kankanhalli et al., 2005; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Leveraging a propensity to help might not seem problematic in heteromated systems. In fact, one might argue that heteromated systems provide a bene<sup>fi</sup>t to such users by providing an opportunity to engage in an activity they like to do

Other work, however, <sup>fi</sup>nds that social incentives may prompt helping. For example, people may help if they know how their e<sup>f</sup>orts will bene<sup>fi</sup>t others (Bellé, 2013). Studies of online communities, electronic networks of practice, open source software, and organizational social media platforms have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed social incentives such as perceived obligations, reciprocity norms, trust, liking, feelings of identity with a community, and desire to enhance one's reputation that promote helping behaviors (Crowston, Wei, Howison, & Wiggins, 2012; Faraj & Johnson, 2011; Leonardi, 2017; Wasko, Teigland, & Faraj, 2009). Because social incentives may be more amenable to design (e.g., actualizing social networks and building social incentives into the technology) than are personality traits (Grant, 2007), some heteromated systems may explicitly provide for them. For example, systems such as LinkedIn and Facebook provide the incentives of identity creation and social connections (Papacharissi, 2009).<sup>2</sup> Overall, however, strong <sup>fi</sup>ndings for the disposition of liking to help (e.g., Grant, 2008; McNeely & Meglino, 1994; Penner et al., 1995) in comparison to weaker <sup>fi</sup>ndings for social incentives (e.g., Bellé, 2013) in the helping literature may suggest that people who help heteromated systems are simply like people who help in other situations: they like to help and no concern for their welfare is warranted.

Although many studies have examined why people help, few studies have examined constraints that may limit or prevent helping. In the context of information systems, Kankanhalli et al. (2005) reported that fear of loss of knowledge power did not keep people from contributing to an electronic knowledge repository, but the costly time to codify knowledge did limit contributions when trust in the community was weak. Leonardi (2017), in a study of a financial services firm, found that emplovees who failed to see strong value in social media felt their lack of an incentive was a barrier to contributing to the <sup>fi</sup>rm's internal social networking site. In general, the dearth of research on constraints is emblematic of the literature's larger inattention to the context of helping. Finding that contextual factors that may shape helping, such as organizational policies, government regulations, or technology materiality, are largel missing from the literature on helping, Bolino and Grant (2016) called for research that examines occupational contexts. Similarly, in their review of public service motivation, Bozeman and Su (2015) called for research that disentangles dispositional from contextua factors.

## 2.4. Research questions

Such issues of context drive our research questions with respect to heteromation's reliance on helping as we explore heteromation as a labor relation and a design strategy.

RQ1. To what extent does the type of heteromated labor and the amount of e<sup>f</sup>ort it requires di<sup>f</sup>er by context? What factors shape these di<sup>f</sup>erences?

RQ2. To what extent do users' interpretations of their helping di<sup>f</sup>er by context? What factors shape these di<sup>f</sup>erences?

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. Research setting: the Brazilian correspondent banking system

Prior to 2001, people in rural or otherwise underserved areas of Brazil had to travel long distances to banks to receive welfare payments. In 2001, to reduce the hardship such travel incurred, the government mandated that people receive their welfare payments in the city where they lived (Alves & Soares, 2006). Through this mandate, Caixa Econômica Federal (hereafter, Caixa), the public bank responsible for payments, faced a logistical and economic predicament: how to reach the expected goal of 5.7 million payments to poor families in a country as large as Brazil at reasonable cost (Licio, Mesquita, & Curralero, 2011). Caixa ruled out new branches as infeasible due to costs; ATMs were both too costly and inappropriate for low-literate, technologically-inexperienced clients (Ivatury, 2006).

Because Caixa ran Brazil's lottery system, they opted to make the bene<sup>fi</sup>t payments through lottery shops, which were digitally connected to, and tightly overseen by, Caixa's IT banking systems. More than 1500 small cities had no lottery shops, however, and it was these cities where many welfare bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries lived. Thus, Caixa recruited retail stores and other non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions to distribute welfare bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and provide bank services as “correspondents” (Alves & Soares, 2006). Later, post o<sup>fi</sup>ces (by mandate) and community banks (by choice) also joined the correspondent banking system. These di<sup>f</sup>erences in correspondents' relationships to banks (e.g., having an embedded tie to the bank replete with tight oversight, as in the case of lottery shops, or being recruited by them, as in the case of retail shops) and participation status (either mandated, as in the case of lottery shops and post o<sup>fi</sup>ces, or voluntary, as in the case of retail stores and community banks) were two of three contextual factors we considered in our analysis (the third factor concerned system policies, which we describe in Section 3.1.3 below).

Although welfare bene<sup>fi</sup>t payments were a priority, Brazil's banking regulators viewed correspondent banking as a means to also enable access to basic transactional banking services (e.g., withdrawals and deposits) to Brazil's poor population (Kumar, 2006). Moreover, because banks in Brazil were responsible for bill payment collection for utilities and other government services since the 1960s (Jayo, Diniz, Zambaldi, & Christopoulos, 2012), the correspondent banking system allowed for bill payment outside of banks. As commercial banks not involved in welfare bene<sup>fi</sup>t payment were added to the system to sponsor retail stores and community banks acting as correspondents, correspondent banking became an attractive way to alleviate the substantial <sup>fl</sup>ow of poor people (nonclients of banks) paying their bills in bank branches (Jayo et al., 2012).

In 2003, the main welfare bene<sup>fi</sup>t program in Brazil assisted 3.6 million families, a number that increased each year (Senna, Burlandy, Monnerat, Schottz, & Magalhães, 2007). In 2010, 70% of the by then 12 million bene<sup>fi</sup>ts were paid through the correspondent banking system (Banco Central do Brasil, 2011), and by 2014, correspondents accounted for 70% of Brazil's banking facilities (Banco Central do Brasil, 2015). Although developing countries in Latin America, Asia, and Africa soon adopted similar systems, Brazil maintained the most outlets, making it the world's largest such system (Dias, Staschen, & Noor, 2015). Brazil's system featured ICT, system actors, and system policies.

## 3.1.1. ICT

Each correspondent's ICT had three components: (1) a keypad for entering personal identi<sup>fi</sup>cation numbers (PINs); (2) a reader for bill barcodes and bank cards; and (3) either a personal computer or a point-of-service device to select transaction types. Connected to bank servers via Internet, General Packet Radio Service, or satellite (Kumar, 2006), this simple, rigid ICT lacked the qualities of <sup>fl</sup>exible technologies (Leonardi, 2011: 147), but nonetheless had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant advantages. The ICT's simplicity kept installation and maintenance costs low by requiring only inexpensive parts and low-skilled technicians (Diniz, Jayo, Pozzebon, Lavoie, & Foguel, 2014; Jayo et al., 2012). The ICT's rigidity also provided security: by design, banking systems typically disallow user alterations (Hertzum, Jørgensen, & Nørgaard, 2007).

In fact, the system's ICT was designed for banks to “guarantee the safety and soundness of the IT platform and risk-management policies” (Mas & Almazán, 2011: 120), with correspondents simply managing cash transactions, either by collecting cash via utility bill payments, for example, or delivering cash via welfare bene<sup>fi</sup>t payments. Thus, correspondents did not have the access that would allow them to search bank, utility, or government services databases, to leave notes on recipients' digital accounts with these organizations, or to enter request tickets for customer service to resolve bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries' problems. In short, this simple, rigid ICT design acted similar to an ATM interface in that passwords enabled a limited set of transactions but no deeper communication with system databases and organizations.

## 3.1.2. System actors

System actors included clients, correspondents, banks, the government, and utility companies. Welfare recipients, or clients, were the intended users. They were to type their PIN on the keypad, slide their bank card through the reader, voice a desired service, and o<sup>f</sup>er documentation (e.g., a utility bill). After such actions, correspondents were to select a service on the interface (such as deposit ), enter the appropriate monetary amount, scan relevant documents, accept cash from or give cash to the client, and provide a receipt. As intended by Caixa when creating the system, correspondents had only a few other designed duties, such as depositing cash at the bank (Jayo et al., 2012). Yet, it was correspondents' helping beyond these limited duties—unintended and unknown by Caixa—that enabled system success (for example, they so frequently took over clients' data entry steps that they became the system's true users), and it was this helping that we studied

Banks provided bank accounts for clients, facilitated bene<sup>fi</sup>t distribution, and provided the banking software (Santos et al., 2011); bank employees, known as bancarios, performed the banking services that correspondents could not (e.g., loan approval) and supervised correspondents. The federal government, through the Ministry of Social Development, sponsored the welfare bene<sup>fi</sup>t program and issued the clients' system cards (Brandão, 2012). Finally, whereas the government issued welfare payments, utility companies issued bills (for water, gas, electricity, and the like) that clients could, if they liked, pay at correspondents (Jayo et al., 2012)

The correspondent banking system provided potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for each of these actors. Clients gained access to banking services that otherwise would have been difficult for them to reach. reducing costs of traveling to pay bills or receive government benefits (Sanford & Cojocaru, 2013). Although each banking transaction yielded only a small fee for correspondents, retailers and lottery shop owners could gain higher sales from increased foot tra<sup>fi</sup>c to their business (CGAP, 2010; Diniz, Birochi, & Pozzebon, 2012; Jayo et al., 2012; Mas & Almazán, 2011). The federal government gained in correspondents an instrument to implement <sup>fi</sup>nancial inclusion policies and the most important channel for delivering welfare bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Kumar, 2006). Banks saw correspondents as the preferred channel for bill payment collection and an e<sup>fi</sup>cient way to prospect new clients among the poor (Jayo et al., 2012). Bancarios, whose ranks the system may have threatened, and postal clerks, for whom participation was mandatory but who were unlikely to see an increase in sales, may have been the only actors for whom the system did not appear bene<sup>fi</sup>cial at the outset (Sanches & Lins, 2014)

## 3.1.3. System policies

Governing this unique system were an assortment of policies. Some policies, such as banking regulations, restricted the <sup>fi</sup>nancial services that correspondents could o<sup>f</sup>er. Other bank policies, such as those to deter thieves in the absence of armed guards and armored vehicles, limited how much cash a correspondent could keep on hand. When the correspondents' cash on hand reached the limit, the system software automatically turned o<sup>f</sup>, forcing correspondents to shut down until they deposited cash at the bank. Such policies provided the rules by which the banking system operated, and factored into how correspondents helped clients. As such, the policies became the third contextual factor that we considered in our analysis, in addition to correspondents' relationship to the bank and participation status, which we discussed earlier.

## 3.2. Research design

We employed an inductive approach that placed us in correspondent settings to directly observe context. Following a case stud methodology (Yin, 1994) combined with strategies for comparative analysis (Eisenhardt, 1989), we used the four primary correspondent settings (retail stores, community banks, post o<sup>fi</sup>ces, and lottery shops) as cases.

## 3.3. Data Collection

We built our case studies upon on-site interviews that we conducted over two 10-day trips in October 2011 and March 2012. Conducting interviews on site allowed us to observe the context of a setting, including interactions with clients and technology. As Table 1 depicts, we interviewed 55 people across a variety of roles to deepen our understanding of the system and the help that its success demanded. We gained access to these 55 people by leveraging the connections that our second author had gained from studying the correspondent system for <sup>fi</sup>ve years prior to our study. These connections enabled us to contact executives in three banks whose help led to various streams of interviewees. For example, sta<sup>f</sup> in the o<sup>fi</sup>ce of Caixa's vice-president provided us with the name of a regional executive o<sup>fi</sup>cer, who connected us to a branch manager in a small town, who connected us to his channel manager, who connected us to correspondents whose work she oversaw When in the towns of those correspondents we also interviewed correspondents who we came across as we walked and drove along the streets. Our interviews occurred in São Paulo, where the banks were headquartered, and the state of Pernambuco, one of the poorest areas of Brazil and one with a long history of correspondent banking (its town Solidão—literally, “loneliness”—was home to the <sup>fi</sup>rst non-lottery shop correspondent, established in 2001).

We interviewed 25 correspondents across the four settings. In retail stores, we conducted our interviews at the banking ICT so that the correspondent might simultaneously serve clients; in a few cases, we interviewed correspondents in their home above or beside the store. Retail store correspondents dominated the banking system; thus, we oversampled them. In the community bank, post o<sup>fi</sup>ces, and lottery shops, whose layouts did not facilitate interviews during transactions, we conducted interviews in small rooms o<sup>f</sup> the lobby.

At bank branches, we interviewed 29 bancarios, including 18 tellers or agents and 11 managers, typically at a desk away from clients. Understanding tellers' and agents' actions was critical for discerning how the type and extent of correspondents' helping were unique to the correspondent banking system and not common to banking in general. Bank managers, responsible for correspondents in their geographic area, provided insight into correspondents' designed actions. Lottery shops were correspondents under Caixa and post o<sup>fi</sup>ces under a private bank; retail stores and community banks could be correspondents under any public or private bank. Thus we sampled bancarios not just from Caixa, but also from the private bank governing post o<sup>fi</sup>ces and a second public bank; combined, the three banks accounted for more than 70% of correspondents (Banco Central do Brasil, 2014). We also interviewed the lead designer of the correspondent banking system (then a member of Caixa's board of directors).

Our semi-structured interviews ranged from 20 min to 2 h, with a median of 1 h. We audio recorded interviews and wrote notes to capture the physical setting, clientele, ICT, user actions, and the like. We conducted almost all interviews in Portuguese, with real time translation; all interview recordings were translated and transcribed. Our questions for bank managers focused on di<sup>f</sup>erences between bancarios and correspondents and how branches managed their correspondents. We questioned the system designer about the history, challenges, and successes of the banking system. Example questions from the correspondent and bancario teller/agent protocols illustrate our interest in helping as an everyday work practice (e.g., in terms of tasks and technology) in a context that may have shaped helping (e.g., in terms of problems):

1. What made you want to become a correspondent/bancario?

2. Can you tell me what kinds of tasks you did yesterday?

3. Could you show me the di<sup>f</sup>erent technologies and tools that you use in your job as a correspondent/bancario?

4. What kinds of problems do you routinely face as a correspondent/bancario?

Missing from our sample were clients. Our stays were too short for us to build the trust necessary to engage the local population; thus, we could not interview welfare recipients. However, correspondents were the actors whose unpaid labor rendered this system a heteromated one, a fact that our data collection con<sup>fi</sup>rmed repeatedly. For example, clients who we observed during interviews with correspondents rarely touched the ICT, let alone acted in more substantive ways. Thus, our inability to interview clients limited our ability to speak of outcomes of helping for them, but did not otherwise detract from our study of why correspondents helped.

## 3.4. Data analysis

Structuring our analysis to <sup>fi</sup>t our study's motivation (Yin, 1994), we <sup>fi</sup>rst sought to con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the correspondent banking system was a heteromated system by identifying and categorizing by type the unpaid labor that correspondents undertook to help the system succeed. Next, we sought to identify how context shaped both helping and correspondents' interpretations of their helping. To this end, we organized our transcripts by setting in ATLAS.ti, a qualitative analysis software.

From close readings of our interviews with bancarios and the system designer, we built sets of designed work actions for correspondents, what critical theorists often call discourses that structure people's approaches towards everyday activities (Deetz & Mumby. 1990). In critical theory, discourses are akin to ideologies or ways of being and knowing. They are produced through talk and shape the way that people see and act in the world. Following Alvesson and Deetz's (2000) advice for critical theory research, we aimed to understand the e<sup>f</sup>ects of such discourses on the correspondents' everyday activities (here, helping behaviors) by exploring how the discourses structured approaches to activities in speci<sup>fi</sup>c settings and then comparing the activities across settings. To follow this strategy in practical terms, we conducted both within-case and between-case analyses.

Beginning with a within-case analysis (Miles & Huberman, 1994), we considered separately each correspondent setting. Within each of the cases, we began by coding episodes in which correspondents enacted helping behaviors to make the system work. We de<sup>fi</sup>ned helping behaviors as any action that a correspondent took in excess of the designed work actions. Examples of such heteromated labor included helping clients operate the keypad and teaching <sup>fi</sup>nancial literacy. With this subset of data identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, we then further coded each episode of helping to uncover what moved correspondents to help and what constrained helping. To do so, we <sup>fl</sup>agged episodes in which they described their reasons for becoming correspondents, their reasons for enacting certain helping behaviors, or the problems that confronted them. Close reading of each episode enabled us to differentiate between cognitive and emotional labor, the two types of heteromated labor among the <sup>fi</sup>ve types that Ekbia and Nardi (2017) distinguished that emerged signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in our study, and to gain a sense of the amount of e<sup>f</sup>ort that each helping behavior entailed. Thus, for example, we categorized operating the keypads as cognitive labor and providing personal advice as emotional labor, and from the descriptions interviewees provided, we could determine that the latter entailed more e<sup>f</sup>ort than the former per episode, although the former occurred much more frequently.

Following the advice of Alvesson and Deetz (2000), our next step was to relate activities within the larger set of discourses that structured them. To do so, we looked at the co-occurrence of all the codes we created for helping behaviors and constraints. Examining these codes together. we uncovered several themes that spoke to the contextual factors that characterized correspondent banking in our study. For example, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed “daily cash limits” (a bank policy) as a common constraint that formed part of the discourse in one setting. We also looked across the cases to explore how interviewees discursively positioned (Deetz & Mumby, 1990) their own helping behaviors in light of the broader discourses in which they operated. Across rounds of coding, we re<sup>fi</sup>ned our scheme in the face of supporting or discon<sup>fi</sup>rming evidence for our developing ideas (Creswell & Miller, 2000). We followed this same method across settings until we fully understood helping and heteromation in each one

Next, we turned to between-case analysis (Miles & Huberman, 1994), looking for di<sup>f</sup>erences and similarities in the way that the three contextual factors we studied (correspondents' relationship to the bank, participation status, and system policies) structured helping behaviors, highlighting the unique discourse of correspondent banking in each setting. We coded our interview transcripts to capture details and quotes related to these factors. We considered these contextual factors alongside our analysis of types of labor and constraints to understand helping in the correspondent banking system as a whole and how correspondents interpreted their heteromated labor.

## 4. Findings: helping in a heteromated system across four settings

We begin our presentation of <sup>fi</sup>ndings by describing helping in each of the four banking settings in turn: retail stores and a community bank (voluntary correspondents) followed by post o<sup>fi</sup>ces and lottery shops (mandatory correspondents). In our accounts, we highlight how context shaped the type of heteromated labor (cognitive or emotional, drawing on the two types among Ekbia and Nardi's (2017) <sup>fi</sup>ve types that emerged signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in our study) and the extent of the heteromated labor that correspondents undertook. Mindful of our research questions, our summary compares and contrasts the factors that shaped helping and correspondents' interpretations of helping across settings.

## 4.1. Helping at retail stores

Recruited by banks, retailers became correspondents for two reasons. First, clients who stopped to cash a check or pay a bill at a retail outlet might be drawn to shop. A rise in store sales far outweighed the pro<sup>fi</sup>t to be had from banking transactions, which paid only a few cents apiece. Second, retailers heard tales of banking hardship from neighbors, relatives, and customers that bred empath and prompted a felt obligation to help, as this retailer explained:

I think it's not my place to say this, but the community tells me that it was a blessing to have Caixa [correspondents] here; it [enhanced] their life profusely. The majority of the [welfare] benefit payments are very small and [to cash them people had to] go to Iguaraci [closest city with bank branches]. That is half a day of work that the beneficiary misses, on top of having to pay for food and transportation. On the other hand, here, they come after work, cash their checks, buy something and go home.

(grocery store correspondent)

Bancarios did not express this same empathy for or obligation to poor people. Rather, with the correspondent banking system put in place speci<sup>fi</sup>cally to handle welfare recipients, bancarios were free to serve the rich. A bank loan agent exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed this class relation:

If a lot of people don't have a bank account, don't have access to a bank, don't have proven income, they [still] have to pay their bills somewhere. And this public, doesn't interest me…The banking correspondent takes out this load of people, leaving my branch wonderfully beautiful, [leaving] not only those who have money, but those who want my money [as loans].

(bank loan agent)

Retailers quickly came to learn that the correspondent banking system would need them to provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant heteromated labor for it to succeed. Their helping came mostly via four actions—operating the keypads, circumnavigating system policies, mediating between institutions and clients, and providing substantive advice on both <sup>fi</sup>nancial and personal matters. Although some of these actions may seem inconsequential, when multiplied over many users over the course of days, weeks, and months, they added up. In addition, these actions were often made tedious or overly intricate by a system that was not designed for them.

Operating the keypads. We described the ICT as simple, and retailers viewed it that way, as a shoe store owner explained, “We just connect to the electricity and that is it.” But for remote and underserved clients, whose technical literacy was low, the keypad was a hurdle. Although designed to face the client (kiosks had a hole in the plastic shield for the cable to pass through and a ledge for the keypad to rest on). the kevpad in every store we visited was positioned beside and facing the retailer. Rather than type in their PIN. clients handed the retailer a small, folded slip of paper on which their PIN was written. The retailer glanced at the slip, keyed in the PIN, and returned the slip to the client. In this manner, retailers provided cognitive labor to the system by operating the keypads on every transaction as they were helping their low-literacy customers.

Circumnavigating system policies. The system would have failed often had retailers not also helped customers by circumnavigating system policies, acts of emotional labor that re<sup>fl</sup>ected more e<sup>f</sup>ort than simply entering PINs on the keypad. For example, the banks cash limit policies, which constrained retailers' helping, were especially troublesome for small retailers in rural locations, who often worked alone on a shift. If retailers reached the cash-on-hand limit when alone, they had to close their store to run to the bank (often in another town) to deposit cash before the system software would restart. Because banking transaction fees paid so little to the retailer, closing the store was less pro<sup>fi</sup>table than simply curtailing transactions until someone could run to the bank. But clients expected retailers to have regular hours just like banks, and grew angry if the system was down when they visited. To soothe clients, retailers developed ways to circumnavigate cash limits, thus keeping the software up. A correspondent explained how she and a nearby grocery shop owner worked together to evade a cash limit by directing clients to each other's store should one hit the limit:

What happens is … when we achieve the limit it [the software] locks, and stays locked until someone withdraws [cash]. Then he [the other owner] sends [the client] to me, if it's something that I can do, too. There isn't rivalry between us, quite the opposite…We work in harmony. (grocery store correspondent)

Mediating between institutions and clients. As retailers entered PINs and circumnavigated system policies, clients' trust in them grew, but so did expectations. In particular, clients came to expect retailers to mediate on their behalf with government agencies and banks. Retailers noted that many clients found agencies and banks daunting, in part because, as poor, often rural people, their dress and behavior stood out in these formal, institutionalized, and urbane environments. One retailer explained:

I meet people who are so simple, who haven't ever gone to a bank, or who are too shy to go there, or they send someone to get their pay checks.

(copy store correspondent)

And so it was that retailers were motivated to act as clients' intermediaries to these institutions. A client, for example, might be distraught because his welfare payment was too small one month. The retailer might calm the client by advising how to contact the appropriate agency or calling for him. Retailers noted the recognition they gained from this emotional labor:

Here is a small town, and they expect that we know everything, things that they expect to resolve here, and we don't know. Things that we cannot resolve here, I always try to chase the answer for them.

(grocery store correspondent)

Providing advice on financial and personal matters. Clients began to call upon retailers as <sup>fi</sup>nancial advisors on a variety of issues, such as whether they should start saving their money at the bank. Clients also sought personal advice, such as asking a retailer to mediate a family property <sup>fi</sup>ght. Providing advice in this manner demanded more emotional labor from correspondents. This labor was not strictly necessary for the operation of the banking technology, and yet it contributed to customers' use of the system (and therefore the banks' pro<sup>fi</sup>ts) by bringing customers into the fold in personal ways.

Pressed into service to help others, retailers' labor far exceeded design intentions, enabling system success. Retailers gained a greater public role and higher status and recognition in their communities, which may have prompted continued helping. As the following comment illustrates, the desire to help their community that initially motivated some retailers to become correspondents grew stronger through their heteromated labor:

I like [being a correspondent] because I can help somehow…I can visit them at their home, and have them come to my store, and I trea them like any other… I think this part of social equality is very important and very rewarding.

(copy store correspondent)

## 4.2. Helping at the community bank

Paulo Freire Bank, a community bank that became a correspondent outlet, sat in an economically depressed São Paulo neighborhood. In the void left when the government and banks vacated the neighborhood, residents organized to create their own economy, including creation of the bank. Their conviction to work for <sup>fi</sup>nancial sovereignty in the context of, but largely separate from, the mainstream economy re<sup>fl</sup>ected the ideals of the Brazilian philosopher Paulo Freire, whose name they adopted for their bank:

The name had to relate to social struggles, so we thought of [Che] Guevara, Margarida Maria Alves [a slain Brazilian activist], until we chose Paulo Freire. And we chose that because it is part of our identity.

(community bank correspondent)

Residents decided to use correspondent banking to expand the bank's <sup>fi</sup>nancial services for their community. However, limits on the range of <sup>fi</sup>nancial services that they could o<sup>f</sup>er as community bankers under the correspondent model constrained their helping. Community bankers pushed at these constraints because the role of correspondent as realized in the case of a retailer (what they termed, “merchant correspondent”) did not <sup>fi</sup>t their strong socialist ideology:

Because our story is about social struggle. We have mobilization; we've been on the streets; we've faced the police; we've faced the city hall, the state government, Brasilia [capital of Brazil]. So there is a history of struggle. We knew that being just a merchant correspondent wouldn't be enough…We don't want to become a conventional bank; we want to difer from it. We want to show that another model of economy exists, which is inside of a solidarity economy. We don't want to duplicate the capitalist economy.

(community bank correspondent)

Thus, correspondents in this community were governed by an ideology that shaped their helping and reflected their empathy towards and felt obligation to help others. They helped in four ways, all of which were forms of emotional labor. To begin, in line with their ideals of empowerment, they taught clients how to operate the keypad rather than doing so for them. Then, they formed a loan committee. To learn to make loan approval or denial decisions, which other correspondents were not allowed to do and which often led to anger and resentment in cases of denial, they attended a week-long credit analysis course, and then began to review loans. Third, they educated the community to improve <sup>fi</sup>nancial literacy so that clients could make wise, independent decisions. As one correspondent noted, “My incentive is to build people's independence.” Finally, to support residents' desire for <sup>fi</sup>nancial sovereignty, they minted a new currency, with bills called freires that became the currency of choice in the neighborhood

More than 100 community banks like the Paulo Freire bank sprang up across Brazil under the correspondent banking system. In this setting, correspondents were activists who were pleased to o<sup>f</sup>er heteromated labor to enable successful implementation of their socialist ideals. Their socialism, however, remained rooted within the capitalism of the banking system, and banks continued to extract economic value from the community bankers' heteromated labor

## 4.3. Helping at the post ofice

Unlike retailers and community bankers, postal clerks were required to become correspondents when the Post O<sup>fi</sup>ce added new banking software to clerks' postal computers, thereby allowing clients to pay bills while at the post o<sup>fi</sup>ce. Although required to become correspondents, post o<sup>fi</sup>ce clerks nonetheless felt empathy towards and an obligation to help the public, just as retailers and community bankers did. As postal clerks, they were accustomed to attending to poor clients' needs and pleased to do so in this new way, seeing social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in clients visiting post o<sup>fi</sup>ces over banks for their bill payment and minor banking transactions:

[T]he customer service of the post ofice is much more humanized in comparison to a bank. A bank is colder; they don't ofer the service that we ofer. It is good for the people and for the post ofice.

(post o<sup>fi</sup>ce correspondent)

Postal clerks helped the system in two ways. First, they provided cognitive labor by operating the keypads for clients who were overwhelmed by the ICT. Second, postal clerks provided emotional labor by taking time with clients, answering their questions in detail and teaching willing clients how to type a PIN. Postal clerks made clients feel welcome, comfortable, and reassured about their banking transactions with the new system through this heteromated labor. In this manner. heteromation leveraged vears of postal clerks' social capital to make the system successful.

Postal clerks di<sup>f</sup>ered from other correspondents in that they were unlikely to become advisors in <sup>fi</sup>nancial or personal matters (like retailers) or advocates for <sup>fi</sup>nancial sovereignty (like community bankers). Although they handled simple banking transactions and bill payments, they did not distribute welfare bene<sup>fi</sup>t payments, which meant they were not exposed to the hardships that the poorest segment of the population faced. Postal clerks also faced greater constraints than did retailers and community bankers: as a bureaucratic institution with an embedded tie to the bank, the Post O<sup>fi</sup>ce provided no leeway to violate bank policies to help clients (such as circumnavigating cash limits by using their own bank accounts, as retailers did), yet with no authority to work with banks to o<sup>f</sup>er new services (such as the loan program that Paulo Freire correspondents initiated).

## 4.4. Helping at lottery shops

Caixa, which ran Brazil's lottery system, mandated that lottery shop owners and their clerks become correspondents; unlike in post o<sup>fi</sup>ces, this mandate included handling welfare bene<sup>fi</sup>t payments. On days when welfare bene<sup>fi</sup>t payments were made, clients su<sup>f</sup>ered through long waits in queue, which aroused lottery shop owners' empathy and a sense of obligation to help clients. Consequently, they provided the system with emotional labor by <sup>fi</sup>ghting to help clients by, for example lobbying Caixa to reduce queue lengths, as these quotes attest:

[I]f you come here on a payday, you will be impressed with the number of people. If you come here next Thursday, you can't get in this shop because of the number of people. When we complain to Caixa about it, do you know what they say? “Don't worry about 30 cents [what the correspondents make on a transaction], go sell loans.” A lot of managers have told me that. I have to worry about my clients, about people fainting in my store. Caixa doesn't care that the lottery shop owner has to deal with people upset for standing in line for more than three hours, that the system is down and people are waiting.

(lottery shop correspondent)

I'm helping people, in a way, because I'm fighting for a better way. I'm looking for a way that they wait less time in line. And under blood and sweat, I'm getting there.

(lottery shop correspondent)

However eager lottery shop correspondents were to make the lines move faster, they faced constraints on their system helping that other correspondents did not. Whereas retailers, for example, could operate at a distance (geographically and organizationally) from the bank's oversight, lottery shop correspondents ran outlets that Caixa tightly controlled, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting their embedded tie. Consequently, lottery shop correspondents never accepted slips of paper with PINs on them from clients because to do so would violate Caixa operational policies that strictly governed their work. Instead, they had to guide clients through keypad use. Nor did they employ their store accounts to circumnavigate cash limit policies because their lottery shop accounts were directly tied to Caixa's databases. Thus, they could not service clients when they had too much cash on hand, which angered clients who were turned away. Similarly, because their system access was limited, they could not resolve client problems that originated in their shop; instead, they had to direct clients to the bank, as this correspondent noted:

For example, in case we have an energy outage when an electrical bill has been paid here, then it doesn't show with the utility company. The customer receives a second note at their house, or even a cut on their service, and they come back to complain, but we don't have any access to this information. We can't access any information to help the client, only Caixa can do that, so they have to go there [to the Caixa branch].

(lottery shop correspondent)

Frustrated with these constraints, lottery shop correspondents' help beyond lobbying for shorter queues was limited to small acts of cognitive labor, such as showing clients how to operate the keypad. In addition, their emotional labor included working extended hours to meet client demand and serving clients who forget their identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, as these correspondents noted:

During the Bolsa Familia [welfare benefit] payment day, which is a governmental program, it's crazy here. We service 4,800 people in ten days; just this program generates 480 people per day here, so it's a lot of people…So we work from 6:30 a.m. to 6:30 p.m. Everybody works. We work in turns, three emplovees in the cash register, doing the rotation and during lunch time

(lottery shop correspondent)

There are people that come here so often that I know their names, date of birth. Sometimes an elderly person forgets to bring a picture ID and it's a requirement from the bank and when they get here, they want to talk to me, because they know I remember. I think this is a good thing.

(lottery shop correspondent)

Lottery shop owners in small towns, where lottery sales were not as strong as in large cities, saw in the correspondent banking system the potential to increase their revenues by o<sup>f</sup>ering more <sup>fi</sup>nancial services, much as the community banks did. However, Caixa did not equip owners in small towns to handle the bank's new <sup>fi</sup>nancial products well. For example, the following correspondent told of a trouble she had with a new life insurance policy (or “death policy,” as it was called) product:

They launched a new product called “seguro vida,” a “death policy.” A guy pays R\$3,000 in a year, and in case he dies, the family will receive R\$200,000, two months of “cesta basica” [Domestic Food Basket, or the ration of minimum essential food], and coverage for the funeral. Ok, that's great, I'm from the country and we know each other; if someone dies at midnight or one in the morning, they're not calling Caixa, they will be knocking on my door. Then what happens is, we ask Caixa and we still don't know: Who will do the funeral? In the big city they are all set up, but not here. So, how long would it take for a corpse to be buried? So, am I going to sell this product? No, I won't. I have an interest in doing it, of course, I can make money with this, but I don't want to be wakened up at midnight by a client tha lost someone and doesn't know what to do, and I don't know what to say.

(lottery shop correspondent)

Cha<sup>fi</sup>ng under restrictions or other constraints that limited what services they could o<sup>f</sup>er, lottery shop correspondents in smal towns expressed their desires for Caixa to alter restrictive operational policies, but Caixa did not do so. Thus, for lottery shop correspondents, their heteromated labor was not o<sup>f</sup>set by <sup>fi</sup>nancial rewards beyond potential increased lottery sales.

## 4.5. Summary

Table 2 summarizes the contextual factors that shaped helping, helping behaviors by the type of labor, and users' interpretations of helping across the four correspondent banking settings that we studied. In each setting, for the system to work, correspondents had to provide heteromated labor. Independent of setting, correspondents interpreted their willingness to help in terms of their empathy, noting their felt obligation to the clients who frequented their outlets. For community bankers and some retailers, aware of clients needs, this empathy is what compelled them to become correspondents. For postal clerks, it was part of their established occupational identity before becoming correspondents. For lotter shop owners and some retailers, it emerged after becoming a correspondent as a consequence of observing clients' hardships. In all cases, this empathy prompted heteromated labor after individuals became correspondents.

Despite this shared empathy that prompted their helping, correspondents varied by setting in their helping behaviors, with helping among retail store and community bank correspondents appearing more expansive and substantive than that by post o<sup>fi</sup>ce and lottery shop correspondents. For example, retailers circumnavigated system policies, mediated between institutions and clients, and provided all manner of advice; community bankers organized a loan program, taught <sup>fi</sup>nancial literacy, and minted currency. By contrast, post o<sup>fi</sup>ce and lottery shop correspondents only marginally extended their helping beyond the keypads. Although these <sup>fi</sup>ndings map onto correspondents' voluntary versus mandatory participation, respectively, other contextual factors appeared more important in shaping outcomes.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the correspondents' relationship to banks provided them with greater (retailers, community bankers) or lesser (posta clerks, lottery shop owners) autonomy from banks, as re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the lists of bank and government policies that governed their work and constrained their helping. For example, as Table 2 shows, post o<sup>fi</sup>ces faced the longest list of such policies and the fewest helping behaviors. Faced with the fewest policies, retailers and community bankers o<sup>f</sup>ered the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant heteromated labor in terms of number of helping behaviors and amount of e<sup>f</sup>ort implicit in them. In addition, their labor appeared to be the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in terms of what it achieved for clients (e.g., mediation and minting currency seem more likely to solve problems and provide op portunities than answering questions).

One of the more intriguing of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings is the di<sup>f</sup>erence in levels of frustration among correspondents, with retailers and lottery shop owners expressing the most frustration at their inability to help. Community banks, with the greatest sovereignty among correspondents, were able to achieve many of their socialist ideals for a separate economy, which may account for how pleased the were to help clients. Postal clerks, although circumscribed in how they could help, also were not exposed to welfare bene<sup>fi</sup>t payment recipients, whose hardships were often the greatest among clients. Retailers and lottery shop owners observed recipients' hardships every payment day, but faced bank and government policies that restricted their ability to do all that they wanted to do to help. Thus, the system placed them in a position to observe clients' hardships, arousing their empathy and their felt obligation to help, yet simultaneously constraining their ability to do so. For them, their heteromated labor was only a partial relief to the compulsion they felt to solve clients' problems and reduce their hardships, and their pleas to the banks to allow them to do more were typically met with refusal or inaction.

## 5.1. Heteromation as a labor relation

The correspondent banking system required for its success heteromated labor in the form of thinly compensated (small transaction fees for retailers and community bankers) or uncompensated (added as extra work for existing employees in post o<sup>fi</sup>ces and lottery shops) helping. From a critical approach, we wish to discuss whether the type of labor and the amount of e<sup>f</sup>ort it required might raise concerns for heteromation as a labor relation. This discussion will entail both objective assessments (e.g., in terms of estimated time to complete) and subjective assessments (in terms of correspondents' interpretations) of the labor provided. In addition, we need to discuss whether correspondents received bene<sup>fi</sup>ts beyond direct compensation for this labor that might count as some manner of reimbursement for it.

The cognitive labor that correspondents provided entailed little more than operating the keypad for clients or guiding them on it, acts that often took but a matter of seconds. However, this e<sup>f</sup>ort, when repeated over many clients over the course of a day, added up. Heteromated labor is important, in part, because of the scale it attains through aggregation of small moments of labor across many people and acts of labor, as Ekbia and Nardi (2017) noted. Moreover, because the system was not designed for this type of helping, this e<sup>f</sup>ort was more entailed than it needed to be: correspondents had to operate the separate client keypad in addition to their system interface rather than simply do all data entry through their interface. Correspondents' emotional labor further enabled system success. As compared to the cognitive labor they rendered, this labor took more time to enact (e.g., minutes to circumnavigate system policies, hours to provide advice, a week to attend credit analysis training) and had a greater emotional toll on correspondents, who felt the weight of clients' problems. Thus, the sum of labor provided by correspondents exacted a time toll, and often an emotional one, on them, making these e<sup>f</sup>orts non-trivial and warranting consideration for their compensation in a labor relation.

Correspondents across all four settings interpreted their provision of cognitive and emotional labor as arising out of empathy They tended to view their felt obligation to help in dispositional terms, contrasting their attitudes with those of bancarios, whom they described as cold and uncaring in their treatment of welfare recipients. If helping were simply dispositional, the imperative to compensate it may arguably be lessened, with helping its own reward. In fact, extensive research on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of tangible rewards on intrinsic motivation would suggest that compensating correspondents for this helping might reduce their desire to help (e.g., Deci, Koestner, & Ryan, 1999; Pavey, Greitemeyer, & Sparks, 2012; Weinstein & Ryan, 2010).

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, however, suggest that setting and contextual factors more so than disposition strongly shaped the sense of obligation to help. While bancarios felt excused from helping poor people by the creation of the correspondent banking system, which allowed them to focus on wealthy clients, correspondents saw poor peoples' hardships <sup>fi</sup>rsthand in their retail stores, community bank, post o<sup>fi</sup>ces, and lottery shops. When clients waited too long in line, for example, lottery shop correspondents were there to see them faint. Being co-present with clients and witnessing their distress lent correspondents a <sup>fi</sup>rsthand understanding of how their heteromated labor would help clients. As Constant, Sproull, and Kiesler (1996:121) noted, “People provide help to people they know, people they like, people who are similar, and people who have helped them…” There was clearly a strong social sense of solidarity with others (as evidenced in interviewees' use of the term and discussion of how they felt about others) that the correspondent banking system was able to leverage. Psychologists refer to this connection to the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries of prosocial behavior as a<sup>f</sup>ective commitment (Grant, 2007); scholars who study contributions to community-based resources often conceptualize this connection in terms of commitment to or identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the community (Kankanhalli et al., 2005; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Similarly, organizational scholars who stud citizenship behaviors talk in terms of commitment to the organization (Constant et al., 1996; O'Reilly & Chatman, 1986).

Research suggests that this connection may be important in explaining helping. For example, in early work, O'Reilly and Chatman (1986) found that identi<sup>fi</sup>cation was positively associated with extra-role behaviors such as helping new employees. Similarly, Chiu, Hsu, and Wang (2006) found that identi<sup>fi</sup>cation was positively associated with contributions to a knowledge depository, and McNeely and Meglino (1994) reported that empathy signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly explained prosocial behavior towards speci<sup>fi</sup>c people (as opposed to the organization). Post o<sup>fi</sup>ce and community bank correspondents exhibited this connection to clients most clearly: they were swayed by strong social justice sentiments, not unlike moral commitment (Perry, Hondeghem, & Wise, 2010). But retail store and lottery shop owners, who were <sup>fi</sup>rst and foremost business people, also came to feel this obligation. Thus, although research suggests that people in non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations are more likely to donate their labor or do unpaid overtime work than are people in for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rms (Gregg, Grout. Ratcliffe, Smith, & Windmejier, 2011), in our study, clients' hardships appeared so immediate and so great that correspondents across settings felt moved to help their community, again re<sup>fl</sup>ecting a strong sense of solidarity with others.

The correspondent banking system did not promote this a<sup>f</sup>ective commitment by design, but it set in place the structural conditions for its development (in the case of retail shops and lottery shops) or pro<sup>fi</sup>ted from a<sup>f</sup>ective commitment already in place (in the case of community banks and post o<sup>fi</sup>ces). Leveraging this a<sup>f</sup>ective commitment to prompt user helping achieved many laudable bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, including less travel hardship for welfare recipients, quick and local distribution of welfare bene<sup>fi</sup>t payments, new channels for banks doing business with poor clients, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial inclusion for remote and underserved people. Yet, the users whose a<sup>f</sup>ective commitment was so leveraged received mere cents for each transaction (cognitive labor) and no <sup>fi</sup>nancial compensation for their more considerable acts of emotional labor, In light of how bank policies often stymied correspondents. banks might compensate correspondents for their labor through shared governance of the correspondent banking system, for example by organizing councils of correspondents and bancarios. In this manner, they would acknowledge the wisdom and experience of the correspondents, potentially making the system better for clients (and, hence, the bank) and providing recognition of correspondents' e<sup>f</sup>orts and expertise. Under the current system, correspondents were not invited to venues of shared governance, despite their caring and their expertise about the needs and conditions of those they served.

One could argue that, through increased sales, retail stores and lottery shops were likely to reap <sup>fi</sup>nancial rewards from their heteromated labor. For example, although a 2010 report noted that retail store correspondents did about 166 transactions per day, for which they received from the bank only US\$4.34, 96% of respondents reported that losing their correspondent status would negatively impact their retail business (CGAP, 2010). In addition, retailers gained community status and recognition for the advice they lent and the problems that they helped to solve in mediating between institutions and clients. Our data are silent on whether correspondents felt these outcomes constituted su<sup>fi</sup>cient compensation for their labor; when pushed to explain why they performed the heteromated labor that they did, correspondents returned again and again to their felt obligation to local people.

## 5.2. Heteromation as a design strategy in ICT4D projects and beyond

Heteromation appears frequently in accounts of ICT4D projects. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, for projects in which service innovation is necessary for some societal bene<sup>fi</sup>t via improvements in industry, such as in agriculture and healthcare, yet existing resources or infrastructure are inadequate, some form of heteromation often appears to arise, although the accounts do not use that term (Barrett et al., 2015; Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015). For example, some authors refer to people who help others (thereby enabling system success) as intermediaries and do not make special note of the fact that this labor is heteromated (e.g., Sein & Furuholt, 2012: 332).

Table 3 provides four examples of studies published at the intersection of information systems and ICT4D that identify individuals or groups whose heteromated labor enabled systems to succeed. These studies illustrate a variety of settings, con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of helpers and the people they served, and incentives for helping, yet the studies all document designs of incomplete systems in which heteromated labor remedied incompleteness. For example, Andrade and Doolin (2016: 411) found that ICT use enhanced social inclusion of refugees in New Zealand by providing opportunities for refugees to “exercise their agency” and “enhance their participation in society and control over their circumstances.” Family members often played critical roles in facilitating ICT-enabled inclusion. In one example, an elderly Ethiopian man's children helped him maintain a sense of identity by helping him find videos in Ethiopia's official language (Amharic) online and otherwise helping him navigate ICT use. As in the case of Caixa and the corre spondent banking system, the designers of the systems in these four examples may not have intentionally drawn upon heteromation as a design strategy. Rather, heteromation may have simply arisen in practice, as it did in our study, as a means of enabling system success. Given these successful examples, however, future system designers may wish to contemplate heteromation as a design strategy.

To that end, our investigation of the correspondent banking system in Brazil prompts three pressing concerns about heteromation as a design strategy. Although we think these three concerns are most relevant for ICT4D projects, in which users are often separated from designers by signi<sup>fi</sup>cant geographic and socioeconomic distances and in which the objective for the designed system is often to address some social problem, the concerns may apply more broadly to other occasions of use, including those in developed countries where such conditions do not hold. However, critical studies of heteromated labor to date have focused on non-ICT4D systems (e.g., Andrejevic, 2015; Fuchs & Sevignani, 2013; van Dijck, 2009) and, in those contexts, have raised concerns centered on the valuation and payment of users' communicative labor (Ekbia & Nardi, 2017) when generating content on social networking sites. In viewing heteromation as a labor relation, these critical studies have included in their purview two groups: the producers of heteromated labor and the <sup>fi</sup>rms that appropriate it. By contrast, our concerns here revolve around the fact that three groups of actors, not two, are typically involved in heteromated labor in ICT4D projects: those who provide the labor (here, correspondents), those who appropriate the labor (in our case, the banks), and those on whose behalf the labor is done and who bene<sup>fi</sup>t from it (e.g., poor and underserved people). In other words, the concerns we raise here extend beyond the economic relations of helping to the socia relations of helping, and as such may pertain most directly to ICT4D projects.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst concern revolves around relations between people providing heteromated labor and system bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries. If in the course of heteromated labor one group of users comes to depend on another, such as remote or underserved Brazilians in our study relying on help from retailers to receive welfare payments or farmers in Jha, Pinsonneault, and Dubé's (2016: 436) study relying on help from “micro-entrepreneurs” to test soil quality, should those systems not also provide avenues for the helpers to address the dependent group's relevant other needs?

We saw such extra helping in our study when community bankers took the time to teach clients <sup>fi</sup>nancial literacy skills to make good <sup>fi</sup>nancial decisions, and retailers helped clients with <sup>fi</sup>nancial advice. Similarly, Venkatesh et al. (2016) saw it in their study of the implementation of healthcare kiosks for reducing infant mortality in rural India. Women attendants sta<sup>f</sup>ed and operated the kiosks for 16 h a day by design, with the intention that the attendants would assist women in overcoming low technical literacy to use the kiosks. Kiosk attendants expanded their roles beyond proxy use of the ICT to include advising mothers and expectant mothers against traditional, non-professional healing practices; this advice proved useful in producing positive outcomes when the women sought appropriate medical care (Venkatesh et al., 2016). Opportunities for expanded helping like that in these studies seem likely in ICT4D contexts.

However, such expanded helping may have its limits in current heteromated systems. For example, retailers and lottery shop owners wanted to enact additional helping behaviors on behalf of their clients, but system obstacles—such as cash limit policies and poor support of new <sup>fi</sup>nancial products—prevented them. Placed in the position of observing bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries' hardships and perceiving their needs, these correspondents lacked system functions that would have allowed greater helping to meet those needs. Designers, then, might consider granting helpers access to resources that would enable signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and broad helping

The second concern is that using heteromated systems as a design strategy in ICT4D projects runs the risk of reproducing and deepening class-based social relationships. Although correspondents in our study aided welfare payment distribution with their heteromated labor, thus producing a social good, in doing so they underwrote social segregation whereby bancarios served wealthy clients in beautiful, marble-laden banks while correspondents served poor people at much less grand retail stores, community banks, post o<sup>fi</sup>ces, and lottery shops. Bancarios noted this increased division of social classes with approval. Had correspondents not provided heteromated labor. the system might have failed. forcing banks to offer facilities to remote and underserved populations that resembled what they o<sup>f</sup>er their more a<sup>fl</sup>uent clients. In other contexts, designing for heteromation made the rich richer. For example, with the introduction of ICT-enabled information services to improve agricultural practices in rural India, micro-entrepreneurs, who alone could a<sup>f</sup>ord the upfront costs of laptops and software to bring agricultural information services to rural regions, increased their wealth by charging rural farmers membership fees to use the service (Jha et al., 2016). In short, heteromated systems in ICT4D projects may simultaneously provide service and disservice to the poor.

A third concern revolves around mandatory versus voluntary helping. Research on volitional versus mandated helping behaviors (Stukas, Snyder, & Clary, 1999) suggests that people who are initially uninclined to voluntarily help tend to be more open to future helping when they complete voluntary service compared to when helping is mandatory. Individuals who are already inclined to help are relatively una<sup>f</sup>ected by the di<sup>f</sup>erences in mandatory and voluntary helping. Accordingly, retailers' voluntary participation in th correspondent banking system indicated that they were likely to continue helping and perhaps to extend their helping behaviors—regardless of the volitional nature of participation. Similarly, community bank and post o<sup>fi</sup>ce correspondents had strong preexisting social commitment to their communities and therefore may have been una<sup>f</sup>ected by the mandate to help. Therefore, these correspondents may have experienced few rami<sup>fi</sup>cations from being required to help others. Weinstein and Ryan's (2010) work, however, suggests that helpers may have speci<sup>fi</sup>c, voluntary motivational characteristics for the helping act that determine the impact of helping on their own well-being, and therefore on future intentions to help. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings, in tandem with our study's results, suggest that correspondents in retail settings may have entered a self-reinforcing cycle by which the initial desire to help led to the desire to take on more and more helping behaviors. Community bank and post o<sup>fi</sup>ce correspondents, on the other hand, felt compelled to help and may not have entered into a similar cycle. These studies raise the possibility that, by expecting helping behaviors, heteromated systems may have negative outcomes for helpers and bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries.

Beyond prompting future, additional helping behaviors, voluntary helping in designed heteromated systems may also provide opportunities for career and social growth for groups. Mandatory helping, by contrast, might place new dependencies on workers who otherwise fail to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the system's success. Retail correspondents acknowledged that they enjoyed elevated status due to being in a position to help the system, whereas community bank correspondents and post o<sup>fi</sup>ce correspondents already held a helping position in the community. Similarly, Leong, Pan, Newell, and Cui (2016) documented how villagers took on new leadership roles in their communities by setting up logistical plans to support an online business startup program in rural China. Designers might consider these potential outcomes when using heteromation as a design strategy, avoiding the potential pitfall called out by Majchrzak, Markus, and Wareham (2016: 273): “Researchers often have a pro-ICT bias and subconsciously veer away from acknowledging ICT-related harms; the e<sup>f</sup>ects of ICT use can sometimes be ill for some stakeholders, while also being good for others.

Heteromation is likely to persist as a design strategy in ICT4D because it provides a way to combat de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in material and knowledge resources by leveraging already-situated intermediaries who may help system bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries and thus enable system success. In particular, programs promoting <sup>fi</sup>nancial and social inclusion stand as testament to the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of designing for heteromation in ICT4D. Our study, in which correspondent bankers help remote and underserved people receive welfare payments and carry out banking transactions, illustrates this point in the case of <sup>fi</sup>nancial inclusion. To best achieve these ends, designers wil need to take into consideration the types of concerns we raise here.

## 5.3. Heteromation as a counterpoint to automation

We are told that “jobs” are going away because of automation, yet the putatively automated banking transactions we examined only reached their intended populations by virtue of human labor. Heteromation raises questions about whether economically valuable labor will continue to be invisibilized—will it be deployed in the production of wealth but submerged from view, eluding appropriate compensation and worker protections?

Automation appears to suggest that the labor theory of value no longer holds, or is much less important, but heteromation indicates that while labor relations are shifting, the necessity of labor to value production is in no way disappearing. Given how central the helping behaviors of heteromated labor were to the success of correspondent banking, we argue that new ethical systems must be fashioned to justly reward people for the fruits of their labor. Indeed, we were struck by the depth of caring and kindness evinced in much of the heteromated labor we observed—human qualities that develop over a lifetime, irreproducible as “training” or “best practices.” If such cultural richness is deployed in service of pro<sup>fi</sup>t-making institutions, it should be recognized and rewarded.

## 5.4. Future research

Future research might examine the ways that prominent societal discourse shapes how heteromation is viewed and how heteromation itself may alter discourse. For example, current discourse around arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence, algorithms, and robotics holds that these technologies will prompt mass unemployment (Brynjolfsson & McAfee, 2014), presumably leaving little room for people to help systems. Yet, recognizing that even simple information systems require heteromated labor (as in the case of correspondent banking) could shift the discourse to acknowledge this labor and design for it. We see early evidence of this shift as researchers have begun to employ heteromation as a useful concept for analyzing work with these technologies. For example, Raval and Dourish (2016) invoked heteromation in their study of novel labor arrangements in the ridesharing industry and Thakor (2017) described heteromated labor in digital image forensics in child exploitation cases. Similarly, in a study of how the social web is in<sup>fl</sup>uencing archaeology, Perry and Beale (2015) drew on heteromation to caution archaeologists against forfeiting unpaid time to laborious online tasks. Other applications of heteromation include explorations of how designers might incorporate human roles into robotics (Ekbia, Nardi, & Sabanovic, 2015) and workshops on how algorithms “facilitate, regulate, and require human labor” (Wagenknecht, Lee, Lustig, O'Neill, & Zade, 2016: 536). At a higher level, new design perspectives, such as Raghavan and Pargman's (2017) sustainable HCI with disintermediation, draw upon heteromation to provide models of how to conduct design that take into account labor economics. Research might thus explore how heteromation forces discourse on new technologies to account for human helping and perhaps, more broadly, to purposefully make room for it.

In the context of design, we have demonstrated the usefulness of the concepts of heteromation and helping in one setting—- correspondent banking. Many similar settings may exist in which humans who are close to intended system users are much more likely than designers to identify, understand, and help meet user needs. These settings appear ripe for heteromated design. Such settings likely arise not only in many ICT4D projects, but in projects in developed countries that provide services for people who are at-risk, under-resourced, or otherwise vulnerable or disadvantaged. For example, researchers might focus their attention on system design for elderly, poor, homeless, disabled, sick, or child populations to ensure that new systems, in serving one population, do not exploit another.

## 6. Conclusion

In our study, we considered heteromation as a labor relation and as a design strategy for information systems. Drawing upon a case study of the Brazilian correspondent banking system, we found that users helped the system by providing two types of heteromated labor: cognitive and emotional. Contextual factors in the form of correspondents' relationship to banks as well as bank and government policies shaped the extent of users' helping by setting; participation status (voluntary or mandatory) was less active in this shaping. One limitation of our study lay in not interviewing welfare recipients and other clients of the correspondent banking system, rendering us unable to speak of the outcomes of helping on the system's bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries.
