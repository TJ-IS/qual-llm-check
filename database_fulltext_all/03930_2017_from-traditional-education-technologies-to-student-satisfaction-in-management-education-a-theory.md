## Abstract

Understanding how information technology (IT) resources create value in Management education requires new and more powerful theories. This research examines the impact of IT resources on Management education using the case method. We theorize that traditional education technologies enable instructors to engage students to increase learning performance, which in turn leads to greater student satisfaction, and that social media applications can amplify these relationships. The empirical analysis, partial least squares path modeling performed on survey and secondary data from 94 Spanish students in a Management course, supports our theory.

Keywords: Traditional education technologies, social media applications, student engagement, learning performance, student satisfaction.

#

## Introduction

Information technology (IT) is changing the way firms and individuals interact and exchange information (Benitez and Walczuch, 2012; Amrit et al., 2015; Ngai et al., 2015; Soto et al., 2016). This change may explain why the estimated global IT investment will be 3.5 trillion dollars in 2017 (Gartner, 2016). With the expansion of Internet to deliver course content, the mode of teaching and interacting with students has also changed (Brower, 2003). In a digital environment, educational organizations and Management scholars should determine what IT resources to use and how to leverage them to improve student learning and satisfaction.

We distinguish between two types of IT resources for potential use in Management education: traditional education technologies and social media applications. Traditional education technologies are more conventional and less interactive course-specific platforms (e.g., Moodlebased teaching platform, email, instructor’s website). Social media applications refer to external social media such as Facebook, Twitter, and YouTube, which are very interactive and popular among students.

IT resources can be used to improve and complement traditional (i.e., face-to-face) lectures. Traditional education technologies enable students to connect with the course any time any place (e.g., Moodle-based teaching platforms). Their main goal is to facilitate communication and exchange of material among instructor and students.

Social media applications also affect teaching activities. Some scholars highlight the negative influence of social media on teaching activities. Students dislike privacy invasion and can be reluctant to share their ideas with instructors through social media applications (Gonzalez et al., 2015). Social media applications can also be time consuming and may contain distractions such as advertisements, warnings, suggestions, or games, which could cause attention deficit, harming the student’s academic performance (Gonzalez et al., 2016). These distractions may be problematic if social media applications are used in class.

Other scholars suggest a positive influence of social media applications on teaching activities (Kane and Fichman, 2009; Daspit and D’Souza, 2012; Tess, 2013). Consistent with this second stream of literature, we argue that social media applications can be leveraged to improve the learning process. Social media applications can be used out of class (before and after class) to engage students and leverage their additional functionalities by taking advantage of their powerful characteristics: user-friendliness, flexibility, closeness, and providing enjoyment. For example, Facebook is perceived by students as an effective learning tool that facilitates interactive discussion, and Twitter is efficient for maximizing time spent per message, providing prompt feedback, and promoting active learning (Tess, 2013).

Prior research on Information Systems (IS) and Management Learning focuses mainly on the comparison of the outcomes between online and classroom-based courses (Arbaugh and Benbunan, 2007) and on the effectiveness of online teaching activities (Brower, 2003; Xu et al., 2014), but prior research on the role of information technologies in classroom and blended learning in Management education has been limited. Kane and Fichman (2009) conceptualize how wiki technology can be used as a collaborative platform to generate teaching activity content. Drawing on Kane and Fichman’s (2009) conceptualization, we explore the role of traditional education technologies and social media applications in blended learning<sup>1</sup> Management courses using the case method.

##

To the best of our knowledge, the role of different IT resources in blended learning Management education in the case method context has received little study. The case method is very useful in Management learning as students can emulate a managerial position and make decisions about real-world corporate problems. The case method is the dominant teaching method for developing managerial skills among participants in the most admired Business Schools in the world (e.g., Harvard Business School). This study aims to answer the following research questions: How do IT resources affect student learning performance and satisfaction in a blended learning Management course? Does complementary use of social media applications amplify the impact of traditional education technologies on student learning performance and satisfaction?

We examine the impact of traditional education technologies and social media applications on Management education using the case method. Our central thesis is that traditional education technologies enable instructors to engage students to increase student learning performance, which in turn can lead to a greater student satisfaction. We theorize that use of social media applications before and after class can amplify/complement the relationships among traditional education technologies, student engagement, learning performance, and student satisfaction. The proposed theory is tested by performing partial least squares (PLS) path modeling on a sample of 94 undergraduate Spanish students in a Management course using the case method. An initial examination shows that the empirical analysis provides strong support for the proposed theory.

Our study theorizes, explains theoretically, and demonstrates empirically how traditional education technologies increase student satisfaction by enabling student engagement and improving learning performance. Social media applications reinforce this sequence of effects in Management education using the case method. This result is the primary theoretical contribution of the paper, which is organized as follows. Next, we present the proposed model and develop the hypotheses. The third and fourth sections explain the research methodology, and empirical analysis and results, respectively. We then present the discussion and conclusions.

## Theory and hypotheses

Technological acceptance model, unified theory of acceptance and use of technology, and community of inquiry framework

Technology adoption theories can help to explain the effect of different factors of traditional education technologies and social media applications on learning outcomes. The technology acceptance model (TAM) explains how users accept and use a technology. Its goal is to predict and explain user acceptance of technology by focusing on two key constructs: perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (Davis, 1989). Perceived usefulness occurs when an individual believes that using a specific technology will enhance his/her performance. Perceived ease of use occurs when an individual perceives that the technology can be used easily, without effort.

The unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) extends the TAM, which postulates performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions as four determinants of user acceptance and behavior (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Performance expectancy is the degree to which an individual believes that using the technology will help him/her to achieve performance gains. Effort expectancy is the level of ease related to the use of technology. Three constructs from prior TAM models comprise the concept of effort expectancy: perceived ease of use, complexity (degree to which a system is perceived as relatively difficult to understand and use), and ease of use. Social influence is the degree of influence other people exert on an individual in using a new technology. Facilitating conditions are the technical infrastructure that supports the new technology (Al-Gahtani et al., 2007).

#

We draw on TAM/UTAUT to explain indirectly how use of traditional education technologies and social media applications can improve student engagement, learning performance, and learning satisfaction (Cao et al., 2013). Students use traditional education technologies because they think these technologies are an easy way to obtain learning outcomes (perceived ease of use, effort expectancy), which facilitates student engagement, performance, and satisfaction (Hu et al., 2003; Venkatesh et al., 2003; Al-Gahtani et al., 2007; Cao et al., 2013). Social media applications provide a useful (perceived usefulness, performance expectancy) and enjoyable environment that encourages students to use the technology to interact with others for learning activities, which in turn can amplify student engagement in and satisfaction with the course. We draw on this framework to link traditional education technologies and student engagement and to explain theoretically the potential moderating effects of social media applications in the proposed model.

The community of inquiry framework provides the key elements needed to explain a successful educational experience theoretically. This framework describes how complementary factors (teaching presence, social presence, and cognitive presence) enable student learning in online environments. The community of inquiry model assumes that learning occurs through the interaction of teaching presence, social presence, and cognitive presence. Teaching presence is the instructor’s ability to facilitate discourse, engage in interactions, and give instructions in virtual environments (e.g., presenting course content or designing learning activities through Moodle-based teaching platforms) (Redpath, 2012). The instructor’s presence (e.g., by sending short messages to guide activities or encourage students to participate through Twitter before/after class) can increase student activity. As tools to design the educational experience through interaction between instructor and students, traditional education technologies and social media applications facilitate teaching presence.

Social presence indicates the perception of openness and communication that facilitates interpersonal communication (student–student and instructor–student interactions) (Redpath, 2012). It facilitates participants’ ability to experience educational immersion in the learning process (Rodriguez and Meseguer, 2016). Social presence is easily achieved in an interactive and social environment (e.g., through social media applications) in which collaborative activities take place (Niehaves and Ortbach, 2016).

Cognitive presence is the basic element of successful learning as it refers to construction of knowledge through communication (Arbaugh and Benbunan, 2006; Cheung and Lee, 2010). Cognitive presence is the student’s ability to absorb and create new knowledge through successful communication (Redpath, 2012). As social media applications are characterized as tools that facilitate collaboration and communication, it is logical to think that knowledge can be built by using them before and after class. The main goal of traditional education technologies is to facilitate communication and material exchange between instructor and students. As a result, knowledge can be built through such communication and material exchange.

The elements of the community of inquiry can enhance learning outcomes (e.g., student engagement, learning performance, and student satisfaction) because teaching presence, social presence, and cognitive presence help to maximize learning because of student involvement through participation, interaction, and contributions during the course. We draw on the community of inquiry framework to explain indirectly the engagement outcome obtained using traditional education technologies and the complementary role of social media in student engagement, learning performance, and student satisfaction.

## IT resources in Management education

IT resources were classified based on both several brainstorming sessions with the students and prior literature on business value of IT and Management Learning (e.g., Melville et al., 2004; Tess, 2013; Benitez et al., 2016a). We differentiate two types of IT resources for potential use in Management education: traditional education technologies and social media applications. Traditional education technologies are the information technologies that enable students to relate to the course any time any place (Limayem and Cheung, 2008). These technologies are more conventional and less interactive course-specific platforms (e.g., Moodle-based teaching platforms, email, instructor’s website), and they are provided internally by the Business School, primarily to facilitate more formal communication between instructor and students (Rehm et al., 2016). Use of traditional education technologies is mandatory for instructors and students in some Business Schools. The most popular traditional education technologies are Moodle-based teaching platforms, which are based on Internet technology (Limayem and Cheung, 2008).

Social media applications refer to external social media such as Facebook, Twitter, Pinterest, LinkedIn, and Google+, which are highly interactive and very popular among our students<sup>2</sup>. While social media applications are mainly used for communicating with other people, they may also be used for teaching activities as these applications facilitate collaboration and connection with/among students (Tess, 2013) before and after class. They may thus be applied to Management education in the context of a course (Kane and Fichman, 2009; Daspit and D’Souza, 2012). The main difference between traditional education technologies and social media applications is technology driven. Social media applications provide real-time communication, helping to create a highly interactive online space through which participants can create, share, exchange, or discuss content about the course in a timely manner. Traditional education technologies do not usually stimulate high levels of interaction among participants, however. ## Traditional education technologies and student engagement

Student engagement refers to the degree and intensity of a student’s emotional involvement and commitment and motivation to collaborate, participate, and contribute during the course activities (Arbaugh and Benbunan, 2007; Brodie et al., 2013; Li et al., 2013).

Using traditional education technologies as a complement to traditional classroom can enable greater student engagement in the course. First, the instructor can use traditional education technologies to provide the course syllabus and teaching material and update course information for the students (i.e., instructor presence). Students will be more engaged in the course if teaching material is provided beforehand through traditional education technologies (Yang et al., 2007; Taras et al., 2013) and if they receive critical information on the course in an agile way through IT (Brower, 2003). For example, Moodle-based teaching platforms and the instructor’s website are suitable tools through which students can access the course contents.

Second, traditional education technologies are suitable tools for organizing teaching material and quickly answering questions about the course (e.g., by email). Students can evaluate positively the instructor’s effort to provide teaching materials in a structured way or to answer questions through traditional education technologies, which encourage the student and involve him/her in the course. Finally, web technologies provide students with more learning flexibility to work from home at their own pace (Arbaugh and Benbunan, 2007). Keeping in touch any time any place with the instructor by traditional education technologies such as email or the instructor’s website provides the student with greater flexibility and freedom to work and be engaged. Students may view flexibility as a benefit for effective time management, which may lead to greater autonomy and responsibility for their learning to increase student engagement. We therefore hypothesize,

Hypothesis 1 (H1): There is a positive relationship between traditional education technologies and student engagement.

## Student engagement and learning performance

Learning performance is the extent to which students achieve the course learning goals in terms of knowledge acquisition, understanding key concepts, and developing managerial skills (Alavi et al., 1997). Student engagement can increase learning performance. If the student feels a sense of commitment to the learning process, his/her knowledge assimilation improves. The greater the student’s involvement, the greater is the probability that the student will achieve better learning performance. First, engaged students collaborate more and better, and create new experiences in class, improving their knowledge acquisition (Lin and Shao, 2000; Hwang and Francesco, 2010; Li et al., 2012). Motivated students explore, construct, and absorb higher levels of learning when they feel they are part of the learning process (Daspit and D’Souza, 2012), and they realize the quality and importance of what they learn. Such results are even more important when the case method is used as the student emulates a top managerial position to cope with business real problems.

Second, as motivated students want to make the most of the course, they believe from the start that they can achieve superior learning effectively by the end of the course (so-called Pygmalion effect). Based on this effect, instructors who like more engaged students tend to create a more positive climate for them, interacting with them more frequently, and giving them more/better feedback. These differences in the instructor’s behavior result in a Pygmalion effect as the students to whom the instructor responds more positively because of their greater engagement learn more (Armstrong et al., 2004; Crossan et al., 2013).

Finally, student engagement increases students’ control over their learning activities, which in turn improves their learning performance (Redpath, 2012). When students are involved in the course, they improve their learning outcomes. For example, in facing decision-making processes with the case method, students feel they are part of the process of learning/goal completion, which enhances their engagement to increase learning performance. We therefore hypothesize,

Hypothesis 2 (H2): There is a positive relationship between student engagement and learning performance.

## Learning performance and student satisfaction

Student satisfaction refers to the degree to which the student’s expectations about the instructor, course, and teaching method are met. Definitions of the key constructs are summarized in Table 1. Learning performance can increase student satisfaction. Students will be satisfied to the extent that they acquire and assimilate new knowledge, understand critical management concepts, learn to identify central topics, make managerial decisions, and solve key business problems. Such satisfaction depends on the instructor’s performance, good course design, and/or choice of appropriate teaching method (Chiu et al., 2007). It is thus reasonable to expect that the student’s degree of learning is positively related to the student’s satisfaction. We therefore hypothesize,

Hypothesis 3 (H3): There is a positive relationship between learning performance and student satisfaction.

Amplifying role of social media applications in the relationship between traditional education technologies and student engagement

We argue that the relationship between traditional education technologies and student engagement will be stronger if the instructor persuades students to use social media applications before and after class; that is, social media applications perform an amplifying role in this relationship. Social media applications are more user-friendly, flexible, agile, newer, and closer to students than traditional education technologies (Greasley et al., 2004).

Social media applications contain functionalities that allow instructors to facilitate discussion and create an environment of openness and communication that facilitates social interaction and communication among students (i.e., social presence) (Daspit and D’Souza, 2012; Tess, 2013). These superior functionalities enabled by social media applications clearly increase the opportunity to leverage traditional education technologies to create a sense of students’ commitment to being part of the learning process, thus increasing student engagement (Teo et al., 2011; Barnett et al., 2015). Complementary use of social media applications can thus increase the quality and quantity of student engagement. For example, many students do not read all the information the instructor uploads to the traditional education technologies (e.g., Moodle-based teaching platforms). Facebook or WhatsApp are efficient tools to communicate informally and get last-minute information from other students. Similarly, Twitter can add significant value to engage students in the context of the case-method-based online discussion before and after class. The instructor can use Twitter to run additional questions prior to the case discussion class, or after class. Twitter can also be used to recognize the best participants in the case discussion.

Finally, students can use social media applications to stay informed about course activities and to be part of the group. For example, student D participates in the after-class discussion created by the instructor in Twitter because all his/her friends (A, B, and C) participate too (i.e., social influence). In sum, social media applications play a key complementary role in student engagement because they are more user-friendly and provide additional functionalities and more opportunities to engage students and to reinforce the student ties with the group, which can be even more important in when using the case method. Hence, student engagement enabled by traditional education technologies will be stronger when the instructor also uses social media tools in the course. We therefore hypothesize,

Hypothesis 4 (H4): Social media applications positively amplify the relationship between traditional education technologies and student engagement.

Amplifying role of social media applications in the relationship between student engagement and learning performance

We argue that the relationship between student engagement and learning performance can also be amplified by using social media applications before and after class. Social interrelatedness enabled by social media applications can promote student collaborative learning in online environments in the way students freely express their opinions and experiences (Brower, 2003; Daspit and D’Souza, 2012; Taras et al., 2013). Learning happens when instructors and students confront different points of view and exchange, share, and integrate knowledge (cognitive presence) (Pan et al., 2015). Interactive technologies such as social media engage more students in the learning process as they enable additional monitoring of student progress (Arenas et al., 2012), which can lead to better learning. The effect of student engagement on learning performance can thus be positively amplified if the instructor encourages use of social media applications before and after class.

In a course using the case method, the instructor can engage students in the learning process by providing key interesting cases (e.g., Benihana of Tokyo, Earl 2004). The instructor can also provide students with additional material about the case’s firm (e.g., videos related to the firm’s operations management) via social media (e.g., Facebook, Twitter, YouTube) so that students become familiar with the firm. For example, because it is very difficult for a student in Spain (or other countries where Benihana does not have restaurants) to imagine Benihana’s operations if he/she has not visited the restaurant, the instructor can provide the Benihana profile video via YouTube (https://www.youtube.com/watch?feature=player\_embedded& $\mathrm { v = e p o I P g Q r A y Q ) }$ . The instructor thus uses social media to bring students closer to the firm by providing unique/complementary teaching material, reinforcing their motivation in the learning process.

Social media applications are tools well suited to provide more thoughtful student comments, encourage shy students to participate, and avoid monopolization of dominant/leader students (Brower, 2003; Redpath, 2012; Templeton et al., 2012), a potential problem in case discussion sessions with many attendees. Engaged students can use social media applications in the virtual/social case discussion as an additional opportunity to participate before and after class in order to improve their learning performance (Hwang and Francesco, 2010). For example, the instructor can use Twitter to run additional questions before and after class discussion to give all students the opportunity to participate after a crowded session.

Social media applications can provide a useful and enjoyable environment that encourages students to interact with others in the learning community, which in turn can amplify the impact of student engagement on learning performance. Social media applications thus amplify the impact of student engagement on learning performance by providing more enjoyment. In sum, social media applications amplify the relationship between student engagement and learning performance because social media (1) is a very good platform for idea interchange, (2) provides unique/complementary teaching material and more opportunities to convert student motivation into student learning, and (3) provides enjoyment to students. Based on this discussion, we hypothesize,

Hypothesis 5 (H5): Social media applications positively amplify the relationship between student engagement and learning performance.

## Amplifying role of social media applications in the relationship between learning performance and student satisfaction

We also argue that the relationship between learning performance and student satisfaction can be stronger in presence of social media applications; that is, social media applications can perform a positive amplifying role. Social media applications enable greater interaction among students and instructor (social presence). Students experience socio-emotional benefits from the onlinelearning community to the extent that they experience less isolation in the learning process (Arbaugh and Benbunan, 2007; Arenas et al., 2012). As complex tasks are perceived as easier with the support of the community (instructor and other students), more interactions between student–student and instructor–student through social media applications can reinforce the student satisfaction generated through learning (Arbaugh and Benbunan, 2007). Social media applications are flexible tools considered as useful for resolving doubts and saving time and hence for creating a positive attitude toward the course.

Use of social media applications provides the instructor with additional platforms to acknowledge and congratulate students on their learning outcomes, which can also amplify the relationship between learning performance and student satisfaction. For example, the instructor can use Twitter to recognize the class for doing a good job once final grades are in and to congratulate the best student(s) in class. Merely providing these tweets does not create student satisfaction, but once the student has learned, running these tweets can amplify his/her learningdriven satisfaction. Finally, social media applications give the student more enjoyment, amplifying the positive effect of student learning on satisfaction. In sum, social media applications decrease isolation and increase flexibility and time saving, providing instructors with additional platforms to acknowledge and congratulate students and students with additional enjoyment. These results in turn can amplify the impact of learning performance on student satisfaction. Hence, we hypothesize,

Hypothesis 6 (H6): Social media applications positively amplify the relationship between learning performance and student satisfaction.

## Research methodology

## Sample and data

Since the purpose of the study is to test the proposed theory in a case method context, we selected the sample from students taking a Management course in which the case method was prevalent. The proposed theory was tested based on a combination of survey and secondary data from 94 Spanish students in a 60-hour Management course offered at the authors’ University. This course was taught through lectures (30 hours) combined with the case method (30 hours). In addition, the instructor used, and gave the students the possibility of using, several traditional education technologies (e.g., Moodle-based teaching platforms, email) and social media applications (e.g., Twitter) to execute and develop the coursework and interact with/among students. Use of the social media applications was voluntary for the students enrolled in the course.

The case method is very popular and useful in Management education as students can emulate a managerial position and make decisions about real-world corporate problems. The case method is the dominant teaching method for developing managerial skills among participants in the most admired Business Schools in the world (e.g., Harvard Business School). Instructors carefully select globally recognized business cases to generate a discussion environment in class. Cases are straightforward narratives that end with a top executive of the company asking questions about the business. They propose real business problems for which no answers are given. Students’ tasks consist of taking the role of a top executive and collaboratively developing decision-making responses after thinking up a solution to the problem posed (Ellet, 2007). The main purpose of the case method is to develop managerial skills in students. Based on the prominent role of the case method in Management education worldwide and its impact on the development of managerial skills in students, this method seemed an appropriate context for this study.

First, we performed several brainstorming sessions with the students to obtain an overall understanding of the role of IT in the course, especially from the students’ perspective. We then carefully designed a questionnaire, wherever possible, by adapting scales from prior research on IS and Management Learning. We pre-tested the questionnaire with four faculty members, seven doctoral students, and eight undergraduate students to improve the items’ clarity and comprehensibility. The questionnaire plus a cover letter with instructions was then given to students in the last session of the course (June 2015). Of the total of 156 students enrolled in the course (three groups), 94 valid responses were obtained, which constitutes a response rate of 60.256%. This response rate can be considered satisfactory as it implies a response rate of about 90% of the students who actively and regularly attended the course sessions and is consistent with response rates obtained in prior literature (e.g., Arbaugh and Benbunan, 2007). The average age of the respondent was 24 (S.D. = 4.951), ranging from 20 to 44 with a median of 22 years; 62% of the respondents were female.

Prior to the data collection, we performed a statistical power analysis to determine the minimum sample size required to estimate the proposed model. Assuming an anticipated effect size of 0.250, a desired statistical power level of 0.800, six predictors (number of links received by the construct student satisfaction), and confidence level of 0.95, the minimum required sample size to estimate the model is 72 (Cohen, 1988). Our sample size was 94, which was adequate to estimate the proposed model. This analysis suggested that our study had sufficient statistical power to detect the effects of interests. Moreover, Henseler et al. (2014) demonstrate that confirmatory composite analysis can detect various forms of model misspecification at a sample size of about 100.

## Measures

Traditional education technologies and social media applications are first-order constructs measured by adapting the scale of Lu et al. (2013). Traditional education technologies assess the extent to which students used traditional education technologies (Moodle-based teaching platforms, email, instructor’s website, laptop) for learning activities. Lu et al. (2013) include several questions about frequency of use of different traditional technologies and social media applications. For example, our item “I have used email in the course” is based on Lu et al.’s (2013) item “How frequently do you use email in blackboard?” Social media applications assess the student’s level of use of external social media platforms (Facebook, Twitter, Pinterest, LinkedIn, Google+) to execute course activities. We focused on these five social media platforms based on (1) prior literature on IS and Management Learning (e.g., Everson et al., 2013; Lu et al., 2013; Tess, 2013) that consider the potential role of Facebook, Twitter, and LinkedIn for educational purposes (e.g., promoting active participation or self-learning) and (2) students in the brainstorming sessions who considered Pinterest and Google+ as useful complementary tools for learning activities. The absence of a scale to measure student engagement led us to create our own measurement scale. On the basis of works with similar ideas in different contexts (Arbaugh and Benbunan, 2007; Brodie et al., 2013; Li et al., 2013), we created a three-indicator scale to measure student engagement.

We measured learning performance by adapting the scales of Alavi et al. (1997) and Daspit and D’Souza (2012). Student satisfaction is specified as a composite second-order construct determined by satisfaction with the instructor, satisfaction with the course, and satisfaction with the teaching method. The scale for satisfaction with the instructor was newly developed for this study. Satisfaction with the course is based on the scale of Arbaugh and Benbunan (2007). Satisfaction with the teaching method is measured by adapting the scales of Alavi et al. (1997), Arenas et al. (2012), and Daspit and D’Souza (2012). All these first-order constructs are specified as composite (Ajamieh et al., 2016; Benitez et al., 2017; Henseler, 2017).

The empirical analysis results may change as older students and students with low prior IT skills may be less pleased with IT use. Gender difference and prior perception of the instructor’s reputation may also determine final student satisfaction with the course. Our study therefore controls for the influence of student age, student gender, prior IT student skills, and instructor reputation (e.g., Arbaugh and Benbunan, 2007) on student satisfaction. We compute student age as the natural logarithm of the student’s age (Chen et al., forthcoming), student gender as a dummy variable (0: Male, 1: Female), prior student IT skills as a two-indicator scale that measures prior student skills in using IT (Arbaugh and Benbunan, 2007), and instructor reputation as a two-indicator scale that assesses perceived reputation of the instructor in terms of teaching quality and equity in grading at the beginning of the course.

## Common method variance

Our model is composite-formative at first- and second-order levels. “Formative indicators are assumed to be error-free (Bollen, 1989: 222-223), and thus incompatible with data that can contain common method variance” (Ronkko and Ylitalo 2011, p. 1). It is thus unlikely that a composite model suffers from common method bias.

## Empirical analysis

We perform PLS path modeling to test the hypotheses and to examine the mediation effects involved in the proposed model. PLS is a highly appropriate choice for the estimation method for

##

the following reasons. First, PLS is a full-fledged SEM approach that can test for exact model fit and be used for confirmatory research (Henseler et al., 2016; Benitez et al., 2017). Second, it is an optimal method to estimate composite models (Chen et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015; Benitez et al., 2017). Third, compared with covariance-based SEM techniques, PLS provides better estimations of complex models with multidimensional constructs (Au et al., 2008; Hair et al., 2012). Finally, PLS is advisable when some model constructs use newly developed scales, as in this study (e.g., Tiwana and Konsynski, 2010). We used the statistical software package Advanced Analysis for Composites (ADANCO) 2.0.0 Professional for Windows (http://www.composite-modeling.com/) (Henseler and Dijkstra, 2015). ADANCO is modern software for variance-based SEM. It models composites, common factors, and single-indicator constructs and facilitates causal and predictive modeling. ## Confirmatory composite analysis

There are two types of formative measurements: composite-formative and causal-formative (see Henseler, 2017). In composite-formative, (1) the indicators make up the construct, (2) high correlations among the indicators are common but not required, (3) the indicators do not involve measurement error, and (4) dropping an indicator alters the composite and may change its meaning. In contrast, in causal-formative measurement, (1) the indicators cause the construct, (2) correlations between indicators are not expected, (3) there is measurement error at construct level, and (4) dropping an indicator increases measurement error on the construct level (Henseler, 2017). Causal-formative measurement models require estimation of a complementary reflective measurement model (Henseler, 2017). All constructs of the proposed model are specified as composite-formative (in short, composite) constructs. The composite construct serves as proxy

#

for the concept under investigation. The composite model can be understood as a recipe for how ingredients (indicators/dimensions or measures) should be mixed and matched to form the composite (Henseler, 2015). Selection of the ingredients thus also represents how an author team understands the concept (composite) under investigation.

We checked for external validity of our composite constructs by performing confirmatory composite analysis (Henseler et al., 2014). This analysis can detect incorrect assignment of indicators to constructs or wrong number of constructs (i.e., model misspecification) and detect the accuracy of the structure of composite measurements (Henseler et al., 2014; Benitez et al., 2017). In performing the confirmatory composite analysis, we evaluate goodness of fit of the saturated model (i.e., a model that enables free correlation among the measurements) at first- and second-order levels (Table 2). We thus examine the standardized root mean squared residual (SRMR), unweighted least squares (ULS) discrepancy $\operatorname { \rho } ( \operatorname { d } _ { \mathrm { U L S } } )$ , and geodesic discrepancy (d<sub>G</sub>) to evaluate goodness of saturated model fit (Henseler et al., 2014; Henseler and Dijkstra, 2015). These measures of goodness of fit evaluate the discrepancy between the empirical correlation matrix and the model-implied correlation matrix (Benitez and Ray, 2012; Henseler, 2015; Benitez et al., 2016b). The lower they are, the better is the fit of the proposed model (Henseler and Dijkstra, 2015). As all values of the discrepancies are below the 95%-quantile of the bootstrap discrepancies $\mathrm { ( H I _ { 9 5 } }$ values), the measurement model should not be rejected based on the alpha level of 0.05, which means that we can ensure with a probability of 5% that the measurement structure of our composite constructs is correct. We can thus proceed to evaluate the specific properties of our composite constructs.

Measurement model evaluation

##

Traditional criteria to evaluate the measurement properties of reflective constructs (reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity) are not appropriate for formative constructs (Petter et al., 2007; Cenfetelli and Bassellier, 2009). We check for content validity, multicollinearity, weights, loadings, and their significance level for the composite constructs (Cenfetelli and Bassellier, 2009; Benitez and Ray, 2012). We test content validity of all constructs included in our study wherever possible by using scales validated in prior research (Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006; Wang et al., 2015). After collecting the data, we test for multicollinearity by calculating the indicator variance inflation factors (VIFs). The indicator VIFs for our constructs range from 1.016 to 3.757, well below the accepted threshold of 10, suggesting that multicollinearity is not a problem in our constructs (Benitez and Ray, 2012). After checking multicollinearity, we should retain a composite indicator when the weight or loading is significant (Petter et al., 2007; Cenfetelli and Bassellier, 2009). Composite indicators/dimensions whose weight and loading are non-significant can be retained, however, at discretion of the author team to preserve construct content validity (Cenfetelli and Bassellier, 2009). For one indicator in the traditional education technologies construct for email use, neither the weight nor the loading is significant. As email was widely used and relevant during the course, we decided to keep this indicator to preserve the content validity of the construct as avoiding the composite changes its meaning (Henseler, 2017).

Since student satisfaction is a second-order construct, we estimate the proposed model by performing the two-step approach (Chin, 2010). In the first step, we freely correlate all first-order constructs and the dimensions of the second-order construct (i.e., satisfaction with instructor, satisfaction with course, and satisfaction with teaching method) to obtain the latent variables’ scores for the dimensions. In the second step, the latent variables’ scores for the dimensions are used as the measures of the construct student satisfaction (Wang et al., 2015). Overall, these analyses suggest that our composite constructs have very good measurement properties. Table A2 (in the appendix) provides detailed information on the measurement model properties.

We thus examined the path coefficients, significance level, $\mathsf { R } ^ { 2 } ,$ and $\mathsf { f } ^ { 2 }$ values to test the hypotheses. The path coefficients, their level of significance, and the $\mathsf { R } ^ { 2 }$ values are individual measures of the model structural quality and explanatory power. Path coefficients around 0.200 are considered economically significant (Benitez and Ray, 2012). The main path coefficients in our model range from $0 . 1 9 3 ^ { * }$ to $0 . 4 9 4 ^ { ^ { \ast \ast \ast } }$ , becoming significant at the 0.05 level. $\mathsf { R } ^ { 2 }$ values higher than 0.200 indicate good explanatory power of the endogenous model variables (Chin, 2010). The $\mathsf { R } ^ { 2 }$ values for the endogenous variables range from 0.176 to 0.815. The empirical analysis supports all our hypotheses at 0.05 level. Traditional education technologies enable instructors to engage students to increase student learning performance, which in turn leads to a greater student satisfaction. Social media applications amplify the relationships between traditional education technologies, student engagement, learning performance, and student satisfaction. None of the control variables has a significant effect on student satisfaction. The results are kept before and after including the control variables, which gives our analysis robustness. Word of mouth indicates a possible causal influence of instructor reputation (in terms of teaching quality and grading equity) on the teaching evaluations completed by the student (i.e., student satisfaction). In the context of our theory, this effect is not significant, contrary to popular wisdom. Future research on Management Learning should explore this relationship. ## Test of robustness

Current PLS path modeling requires taking the robustness of the estimated model seriously (Benitez et al., 2016b). We check for robustness of the proposed model in two ways. First, we triangulate the measurement model by measuring learning performance with secondary data on the final grade that the student obtains in the course. We correlate learning performance measured with survey data and learning performance measured with secondary data, and these correlate well (0.403\*\*\*), giving additional credibility to our perceptual measure on learning performance (Benitez and Ray, 2012). This analysis suggests that using a perceptual measure to evaluate learning performance is not a concern in our empirical analysis. Second, we check the robustness of the structural model by estimating a second alternative model that considers the dimensions of student satisfaction separately while keeping every other relationship the same, as in the proposed model (model in Figure 2). The results obtained from this alternative model show that learning performance is converted into greater satisfaction with the instructor and the course in the presence of social media applications. This alternative estimated model does not have a better fit than the proposed model. In other words, the goodness of fit of our estimated model is not statistically worse than the fit of this alternative model, which indicates that our proposed model is a very good explanation of the world (Management Learning) based on our data set (Henseler et al., 2016; Benitez et al., 2017).

Since it is debatable whether greater student satisfaction is positively related to learning performance (Cao et al., 2013), we perform a test of endogeneity on the relationship between learning performance and student satisfaction. ## Discussion and conclusions

## Summary and discussion of results

This research examines the effect of IT resources on Management education using the case method. We theorize that traditional education technologies enable instructors to engage students to increase learning performance, which in turn can lead to a greater student satisfaction, and that social media applications can amplify the relationships between traditional education technologies, student engagement, learning performance, and student satisfaction. The empirical analysis supports our theory.

#

We find that traditional education technologies influence student satisfaction through student engagement and learning performance and that social media applications reinforce these effects. First, using traditional education technologies as a complementary resource in the classroom increases student engagement. Providing teaching materials in a timely way, updating course content information, and answering questions quickly through information technologies (e.g., Moodle-based teaching platforms, email) increase students’ engagement. Second, student engagement improves learning performance. Engaged students are more motivated to participate and to make an effort to learn. Third, the students’ learning outcomes increase their satisfaction with the instructor, course, and teaching method. Acquiring critical knowledge and developing managerial skills to solve real business problems increase student satisfaction. Finally, social media applications play an amplifying role in these relationships because social media applications (1) are user-friendly, flexible, newer, and closer to students; (2) are appropriate platforms to exchange ideas; (3) increase quality and quantity of student engagement; (4) provide additional functionalities to instructor/student in the learning process; and (5) provide enjoyment to students.

Next, we answer the research questions asked in this study: How do IT resources affect student learning performance and satisfaction in a blended learning Management course? We distinguish between two types of IT resources in Management education: traditional education technologies and social media applications. Traditional education technologies enable instructors to engage students to increase learning performance, which in turn increases student satisfaction, and social media applications amplify these relationships. Does complementary use of social media applications amplify the impact of traditional education technologies on student learning outputs? Yes. Social media applications amplify the relationships between traditional education technologies, student engagement, learning performance, and student satisfaction.

#

## Limitations and future research directions

This study has two main limitations. First, as the study was conducted on a sample of 94 students in a Management course that employed the case method at a Spanish university, the findings can be only generalized to this context. Future research should explore whether the theory proposed and tested is also useful in other countries, in courses with different concentrations (e.g., a core IS course), and/or in courses using other teaching methods (e.g., problem-based learning) to investigate whether the proposed model could work in a non-case method context. Second, although using social media applications as a complementary IT resource was theorized and proposed as voluntary, the use of social media applications in Management education may be controlled and limited by university policy or law, which may limit that use in some contexts<sup>3</sup>. This issue seems to be a very promising topic for in-depth exploration in future research.

## Implications for IS and Management Learning research

Prior research on IS and Management Learning focuses mainly on comparison of the outcomes between online and classroom-based courses (Arbaugh and Benbunan, 2007) and on the effectiveness of online teaching activities (Brower, 2003; Xu et al., 2014). Prior research on the role of information technologies in classroom and blended learning Management education has been limited. Kane and Fichman (2009) conceptualized how wiki technology can be used as a collaborative platform to generate contents for teaching activities. Drawn on Kane and Fichman’s (2009) conceptualization, we explore the role of traditional education technologies and social media applications (i.e., two types of IT resources) in blended learning Management courses using the case method. Our study theorizes, explains theoretically, and demonstrates empirically how traditional education technologies increase student satisfaction by enabling student engagement and improving learning performance and how social media applications reinforce this sequence of effects in Management education using the case method. This is our paper’s primary theoretical contribution.

The second theoretical contribution of this study is its conceptualization of IT resources for Management education and its classification of traditional education technologies and social media applications as enabling (teaching platform) and complementary resources (social media applications) in Management education using the case method. The main function of traditional education technologies is to facilitate teaching material of the course and communication between instructor and students. Social media applications are user-friendly, close, and enjoyable complementary resources that provide additional functionalities to both instructor and students (e.g., running additional questions of a case discussion in Twitter before and after class).

Finally, we consider learning performance as an explanatory variable of student satisfaction. Our study is one of the first to empirically explore the direct relationship between learning performance and student satisfaction in the field of IS. While some studies consider interactions (among students and between students and the instructor) and social capital as antecedents of perceived learning and student satisfaction (Arbaugh and Benbunan, 2007; Lu et al., 2013), surprisingly, the direct relationship between learning performance and student satisfaction has received little study. After checking for endogeneity, our research shows that learning performance influences student satisfaction in the context of case method.

## Implications for instructors

This study also provides two key lessons to instructors. A case method-based Management course can be improved by using traditional education technologies (Moodle-based teaching platforms, email, instructor’s website) and social media applications (Facebook, Twitter, Pinterest, LinkedIn, Google+). Using traditional education technologies engages the student to improve his/her learning performance, which in turn improves student satisfaction. Providing students with the teaching material in a Moodle-based teaching platform before the class is critical in case method instruction and engages students. Highly engaged students invest more energy and time in pursuing and achieving learning. Students are satisfied when they acquire and assimilate new concepts, and they develop new skills to resolve business problems in the real world. In other words, they are totally satisfied when they really learn in the course. Appropriate selection of the best traditional education technologies is thus critical to engaging students and to increasing their learning performance and the instructor’s teaching evaluations.

Faculty members are usually very busy. They must therefore choose resources that merit time investment to be used in the classroom. Should an instructor invest time in learning to implement any of the social media applications as a complement to the course? It would be logical to use and implement social media applications in the classroom if this decision positively improved the instructor teaching evaluations. This study explains and demonstrates that social media applications are a complementary IT resource before and after class in Management education using the case method. Using social media applications amplifies the relationships between use of traditional education technologies, student engagement, learning performance, and student satisfaction, which in turn may positively affect the instructor’s teaching evaluations.
