# Team Processes in Virtual Knowledge Teams: The Effects of Reputation Signals and Network Density

TAHA HAVAKHOR AND RAJIV SABHERWAL

TAHA HAVAKHOR (taha.havakhor@okstate.edu; corresponding author) is an assistant professor of management science and information systems in the Spears School of Business at Oklahoma State University. His research interests include social media platforms, value creation with information technology (IT), and IT governance. He serves on the editorial board of IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, and his work has been published in venues such as Information Systems Journal.

RAJIV SABHERWAL (rsabherwal@walton.uark.edu) is department chair (Information Systems) and Edwin and Karlee Bradberry Chair in the Walton College of Business at the University of Arkansas. His research interests include information systems planning and success, and utilization of emergent technologies to manage information and knowledge. He has published over 50 refereed papers in journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and Management Science. He is editor in chief of IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, senior editor of Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and a fellow of the Association for Information Systems.

ABSTRACT: Virtual knowledge teams (VKTs) depend on team processes that facilitate expertise coordination. VKTs use technology to map expertise, and thereby address the lack of familiarity among members. Despite the considerable interest in studying expertise coordination in teams, expertise coordination in VKTs is less understood. Moreover, technology’s role in expertise coordination in VKTs and the team processes, including expertise coordination, has received limited attention. This study argues and shows— through an online experiment—that technology enables VKTs by: connecting individuals through network ties; helping individuals to locate expertise, providing reputation signals; improving interpersonal processes through enhanced ties and signals; and enabling better performance through improved ties, signals, and processes. It contributes to theory by providing insights into how IT enhances expertise coordination and performance of VKTs. It contributes to practice by providing insights into the reputation signals that broadcast team members’ expertise and the effects of technology on team processes.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: event sequence analysis, network ties, process research, reputation signals, team expertise, team performance, team processes, virtual knowledge teams, virtual teams.

The popularity of teleworking, along with the increasing rate of globalization (e.g., [75]), has made virtual teams crucial to today’s business operations [27]. Virtual teams focusing on knowledge-intensive tasks (hereafter virtual knowledge teams, or VKTs), that is, tasks that intrinsically involve information and knowledge exchange among the team members, are especially important ([5, 14]). In VKTs, such as software development teams, successfully accomplishing the team tasks requires using diverse expertise (e.g., specialized knowledge like database development and web programming in case of software development) of team members, making expertise coordination integral to their success [26]. Expertise coordination in VKTs is more complicated than is the case in conventional face to face (F2F) teams, due to the distributed nature of VKTs and relative unfamiliarity among their members [66].

Expertise coordination is often understood by relying on transactive memory system (TMS) theory [47, 43, 42, 34, 66].<sup>1</sup> A TMS includes both: (a) a structural component to store the knowledge about who-knows-what; and (b) a processual component, which has been labeled as “knowledge-related transactive processes” and involves “encoding, storage, and retrieval processes” ([43], p. 1256). Expertise coordination includes the recognition of need for knowledge, the location of expertise, and the bringing to bear of expertise [26], which may be viewed as comprising the processual component of TMS.

Despite studies on the potential impact of information technology (IT) artifacts, such as repositories, on the performance of teams [4] in general, and TMS (e.g., [21, 13, 55, 70]), specifically, serious doubts are cast over the use of IT to enable TMS and team performance [43]. Some prior empirical studies treat the technology enhancing TMS in teams as a black box, that is, they do not examine the technology features and their effects, and instead view the technology enabling communication only as the context (e.g., [13, 36]). Some other earlier studies, which have focused on the specific artifacts, have found inconsistent effects on team performance, such as beneficial impacts on some aspects such as agreement but not on other aspects or overall performance (e.g., [70]). Research on the specific aspects of IT artifacts and their links to team performance can shed light on IT’s effects on expertise coordination. Also, little is known about how the transactive team processes in a team (hereafter, team processes) depend on the IT artifacts that enable the structural component of TMS. Hence, this study seeks to understand the effect of IT artifacts on team performance by examining how they affect the processual component of the TMS in VKTs.

TMS theory assumes that the structural component of a TMS is built, and shaped through transactive processes [43], but VKTs may use technological features that enable the structural component in teams even before transactive processes happen. One instance of such technological features is a knowledge reputation signal that can be used to map the location of expertise. We distinguish between two kinds of IT-enabled reputation signals—aggregated signals that reflect the overall knowledge of individuals and dimensional signals that reflect knowledge in each area of expertise. In addition to including features enabling the structural component of TMS, information technology operates as a medium of communication in which a team’s transactive processes are embedded. To better conceptualize the role of IT in VKTs, as both enabling the structural aspect of TMS (through reputation signals) and providing the channel of communication for the processual aspect of TMS, we adopt a social network lens, previously used in some studies on knowledge teams (e.g., [65]). This is because in such teams, “performance depends on the movement of information and knowledge through social networks” ([14], p. 443). In a social network framework, communication technology is conceptualized as electronic ties connecting human nodes [38]. We view the density of the utilized electronic ties in a team (i.e., network density) as an important aspect—in addition to reputation signals—of the IT enabling the VKTs, and examine how both these attributes affect the team processes.

To understand team processes, which have been defined as “members’ interdependent acts that convert inputs to outcomes through cognitive, verbal, and behavioral activities” [50, p. 385], we include activities beyond those directly associated with the TMS (i.e., expertise coordination). This is consistent with Faraj and Sproull’s [26] distinction between expertise coordination and administrative coordination, which includes activities such as task assignment, resource allocation, resource dependency management, and output integration. We draw upon the Marks et al. [50] classification of team processes, which groups ten types of events into three broad categories—transitioning events, action events, and interpersonal events. Given the interdependent nature of expertise coordination and administrative coordination, activities pertaining to both these aspects are considered when examining the team processes.

In examining team processes, we depart from a view of TMS as collection of its indicators (e.g., expertise location, cognitive-based trust), and instead inductively identify the activities comprising the team processes in VKTs and understand their interplay through the lens of event sequences [60]. More specifically, we define the team process in a VKT as a sequence of interpersonal events that occur from start to finish of the VKT’s task. This enables us to capture the emergent and dynamic nature of team processes [26]. Thus, this study examines how the event sequences in a VKT are affected by the aspects of the IT that enable the VKT, and how those aspects of the IT and the sequence of team activities affect team performance. We also use an aggregated sequence-based measure of process quality (computed as the proximity between the sequence of events in a VKT and an “ideal” sequence based on the top-performing VKTs). Such identification of the “ideal” event sequence based on the top-performing teams is consistent with the development of the “ideal” profile based on top-performing firms in prior research on strategic alignment e.g., [69]. Thus, the study addresses two broad research questions:

RQ1: How do aspects of the IT enabling VKTs (i.e., reputation signals and network density) and the team process (in terms of sequence of events and process quality) affect team performance?

RQ2: How do aspects of the IT enabling VKTs (i.e., reputation signals and network density) influence the team process (in terms of sequence of events and process quality)?

Also, adopting an inductive approach, we use the event sequences to generate representative sequences for low and high levels of network density (RQ2), reputation signals (RQ2), and team performance (RQ1), and then use these representative sequences to examine how the sequences of events unfold in various situations.

## Theoretical Development

## Transactive Memory Systems

TMS theory is a theory of group-level cognition that explains how individuals in a team “learn, store, use, and coordinate their knowledge” ([47], p. 151) to accomplish their collective task. It suggests that in teams relying on expertise coordination, effective and efficient execution of a knowledge task depends on the individuals’ learning of whoknows-what, dividing the task based on the expertise, and using the knowledge of experts. In explaining the nature of a TMS, Lewis and Hendron [43] distinguish between the indicators of a TMS system and its components. They note expertise location, cognitivebased trust, and knowledge-task coordination as indicators of the presence of a TMS, and suggest that the system consists of: (a) a structural aspect, which focuses on individuals’ knowledge about who-knows-what; and (b) a processual aspect, which consists of interpersonal transactions for identifying where expertise resides, storing and retrieving expertise, and coordinating it to use the members’ knowledge for group tasks [44].

The structural and processual aspects of a TMS are intertwined [44]. The conventional assumption is that knowledge about who-knows-what emerges from transactional processes and gets stored in the structural component of a TMS. The transactive processes follow a sequence of activities that result in the realization of the expertise of each of the team members, and efforts to effectively store and retrieve it [44]. Faraj and Sproull [26] label this sequence of activities as locating expertise. Once the structural component is shaped, successful teams engage in activities that involve: identifying when expertise is needed (this parallels knowledge-task coordination aspect of a TMS [40]), bringing the expertise to bear (although not an explicit activity, but trusting members with their expertise and asking them to share their expertise, i.e., cognition-based trust, is necessary in this step [36]), and a series of administrative coordination activities [26], which include allocating resources, integrating outputs, and managing interpersonal relationships.

Despite the insightful studies analyzing TMS, two aspects of a TMS and the eventual effects on team performance require more attention. First, the prior literature does not examine how the sequence of starting activities (i.e., the sequence of activities leading to locating expertise) in a team changes when the structural component of a TMS is initially influenced by IT artifacts, such as reputation signals, that provide team members with an explicit map of who-knows-what. Second, less is known about how the preferred sequence of later activities (e.g., administrative coordination, bringing expertise to bear) changes both in general and in cases when the structural component of a TMS is influenced by the above IT attributes. Striving to shed further light on these aspects, we draw on the general literature on team processes, which offers a sequential view of team activities, to theorize about the transactive processes in VKTs.

## Team Processes

The team process literature has generally examined processes in terms of either the set of performed activities or the activities needed to enhance performance (e.g., [52, 34]). For example, Mathieu et al. [52] view team processes in terms of activities such as goal specification, monitoring, and affect management, with their greater use being associated with positive outcomes. Moreover, team processes have been viewed using multifaceted constructs, using a variance approach (e.g., [51]) that overlooks the sequential dynamics of the activities making up the processes (e.g., [60]). However, in a seminal study on team processes, Marks et al. [50] note that these processes are not a mere collection of different actions; instead, their sequential<sup>2</sup> order should also be considered to best understand them. Similarly, the sequence of knowledge-sharing activities is important in enhancing innovative outcomes in online settings [48].

In this study, we extend the literature on team processes by using the Marks et al. [50] typology of team processes as the starting point, adding three emergent categories of TMS-related activities based on work on expertise coordination, and a miscellaneous emergent category, and then use a process approach to examine the two aspects of team processes: the sequence of events and process quality. Two team processes differ in the sequence of events if they involve: (a) some different activities; (b) the same activities but in different order (sequence); or (c) a combination of (a) and (b). Thus, the distance between two sequences reflects the differences in terms of both events that occur and their order. By contrast, the quality of the team process, examined from a process lens, reflects whether the process includes the needed activities and in the appropriate order. More specifically, we argue that for a given situation, there is an “ideal” sequence that captures the appropriate events in the appropriate chronological order and leads to the best task performance. Moreover, as mentioned earlier, and consistent with the considerable prior literature on strategic alignment (e.g., [74]), which uses a portion of a sample (i.e., the calibration sample) to identify the “ideal” way of conducting certain activities in an organization, and then measures alignment as the distance from the ideal profile of doing those organizational activities, we use the top-performing teams in each situation to identify the ideal sequence for that situation. The quality of the team process can then be viewed in terms of the distance between the sequence of activities in the team and the ideal sequence of activities based on the top-performing teams. Thus, both the sequence of events and quality of the team process incorporate activities and their order. However, the sequence of events in a team process is descriptive of the process while the quality of the team process is evaluative of the process.

Prior literature finds that teams that perform tasks better follow different activities in different phases of performing the team task [52]. Thus, the sequence of activities in a team can affect the trajectory of success. For example, although it is important for a team to formulate strategy [50], doing so in late stages may not be effective, as it does not leave enough time for team members to build on their strategy and effectively coordinate tasks. Similarly, while reactive conflict management actions [50] are important to maintain the well-being of social interactions, performing them is effective only if they are made in quick response to heightened conflicts [11]. Hence, we propose that successful teams undergo certain sequences of activities that differ from the sequences in unsuccessful teams.

The theories about team processes (e.g., [50]) and coordination predict that certain sequences lead to greater success in knowledge-intensive teams, but the exact activities and the order in which they are performed in such sequences are less studied. Coordination theory suggests that teams with interdependent tasks should engage in explicit actions, such as task decomposition to manage task dependencies (e.g., [49]). The patterns through which coordination mechanisms (e.g., orchestrating the timing of interdependent actions) gel with other generic team events (e.g., affect management) to produce success in VKTs have remained understudied. In addition to empirically evaluating the presence of a certain sequence of events that leads to higher performance in knowledge-intensive VKTs, this study unfolds the structure of such a sequence and discusses how coordination mechanisms in VKTs are interspersed with other team activities to create success. If following certain activities in a specific order matters for enhancing team performance, as a team’s sequence of activities becomes closer to the converging sequence of events that happen in highperforming teams, its performance would improve. Thus, the converging sequence of events in the highest-performing teams is a desirable pattern, and other teams following this pattern would perform better. In other words, the similarity of a team’s process to that of the top-performing teams (team process quality) improves the team’s performance, consistent with the prior arguments that higher-performingteams involve high-quality team processes [34].<sup>3</sup> So, we posit:

Hypothesis 1: The quality of a VKT’s process, that is, the proximity of its sequence of events to the sequence of events in the top-performing teams, is 4 positively associated with the VKT’s performance.

## Reputation Signals

It is important to determine the location of expertise in VKTs where the task-related expertise is diverse [26]. In F2F networks, individuals share mental maps of expertise through TMSs (e.g., [77]). By facilitating coordinated and quick access to network members’ expertise [42], the structural component of a TMS facilitates expertise utilization (e.g., [43]) and team performance (e.g., [3]). Building an effective structural component of a TMS may be more difficult in VKTs than in conventional networks due to the likely unfamiliarity among members. Many contemporary VKTs are formed on an ad hoc basis [2], with shorter life cycles than the time needed for an effective structural component of TMS to emerge. However, certain technological features can aid mapping the location of expertise in virtual settings [43]. Specifically, knowledge reputation signals, that is, signals that indicate the knowledge of a user in a virtual setting, help locate expertise in VKTs. Reputation signals have been primarily used in contexts such as electronic communities of practice and online forums. For example, vBulletin is extensively used in online forum platforms, and signals users’ knowledge based on knowledge contributions (i.e., number of posts) [56]. These reputation signals can map members expertise in a VKT, and help build the structural component of a TMS. However, a singular and “aggregated” reputation signal may not effectively map individuals knowledge in various areas of expertise.

Individual knowledge is complex and multidimensional [41], including distinct elements or areas of expertise, such that an individual can be expert in certain areas and novice in others. Due to individuals’ knowledge being multidimensional, reputation signals in VKTs should move beyond reflecting overall knowledge to indicate everyone’s knowledge in each relevant area of expertise to effectively inform members about “who-knows-what,” that is, know-who [8]. Hence, contrary to the singular signals of reputation—or aggregated reputation signals—that are used in popular platforms, we suggest that the VKT should broadcast a set of signals regarding the various areas of expertise for everyone. We label this type of signal as a “dimensional” reputation signal. We adopt the metaphor of “prisms” from Podolny [61] to examine dimensional reputation signals in a VKT. Like a prism being used to divide light into its constituent spectral colors, a dimensional reputation signal divides an individual’s knowledge into its constituent areas.

While an aggregated signal can confirm the overall credibility of a knowledge source, it does not shed light on whether the person is reliable in the expertise area needing answers. Thus, relying on aggregated signals of knowledgeability can result in extraction of knowledge from a source in an area in which that source is a novice. If the inferior knowledge extracted from novice members diffuses across a VKT, performance is negatively affected. A dimensional reputation signal decreases the possibility that a knowledge source is used outside of his/her area of expertise, increasing the chance of spreading credible knowledge among the team members, and thereby improving team performance. Thus, we posit:

## Hypothesis 2: As compared to aggregated reputation signals, dimensional reputation signals lead to higher team performance in a VKT.

In addition to their effects on directing questions to credible sources, dimensional reputation signals influence the “transitional process,” that is, activities by members to formulate the course of action to accomplish a specified task [50]. The development of proper courses of action requires members to consider their constraints and capabilities (e.g., [50, 79]) to effectively divide the task [20]. An individual knowing other members’ expertise in various areas can define their capabilities and constraints when performing a knowledge task, and would therefore be able to decide how the task should be divided [39]. When aggregated reputation signals are used, members have less information to divide the task. Such reputation signals help rank individuals’ capabilities with respect to their overall knowledge, but do not provide information on who is capable in a specific area. However, when dimensional reputation signals are used, members can decide who is more capable of taking responsibility for each part of a divided task [72].

The sequential view of the team process suggests that later activities depend on the initial transitional process activities [50]. Specifically, dividing the task effectively helps coordinate and monitor progress, two important team activities in later phases. Failure to effectively divide the task hinders coordination and execution of later activities. Elaborate coordination of the timing and sequencing subtasks (i.e., which subtasks should be run in parallel, and which sequentially) may not be possible if individuals responsible for each subtask are unidentified. In such a case, teams may skip elaborate coordination and complete tasks based only on urgency. Alternatively, teams may engage in additional activities to unfold who-knows-what and then divide the task efficiently. This means that facing similar time constraints, teams with aggregated signals may skip important activities that teams with dimensional reputation signals are able to perform. Moreover, without an effective assignment of subtasks, monitoring the progress toward the goals is hindered as there is less clarity regarding the outcomes expected from teammates. Hence, instead of periodic monitoring of the task in later phases, teams may spend their time finding out about or reducing redundancies between what team members do, which may escalate interpersonal conflicts and require conflict management [48, 17]. This contrasts teams with successful task division, where teammates can effectively monitor the task and streamline knowledge sharing in later stages. Thus, dimensional reputation signals can trigger transitional processes that change the sequence of the team’s activities. So, we posit:

Hypothesis 3: As compared to aggregated reputation signals, dimensional reputation signals lead to higher quality of process in the VKT, that is, greater proximity of the VKT’s sequence of events to the sequence of events in the topperforming teams.

In the light of the hypothesized (H2) effect of reputation signals on team performance, the hypothesized (H3) effects of reputation signals on quality of team process, and the effect of quality of team process on team performance (H1), we also expect the quality of team process in VKTs to mediate the relationship between reputation signals and team performance. However, these partial mediations are not stated as formal hypotheses to avoid potential redundancy.

## Network Density

In addition to carrying signals of reputation, a VKT’s technology facilitates communication across individuals [32]. Kane and Alavi [38] note that communication technologies primarily operate as ties that connect individuals. They emphasize that the presence of a communication medium between individuals alone does not constitute a tie between them. Instead, a tie only manifests once the medium is used to interact. We adopt a similar view, and consider communication technologies in VKTs as a tie between two members once they use it to interact. Given the unfamiliarity of team members in VKTs and absent certain team-banding efforts, or due to intentional efforts to encourage bounded connectivity (e.g., [37]), some potential communication channels’ VKTs may be underused.

Reputation signals influence the structural aspect of a TMS in teams, whereas the extent of using communication channels in a team, that is, the network density, can influence the processual component of a TMS, which relies on transactional activities that allow planning, coordinating, and utilizing the expertise of team members [44]. In teams of highly connected members, positive interpersonal processes (which enhance team emotion [50]), such as expressing friendliness [50], are more prevalent (e.g., [53, 54]). Dense networks carry strong ties [29], which are conduits of support [9] and a sense of belonging [62], and can therefore foster friendly acts that enhance team emotions. Interpersonal processes that enhance team emotions can change the consequent team activities to those that help establish an effective TMS. An effective TMS requires establishing trust in others’ expertise (e.g., [43]), especially in a virtual collaboration [12, 19]). Absent trust, the expertise accessible through the structural component of a TMS may not be coordinated, such as through monitoring and feedback [50], by team members (e.g., [36]). Interpersonal processes that enhance team emotions improve trust and enable teams to allocate more time to monitoring task progress (e.g., [50]), instead of monitoring teammates or validating their credibility. Moreover, a key aspect of an effective TMS is bringing expertise to bear when needed [26]. Bringing expertise to bear involves freely sharing hard-to-find expertise with teammates ([26], p. 1562). Prior literature on sharing knowledge and expertise highlights the importance of trust (e.g., [29]). Thus, in teams with higher density, expertise can be more smoothly brought to bear due to the enhanced trust among team members.

As the density in a VKT increases, the sequence of activities emerging in a team changes so that the increased communication triggers emotion-enhancing activities, such as expressing friendliness, and through enhancing trust, allows teams to change the subsequent actions of teammate monitoring into task monitoring. This leads to a more streamlined sharing of knowledge by experts (bringing expertise to bear) in later phases, instead of facing assertiveness by knowledge seekers (due to lack of trust) and low or reluctant knowledge sharing by potential knowledge sharers. Thus, enhancing density changes the sequence of team activities and boosts the quality of team process. Hence, we posit:

Hypothesis 4: Increase in network density leads to higher quality of process in the VKT, that is, greater proximity of the VKT’s sequence of events to the sequence of events in the top-performing teams.

Network density’s effect on team performance is not formally hypothesized due to its well-accepted nature. In light of this effect and the hypothesized effects of network density on quality of team process (H4) and the quality of team process on team performance (H1), we expect (but do not formally posit to avoid redundancy) the quality of team process to mediate network density’s effect on team performance.

## Interaction Effect of Network Density and Reputation Signal

Locating expertise is a necessary condition for an effective TMS, but not sufficient. Rather, the located expertise needs to be brought to bear when needed [26]. Moreover, teams must be able to conduct the administrative coordination to aggregate inputs from different members and monitor the task’s progress [46, 26]. Network density provides the complementing conditions that help bring expertise to bear. As explained above, it complements dimensional reputation signals by changing the subsequent interpersonal interactions to those that enhance the team emotion and trust, bring expertise to bear (e.g., through a smooth knowledge sharing by experts, without arising interpersonal conflicts), and allow individuals to monitor tasks rather than teammates. Thus, in teams with higher network density, the structural component of a TMS, enabled by dimensional reputation signals, facilitates more effective transactive processes. Without a high level of network density, a team may have an effective structure to store the location of expertise (due to dimensional signals) but may lack the subsequent transactive processes that ensure the knowledge about who-knows-what is leveraged when needed in a timely fashion. ## Methodology

## Procedures and Sample

To test the research hypotheses, we used Microsoft Team Foundation Server (TFS) as the communication tool and manipulated reputation signals in the VKT as dimensional (reflecting knowledge in each dimension) or aggregate (reflecting the aggregated knowledge across all dimensions). Then, we formed VKTs and assigned each VKT to a block of the experiment to study the team process in it, and measure its network density and performance. We allowed communication to emerge in each team, and measured network density based on the interpersonal communication. Thus, we designed an experiment with two treatments, one where teams are provided a dimensional reputation signal and the other where teams are provided aggregated reputation signals. Within each treatment bock, data on performance of 31 VKTs was collected, leading to an overall sample of 62 VKTs. Each VKT included six individuals who remain anonymous to each other and communicate electronically to accomplish a team task.

The subjects of the experiment were recruited from Amazon’s Mechanical Turk in two stages.<sup>5</sup> First, our task advertisement called for individuals familiar with the design of web pages to sign up for the task. Next, immediately after signing up, the subjects were asked to individually answer 60 questions related to web design, which formed the initial task. These 60 questions included 15 each on: The design of databases; the basics of HTML/CSS; JavaScript; and interacting with databases through web forms. SQL/Database, HTML/CSS, JavaScript, and server connection through web forms were the four areas of expertise that were included in the experimental task.<sup>6</sup> Upon completing the initial task, subjects’ overall number of correct answers was used to: (a) eliminate one-fourth of the subjects with the lowest cumulative score; and (b) form VKTs such that each team has at least one member familiar with each of the above four areas, and the teams are comparable in terms of their members’ total overall score in the first task.<sup>7</sup> Moreover, the individual’s performance on the initial task was used to measure each member’s reputation and signal it to other VKT members in the second task. Overall, 372 subjects, including 251 males, were recruited from the United States for the final task, with an average age of 23.78 years. All the subjects had completed at least a two-year college degree related to computer science or information systems (IS), and had an average of 13.2 months of experience in programming or IS, including internships. A 30- minute socializing window was given to each team’s members to get acquainted. The subjects were told that they would be working with another five individuals on a team project, but they were not provided with the details of the task and were asked not to discuss the task before it started. Upon completing the socializing session, the team members were presented with a brief survey that evaluated their trust in each other. In the second task, subjects were allowed and asked to communicate with other members of their VKT, and design two related web pages based on the specifications provided to each team. Each team’s performance was evaluated based on the correctness of the code and matching the task specifications. Each team was given two hours to complete the task and the subjects were informed that they would be compensated with \$7 upon completion of the second task regardless of their performance, and the winning team members would each receive \$50.

## The Experimental Task

Based on the prior literature [43], tasks that are the most relevant to expertise coordination: (a) have execute processes;<sup>8</sup> (b) have an intellective rather than selective output (i.e., have a demonstrably correct solution); (c) have a cooperative rather than conflictual goal structure; and (d) are divisible rather than unitary [43]. Accordingly, we selected the task of designing two related web pages: (1) a user registration page; and (2) a home page with a section for registered users to log in. We required the web pages to be dynamic and asked for design of a database backend to store user information. We believe this made the task: (a) an execute task, as it required actual execution of operations; (b) intellective, as the final product and its subsections could be demonstrably correct; (c) cooperative, as the subsections of the task are interdependent; and (d) divisible, as there are distinct modules to develop and put together to shape the final product. Finally, prior literature on coordination in VKTs has explicitly mentioned software development as a team task where process and technical coordination is required to succeed (e.g., [25, 16]).

The web pages were developed using .NET framework in the Microsoft Visual Studio environment. Visual Studio is an integrated development environment (IDE)

that consolidates the access to and creation of SQL databases, creation of ASP.NET forms, and use of AJAX tools to enhance the dynamism of web pages. Further, it allows source code control through Microsoft’s Team Foundation Server (TFS). Subjects were required to communicate about the project through a team room created in the TFS portal. The logs of communications in the TFS were used to assess the team processes in the experiment. This procedure of using chat logs to understand coordination processes has been followed in prior literature (e.g., [24]).

## Quality of Team Process

We measured team process quality using a method that considers the activities comprising a team process as well as their sequence. To do so, we build on the event sequence literature (e.g., [1]) and the complementing literature (e.g., [69]) that explains how such a process measure can be used in a variance model (e.g., OLS, PLS). To explain this measure, we first describe how the sequence of activities in each team is identified. Then, we discuss how we measured the dissimilarities among the team processes, and thereby measured the quality of a team process based on its proximity to an ideal sequence of activities that warrants better performance in teams.

## Team Process

We used the logs of subjects’ communications in TFS, and then analyzed them qualitatively to extract the sequence of comprising activities, that is, the team process. We utilized the typology of actions proposed by Marks et al. [50] as well as by others (e.g., [51]) as the basis to represent the team process. This typology classifies team processes into three broad types: transition process, action process, and interpersonal process.<sup>9</sup> Transition process includes planning, goal specification, and strategy formulation; action process includes monitoring and coordination; and interpersonal processes include conflict management, motivation building, and affect management. The sequence of actions by VKT’s members was then used to examine the sequence of the team activities; we treat this sequence of activities for each team as its team process. Event sequences have similarly been used in several contexts to examine process (e.g., [60]), including in IS literature (e.g., [69]).<sup>10</sup> We divided each VKT’s conversation into chronological sentences, each treated as an “activity” to be coded. Together, the sentences related to a VKT’s conversation represent its team process, which was converted into a sequence of activity codes following the coding of each sentence.

To code the individual activities, we started with the broad categories of team processes, as suggested by Mark et al. [50]. The coding schema was refined for the context of VKTs dependent on expertise coordination through the iterative process recommended in prior literature (e.g., [68]). First, two research assistants independently coded a portion (10 out of 62) of the transcripts, and then resolved discrepancies through discussion, and made the needed modifications to the coding scheme. One research assistant next categorized the 52 remaining transcripts. A second subsample of 10 transcripts was then chosen from these 52 to assess interrater reliability. The two assistants agreed in over 90 percent of events of in these 10 transcripts. These discrepancies were resolved through discussion, after which one of the authors made needed adjustments to the rest of the coding. Appendix B presents the emergent coding schema along with an illustrative example of each category of actions.

The emergent schema includes seven broad categories of activities: (1) information events, which enable information regarding task questions to be circulated among members, and include: questions and answers regarding general information (such as the time left to complete the task), seeking and sharing task-specific information (i.e., regarding the web programming), agreement and disagreement with others; (2) transitional events, which include planning (i.e., determining the timing and order of subtasks), and member allocation (i.e., determining who does what); (3) action events, which include task monitoring (i.e., determining the extent of progress in each subtask), teammate monitoring (i.e., evaluating the outputs returned by each team member), and coordination (i.e., orchestrating the sequence of interdependent subtasks); (4) interpersonal events, which include five subtypes of actions by members to manage interpersonal relationships: ingratiation (i.e., praising other members performance), rational argumentation, referring to others to support own thoughts, assertiveness toward other members, and self-deprecation in the face of appraisals; (5) signal responses, which include explicitly citing others’ reputation signal and explicitly ignoring others’ reputation signal; (6) perception actions, which include two types: complaining about the task and explicating satisfaction with performance; and (7) greetings, which simply include one type of events, focusing on acknowledging or welcoming other members of the team.

## Similarities and Dissimilarities Between Team Processes

The coded activities were used to represent each team process as a string of chronological activities, or sequence. Sequences have been previously used to quantitatively analyze process (e.g., [57]). One prominent way to do so focuses on the distance between pairs of sequences of events, computed as the least effort needed to transform one sequence to another. To calculate the distance between two sequences, three types of transformation are used: insertion (for example, sequence A → B can be transformed to $\mathrm { A \to C \to B }$ by inserting one C right after event A), deletion (for example, sequence $\mathrm { ~ A ~ }  \mathrm { ~ C ~ }  \mathrm { ~ B ~ }$ can be transformed to $\mathrm { ~ A ~ }  \mathrm { ~ B ~ }$ by deleting C), and substitution (for example, sequence $\mathrm { ~ A ~ }  \mathrm { ~ D ~ }  \mathrm { ~ B ~ }$ can be transformed into $\mathrm { A \to C \to B }$ by substituting D with C). As two sequences become more different, more moves of insertion, deletion, or substitution are needed to transform one to another, and so the distance between them increases. Therefore, the effort required to transform one sequence to another is an appropriate measure of their dissimilarity. To estimate the effort needed to transform one sequence to another, the minimum cost of transformation should be estimated. To do so, the cost of one insertion, deletion, and substitution needs to be identified. The cost associated with any one process of transforming one sequence into another is computed based on the numbers of insertion, deletion, and substitution operations that are needed for the transformation, and the costs associated with one operation of each type. For a given pair of sequences, numerous alternative transformation process can be identified, each with an associated cost. The lowest of these transformation costs is viewed as the distance between the two sequences. Since the number of alternative transformation process increases as each of the sequence becomes longer in terms of number of events, and numerous pairs of sequences are involved in a project, automating the computation of the lowest transformation cost is important. The optimal matching algorithm [1] has led to such automation through packages in statistical software, including STATA.

To ensure that the cost of transforming sequence A into sequence B is the same as the cost of transforming B into A, insertion and deletion costs needs to be kept equal [35, 68]. We set both deletion and insertion costs to 0.5. Furthermore, the cost of substituting one type of action with another should reflect the dissimilarity between those types of actions [35, 68], such that pairs of actions from the same broad category of coding should be considered more similar and assigned a lower substitution cost than pairs of actions from different broad categories. Therefore, we assigned a cost of 0.6 for substitution of actions within the same broad category and a cost of 0.9 for cross-category substitution.<sup>11</sup> The intersequences distances are divided by the length of the longer of the two sequences ([1, 68, 69]). Optimal matching through STATA’s SQ package was used to compute intersequence distances.

## Quality of Team Process

To measure the quality of team process, we first used a subset of our VKTs to define a sequence of actions that corresponds to high performance and consider it as the ideal sequence. Following the approach used in the strategic alignment literature (e.g., [74]), as discussed earlier, we formed an ideal sequence using the six (i.e., top 10 percent) top-performing VKTs. This involved three steps. First, we identified the actual centroid sequence for VKTs in each situation, that is, at each level of our manipulated treatment (e.g., dimensional reputation VKTs), for low and high levels of network density (based on median split), and for low and high performance. Thus, the centroid sequence is the one actual sequence among the top-performing teams that has the lowest mean distance from the other sequences in that situation. Next, we visually inspected the various sequences of all top-performing teams [1]. We spread the sequences in each group across the page such that the matching parts of various sequences were aligned vertically. Such visual techniques are suitable when each group has a small number of cases (e.g., [1, 68, 69]). Finally, based on the information from these two steps, we identified the typical sequence for topperforming teams, that is, the hypothetical sequence that best represents the common sequence of activities in top-performing teams. The centroid sequence provides a more limited view, based on only sequence, whereas the typical sequence augments the information available from the centroid sequence with the information about all other sequences within that group. Therefore, the typical sequence better represents sequences associated with team success than the centroid sequence, and is used as the ideal sequence. 12

Since the calibration sample based on six VKTs is used to compute the ideal sequence, these six VKTs should be excluded from the subsequent analysis. However, only excluding them would bias the sample, so we also excluded the six worst-performing VKTs—consistent with Venkatraman and Prescott [74], who also discarded the bottom 10 percent—and used the remaining 80 percent, that is, 50 VKTs, as the study sample. Next, for each VKT’s team sequence, we calculated the distance from the ideal sequence, and then subtracted its standardized value from one to compute team process quality.

## Analyses and Results (Deductive Analysis)

Recognizing the need to include both mediation and moderation, a seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) procedure was used [63]. SUR allows considering mediational paths, and benefits from an advantage relative to other estimations with a capability to estimate the significance of indirect paths especially those from interaction terms (i.e., mediated moderation). It allows estimating the significance of the path from the interaction term to the dependent variable through the mediator. SUR estimates multiple ordinary least squares (OLS) regressions while accounting for the interrelation of the error terms for those multiple OLS regressions. Using SUR to estimate the mediated moderation path is consistent with prior recommendations ([22, 27]). All continuous variables are mean-centered in the analyses. Table 2 presents the results. Models 1 to 3 present the results for the quality of team process as the dependent variable. Model 1 includes only the control variables. Model 2 presents the results pertaining to H2b and H4b, which indicate that reputation signal affects the quality of the VKT’s team process (supporting H4b), but network density does not have a significant direct effect (not supporting H2b). However, Model 3 suggests a positive and significant interaction between dimensional signal dummy and network density (supporting H5). The interaction plot, presented in Figure 3, shows that when VKTs access dimensional reputation signals, an increase in network density enhances the performance.

Models 4 to 7 present the results for team performance as the dependent variable. Model 4 includes only the control variables. Model 5 adds dimensional signal dummy and network density as independent variables. It finds that the dimensional signal dummy has a positive and significant effect (supporting H3). As expected, but not formally hypothesized, network density also has a positive and significant effect on team performance. Model 6 adds the interaction between dimensional signal dummy and network density. This interaction has a significant effect in Model 6, but the interaction becomes nonsignificant in Model 7, when the quality of team process is added as an independent variable. Model 7 shows that the quality of team process has a significant effect on team performance (supporting H1b).

Table 2 also provides the results related to the expected, but not formally hypothesized, partial mediating role of the quality of team process in the effects of network density and dimensional reputation signal on team performance. Following Baron and Kenny [6], if the positive effect of dimensional reputation signal (or network density) on performance is mediated through the quality of team process, we would expect: (a) dimensional reputation signal dummy (or network density or the interaction term) to lead to higher quality of team process (Model 3); (b) dimensional reputation signal dummy (or network density or the interaction term) to lead to better performance without controlling for quality of team process (Model 6); and (c) when quality of team process is added to Model 6 (i.e., Model 7), it would be significant but the effect size for the dummy for dimensional reputation signal (or network density or the interaction term) would decrease. Results for Model 3 (conforming to step [a]) suggest that the dimensionality of reputation signals and the interaction term, but not network density, significantly and positively affect quality of team process. The results for Model 6 (conforming to step [b]) show that the dimensionality of reputation signals and the interaction term also significantly affect performance, and the results for Model 7 (conforming to step [c]) show that the effect sizes of dimensionality of reputation signal and the interaction decrease on adding quality of team process, which itself significantly affects performance.

## The Effects of Reputation Signals

We find that individuals in VKTs with a dimensional reputation signal explicitly mention the signals in the transitional processes prior to strategizing about the VKT roles, in a typical subsequence of signal utilization → member allocation → planning → friendliness (highlighted in light blue in Table 3). All but two teams with dimensional reputation signals utilized the signals prior to member allocation and planning (93 percent), but only 22 percent of teams with aggregated reputation signals used it before their initial transitioning actions or in the early phases of their communication. Specifically, when reputation signal is mentioned in earlier stages, the purpose of doing so is usually to present the case for allocating members based on expertise. For example, in the following illustrative subsequence of comments among VKT members, individuals’ knowledge was brought up to make them responsible for specific parts of the tasks:

“seems you are the only one among us who has used JQueries before” → “I know few things about it” → “guess we need to use it to make it fit different browsers” → “yeah probably” → “do you want to do it after I create the forms?” → “yeah that will do, or, I can wait until Sarah finishes all the code behind” → “Agree!”

However, when the reputation signal is used later in the process, it functions as a proof of how an individual is effective, or to appeal to a reputable individual to justify his or her own answers. In 84 percent of instances where signal utilization occurred at later stages of the communications it was preceded or followed by a referring to others event. The following quotes illustrate these two uses of reputation signals.

“I think we have tested it enough, I cannot find any other exceptions” → “I asked Tom and he thinks we are good” → “I know, but Tom did not connect it to server” → “Seems he has done it before, though, did you check his test score?”

This difference in utilizing signals causes a stark difference in transitional processes that teams with aggregated signals follow, where a series of question and answers, instead of signal utilization, trigger member allocation in a typical subsequence of question → answer → question → member allocation (highlighted in dark blue in Table 3). Another difference between teams with dimensional and aggregated signals is in elaborate coordination that is prevalent in the former, but not the latter. While 9 percent of communications conducted by teams with dimensional reputation signal pertains to coordination among interdependent tasks, the corresponding ratio is only 3 percent for teams with aggregated reputation mechanisms. Also, while 100 percent of the teams that follow a transitional process of signal utilization → member allocation → planning (mostly teams with dimensional reputation signals) have at least one coordination event in their sequence, this proportion is at 48 percent for teams that do not follow such trend (i.e., mostly teams with aggregated reputation signals). Further analysis suggests a possible explanation. When reputation signals are used early on to allocate members to different subtasks, in 87 percent of cases, the lowest scoring member in all aspects of the initial test is allocated to a managing role where he or she becomes responsible for orchestrating the sequence of key interdependencies, and in the action event, 93 percent of the time, the managing role is assigned at some time to maintain the smooth flow of work across subtasks. This mostly happens in teams with dimensional reputation signals. The following sequence depicts this trend:

“Well, I’m certainly not all that knowledgeable about this whole thing given how I did” → . . . “Kevin, can you keep track of the specs and let us know if we need to change the tempo? ” → “I think 2 hrs is enough but Tina should pass me the queries by the time I finish all other code-behinds” → “just check her source and when she was done five me a buzz or something” → “Ok sure.”

By contrast, teams with aggregated reputation signals rarely follow a signal utilization → member allocation → planning sequence, and instead follow a series of interpersonal interactions and information events before the transitioning event of member allocation. As explained earlier, the typical subsequence is question → answer → question → member allocation. Absent technological features that provide teammates with clues to divide the task, the use of interpersonal actions to achieve a map of who-does-what is consistent with prior studies [36]. During this extended period of task division only 26 percent of teams allocate a member for coordinating processes. This may happen because the extended periods leading to transitioning actions can deplete team members of cognitive resources required to focus on the importance of coordination and its planning. Therefore, while the typical sequence in teams with dimensional reputation mechanisms involves elaborate coordination and sharing of the subtasks that are inputs to others (coordination → seeking → sharing; highlighted in purple in Table 3), the typical sequence in teams with aggregated reputation mechanisms involves no common elaborate coordination events;<sup>14</sup> instead, coordinating mechanisms are replaced by task monitoring and questions and answers about where things are, often followed by assertiveness (task monitoring → question → seeking → agreement → assertiveness; highlighted in brown in Table 3):

“hey I just checked the source for SQL” → “when do we get those read?” → “also we need the password queries to parameterize it” → “sure just give us few more mins” → “we are left with a TON of other stuff here, just saying.”

## The Effects of Network Density

A review of typical team sequences under low and high network densities, as discussed above, indicates that as network density decreases, VKT members spend less time on interpersonal processes, especially positive ones (highlighted in green in Table 3). Overall, 18 percent of communications in high-density teams are interpersonal, while this proportion is only 7 percent for teams with low density. For example, when density is high, individuals share feelings about the team’s progress or express friendliness toward those lacking the ability to finish their tasks. The following initial sequence of comments illustrates how members of VKTs with high network density start their conversation:

“I am Jason, btw”→ “hello Jason” → “sup everyone” → “nice to sort of meet you folk, I’m Brian” → “n2my Brian” → “glad to talk to actual Turkers finally” → “Do you have a programming background as well?” → “I do”→ “great!” → “the task seems doable to me” → “yeah I think so.”

As may be seen above, members introduce themselves to create a sense of mutual sympathy and optimism regarding the task within the team. However, when network density is low, individuals mainly follow brief transitional processes, for example, planning and member allocation, followed by action processes such as answer and sharing regarding the task. The following illustrative partial sequence of comments shows how members of VKTs with low network density start their conversation:

“Hey” → “uh, how do we want to do this?” → “I can start on the SQL part real quick” → “Alright, but what about others” → “hey guys, what all you want to do?” → “I could do the front-end of forms if you figure out how we want to do the rest” → “Ok, I wait for you to finish” → “do we do all of it in one table or what?” → “I don’t think he cares” → “It doesn’t matter just pass me the script later.”

Unlike the example of the high network density VKT, members of this VKT start with a brief greeting, followed by episodes of seeking and sharing, without friendly gestures (i.e., positive interpersonal processes). Rather, assertiveness as an example of negative interpersonal processes (highlighted in red) is more prevalent in teams with lower density. This is consistent with previous studies regarding the effect of network density on VKT interactions. Prior literature postulates that members interact more under higher network density [71], and greater interactions foster a higher sense of accountability toward other members [53]. Also, social cohesiveness creates a sense of unity among members (e.g., [64]), leading to more positive interpersonal interactions. Table 3 summarizes the differences between the typical sequences in different blocks of network density.

Another key difference between their typical sequence and the typical sequence of teams with high density concerns monitoring action events in later stages. Specifically, teams with lower density engage in more teammate monitoring (highlighted by underlining) than teams with high density. This is in contrast to teams with higher network density that show more prevalence of task monitoring (highlighted by bolding). The following is an example of teammate monitoring in a team with low density:

“hey I took a look at the SQL scripts and seems all fields are specified as not null” → “what’s wrong with doing that” → “it will throw exceptions when we complete the register form” → “Ok, I didn’t know it would do that” → “well, yes, can you edit it?” (question mark added).

Teammate monitoring is an effective way to identify errors and enhance performance, but indicates a lack of task credibility and trust among members [45]. In teams that emerge without proper transitioning processes, such as member allocation, or do not follow coordination actions, teammate monitoring can reduce the damage to some degree. However, teammate monitoring can also cause a waste of time when other mechanisms such as coordination and member allocation are in order. Thus, depending on other events in a team, a team’s density can enhance or reduce team process quality. We believe that the lack of direct effect on team process quality may be due to the effect of density on later monitoring actions.

## The Interacting Effect of Network Density and Reputation Signals

The regressions and interaction plots show that in teams with dimensional reputation mechanisms, increasing network density improves the quality of team process. We now examine the potential reasons. Greater network density enhances the use of positive interpersonal processes in teams with dimensional reputation signals (highlighted in green and contrasted with red highlights of negative interpersonal processes in teams with lower density and dimensional signals). As discussed in the section on reputation signals, teams with dimensional reputation signals allocate members based on reputation signals and appoint a member to coordinate tasks. Teammate monitoring, prevalent in teams with low network density and potentially caused by lack of positive interpersonal processes, can hurt performance because it can waste a team’s time and human resources especially in the case of effective early transitioning (i.e., member allocation based on expertise) and action (i.e., coordination) events. Further, Liang et al. [45] show that teammate monitoring can be a sign of underdeveloped cognitive trust among team members and can adversely affect the team morals.<sup>15</sup> Consistent with this explanation, the typical sequence for teams with dimensional reputation mechanisms and high network density shows that the late-stage transition processes focus more on task monitoring than teams with dimensional reputation mechanisms and low network density. Only 26 percent of teams with high network density and dimensional reputation signals include teammate monitoring events, compared to 68 percent of teams with low network density and dimensional reputation signals. Table 3 highlights the prevalence of teammate monitoring (by underlining) in cases where network density is low in teams supplemented with dimensional reputation mechanisms.

## Discussion

Focusing on expertise coordination in VKTs, this study has examined how prisms (i.e., reputation signals) and pipes (i.e., network density) as attributes of the IT influence team processes that affect the VKT’s performance. Building on the TMS theory, we suggest that while reputation signals influence the structural component of a TMS in VKTs, the extent to which IT as a communication channel permeates VKTs, that is, network density, changes the way the structural component is leveraged and utilized. We have found that by affecting the processual component of a TMS in VKTs, reputational signals change the sequence in which transactive processes in a VKT emerge, such that the emerging sequence of activities in a team resembles the sequence of activities that highly successful teams follow. Notably, we showed that dimensional reputation signals, with their explicit mapping of whoknows-what, become a prevalent point of reference when dividing the task among the members of team, and that a reliance on those signals enables a coordinated way of bringing expertise to bear, that is, knowledge sharing. Conversely, aggregated reputation signals, only showing who-knows-more, do not become a part of the transitioning processes of planning and member allocation. Consequently, bringing expertise to bear follows a less streamlined process, where—observing lack of progress toward team goals and applying assertiveness—members engage in a less streamlined sharing of knowledge. Moreover, the findings show that positive interpersonal processes, enabled by the strong ties of dense networks, foster an environment wherein the later monitoring efforts are focused on the task instead of the teammates. This reduces consequent conflicts and negative explication of emotions (e.g., assertiveness) that happen when team members feel they are checked by others, and encourages experts to share the knowledge with those they can trust. Consistent with Faraj and Sproull’s suggestions, we have found that locating expertise, through dimensional reputation signals, is insufficient for reaching a high level of success in VKTs. Instead, high levels of density and its effect on enhancing cognitive-based trust are needed to help VKTs bring the expertise to bear.

## Limitations

The study’s findings should be viewed in light of its limitations. First, it has viewed team processes in terms of sequences of activities. Other aspects of processes, beyond the sequences, such as individuals’ perceptions and emotions during the process, were therefore not considered. Moreover, it is possible that other types of activities, beyond those that were initially included or emerged from the qualitative data, may occur in virtual teams in other contexts.

Second, the study is based on an experiment with 62 virtual teams. The use of an experiment allowed us to manipulate the prisms in VKTs, which is not possible in the field. It also allowed us to form virtual teams with randomly assigned subjects. The experiment followed the recent practices of IS research on performance impacts of design changes in IT platforms (e.g., [78]), but the VKTs do not fully resemble real-world cases, which may involve larger teams and more complex tasks. Both the size of the VKT and the nature of the VKT’s task may interact with pipes and prisms enabled by VKTs to enact team processes and change performance. Studying larger teams can also increase the variability of network density, which was a key construct in this study. Also, even though the subjects in our sample reported having at least two years of college education in the fields of computer science or IS, their behavior may diverge from that of professional programmers. Field studies may shed light on the generalizability of our results, although subjects recruited form Amazon Mechanical Turk outperform student subjects and produce results comparable to company employees [73].

Third, in conceptualizing VKTs, we focused only on nonfamiliarity and spatial distance among members as the defining features of virtuality. In addition to these aspects, virtuality can be defined by other aspects (e.g., [30, 40, 46]) such as temporal (e.g., [23, 24]) and functional/organizational (e.g., [23]) distances among team members. Espinosa et al. [23] contend that the different types of distances among a VKT’s team members can have different effects on team process and outcome. Also, Cummings et al. [18] suggest that temporal barriers are harder to overcome by technologies in VKTs. Since this study focuses on the role that IT features play in enhancing expertise coordination in VKTs, one fruitful avenue for future research is focusing on experiments where members of the VKT are also temporally distant.

Finally, the experiment focused on team processes and performance over a short time period and on an initial individual task followed by a subsequent team task. Each VKT’s members remained the same for the duration of the team task. Real VKTs perform multiple different tasks over long time periods, and may encounter different patterns. Social processes are dynamic, and change over time punctuated by incidents, achievements, and anomalies. Accordingly, the nature of the team processes can change over time and cause performance outcomes to change as well. Specifically, in VKTs where subjects initiate interactions under the condition of unfamiliarity, as was the case in this study, VKT members would develop social attachments over time, and VKT members could leave and be joined by new members. Such changes were not included in the experiment upon which this study is based. Further longitudinal research is needed to examine these aspects, and could benefit from combinations of experiments with changing VKT members over time, and field research using interviews along with online objective data from real VKTs.

## Implications for Practice

Despite the above limitations, this study has implications for managers and designers of VKTs. Our results guide team designers to focus on the design of knowledge reputation signals that reflect location of expertise in VKTs. Also, the team managers should facilitate bonding conditions that help members extend their ties with others. Moreover, our results inform managers about the effects of the VKT’s design on team process. The results indicate that besides facilitating knowledge flow and being means of communication, VKTs change the interpersonal dynamics, including how individuals strategize and divide knowledge tasks. Finally, the study informs managers about the types of team processes that lead to higher performance in VKTs, and thus enable them to monitor processes and use them to provide feedback for corrective actions. Especially, the consideration of sequences in these processes enables managers to make their process expectations contingent upon the timeline of the team task, and guides them about the desirable actions in the beginning, middle, and late stages.

## Implications for Research

The study also has some potential implications for research on VKTs as well as other areas. First, it contributes to the literature on VKTs by providing empirical support that IT can enhance team process quality by enabling planning and coordination of expertise. While previous research casts doubt on the role of attributes of IT in enabling expertise coordination (e.g., [43]), this study provides support that utilizing dimensional reputation signals enables teams to effectively orchestrate the expertise of their members and perform their task. Prior literature on expertise coordination in VKTs has mostly focused on non-IT factors such as communication [36] and team configuration [58] as drivers of expertise coordination with only one exception. Choi et al. [13] considered the explicit role of general IT support in triggering successful expertise coordination in VKTs, but acknowledged the need to consider specific IT designs that are aimed at directly supporting expertise coordination:

It is also possible to speculate that the impact of ITS on TMS is likely to be stronger if firms implement tools such as an expertise directory that are aimed at providing more direct support for TMS among team members. Given the recent interest in IT materiality, our study suggests that a fruitful avenue for future research lies in examining the linkage between specific IT materiality to the development of TMS. ([13], p. 865; emphasis added)

Heeding Choi et al.’s call, this study has examined the role of dimensional reputation signals as a specific IT design feature intended to provide an expertise directory to a VKT’s members, in enabling expertise coordination, and provided empirical evidence supporting their positive impact on the performance of VKTs. Specifically, the study highlights the role that IT plays in VKTs by: (a) effectively mapping who knows what; and (b) enabling engaging in team activates in a desirable order.

Second, this study contributes to the literature on quality of collaboration in the team settings [8] and VKTs by conceptualizing team process in a dynamic manner, contrary to the previous research, which has used variables to examine the different aspects of team process. Findings of the study suggest that, for VKTs, if the same types of activities are performed in two team processes, they may not lead to similar performance. Rather, the sequence in which these actions are performed is also important. For example, the results show that VKTs engaging in reputation signal utilization at early stages of their interactions are more successful than those doing so later in the process. Moreover, the results suggest that team actions at different points of times enact different types of consequent actions. For example, utilization of reputation signals in early stages enacts member allocation events, but their use in later stages enacts rational argumentation.

This study also offers some methodological implications. First, it is among the first studies to execute a laboratory experiment on VKTs through online crowdsourcing markets (OCM). Recently, use of the OCMs are suggested as a viable source to conduct research in the IS field ([73]). In this study, we show that given the distributed nature of subjects in OCMs such as Amazon Mechanical Turk, they provide a suitable context to form VKTs and conduct virtual experiments. This method extends the literature on team processes where teams are usually in collocated settings (e.g., [67]). Second, this study demonstrates how sequence analysis techniques can be used to obtain insights into process that unfold in the context of VKTs. Like the multivariate approaches used in the variance approach, sequence analysis considers different actions that make up team process, but it also incorporates the temporal ordering of those actions. Through the integrated use of sequence analysis and mediation testing, this study provides researchers with insights into how the power of longitudinal measurement of a process can be effectively combined with strong hypothesis testing methods of variance research such as meditational analysis.

## Appendix D: Robustness Tests

## Alternative Cost Sets

When using sequence analysis, insertion, deletion, and substitution costs need to be decided [1, 6]. We set insertion/deletion costs at 0.5 and substitution costs at 0.6 (within the same category) or 0.9 (across different categories). To evaluate the robustness of the results, we repeated the analyses using five alternative cost sets.

In the first two robustness tests using alternative measures of team processes quality, we calculated the distance from the ideal sequence based on events modeled at the broad level of categorization. We only considered all events in the same broad category (e.g., seeking and sharing, which are both information actions) to be equivalent, that is, have a zero substitution cost, while considering two events to be different, and therefore have a nonzero cost of substituting one with the other, if they differ at the highest level (e.g., one event is an information action whereas the other is an interpersonal action). In one robustness test, we set this substitution cost as 1.0, while in the other we set it as 0.9 (i.e., Robustness Test 1: insertion/deletion cost = 0.5, substitution cost = 1.0; Robustness Test 2: insertion/deletion cost = 0.5, substitution cost = 0.9). The results of all the robustness tests are given at the end of this appendix.

In the other three robustness checks with alternative measures of the quality of team, we computed the quality of team process by using three different sets of insertion/deletion and substitution costs (Robustness Test 3: insertion/deletion cost = 0.6, within-category substitution cost = 0.7, between-category substitution cost = 1.1; Robustness Test 4: insertion/ deletion cost = 0.5, within-category substitution cost = 0.9, between-category substitution cost = 1; Robustness Test 5: insertion/deletion cost = 0.5, within-category substitution cost = 0.8, between-category substitution cost = 1).

## Sentiment Analysis

The sixth robustness test used sentiment analysis of the communication among team members to measure the quality of team process. Prior literature (e.g., [2]) suggests that the quality of team processes can be assessed by team member’s perception about the process. While a survey is a potential alternative to measure emotional perceptions about the quality of team process, sentiment analysis of the actual communications between the team members can provide a more objective view of the individuals’ feelings about the team process quality. Following Johnson et al. [3], the sentiment of each team’s communications was analyzed based on the polarity of words used. Accordingly, AFINN, a dictionary of lexicons that assigns a positive score to words with positive polarity, 0 score to neutral words, and negative scores to words with negative polarity [5], was used as the dictionary of emotionally rated English words. Aword processor, written in Python, parsed the communication log for each team and estimated the quality of each team’s process as the sum of ratings across the communication in that team.

## Additional Controls

The last robustness test included controlling for two additional variables (Robustness Test 7). First, individuals with lower baseline trust (trust at the beginning of the team task) may be less willing to share and contribute. Therefore, teams with lower average trust among their members may suffer of lower quality processes and team performance. Specifically, since subjects are informed of individual performance of other team members in the first task before they start the team task, their baseline trust may be affected. Therefore, we assessed the cognition-based trust among team members, using items developed by Kanawattanachi and Yoo [4], at the end of the socializing session and controlled for it in the seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) models.<sup>18</sup> Also, although the measurement of intersequence distance normalizes for the length of communication, the extent of communication in a team may affect team process quality and performance. We controlled for the extent of communication by using the natural log of number of messages exchanged in the Team Foundation Server (TFS) environment, following Kanawattanachi and Yoo [4].
