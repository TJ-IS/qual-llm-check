# Knowledge sharing intention in the United States and China: a cross-cultural study

Yu-Wei Chang<sup>1</sup>, Ping-Yu Hsu<sup>2</sup>, Wen-Lung Shiau<sup>3</sup> and Chung-Chih Tsai<sup>4</sup>

Correspondence: Ping-Yu Hsu, Department of Business Administration, National Central University, No.300, Jhongda Rd., Jhongli City 32001, Taiwan. Tel: +886 3422 7151 ext:66168; E-mail: pyhsu@mgt.ncu.edu.tw

## Abstract

In today’s knowledge-based economy, most of the knowledge-sharing studies have investigated the effects of cultural values at the national level. However, individual beliefs and behaviors might also be influenced and modified by individual cultural values. To understand the effects of cultural values, cultural effects at both the individual and country levels should be considered. This study is to investigate the integrative effects of the individual’s and country’s cultural values on knowledge sharing. We examine motivational factors affecting knowledge-sharing intentions based on social exchange theory and incorporate individualism/collectivism and uncertainty avoidance as moderators. On the basis of a survey of 394 employees conducted in the United States and China, the results show that rewards are significantly related to knowledge-sharing intentions for Chinese employees but not for American employees. Reciprocity and knowledge self-efficacy significantly impact knowledge-sharing intentions in the two countries. Our results also demonstrate that the individual’s and country’s cultural values play important roles in knowledge sharing. In the United States, individualism/collectivism is found to moderate the relationship between rewards and knowledge-sharing intentions. In China, individualism/collectivism is found to moderate the relationship between reputation and knowledge-sharing intentions, while uncertainty avoidance is found to moderate the relationship between knowledge self-efficacy and knowledge-sharing intentions. Keywords: knowledge sharing; social exchange theory; national culture

## Introduction

In today’s knowledge-based economy, knowledge is considered to be an important resource. Effective knowledge management can help organizations realize synergies among units, achieve higher added value for customers, accelerate innovation, and boost revenue for market development (KPMG, 2003). Thus, several organizations have made a very large investment in implementing knowledge management systems. A new report indicates that knowledge management market revenues exceeded US\$57 billion in 2012 (PRWEB, 2013).

To foster the knowledge contribution to systems, social exchange theory has been widely used to investigate motivational factors. This theory posits that people engage in social interactions based on the perceptions of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs (Blau, 1964). The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts can be direct rewards, expected gains in reputation, anticipated reciprocity, the perception of ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and altruism (Thibaut & Kelly, 1959). The impacts of these factors on knowledge sharing have been con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in prior studies (Bock & Kim, 2002; Bock et al, 2005;

Kankanhalli et al, 2005; Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Lin, 2007; Hsu & Lin, 2008; Huang et al, 2008; Hung et al, 2011; Jeon et al, 2011; Casimir et al, 2012).

Knowledge-sharing motivations are also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by culture because motivational issues do not universally hold across cultures (Srite & Karahanna, 2006). In the case of multinational corporations, although employees could work in the same organization, they could live in different countries, be educated in different systems, grow up in different environments, and believe in different religions. These factors shape different cultural values, which in turns affect different individual beliefs, values, and selfmotivated behavior (Tyler et al, 2000; Thatcher et al, 2003).

To investigate the effects of cultural values, Hofstede’s (1980) four cultural dimensions: power distance, individualism/collectivism, masculinity/femininity, and uncertainty avoidance are widely used. While some IS studies have lent Hofstede’s country scores to theorize differences between countries (McCoy et al, 2005; Jin et al, 2008; Dai & Palvia, 2009; Dinev et al, 2009), others have treated cultural values as moderating effects between individual beliefs and behavioral intentions (Straub et al, 1997; Choi & Geistfeld, 2004; Gefen & Heart, 2006; Zhang & Maruping, 2008; Posey et al, 2010).

However, most of the knowledge-sharing studies have investigated the effects of cultural values at the country level and not at the individual level (Lam, 1997; Chow et al, 2000; Weir & Hutchings, 2005; Grif<sup>fi</sup>th et al, 2006; Jiacheng et al, 2010). These studies have largely overlooked individual differences (Srite & Karahanna, 2006). For example, the United States is usually classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as an individualistic country. The population in the United States includes immigrants from other countries, whose people tend to adhere to collectivism. In other words, although two individuals live in the same country, they could have different cultural values (Lee et al, 2007). Therefore, the studies that use country assessments to explain individual knowledgesharing intentions might be off the targets.

On the other hand, a research investigates only the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of individual cultural values, might be misleading because national culture is an environmental factor affecting individual behaviors (Granovetter, 1973; Burt, 1997; Jeyaraj et al, 2006). For example, China and Taiwan inherit the same Confucian cultural roots, speak the same language, and live in the same geographic area (Hofstede & Bond, 1988), but the two countries have very different country scores in cultural values. China has a low score of 30 on uncertainty avoidance, while Taiwan has a high score of 69 on this dimension (Hofstede, 2001). This <sup>fi</sup>nding implies that although people share similar cultural roots, their individual beliefs and behaviors could still differ in different countries.

Cultural effects at both the individual and country levels should be considered when studying knowledge sharing to obtain the full picture of the effects. The effects should be especially clear for multinational corporations whose employees scatter all over the world and each employee has different educations, religions, and personality traits.

Therefore, the integrative effects of both individual’s and country’s cultural values should be taken into consideration when deploying knowledge management systems and implementing the policies to encourage employees to use the systems.

This study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to investigate the integrative effects of the individual’s and country’s cultural values on knowledge-sharing motivations and system usage intentions. The country’s cultural values are examined using different data sets collected from the United States and China, while individual cultural values of individualism/ collectivism and uncertainty avoidance, which are related to the perceptions of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and risk costs are directly incorporated into the research model as moderators.

This study as well as prior studies treats individual cultural values as moderating variables (Karahanna et al, 2005; Srite & Karahanna, 2006; Li et al, 2009; Zhang & Maruping, 2008; Yoon, 2009) because the relationship between individual beliefs and behavioral intentions might be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by culture (Tyler et al, 2000). Human interactive behaviors are based on anticipated rewards in social exchanges (Blau, 1964). Thus, the perceived expectation and interpretation of the value of rewards determine whether people participate in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c kind of social exchange activities (Kroeber & Parsons, 1958). On the other hand, culture is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘belief systems that individuals have toward human’s behavior, relationship, reality, and truth’ (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006). Through belief systems, people interpret and react to the events unfold surrounding them and make decisions to take further actions. Thus, culture affects people’s reactions to rewards during social exchange. Given the above discussion, this study posits that cultural values moderate the effects of motivational factors on knowledge-sharing intentions.

In particular, this study tries to answer the following research questions:

(1) What are the motivational factors affecting knowledge-sharing intentions in the U.S. and China?

(2) What are the moderating effects of individual cultural values on the relationships between knowledge-sharing motivations and intentions in the two countries?

By answering the research questions, this study can help researchers and practitioners enhance the understanding of how people perceive and react to bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs in knowledge sharing under the effects of cultural values at both the individual and country levels. From a theoretical perspective, the investigation is based on a model grounded on social exchange theory and re<sup>fi</sup>ned with Hofstede’s conceptual arguments. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that cultural differences between the United States and China in<sup>fl</sup>uence employees’ perceptions regarding knowledge-sharing motivations. In addition, there are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in the moderating effects of cultural values on the relationships between knowledge-sharing motivations and intentions in the two countries. Thus, our results demonstrate that the individual’s and country’s cultural values play important roles in knowledge sharing. From a pragmatic perspective, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings also help multinational corporations develop policies to promote the usage of knowledge management systems. Especially, we expect that the results can encourage organizations to adjust knowledge management systems based on different cultural backgrounds.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The theoretical foundations of the study are described next. Social exchange theory is used as the foundation for this study. On the basis of Hofstede’s cultural framework, cultural values are incorporated into the research model. The section after that describes the research model and hypotheses. The following section explains the methodology and data collection. The next section compares the effects of cultural values between the two countries. The following section discusses the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings. The penultimate section provides the theoretical and practical implications. The conclusions and limitations are presented in the <sup>fi</sup>nal section.

## Literature review

## Social exchange theory

Social exchange theory was developed in the 1960s by Homans (1961). Homans posits that human interactive behaviors are formed by the perceptions of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs. He introduced these psychological concepts and proposed that social rewards (e.g., approval, status, and respect) are important for explaining and predicting social behavior. After Homans founded the theory, Blau (1964) introduced an economic and utilitarian perspective within social exchange theory. His utilitarian perspective posited that people engage in social interactions based on anticipated rewards (e.g., increased pay, bonuses, job security, or career advancement). Therefore, from Homans and Blau’s ideas, economic and social rewards should be accounted for to explain the behaviors of social exchange.

During social exchange, people tend to maximize their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and minimize their costs (Molm, 1997). Thus, people can be motivated to exchange by bene<sup>fi</sup>t maximization. The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that make people to engage in a social exchange are direct rewards, expected gains in reputation, anticipated reciprocity, the perception of ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and altruism (Thibaut & Kelly, 1959). As knowledge sharing is a form of social exchange (Bock et al, 2005), several prior studies have used these motivational factors to explain and predict knowledge-sharing intentions (Bock & Kim, 2002; Bock et al, 2005; Kankanhalli et al, 2005; Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Lin, 2007; Hsu & Lin, 2008; Huang et al, 2008; Hung et al, 2011; Jeon et al, 2011; Casimir et al, 2012).

For example, people could be motivated to share knowledge by economic rewards, such as increased pay or bonuses (Beer & Nohria, 2000; Hall, 2001). People could enhance their image and gain others’ recognition when sharing helpful knowledge (Constant et al, 1994; Constant et al, 1996; Ba et al, 2001). After sharing knowledge, people could receive reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from others who share knowledge in the future (Kollock, 1999; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). People who have con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their ability to provide valuable knowledge could be more willing to share knowledge (Constant et al, 1994; Constant et al, 1996).

As social exchange involves the collective perceptions of the values of social interactions (Kroeber & Parsons, 1958), the behaviors of social exchange should be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced and modi<sup>fi</sup>ed by culture (Tyler et al, 2000; Thatcher et al, 2003). The same viewpoints have been echoed in several knowledge-sharing studies (Lam, 1997; Chow et al, 2000; Weir & Hutchings, 2005; Grif<sup>fi</sup>th et al, 2006; Jiacheng et al, 2010).

## Culture

Kroeber (1952) de<sup>fi</sup>nes culture as a factor that distinguishes people by ‘the historically differentiated and variable mass of customary ways of functioning of human societies’. Later, the concept of culture affecting human behavior is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as ‘transmitted and created content and patterns of values, ideas, and other symbolic-meaningful systems as factors in the shaping of human behavior and the artifacts produced through behavior’ (Kroeber & Parsons, 1958). Hofstede (1980) summarizes and simpli<sup>fi</sup>es the de<sup>fi</sup>nition as ‘the collective programming of the mind that distinguishes the members of one group or category of people from another’.

Hofstede’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition of culture is the most widely accepted in cross-cultural studies. To measure national cultures, Hofstede (1980) developed a questionnaire that contained approximately 60 questions to assess people’s basic values and beliefs. The questionnaire includes 14 job characteristics (e.g., earnings, job security, challenge, freedom, cooperation) and was sent to 116,000 IBM employees in 72 countries. Finally, the items in the collected questionnaires were weighted together to compute the scores (from 1 to 120) in four cultural dimensions, including power distance, individualism/collectivism, masculinity/femininity, and uncertainty avoidance.

Power distance refers to the extent to which the less powerful people in organizations and institutions accept and expect that power is distributed unequally (Hofstede, 1980). Countries that score high on the dimension of power distance include Guatemala and Panama (95 and 95, respectively); Austria, Israel, Denmark, and Sweden show low scores (11, 13, 18, and 31, respectively).

Individualism/collectivism is the extent to which people are integrated into groups (Hofstede, 1980). For example, people with high individualism are usually expected to look after themselves (Yoon, 2009). On the dimension of individualism/collectivism, the United States, the United Kingdom, and Canada score high (91, 89, and 80, respectively), while Guatemala, Panama, and Venezuela score low (6, 11, and 13, respectively).

Masculinity/femininity is the extent to which people express masculine values (e.g., assertive and competitive) or feminine values (e.g., nurturing) (Hofstede, 1980). For example, in a masculine culture, people emphasize work goals and assertiveness (McCoy et al, 2005; Srite & Karahanna, 2006). On the dimension of masculinity/ femininity, Japan and Venezuela score high (95 and 73, respectively), while Sweden, Norway, and Costa Rica score low (5, 8, and 21, respectively).

Uncertainty avoidance refers to the extent to which people in a culture feel threatened by uncertain or unknown situations (Hofstede, 1991). For example, people in high uncertainty avoidance cultures tend to avoid ambiguous situations (Li et al, 2009). On the dimension of uncertainty avoidance, Guatemala, Uruguay, and Belgium have high scores (101, 100, and 94, respectively); Singapore, Denmark, and Sweden have low scores (8, 23, and 29, respectively).

In this study, the United States and China are selected when there are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in the cultural dimensions (Hofstede, 1993). For example, the United States is more individualistic than China (the United States has a score of 91, and China has a score of 20). Furthermore, the United States has more uncertainty avoidance than China (the United States has a score of 46, and China has a score of 30). As such, the United States is representative of an individualistic and strong uncertainty avoidance culture, and China is representative of a collectivistic and low uncertainty avoidance culture.

## Cross-cultural studies in knowledge sharing

Hofstede’s cultural framework has been employed to examine the country’s cultural values in several crosscultural knowledge-sharing studies. Chow et al (2000), in a comparative study of the United States and China, found that the two nations’ levels of openness in knowledge sharing were related to their different degrees of collectivism. Grif<sup>fi</sup>th et al (2006) investigated how <sup>fi</sup>rms from Japan and the United States perceive the links between relationship resources and knowledge resources. A relationship resource refers to the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of trust on commitment, while a knowledge resource refers to the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of information sharing on problem resolution. The results indicated that cultural differences in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the linkages between the two resources. Jiacheng et al (2010) investigated a cognitive model of knowledge-sharing motivations between the United States and China. The results showed that the effects of these motivations were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different in the two countries.

However, it is inappropriate to use country scores to predict individual behavior (Srite & Karahanna, 2006). While the country’s cultural value is a macro-level phenomenon, it often lacks precision in explaining individual behavior (Straub et al, 2002). Although two individuals live in the same country, they could have different cultural values (Lee et al, 2007). The cultural values might be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the ethnic background, region, and society (Gallivan & Srite, 2005). Thus, Srite & Karahanna (2006) suggest that cultural values should be measured at the individual level of analysis.

People from the same country should be motivated by different knowledge-sharing motivations because they could have different individual cultural values. This argument is implicitly supported by three different studies in knowledge sharing. First, a survey of 212 blog participants in Taiwan found that altruism and reputation signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the intention to write and comment on blogs (Hsu & Lin, 2008). Second, a survey of 225 R&D employees in 10 organizations in Taiwan found that altruism was the only knowledge-sharing motivation (Hung et al, 2011). Third, a survey of 172 employees from 50 organizations in Taiwan showed that reciprocal bene-<sup>fi</sup>ts, knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and enjoyment in helping others were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with employee knowledge-sharing intentions (Lin, 2007). Given the above empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the effects of the motivational factors might differ even if they were undertaken in the same country, Taiwan.

A review of the literature reveals that although most of the knowledge-sharing studies have investigated the effects of motivational factors between countries (Lam, 1997; Chow et al, 2000; Weir & Hutchings, 2005; Grif<sup>fi</sup>th et al, 2006; Jiacheng et al, 2010), they have largely overlooked individual characteristics and differences (Srite & Karahanna, 2006). At issue here are both the individual’s and country’s cultural values play important roles to in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge-sharing motivations and intentions. Therefore, this study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to investigate the integrative effects of the individual’s and country’s cultural values on knowledge sharing. That is, the individual’s cultural values will affect personal decisions of knowledge sharing while the collective minds in each country will also shape peculiar patterns of behaviors.

## Research model and hypotheses

## Knowledge-sharing motivations

On the basis of social exchange theory, rewards, reputation, reciprocity, knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and altruism can facilitate successful knowledge sharing (Thibaut & Kelly, 1959). Among these motivational factors, altruism, which is the degree a person enjoys in helping others (Wasko & Faraj, 2005), is excluded from this study. The reason is because the subjects are knowledge-intensive workers, whose values in the organization are judged by their expertise of maintaining machines. Sharing the knowledge and helping others to gain expertise actually are against their professional interest because the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary could potentially advance more quickly in the organization. In such an environment, the pure pleasure of helping others may be outweighed by the importance of career advancement or job security. In fact, altruism is also not included in other studies investigating knowledge-sharing motivations in corporate environments (Bock & Kim, 2002; Bock et al, 2005; Huang et al, 2008; Casimir et al, 2012). Therefore, this study adopts four motivational factors: rewards, reputation, reciprocity, and knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy.

Rewards in knowledge sharing are de<sup>fi</sup>ned to which people believe that economic incentives provided for knowledge sharing are important (Ba et al, 2001; Hall, 2001; Kankanhalli et al, 2005). Reputation in knowledge sharing is de<sup>fi</sup>ned to which people believe that they can increase their image and social status through knowledge sharing (Constant et al, 1996; Kollock, 1999; Hsu & Lin, 2008). Reciprocity in knowledge sharing is de<sup>fi</sup>ned to which people believe that sharing knowledge will lead to future requests for knowledge being met (Davenport & Prusak, 1998; Kankanhalli et al, 2005). Knowledge selfef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the con<sup>fi</sup>dence in one’s ability to provide knowledge that is valuable and correct to the organization (Kinch, 1973; Constant et al, 1996; Kalman, 1999; Kankanhalli et al, 2005). Several prior studies have proposed that motivational factors have a positive effect on knowledge-sharing intentions.

Rewards are useful for motivating people to share their knowledge (Ewing & Keenan, 2001; Husted & Michailova, 2002). Thus, when employees believe that rewards for knowledge sharing are important, their knowledge-sharing intentions will be increased (Bock & Kim, 2002; Bock et al, 2005; Kankanhalli et al, 2005; Lin, 2007; Huang et al, 2008; Casimir et al, 2012). People could establish their social status through knowledge sharing (O’Dell & Grayson, 1998; Huang et al, 2008). Thus, if employees believe that they can earn respect or a better image through knowledge sharing, they will be more willing to share knowledge (Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Huang et al, 2008; Hsu & Lin, 2008; Jeon et al, 2011). Reciprocity provides an effective motivation to facilitate knowledge sharing (Huang et al, 2008). Thus, if employees believe that they can obtain reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts by cooperating with others, they will have a high willingness to share knowledge (Bock et al, 2005; Lin, 2007; Jeon et al, 2011). In addition, employees will be more willing to share knowledge, when they have high con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their ability to provide valuable knowledge (Bock & Kim, 2002; Kankanhalli et al, 2005; Lin, 2007; Huang et al, 2008; Casimir et al, 2012). Accordingly, we propose the following:

Rewards are positively related to knowledge-sharing intentions.

Reputation is positively related to knowledge-sharing intentions.

Reciprocity is positively related to knowledge-sharing intentions.

Knowledge self-efficacy is positively related to knowledgesharing intentions.

## Cultural effects on knowledge sharing

Among Hofstede’s four cultural dimensions, individualists seek to satisfy personal goals and self-interest when making decisions (Radford et al, 1991). Uncertainty avoidance refers to the degree to which people can accept risk (Srite & Karahanna, 2006) and re<sup>fl</sup>ects an individual’s risk-taking propensity (Yamagishi & Yamagishi, 1994; Bontempo et al, 1997; Weber & Hsee, 1998; Taylor et al, 2000). In other words, uncertainty avoidance is a re<sup>fl</sup>ection of risk orientation (Dawar et al, 1996). In addition, Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) argue that risk can be considered to be costs. The psychological uncertainty and perception of risk are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as uncertainty costs (Hirschheim & Newman, 1988; Krovi, 1993; Jiang et al, 2000). As uncertainty causes negative psychological reactions (Inder & O’Brien, 2003), people tend to be averse to these costs when making decisions (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009). Posey et al (2010) also propose a similar concept and model risks as costs when disclosing personal opinions in online communities. They found that perceived risk decreased the intention to share thoughts, feelings, and experiences in online communities. Therefore, individualism/collectivism and uncertainty avoidance are related to the perceptions of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and risk costs when sharing knowledge. Only the cultural values of individualism/collectivism and uncertainty avoidance are selected in this study.

On the other hand, power distance refers to the degree to which people accept and expect that power and rank are distributed unequally (Hofstede, 1980). Therefore, people with higher power can exercise authority and coerce others with lower power to behave in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c way (Maloni & Benton, 2000; Zhao et al, 2008), even if the result might con<sup>fl</sup>ict with the interest of the low-power people. However, social exchange theory posits that people behave in ways that maximize bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and minimize costs (Molm, 1997). As power distance and social exchange theory describe incompatible mentalities, the cultural dimension is excluded from this study.

Masculinity/femininity refers to the degree to which people tend to have an assertive attitude and con<sup>fi</sup>dence when facing a confrontation or challenge (Hofstede, 1980). According to White (2006), the cognitive psychology of masculinity and self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy are similar. As Bong (1997) suggests, ‘This cognitive aspect of masculinity seems to resemble self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in that self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is a cognitive assessment of one's prospective performance’. In another report, Grodan (2008) even notes that the degrees of masculine and self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy are highly correlated. As self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is an important exogenous variable, masculinity is excluded from this research model.

Scholars have suggested a positive relationship between rewards and knowledge-sharing intentions or between reputation and knowledge-sharing intentions (Bock & Kim, 2002; Bock et al, 2005; Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Lin, 2007; Hsu & Lin, 2008; Huang et al, 2008; Jeon et al, 2011; Casimir et al, 2012). These relationships are likely to be reinforced by individualism/collectivism because people with individualistic inclinations tend to look after their own interests and personal goals (Choi & Geistfeld, 2004; Lee et al, 2007). Also, people with individualism are found to be sensitive to monetary value and other personal interest (Radford et al, 1991; Lee et al, 2007). Thus, individualism should strengthen the effects of rewards and reputation on knowledge-sharing intentions.

Furthermore, reciprocity is positively related to knowledge-sharing intentions (Bock et al, 2005; Kankanhalli et al, 2005; Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Lin, 2007; Huang et al, 2008; Hsu & Lin, 2008; Jeon et al, 2011). The relationship might be reinforced by the cultural value of individualism/collectivism because the anticipated favors returned from colleagues can contribute to personal interest. Therefore, individualism should increase the effect of reciprocity on knowledge-sharing intentions. Given the above discussion, we propose the following:

The higher the degree of individualism, the higher the effect of rewards on knowledge-sharing intentions.

The higher the degree of individualism, the higher the effect of reputation on knowledge-sharing intentions.

The higher the degree of individualism, the higher the effect of reciprocity on knowledge-sharing intentions.

Scholars have suggested a positive relationship between knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and knowledge-sharing intentions (Kankanhalli et al, 2005; Lin et al, 2007; Huang et al, 2008) because people with high knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy think that their knowledge is valuable and correct to the organization and others. On the other hand, prior studies have con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed that perceived self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is inversely associated with perceived risk (Schaninger, 1976; Kleinhesselink & Rosa, 1991; Choi & Geistfeld, 2004). People with strong uncertainty avoidance tendencies are highly intolerant of risk and ambiguity (Choi & Geistfeld, 2004; Lee et al, 2007; Li et al, 2009). They are more averse toward taking the risk of sharing valueless or incorrect knowledge than employees with low uncertainty avoidance. Therefore, strong uncertainty avoidance will reduce the effect of knowledge selfef<sup>fi</sup>cacy on knowledge-sharing intentions. Thus, we propose the following:

The higher the degree of uncertainty avoidance, the lower the effect of knowledge self-efficacy on knowledge-sharing intentions.

As cultural values can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into the individual and country levels, the individual’s and country’s cultural values are included in this study. Individual cultural values are directly incorporated into the research model as moderators, while the country’s cultural values are examined using different data sets. In other words, the country’s cultural values are investigated through a comparative analysis between the United States and China.

## Research methodology

## Subjects

The subjects of this study are engineers in a multinational corporation that researches, develops, manufactures, and sells manufacturing machines. These engineers are responsible for maintaining the machines sold to customers. As the majority of the customers are distributed throughout North America, Europe, and Asia, the branch of<sup>fi</sup>ces and service departments of the organization are located in 16 countries, including the United States, China, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan. We examined our hypotheses using data sets from the United States and China, selecting American and Chinese engineers to participate in this study.

These engineers are knowledge-intensive workers who contribute their experiences of maintaining machines to the document management system. The system provides users the interfaces to upload and query PowerPoint documents, explaining problems encountered and solutions provided. The upload interface requires users to key in machine types, symptoms of errors, and error codes. The query interface is provided to retrieve Es based on machine types, symptoms, error codes, and a document identi<sup>fi</sup>er. Thus, the studied system covers the core functions of knowledge management systems, such as content creation, publication, and access (Alavi & Leidner, 1999; Gupta et al, 2004; SAP, 2011).

## Measurement development

This study developed a scale for measuring knowledgesharing motivations including reward, reputation, reciprocity, and knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy items derived from Kankanhalli et al (2005) and Hsu & Lin (2008). We also modi<sup>fi</sup>ed knowledge-sharing intention items derived from Moon & Kim (2001). In addition, our cultural scale items for individualism/collectivism and uncertainty avoidance are from Yoon (2009). A pretest was conducted with 10 American and 10 Chinese employees. On the basis of the results of the pretest, the <sup>fi</sup>nal 22 items were developed (Appendix). The English measurements were translated into Chinese, and the back-translation method was applied to ensure consistency between the two versions (Mullen, 1995). All of the items were measured using a 7-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7).

## Data collection

This study distributed 480 and 520 questionnaires to the United States and China, respectively. A total of 394 questionnaires were returned (the United States = 194, China = 200), and the total response rate was 39.4%. The majority of the American respondents are employees (84.5%) and have a bachelor’s degree (72.2%). Most of the respondents range from 36 to 45 years of age (39.2%) and have been employed between 11 and 15 years (28.4%). The majority of the Chinese respondents are also employees (89%) and have a bachelor’s degree (64.5%). Most of the respondents tend to be young, with a range from 26 to 35 years old (58.5%), and have less work experience, with a range from 3 to 5 years (36%). ## Results

## Common method bias

To assess common method bias, Harman’s one-factor test is examined using a principal component analysis. If a single construct accounts for more than 50% of the variance, common method bias might threaten the validity (Harman, 1976; Podsakoff et al, 2003; Tang et al, 2006). The results show that the combined seven constructs in the United States and China account for 83.50 and 77.45%, respectively, of the total variance. The variance of the seven constructs ranges from 3.95 to 18.54% in the United States and from 8.42 to 15.79% in China, all of which are less than 50% of the variance.

## Discussion

## Construct comparison

As there are no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in reciprocity, knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and knowledge-sharing intentions between the United States and China, American and Chinese employees have similar attitudes toward these constructs. However, there are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in rewards, reputation, individualism/collectivism, and uncertainty avoidance between the two countries. The mean values for these four constructs in China are higher than they are for those constructs in the United States.

## Comparative analysis of direct effects

Although the United States and China have very different Hofstede’s country scores, this study <sup>fi</sup>nds that reciprocity and knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly related to knowledge-sharing intentions in the two countries. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings conform with prior studies that have been undertaken in a single country (Bock & Kim, 2002; Bock et al, 2005; Kankanhalli et al, 2005; Lin, 2007; Huang et al, 2008; Jeon et al, 2011; Casimir et al, 2012). Our results also demonstrate that the two motivational factors are appropriate to explain and predict knowledge-sharing intentions under the cross-country environment.

Rewards have a direct effect on knowledge-sharing intentions for Chinese employees but not for American employees. Rewards are primarily composed of monetary incentives and promotions. As the surveyed organization offers very limited salary or bonuses for knowledge sharing, the monetary incentives are relatively low (the mean values of the two items are 2.87 and 2.92, respectively). In addition, because the job market in the United States has been stagnant for quite a few years (Quinn, 2010; Sherter, 2013), American employees do not expect improved chances for promotion from knowledge sharing.

The relationship between reputation and knowledgesharing intentions is insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant in the two countries.

This might be because the knowledge management system does not provide reader feedback functions, such as ‘like’ buttons, ratings, and document reviews, which have been integrated into most information-sharing platforms, such as customer reviews for products in Amazon, and reader comments in Blogger and YouTube of Google. These functions can accumulate the bloggers’ personal image, as noted in some studies (Lu & Hsiao, 2007; Miura & Yamashita, 2007; Shao, 2009).

## Comparative analysis of moderating effects

Existing cross-country studies have investigated the effects of knowledge-sharing motivations with model comparison where each country is represented by a distinct model (Lam, 1997; Chow et al, 2000; Weir & Hutchings, 2005; Grif<sup>fi</sup>th et al, 2006; Jiacheng et al, 2010). Individual cultural value plays no roles in the above-mentioned studies. This study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to extend the research from the country’s cultural values to both the individual’s and country’s cultural values in knowledge sharing.

Individualism/collectivism is found to negatively moderate the relationship between rewards and knowledge-sharing intentions in the United States. Employees in an individualistic culture tend to maximize their selfinterest. In this case, they do not expect that knowledge sharing leads to sizable rewards from their organization or management (Sherter, 2013). To make things worse, sharing knowledge wastes their time and energy and might in fact advance other colleagues’ expertise and potentially harm the contributors’ interest. Therefore, although the employees believe that rewards for knowledge sharing are important, they are unwilling to share knowledge.

Individualism/collectivism has no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating effect on the relationship between rewards and knowledge-sharing intentions in China. For most Chinese employees, the monetary incentives are enough to motivate them to share their knowledge. However, individualistic employees in China could not be effectively motivated by the monetary incentives because they view knowledge sharing possibly harmful to their future bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. As China’s job market is booming in recent years (Rabinovitch, 2013; Yaolan, 2013), individualistic employees expect high chances of being promoted or hired to other organizations with better offerings by the advancement of expertise. As these employees view knowledge as a stepping-stone to a higher position or to better offerings in other organizations, their knowledgesharing intentions are low. The converse dual effects reduce the strength of individualism/collectivism. Therefore. rewards do not significantly affect individualistic employees to share knowledge in China.

Individualism/collectivism is not found to moderate the relationship between reputation and knowledgesharing intentions in the United States. Americans are characterized as individualistic (Hofstede, 1980)

because the United States has a high score of 91 on individualism/collectivism (Hofstede, 2001). People with individualistic tendencies are expected to take care of their own interests (Choi & Geistfeld, 2004; Lee et al, 2007). As increasing reputation is one of the ways to enhance personal interests (Kankanhalli et al, 2005; Wasko & Faraj, 2005), individualistic employees in the United States should be more inclined to pursue recognition from others. However, because the interfaces do not provide any mechanism to show reader appreciation, the effect of the reputation enhancement is greatly reduced. The converse dual effects reduce the strength of individualism/collectivism. Therefore, reputation does not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence individualistic employees to share knowledge in the United States.

Individualism/collectivism has no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating effect on the relationship between reciprocity and knowledge-sharing intentions in the two countries. Reciprocity refers to the belief that current knowledge sharing would lead to future requests for knowledge being met (Davenport & Prusak, 1998; Kankanhalli et al, 2005). Reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts tend to be derived from longterm mutual cooperation (Bock et al, 2005; Kankanhalli et al, 2005). People with individualistic inclinations tend to have their interests ful<sup>fi</sup>lled in the short term. Therefore, reciprocity does not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly motivate individualistic employees to share knowledge.

Uncertainty avoidance is not found to moderate the relationship between knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and knowledge-sharing intentions in the United States. The reason might be from the dual effects of individual and country cultures. As Western people have an independent construal of self, they tend to express their own thoughts, feelings, and opinions (Markus & Kitayama, 1991; Heine et al, 1992). As a result, American employees with strong uncertainty avoidance tendencies would also be likely to post their opinions. The converse dual effects might reduce the strength of uncertainty avoidance. Therefore knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy does not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect high uncertainty avoidance employees to share knowledge in the United States.

## Implications for research and practice

## Implications for research

Social exchange theory has been widely used to investigate knowledge-sharing motivations and intentions. In this theory, rewards, reputation, reciprocity, and knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy are considered to be motivational factors for knowledge sharing. Most of the knowledgesharing studies have mainly discovered peripheral constructs that might in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge-sharing intentions but not considered the effects of cultural values (Bock & Kim, 2002; Bock et al, 2005; Kankanhalli et al, 2005; Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Lin, 2007; Hsu & Lin, 2008; Huang et al, 2008; Hung et al, 2011; Jeon et al, 2011; Casimir et al, 2012).

Although some studies have acknowledged the effects of the country’s values, they have mainly focused on the differences between countries (Lam, 1997; Chow et al, 2000; Weir & Hutchings, 2005; Grif<sup>fi</sup>th et al, 2006; Jiacheng et al, 2010). They found that cultural differences among countries indeed in<sup>fl</sup>uence individual beliefs and behavioral intentions. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings are also con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by the results obtained in this study. For example, rewards are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly related to knowledgesharing intentions for Chinese employees but not for American employees, but reciprocity and knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with knowledgesharing intentions in the two countries.

This study extends the assessments of cultural effects from the country level to both the country and individual levels. The results con<sup>fi</sup>rm that individual cultural values indeed moderate the effects of motivational factors on knowledge-sharing intentions. In particular, individualism/collectivism and uncertainty avoidance play important roles in knowledge sharing. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that individualism/collectivism moderates the effect of rewards on knowledge-sharing intentions in the United States. In China, individualism/collectivism moderates the effect of reputation on knowledge-sharing intentions, while uncertainty avoidance moderates the effect of knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy on knowledge-sharing intentions.

This study reveals the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of the individual’s and country’s cultural values on knowledge-sharing motivations and intentions. Our results suggest that future research studying knowledge sharing should consider the effects of individual cultural values, individualism/ collectivism, and uncertainty avoidance especially, when the sources of the samples are constrained to a single country. When sampling is performed across country boundaries, cultural effects at both the country and individual levels should be examined.

## Implications for practice

This study provides insights to help foster knowledgesharing intentions in two different countries: the United States and China. Rewards are found to be more important for Chinese employees than for American employees. Chinese employees consider rewards, such as salary incentives, bonuses, promotions, or job security when sharing knowledge. This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that managers in China should encourage their employees’ knowledge-sharing intentions by building a good reward system.

The common motivational factors affecting knowledge-sharing intentions in the two countries are reciprocity and knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy. American and Chinese employees tend to place more importance on reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and self-con<sup>fi</sup>dence when sharing knowledge. This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that managers should create and maintain a norm of reciprocity in the workplace to enhance employees’ reciprocal expectations. Moreover, employees who believe in their ability to contribute valuable knowledge tend to share knowledge. This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that managers should improve employees’ self-con<sup>fi</sup>dence and relevant expertise by providing useful feedback and professional training.

In the United States, individualistic employees who emphasize self-interest are less likely to be motivated by the current reward systems. This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that managers could have to invest more resources to motivate these employees. However, because many managers view rewards as temporary incentives (Kelman, 1958), any increase in the rewards should proceed with caution. Because the possible reason for reputation not being moderated by individualism/collectivism in the United States is that the knowledge management system does not provide proper interfaces to show reader appreciation, the version of knowledge management systems deployed in the United States should be enhanced with reader feedback functions, such as customer reviews for products in Amazon, and reader comments in Blogger and YouTube of Google. On the other hand, individualistic employees in China have been motivated by the current interfaces. To keep the system interfaces as simple as possible, the feedback functions might not need to be implemented in China.

In China, strong uncertainty avoidance employees are unwilling to share knowledge even if they have high knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy. This <sup>fi</sup>nding means that managers cannot simply increase their employees’ selfcon<sup>fi</sup>dence. We suggest that organizations can offer support to reduce uncertainty and risk in knowledge sharing. For example, through team support, experienced members can provide assistance to other members who intend to share the knowledge. The supports can include a preview of the knowledge shared and can provide templates for sharing speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of knowledge. These approaches have also been proposed and veri<sup>fi</sup>ed in prior studies to be ef<sup>fi</sup>cient measures for enhancing self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy (Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Igbaria & Iivari, 1995).

## Conclusions and limitations

The purpose of this study is to explore the effects of the individual’s and country’s cultural values on knowledgesharing motivations and intentions. First, based on social exchange theory, we examine the motivational factors affecting knowledge-sharing intentions in the United States and China. The results show that reciprocity and knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact knowledge-sharing intentions in the two countries. However, rewards are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly related to knowledge-sharing intentions for Chinese employees but not for American employees. Second, we examine whether the effects of individual cultural values signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly moderate the relationships between knowledge-sharing motivations and intentions in the two countries. The results show that individualism/collectivism is found to moderate the relationship between rewards and knowledge-sharing intentions in the United States. In China, individualism/collectivism is found to moderate the relationship between reputation and knowledge-sharing intentions, while uncertainty avoidance is found to moderate the relationship between knowledge self-ef<sup>fi</sup>- cacy and knowledge-sharing intentions.

This study notes some limitations that require further examination and additional research. First, the generalizability of our results might be limited because the surveyed sample is from only a single multinational corporation. Second, we focus only on the motivational factors provided by bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Future studies should examine the costs affecting knowledge-sharing intentions, such as a loss of knowledge power.
