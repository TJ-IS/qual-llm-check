## Introduction

Cloud computing is an evolution of computer technology and a dominant business model for delivering information technology (IT) infrastructure, components, and applications. With cloud computing, a product-centric model for IT provisioning is transformed into a global, distributed, service-centric model, leading to a disruptive shift from IT-as-a-product to IT-as-a-service [4, 24]. Cloud computing enables individuals and organizations to access IT resources on-demand, from any platform or device at any time as a measured service [32]. Cloud providers offer an everincreasing number and variety of services that are built on a shared pool of computing resources and able to elastically scale to growing computing demands. Market researchers predict the public cloud market to hit US\$ 236 billion by 2020, a growth of 23 percent from 2014 [16]. Defying initial concerns, the move toward cloud computing proceeds with unrelenting force, changing the way we collaborate, co-create, and do business.

We rely on cloud services in our daily lives, for example, for messaging (e.g., WhatsApp), collaborating in teams (e.g., Asana), managing businesses (e.g., SAP ByDesign), and playing games online (e.g., GamingAnywhere). Cloud computing also provides the infrastructure that has powered other key digital trends including mobile computing, the internet of things, big data, and artificial intelligence, thereby accelerating industry dynamics, disrupting existing business models, and fueling the digital transformation [7, 22]. Today, cloud computing has become a critical IT infrastructure for almost all aspects of our everyday lives, and it will continue to transform the world we live in on multiple levels and in various ways.

While the interest in cloud computing has continued undiminished, the focus of research has been primarily on adoption, operational issues, and cloud computing impacts on IT value. Researchers have intensively studied individuals’ and organizations’ cloud service adoption [3, 5, 8, 20, 27, 34, 37] and usage intention [6] as well as the suitability of incentives in cloud service contracts [41]. Other research has analyzed the role of cloud computing as an enabler for organizations to become more agile and adapt to changes [2] and how to organize IT functions when using cloud services [10, 47]. Related streams of research have analyzed cloud business models [18, 26, 35] and cloud service supply chains [13], as well as pricing and licensing models for cloud service offerings [9, 30]. Technologically focused literature addresses the issues of cloud service security by evaluating the security environment and proposing technical safeguards [1, 11].

Despite these important advances, prior research has dedicated far less attention to the transformative and value-creating capacity of cloud computing. We understand the transformative value of a technology as the realized or unrealized potential that widespread diffusion of this technology leads to fundamental and large-scale innovations that benefit individuals, organizations, markets, and societies. Transformative value sets itself apart from traditional dimensions of IT value that largely focuses on the economic value of IT within or between specific firms or organizations. Due to its inherent capabilities (e.g., on-demand self-service, resource pooling, elasticity, and extensibility), cloud computing exhibits transformative mechanisms that enable truly innovative services and business models, ultimately creating a wealth of possibilities for individuals, teams, organizations, and societies.

To guide future research in cloud computing, this Special Section Introduction presents a framework for research, the Transformative Impact of Cloud Computing (TICC) framework. The TICC framework delineates IT value from transformative value and describes three key mechanisms through which cloud computing capabilities, individually and in combination, generate transformative impacts (hereafter referred to as transformative mechanisms): (1) decoupling, (2) platformization, and (3) recombination of services. We first evaluate how the transformative mechanisms arise from each of the technical layers of cloud computing (i.e., infrastructure resources, components, and applications). Then, we analyze and extrapolate how each of the transformative mechanisms leads to impacts on individuals, organizations, and societies/economies (i.e., hereafter also referred to as the real-world layers). We close by presenting an agenda for future research and mapping the three papers included in this Special Section to the Transformative Impact of Cloud Computing (TICC) framework.

## Transformative Impact of Cloud Computing Framework

Cloud computing is defined as “a model for enabling ubiquitous, convenient, ondemand network access to a shared pool of configurable computing resources [. . .] that can be rapidly provisioned and released with minimal management effort or service provider interaction” [32]. Cloud computing technology and services are commonly divided by the types of the resources provided “as-a-service” into three layers with increasing levels of abstraction: infrastructure resources, components, and entire applications. Infrastructure-as-a-service (IaaS) offerings comprise computing resources including computational power, storage, and networking that are made available as virtualized resources over the Internet. Components include platform-as-a-service (PaaS) offerings with ready-made components for building cloud-based applications and development and execution environments as well as complementing services provided by third-parties, for example, via Application

Programming Interfaces (APIs). Cloud applications include software as a service (SaaS), for instance, cloud applications provided in public cloud deployment models as well as cloud applications hosted in private or hybrid clouds.

The TICC framework in Figure 1 illustrates how these technical cloud computing layers impact actuality (the “real world”) through three transformative mechanisms: (1) decoupling, (2) platformization, and (3) recombination. These mechanisms are processes afforded by one or multiple technological capabilities of cloud technology and they can occur individually, in combination, or sequentially over time. Their distinct effects, however, likely amplify each other causing progressively increasing transformative impacts, which can be witnessed through sociotechnical changes in the real world. Today, cloud computing impacts are already observable and tangible on the micro level (i.e., on individuals), and they become more indirect and temporally delayed toward the higher levels. The reasons for these delays of impacts lie in the innovation diffusion processes that are characterized by various inhibitors. Inhibitors on individual and societal levels include information privacy, information security, and other regulatory concerns [28]; in addition to that, on the organizational level integration and compliance play a role [29, 39]. While cloud computing contributes to creating IT value on the technology layer, the impact on the higher real-world layers generates transformative value.

## Transformative Mechanisms of Cloud Computing

Decoupling describes a process in which one element of a system becomes an independent service with a defined service interface such that internal changes in this service will not disrupt the functioning of other dependent elements [44]. In cloud computing, decoupling started on the infrastructure level. Enabled by virtualization techniques, application systems have become independent from their underlying physical resources. For example, large resource-intensive applications run on virtualized layers that utilize multiple physical instances. Virtualization allows elastically scaling these resources up and down―a key characteristic of cloud computing.

Cloud computing also exploited and spurred increasing decoupling on the component level. While modularization and decoupling has been a long trend in software engineering in both monolithic and distributed systems, widespread web service protocols (e.g., SOAP, REST) have increased the level of decoupling and made the reuse of third-party services more commonplace. Applications use functionality and resources from remote services, for example, for retrieving geolocation information from Google maps.

On the cloud application level, decoupling has led to a greater separation between those who use applications and those who provide them―a logical continuation of the outsourcing trend [37, 38]. Traditional IT outsourcing arrangements were singletenant with IT resources dedicated to a specific user organization [48]. Cloud computing services are provided for use by multiple tenants with a postulate of minimal service provider interaction, which further decouples the provider–user relationship.

The second mechanism, platformization, builds on decoupling and characterizes the process in which an entity (a provider organization) creates access and interaction opportunities centered around a core bundle of services (the platform) within an ecosystem of consumers, complementors, and other stakeholders [12, 44]. On all cloud computing layers, new players have emerged that develop capabilities by providing infrastructure resources, applications, and component services, leading to the commercial reality of IaaS, SaaS, and eventually PaaS. Consumers harness cloud platforms to gain access to easily connectable services, and are able to satisfy their unique needs as cloud platforms provide greater variety, service quality, and business flexibility.

On the resource layer, Amazon, for example, made unused virtualized computing resources available to the external market with offerings such as the EC2 (the elastic cloud) and swiftly became a market leader for IaaS. From a consumer side, the large data centers of public cloud providers enabled practically infinite possibilities for scaling computing resources. On the component level, PaaS providers, such as Google App Engine, IBM Bluemix, and Heroku, bundled online development and execution environments with an increasing number of services, which allowed developers to design cloud-based applications from reusable components rather than building software from scratch. On the application level, cloud-native software vendors such as Salesforce.com specialized in specific enterprise software segments and, thereby, earned tremendous success since they provided cost-effective solutions to markets that would otherwise not have afforded these systems. These IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS providers have quickly turned into platform businesses and rallied around them an ecosystem of consumers, complementors, and other third parties that exchange knowledge and other resources with or via the platform [19]. Cloud platform players have gained the critical size to let their ecosystems flourish and co-create new services, which ultimately benefits cloud consumers.

Recombination, the third transformative mechanism, refers to the process in which innovation potential is generated by combining cloud services and integrating them with other transformative technologies within and across platform ecosystems. Recombination takes place on all three technical cloud layers. For example, IaaS platforms allow third-parties (complementors) to offer basic resources (e.g., preconfigured virtual instances, internet of things utilities) on marketplaces where users can choose from myriads of configuration options that will fulfill their specific needs. On the component level, PaaS has opened the door to the application programming interface (API) economy [42], which not only allow PaaS platforms such as Amazon AWS, Microsoft Azure, and IBM Bluemix complementors to offer value adding components (e.g., analytics, artificial intelligence, and blockchains), but allow developers to integrate cross-platform via APIs with other open services from the programmable web to provide the best of breed solutions that were previously unthinkable. On the application level, all major software vendors aim to cultivate marketplaces with apps from third-party developers that offer similar innovation opportunities that the platform alone could never seize. Force.com, SAP App Center, and Microsoft Dynamics Marketplace are only a few examples from the large enterprise software vendors that have successfully created the app marketplace in the enterprise software world.

The three described mechanisms—decoupling, platformization, and recombination in themselves propel a transformation of the IT industry. From a cloud user organization perspective, however, cloud computing has been traditionally viewed as a means to generate IT value, for instance, economic advantages through the deployment and use of IT [25, 37]. IT value can result from different dimensions including IT efficiency (e.g., reducing cost of IT operations), business effectiveness (e.g., reducing cost of business operations), and innovation (e.g., generating new business through IT) [33]. For example, through decoupling IT resources from their use, IT providers (internal and external) generate economies of scale that are shared with their (internal or external) consumers. Through platformization of cloud services, cloud user organizations today find a seemingly inexhaustible market of cloud resources, components, and applications that will provide for almost every (business) need. Through recombination, user organizations have the ability to innovate by building on best-of-breed components from cloud platforms and integrate with partners along their value chain. In sum, from a user organization standpoint, cloud computing enables the creation of IT value to businesses through the use of cloud applications, components, and resources.

## Transformative Impact of Cloud Computing on Real World Layers

It is important for scholars and practitioners to understand how the three transformative mechanisms (i.e., decoupling, platformization, and recombination) impact value on the real world layers that go above and beyond IT value.

The benefits that decoupling has can be characterized by attributes such as flexibility, ubiquity, and accessibility. On the individual level, ubiquitous access and self-service opportunities allow consumers, citizens, and employees to draw on and leverage virtual resources (e.g., data and applications) more flexibly on the go and independent of their location. Almost any application system can be used as a service nowadays (“everything-as-a-service”), and increasing bandwidth and scalability of cloud computing infrastructures allow the supply and consumption of datarich services. Cloud platforms that build their value propositions around online streaming services such as YouTube showcase how cloud infrastructures have greatly changed the way people interact with and learn from one another. On a more critical note, ubiquitous access to virtual resources can also lead to an increased blurring of work-life domains (e.g., using a private Dropbox account on a business laptop) changing employees’ behavioral patterns, rhythms, and work-life balance that may require new coping strategies against technostress [43].

Decoupling through cloud computing also allows more flexible work patterns in organizations. Offering virtual or distributed working spaces in firms will be the norm rather than the exception in the future. The gamut ranges from so-called “coffices”—the coffee shop-offices where road warriors go to connect, catch up, and restart their work—to co-working spaces that are dedicated professional work spaces with guaranteed facilities. Decoupling will also allow organizations to more flexibly outsource parts or their entire IT stack to cloud providers and scale cloud services in tune with fluctuations in demand. Avira, an IT security software company, for example, hosts computer protection services via the AWS cloud that scan millions of consumer files by extracting them to the cloud for threat detection before returning them to the local device.

On higher levels of the TICC framework, cloud computing has the potential to help developing countries reap the benefits of IT without the significant upfront investments that have stymied past efforts. Decoupling through cloud computing on a global level means that IT applications and infrastructures can be used wherever there is a (reasonable fast and reliable) Internet connection. In fact, cloud computing might do to computing in developing countries what mobile phones did for communications.

Platformization through cloud computing creates new ecosystems of value on higher real-world layers linking multiple stakeholders and emphasizing concepts such as transparency, elasticity, and agility. For private users or consumers, various platform business models leverage cloud computing to enable new forms of collaboration and sharing [49]. For example, sharing (or collaborative, Gig) economy platforms (e.g., AirBnB, Etsy, Getaround) facilitate the supply of individuals’ or their owned physical objects’ spare capacity. Cloud computing technologies are foundational in this regard by providing high information transparency and realtime consumer services. Cloud computing technology is also increasingly permeating e-government platforms for which distributed, secure, and elastic computing is vital to offer services to citizens such as lodging tax returns, issuing identity cards, or other administrative procedures. Singapore is a worthwhile role model that offers citizens and businesses more than 1,600 online services and more than 300 mobile services, many of which leverage cloud computing technologies. Numerous Open Government Data (OGD) initiatives are also based on cloud computing platforms, connecting citizens, third-party complementors, government policy makers, and public administrators.

Platformization through cloud computing can also help organizations increase their business agility in various ways. For example, some organizations leverage the possibilities of rapid experimentation with new services in cloud environments through methods such as A/B testing (i.e., hypothesis-testing methods often used in online settings). PaaS and IaaS technologies offer an adequate sandbox to quickly experiment with alternative options and ideas that may lead to substantial changes in the product/service landscape or even the strategic thrust of a company. Netflix, for example, has built testing into its DNA, through simultaneously A/B-testing hundreds of variants of its website and apps in the cloud and measuring their impact on viewing hours.

Platformization also brings about opportunities to provide ecological and environmental benefits to economies and societies. The move to the cloud is an integral part for building the much needed digital infrastructure, which has also inherent green benefits, since it is less expensive to transport computing services than energy. Cloud providers that want to credibly position their services as green mega-data centers powered by renewable energy sources such as wind, solar, or hydroelectricity. Taken together, cloud computing holds the potential to be an integral part of green IT and sustainability initiatives to improve the ecological balance.

Lastly, the recombination of decentralized resources and service components via cloud computing platforms creates a plethora of new opportunities that often emerge in originally unintended ways and offer new ways of value co-creation [21, 48]. Cloud computing can be seen as a strong transformational engine that fosters concepts such as innovation, co-creation, and openness. Individual users, for example, increasingly leverage open cloud-based platforms to invent, as well as codevelop new products or interact as citizens to collaboratively work on innovative and sustainable services of future smart cities. As a digital infrastructure, cloud computing thus makes highly interactive participatory processes possible at a local and global level and creates a space of possibilities that can serve as a melting pot for open innovation.

Cloud computing also provides the backbone infrastructure for smart products and services including smart home appliances and smart city solutions. Almost anything—person, object, process or service—can become digitally aware and networked through cloud computing and the internet of things and can be made smarter by turning the mountains of data gathered from their various interactions into real insights using sophisticated algorithms and powerful supercomputers. This kind of information-based artificial intelligence is helping organizations and economies become more efficient, productive, and responsive. There is a good chance that companies of various industries will rally to enter this arena to cocreate products and services as complementors of wider platform ecosystems. In the networked home, for example, companies from different industries providing services such as utility, heating, food delivery, and entertainment team up to provide integrated services. The next wave of digitized cars with their radar sensors, navigation systems, and built-in cellular connections become rolling listening posts that track driving habits, log queries to websites, record media usage, and even alert drivers when they are about to break traffic rules. Automakers, local governments, retailers, insurers, and media companies are eager to leverage this information by recombining each of their strengths, especially as cars transform from computers on wheels into self-driving shuttles.

On a societal and economic level, cloud computing is an enabler of the digital transformation of industries. For example, in the healthcare sector, ever-present sensors combined with apps and systems that emphasize human-centered design and advanced analytics to analyze the accrued data volumes could collectively revolutionize healthcare and wellness. To secure a central position in a new medical ecosystem, established companies such as IBM, General Electric, or

Siemens offer their own Health Clouds, which not only sell server capacity, but also rent software for the healthcare system. Such cloud services not only help connect the different parties along the healthcare value chain (e.g., treating physicians, clinics, aftercare providers) but also facilitate clinical workflows and the exchange of health information. By linking to millions of healthcare devices including imaging machines, patient monitoring, diagnostics, and various other data sources, health cloud platforms may cover a holistic and consistent patient journey that recombines insights from various touchpoints. ## Directions for Future Research

Our TICC framework in Figure 1 is intended to offer a theoretical foundation for future research on cloud computing’s transformational impact. To spawn future research in this stream, we propose a research agenda as shown in Table 2 and present a brief sampling of opportunities for expanding research into the transformative value of cloud computing.

1. Advancing knowledge on decoupling, platformization, and recombination mechanisms: We view decoupling, platformization, and recombination as potentially significant mechanisms to yield transformative value. Past information systems (IS) research has delineated the concept of generativity from layered modular architectures, which describe a “technology’s overall capacity to produce unprompted change driven by large, varied, and uncoordinated audiences” [48]. Generativity is facilitated through modularity and re-combinability within and across layers whereby innovations can pop up independently at any layer, leading to ripple effects on other layers generating multiple co-creation opportunities [44, 51]. Cloud computing is a crucial backbone technology to bring about and nurture such generativity and it may be fruitful to understand the pathways and mechanisms of such generativity that unfold within and across cloud computing layers and platforms. For example, future research may want to unpack how value emerges from recombining distributed resources located in the cloud, how it cascades through different cloud computing layers, and how it is appropriated by organizations, economies, and societies.

By orchestrating an ecosystem of complementors, consumers, and other thirdparties, platform-based cloud computing providers create transformative value by controlling a nonlinear value chain. Cloud computing platform ecosystems have emerged as a unique boundary condition to understand transformative value creation. As individuals, organizations, and societies are digitalized in cloud computing platforms, privacy and security issues move to the forefront [15, 40]. Although existing studies have identified these concerns and have begun to devote effort in developing tools and software product strategies to better protect and prove privacy and information security (e.g., [1, 11, 50]), we call for additional research directly examining the causal impacts of the strategic use of cloud computing on privacy and information security related outcomes at different levels. Understanding the privacy and security consequences of cloud computing usage will benefit firms’ and societies’ formulation of strategies in ensuring positive transformative impacts.

Looking at the transformative impact that has shaped economies, for example, future research may take a deeper look at cloud computing’s transformative value on economic growth and environmental sustainability, including longevity of cloud service business models in highly dynamic service markets, and the long-term profitability of green data centers powered by renewable energy sources. Worldwide ubiquitous access to cloud services has blurred boundaries of countries and their local legislations over time [37], leading to a pressing need for new, supranational regulations, directives and norms as well as innovative means to (continuously) prove compliance to ensure security and privacy (e.g., new EU-General Data Protection Regulation)—comparable to the diffusion of cars that lead to the development of innovative traffic regulations.

Taking a societal perspective, it is still an open question whether the ubiquitous access to cloud services and the changing usage patterns of the young versus the elderly will aggravate or alleviate the challenges of the digital divide [45]. Given cloud computing’s potential for facilitating information transparency and inter-connections among individuals and organizations, cloud computing enabled platforms or e-government initiatives may play a more direct role in revolutionizing society. Thus, efforts in understanding the transformative impact of cloud-enabled platforms and initiatives on societal challenges such as inequality, corruption, cybercrime, globalization, and trading fairness could be increased in future research.

3. Contextualizing transformative cloud computing impact in business ecosystems: Although we theorized three important mechanisms (i.e., decoupling, platformization, and recombination) of achieving cloud computing’s transformative impacts, it is worth noting that not all mechanisms are prerequisites for value creation. Relationships among platform owners, complementors, partners, competitors, and consumers are context-specific units of analysis [23]. Therefore, depending on crucial contextual factors, cloud computing may generate business value through a single mechanism or a combination of two or all three mechanisms. It is important to understand what could be contextual factors and the configurations of mechanisms to generate and sustain cloud computing’s transformative influences. To do so, future research can develop configurational theories of cloud computing’s transformative value. The methodological advancement of fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) provides a great foundation to explore this important research stream [14].

We particularly encourage sector-specific inquiries on cloud-enabled business ecosystems. While most cloud providers (e.g., Amazon, Salesforce) have turned into multipurpose digital platforms that orchestrators tailor to their unique preferences, on the higher layers cloud technology enables business community platforms (i.e., platforms tailored to the needs of a specific user or business community—e.g., Netflix tailored Amazon to video streaming and Veeva tailored Salesforce to the life science industry). Business communities share unique commoditized business processes [31], which represent an underexploited opportunity for research to immerse into the idiosyncrasies of industry-specific platforms that leverage cloud computing [46].

Finally, industries take a different pace in traversing the underlying decoupling– platformization–recombination mechanisms. Healthcare, for example, is commonly thought of as a slower moving sector with specific barriers—famously, Google pulled out of their health data platform. Future research may address how cloud computing, in combination with other capabilities, can successfully enable health information exchange and, thus, support health-specific processes in prevention, treatment, and care (e.g., cloud-based processing of big genome data) [17]. Other industries have embraced cloud technology at a much faster pace. In the automotive industry, for example, cloud computing enables the combination of in-vehicle onboard data with environmental off-board data and, thus, helps bring to bear valueadding or even ground-breaking services such as remote diagnostics or autonomous driving. The Fintech revolution would be unthinkable without underlying cloud capabilities and even large incumbent banking technology is soon to be replaced by industry-specific cloud infrastructures. To date, we know a minimal amount regarding how these industry platforms create and appropriate value [36]. Future research should embark on examining the generative capacity and transformative value of cloud computing in specific industries. IS scholars should also develop contextualized theories on how contextual factors (such as industry characteristics, market dynamics, consumer attributes, and macro-environment structures) shift the mechanisms of cloud computing’s transformative impacts.

## Positioning Papers in the TICC framework

The Special Section received 38 submissions, of which three papers appear here. These best of the best research papers showcase how high-quality academic research can address real-world problems regarding the transformative capacity of cloud computing. They span across diverse levels and mechanisms of the TICC framework as indicated by the numbered arrows in Figure 1. The authors come from different parts of the world and use a variety of research methods reflecting the diversity and richness of the work in our research community.

The paper by Kathuria, Mann, Khuntia, Saldanha, and Kauffman, titled “A Path for Strategic Value Appropriation for Cloud Computing,” focuses on three cloud computing-based organizational capabilities, namely cloud technology, cloud integration, and cloud service portfolios, and how they can directly and indirectly affect firm performance via an organization’s business flexibility. Integrating the resourcebased view with the perspective of operand and operant resource hierarchies, the authors conduct a cross-sectional field survey of 147 cloud user organizations in India to show that cloud technology deployment must go hand in hand with the development of cloud integration and service capabilities to increase business value through making firms more flexible and agile. A noteworthy feature of the study is the conceptualization of the cloud’s value-creating capacity through a strategic value appropriation path from cloud implementation to business value creation. A broader finding of the study points to the necessity that firms, in order to appropriate the business value from cloud computing more comprehensively, must evolve from an artifact-focused view toward a more integrated and service-oriented view of cloud computing. Mapping the paper to our TICC framework, the study spans multiple transformative mechanisms at the organizational level as indicated by the numbered Arrow 1 in Figure 1.

The paper “Key Affordances of Platform-as-a-Service: Self-Organization and Continuous Feedback” by Krancher, Luther, and Jost provides a substantive theory how the two essential cloud characteristics of elasticity and abstraction afford continuous feedback and ultimately enable agility in software development teams. The authors used the grounded-theory method based on data from 48 interviews at

16 software development teams to explore the impacts of hitherto under-researched cloud services on the PaaS level. The study builds a crucial linkage between the cloud literature and the software development literature that has long been interested in the move to self-organizing (or organizational decoupling) and continuous feedback. Worth noting, the authors found explanations for variations in the level to which teams actualized PaaS affordances in two work environment characteristics. In an effort toward a theory of transformative cloud impacts, they extrapolate their findings on the role of work environment characteristics on other cloud service models, such as SaaS. Mapped to our TICC framework, this study showcases the impacts of the decoupling mechanism on the PaaS level both towards generation of IT value through agility, as well as the impact of decoupling on organizational work practices, as indicated by the numbered Arrows 2 in Figure 1.

The third paper, “Harnessing the Power of the Cloud: Revenue, Fairness, and Cloud Neutrality,” by Joe-Wong and Sen presents a holistic analytical framework that accounts for user utility, endogenous pricing decisions of a cloud provider, and fairness in multi-resource allocation. The introduced framework discusses and considers provider prices and combines them along with resource allocation decisions to debate on revenue, fairness, and trade-offs between revenue and fairness in cloud computing environments. One of the main findings of this study is the optimization formulation to quantify the fairness-revenue trade-off for varying degrees of similarity in resource requirements of users in a cloud context. By comparing the actions of a cloud provider to that of a regulator’s benchmark, this work promises to help in the design of economic policies that produce socially desirable outcomes. Regarding the transformative value of cloud computing, this paper provides insights into when regulatory interventions for cloud platform providers with dominant market positions may be needed and how they can be implemented to make a step towards neutrality in the cloud computing market, as highlighted by Arrow 3 in Figure 1.
