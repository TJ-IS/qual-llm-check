# The bene<sup>fi</sup>t of information asymmetry: When to sell to informed customers?

Hemant K. Bhargava <sup>1</sup>, Rachel R. Chen ⁎

Graduate School of Management, University of California Davis, Davis, CA 95616, United States

a r t i c l e i n f o

Available online 1 February 2012

Keywords: Buyer valuation uncertainty Product sampling Product preview Advance selling Informed consumers Information disadvantage

## a b s t r a c t

Buyers are often uncertain about product valuations before they commit to purchase. In such situations, <sup>fi</sup>rms have an opportunity to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the accuracy with which buyers can estimate their valuations. When buyers are uncertain about the product's <sup>fi</sup>t with their personal preferences, <sup>fi</sup>rms can help resolve this uncertainty by offering product previews, sampling, trials or return guarantees. When a buyer's valuation uncertainty is caused by unpredictability in their consumption state, <sup>fi</sup>rms can choose advance selling – which makes buyers decide while they are unsure about their valuations – or enable buyers to make an informed decision by offering spot sales. This paper examines <sup>fi</sup>rms' incentive to adopt mechanisms that endow buyers with private information – hence increase information asymmetry against the <sup>fi</sup>rm – and are mean preserving in the aggregate across the market. Prior studies suggest that in this setting, selling to uninformed buyers is advantageous because it presents the <sup>fi</sup>rm with a narrower dispersion in valuations, making it easier to extract consumer surplus. Countering this intuition, we show that when the market consists of distinct heterogeneous consumer segments ex ante, selling to privately informed buyers can improve the <sup>fi</sup>rm's pro<sup>fi</sup>t. This is because under certain conditions, the demand curve can become <sup>fl</sup>atter in a relatively high price range as buyers become informed, enabling the <sup>fi</sup>rm to extract higher surplus. Our work suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms may want to employ product preview mechanisms even when they increase dispersion in valuations while only maintaining (rather than increasing) the mean. Our study highlights the interplay between buyer valuation uncertainty and consumer heterogeneity in determining a <sup>fi</sup>rm's selling strategy.

## 1. Introduction

Buyers are often uncertain about their product valuations before they commit to purchase. This can occur for several reasons. For products with complex features, buyers may be unsure about the fit between product characteristics and their personal preferences. For products purchased well in advance, buyers may be uncertain about their state at the time of consumption, which can be affected by unpredictable factors such as health, mood, companions, workload, travel, or scheduling con<sup>fl</sup>icts. Additional information can help resolve valuation uncertainty. Lewis and Sappington [20] note that <sup>fi</sup>rms can control buyers' valuation uncertainty by offering product trials or return guarantees, sponsoring training seminars, or by advertising features for which consumers have idiosyncratic preferences. For digital experience and information goods, product sampling is a common way to control buyers' valuation uncertainty [6,34]. When it is possible to separate purchase from consumption, this control can be exercised through the sale timing decision: advance selling makes buyers decide based on their expected valuations, whereas spot selling allows buyers to make an informed decision after they become aware of their consumption state. Modern information technologies such as e-tickets and web retailing allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to sell products far in advance of consumption, which can render buyers more uncertain about the value they will receive upon consuming the product in the future [8,30].

This article studies a seller's incentives in enabling potential buyers to become better informed about their product valuations, even when such learning occurs privately to the buyer, thereby increasing information asymmetry and putting the seller at an information disadvantage. Providing information to consumers, or giving them time to become better informed, transforms the demand curve through revision in valuations of individual consumers. To examine the impact of this tactic, we focus on information that, while it can in<sup>fl</sup>uence individual buyers' valuations upwards or downwards, is mean-preserving across the market [12,20,36]. In such cases, should the <sup>fi</sup>rm sell to uninformed buyers (those who are unsure about their valuations), or should it allow buyers to become privately informed about their valuations prior to making a purchase?<sup>2</sup> The received wisdom in the literature is that such mean-preserving transformations are detrimental to the seller. Lewis and Sappington [20] show that when marginal cost is very low, the <sup>fi</sup>rm always prefers selling to uninformed buyers. The argument is that enabling buyers to become privately informed increases the dispersion of valuations in the market, making it harder to extract surplus [8,26]. Xie and Shugan [36] vividly demonstrate these arguments with a simple example (reproduced here as Fig. 1). Under advance selling (to uninformed buyers), the <sup>fi</sup>rm extracts the entire expected surplus, therefore spot selling can do no better, even if the <sup>fi</sup>rm perfectly price discriminates consumers in the spot period.

This compelling argument in favor of selling to uninformed consumers is at odds with common practice of product previews and sampling mechanisms. Firms that sell software often provide white papers, limited-time trials and seminars, which enable buyers to become better informed, at the risk that some of them may not purchase if they discover a mis<sup>fi</sup>t during the trials. Online music vendors usually provide previews for music (or music videos) sold on the Internet. Satellite TV providers, such as DirecTV, routinely sell video streaming for a number of games played worldwide. A buyer's valuation for watching a game depends on various factors such as winning and losing teams, player performance, and his own consumption state. Subscriptions for watching the game, however, are typically made available several months in advance, where the price remains the same over time. With no discount for early purchase, buyers will make the purchase after they become con<sup>fi</sup>dent about their consumption utilities. Therefore, the <sup>fi</sup>rm essentially sells to buyers who are informed of their valuations.

In this paper, we show that when the market consists of distinct heterogeneous consumer segments ex ante, selling to informed buyers can be pro<sup>fi</sup>table even when the additional information maintains, rather than increases, the mean valuation. The existing literature on this topic either assumes homogeneous consumers ex ante (e.g., [30,36]), or considers a continuous distribution of heterogeneous consumers (see Section 5 for a detailed discussion). But as discussed in [35], many markets have two (or more) segments where the cross-segment differences are much sharper than within-segment variations. For instance, the demand for online video stream of a live entertainment event consists of a large segment of general viewers and a small segment of music a<sup>fi</sup>cionados with much higher valuations. In such markets, the demand curve can become flatter in a relatively high price range as buyers become informed, leading to higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t. Our analysis underlines the importance of understanding the nature of consumer heterogeneity, and how it interacts with valuation uncertainty in transforming the demand curve. We demonstrate that when there exist distinct consumer segments, merely focusing on dispersion in valuations can lead to incorrect conclusions about selling strategy. Our results suggest that <sup>fi</sup>rms should recognize the pro<sup>fi</sup>t potential by employing product preview mechanisms. Also, while information technologies make advance selling more feasible, it is not necessarily advantageous for <sup>fi</sup>rms to adopt advanced selling more often.

This paper makes two key contributions. It is the <sup>fi</sup>rst, to our knowledge, to identify and explicate how the nature of consumer heterogeneity impacts the <sup>fi</sup>rm's strategic choice regarding selling to informed vs. uninformed buyers. Second, it provides new explanations for why a <sup>fi</sup>rm might want to enable buyers to become privately informed, even though such an action increases information asymmetry against the <sup>fi</sup>rm. To tie our <sup>fi</sup>ndings with practice, we identify three possible motives for a <sup>fi</sup>rm to prefer selling to informed buyers: (i) targeting a set of “premium” consumers who realize high valuations upon being informed, (ii) serving an “expanded middle class” of informed buyers rather than navigating a stark tradeoff between margin and volume when selling to uninformed buyers, and (iii) price discriminating uninformed and informed buyers over time if the <sup>fi</sup>rm can advance sell. These motivations are valid when consumers are moderately heterogeneous ex ante, whereas selling to uninformed buyers is more attractive when the ex ante segments are quite similar or extremely disparate. We also identify practical situations for the <sup>fi</sup>rm to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from these three motivations.

Before proceeding with the main analysis, it is useful to qualitatively compare alternate mechanisms for endowing buyers with private information about their valuations. The product preview mechanism informs consumers about the fit between the product and their personal preferences, and the process usually requires some effort from the seller (e.g., provide sampling or trials) or the buyer (e.g., learn how to use the product) [13,34]. By contrast, in the sale timing problem the uncertainty is mostly caused by the buyer's consumption state, which will be revealed over time (by nature). While existing literatures on these two topics are largely disconnected, both mechanisms allow for strategic control of whether or not selling to informed customers. The product preview mechanism contains two basic strategies: sell to uninformed customers (referred to as uninformed selling), or provide opportunities so that buyers become fully informed when they make the purchase (referred to as informed selling). In the sale timing problem, selling in the advance or spot period corresponds to uninformed or informed selling, respectively. However, two special issues arise due to temporal pricing. First, the sale timing mechanism imposes a temporal sequence between the two alternatives, i.e., uninformed selling can only occur in the advance period. In this case, buyers may strategically delay their purchase by reasoning that the <sup>fi</sup>rm might offer a lower spot price in the future. As a result, it can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to implement uninformed selling (i.e., sell in advance period only). Second, with the sale timing mechanism, the <sup>fi</sup>rm can offer both advance and spot selling, with a higher spot price in return for an informed purchase. In contrast, offering both choices is meaningless under the product preview mechanisms: the buyer can use the higher-priced choice (the product trial) to become informed and then make a purchase under the lower-priced (uninformed) arrangement. In this paper, we <sup>fi</sup>rst introduce the product preview mechanism with two basic models (of selling to uninformed and informed buyers), and then present the sale timing problem with a focus on these two issues.

This paper is particularly relevant to <sup>fi</sup>rms that sell information goods, which are more likely to have uncertain valuations for buyers. Many information products are experience goods [25], for which buyers do not know their true valuations until they consume, rather than merely purchase, the product [6,29]. Examples include computing services, information content (e.g., an industry research report), online information services (e.g., real-time traf<sup>fi</sup>c updates), and online streaming of multimedia content (e.g., a sports or entertainment event). Moreover, it is common for information goods to have consumer segments such that cross-segment preferences have far more diversity than within segment preferences (e.g., novice and IT-savvy, die-hard fans and general spectators, or heavy and light users). Our result is consistent with the observation that markets for information goods are rife with mechanisms that enable buyers to become better informed.

This paper is organized as follows. After a brief literature review in Section 2, we analyze the product preview mechanisms in Section 3. We then modify the analysis to examine the sale timing strategy in Section 4, with a focus on issues related to intertemporal pricing. In

Section 5 we discuss the importance of understanding the nature of consumer heterogeneity and its impact on <sup>fi</sup>rm's strategic choice of selling to informed or uninformed buyers. Section 6 concludes.

## 2. Related literature

Our work is closely related to the research stream that examines a <sup>fi</sup>rm's strategies for information goods under uncertain valuations. Chellappa and Shivendu [6] model the pricing and sampling strategies for digital experience goods in vertically segmented markets. They focus on how to mitigate the effect of piracy, which is an illegal form of sampling that can remove buyers' valuation uncertainty. Wang and Zhang [34] provide an analytical model to study the economic rationale behind free online samples. They also consider two segments of consumers, however the mean of the valuation across the market increases after buyers sample the product. Gundepudi et al. [17] were the <sup>fi</sup>rst to discuss the sale timing problem for information goods and articulate the consumers decision process when evaluating advance and spot purchases. Our result on intertemporal price discrimination is consistent with their insight derived from a simpli-<sup>fi</sup>ed case of two consumers.

Similar to [10], our paper considers uncertainty caused by variation in consumer tastes, rather than due to disbelief about product quality. Lewis and Sappington [20] show that with low marginal cost, selling to uninformed buyers effectively extracts rents from homogeneous consumers, and thus, is more pro<sup>fi</sup>table. DeGraba [12] elegantly illustrates this logic for a product with zero marginal costs. Courty [8] employs this argument to explain why a ticket-<sup>fi</sup>rm should prohibit resale (or refund), while Saak [26] offers a similar logic for selling to ignorant buyers. Johnson and Myatt [19] note that activities such as advertising and variations in product design (general vs. idiosyncratic features) also impact buyer valuations. In the context of Internet-based sales, Granados et al. [16] investigate the implication of displaying product information in the design of online selling mechanisms.

Shugan and Xie [30] point out that buyer valuation uncertainty can arise from the separation of consumption from purchase, because a buyer's valuation often depends on his consumption state, of which he does not have precise knowledge at the time of purchase. They show that <sup>fi</sup>rms can strategically pro<sup>fi</sup>t from such uncertainty by advance selling to uninformed buyers. Other than buyer valuation uncertainty, advance selling can be motivated by price discrimination (as in yield management), demand forecasting [31], and inventory management [32]. On the other hand, <sup>fi</sup>rms may prefer spot selling due to operational factors such as capacity constraints [14], high setup costs for production or inventory reorder [5], or when buyers underestimate product quality [4] or are concerned about product malfunction [21]. Our analysis controls for these factors by assuming zero or sunk variable cost, no capacity constraints, and equal awareness of the product quality by the <sup>fi</sup>rm and buyers. Similar to [30], the driving factor here is buyer valuation uncertainty rather than capacity constraints or price sensitivity of early and late arrivals as in the classical airline-pricing literature. However, our study differs from [30] by allowing heterogeneous customers ex ante.

Our work is also related to the literature on the use of contractual variables such as returns or money-back guarantees [11,24], which in<sup>fl</sup>uence how much information buyers have when their purchase becomes a costly commitment. Offering these mechanisms is akin to enabling buyers to purchase after they become informed, therefore the use of these mechanisms also involves a strategic decision of selling to informed or uninformed buyers. Previous literature shows that these mechanisms work because the <sup>fi</sup>rm has private information about product quality or there are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant transaction costs for returning products. Our study adds another explanation why <sup>fi</sup>rms might want to equip their buyers with more information before purchasing.

## 3. Product preview mechanism

Lewis and Sappington [20] note that <sup>fi</sup>rms can employ mechanisms such as onsite appraisals, training seminars, demonstrations, sampling and test-drives, which enable buyers to obtain additional information about the product before they make a <sup>fi</sup>nal commitment on purchasing the product. A generous return policy also allows buyers to learn different features of the product and make an informed decision. In this paper, we refer to all such arrangements as product preview mechanisms, which endow buyers with private information about the <sup>fi</sup>t between the product and individual preferences.

## 3.1. Model setup

Consider a product consumed by multiple market segments. For an uninformed buyer of segment i, his actual valuation can be either high (H ) or low (L ) with probability α and $1 - \alpha _ { i } ,$ respectively. With additional information, the buyer will become privately informed of his actual valuation, which puts the <sup>fi</sup>rm under information disadvantage. The <sup>fi</sup>rm faces the strategic decision of whether or not to enable buyers to become privately informed before their purchase. Several assumptions adopted by the existing literature are retained in this study [8,12,20,36]: (a) the additional information is meanpreserving across the market, (b) the <sup>fi</sup>rm (or buyers) incurs no extra cost in providing (or obtaining) the information, (c) the values H, L and α are public information, and (d) the <sup>fi</sup>rm is unable to price discriminate within either the informed or uninformed market. Finally, consistent with the focus on information goods, we assume zero marginal cost, which can also be interpreted as unlimited capacity as typically is the case for information goods. Prior literature has shown that positive marginal cost or limited capacity favors selling to informed buyers [20]. Thus, our study provides a conservative setting for selling to informed buyers to be optimal.

To model consumer heterogeneity, we assume that the market consists of two distinct segments $( i = 1 , ~ 2 )$ . Segments 1 and 2 correspond to the mass (high volume, low valuation) and niche (low volume, high valuation) segments, with their sizes normalized to N and 1 (we assume, but do not require, $N { > } 1 )$ . For instance, the demand for video games often consists of a large segment of novice users and a small segment of advanced users. As Segment 2 has higher value, we have $H _ { 2 } > H _ { 1 }$ and $L _ { 2 } > L _ { 1 }$ , whereas L can be either higher or lower than $H _ { 1 }$ . This discrete-segment formalization of heterogeneous consumers distinguishes our work from previous studies on selling to informed vs uninformed buyers, and contributes to the contrast between our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and those of prior research. We elaborate on this issue in Section 5, and comment on the application of our results to settings with continuous demand distributions.

Without additional private information, buyers and the <sup>fi</sup>rm are equally informed about the distribution of valuations, from which they can compute the expected valuations,

$$
v _ {i} = \alpha_ {i} H _ {i} + (1 - \alpha_ {i}) L _ {i}, i = 1, 2.
$$

Without loss of generality, we assume $v _ { 2 } \geq v _ { 1 }$ . Fig. 2 depicts the buyer valuation of the two segments, for the case $H _ { 1 } < L _ { 2 } .$ . Let $( p ^ { u } , p ^ { i } )$ $( q ^ { u } , q ^ { i } )$ and $( \pi ^ { u } , \pi ^ { i } )$ denote the prices, sales and the optimal pro<sup>fi</sup>ts when the <sup>fi</sup>rm sells to uninformed and informed buyers, respectively. The <sup>fi</sup>rm faces two possible strategies: uninformed selling or informed selling. Depending on which strategy produces a higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t, there are two possible equilibrium outcomes speci<sup>fi</sup>ed below. To indisputably demonstrate the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>t in enduring information disadvantage, we follow [36] and take the conservative perspective in de<sup>fi</sup>ning these equilibria, requiring that pro<sup>fi</sup>t π<sup>i</sup> be strictly greater than $\pi ^ { u }$ for informed selling to be optimal.

If the <sup>fi</sup>rm sells to uninformed buyers, a segment i buyer will make a purchase if his expected valuation $\nu _ { i }$ exceeds the price $p ^ { u } .$ . The <sup>fi</sup>rm can either charge a high price $\nu _ { 2 }$ to Segment 2 buyers only, or price lower at $\nu _ { 1 }$ to cover the entire market. Comparing these two cases yields the optimal pro<sup>fi</sup>t of uninformed selling:

## 3.2. When are product previews desirable?

We now examine the desirability of product previews and other mechanisms that enable buyers to become privately informed. We will demonstrate two countervailing forces that transform the demand curve in a way that enables the <sup>fi</sup>rm to extract higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t from informed buyers, despite their more dispersed valuations. Our results complement prior work that sampling of information goods can bene<sup>fi</sup>t the <sup>fi</sup>rm [34]. However, their result relies on a segment of “pessimistic” consumers, whose valuations increase after product sampling. In contrast, we show that the product preview mechanism can be attractive even when it preserves the mean of buyer valuations.

3.2.1. Targeting informed premium buyers: the “baseball batting averages” paradox

As discussed in [20], by endowing potential buyers with private information, the <sup>fi</sup>rm creates opportunities for market segmentation, i.e., selling to subsets of the population who realize high valuations, while forfeiting the ability to sell to all buyers at their expected valuations. For homogeneous consumers ex ante with expected valuation at $\nu = \alpha H + ( 1 - \alpha ) L$ , the <sup>fi</sup>rm will never bene<sup>fi</sup>t from targeting the ex post segment H, because αHbv. In fact, selling at v to uninformed buyers already extracts the entire expected surplus of the market.

In our model setup, there are two distinct segments ex ante. Considering each segment separately, it is optimal to sell to uninformed buyers at the “average” price rather than sell only to buyers who realize high valuations $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \alpha _ { 1 } H _ { 1 } < \nu _ { 1 }$ and $\alpha _ { 2 } H _ { 2 } { < } v _ { 2 } )$ . Remarkably, however, when the market consists of both segments, it can be optimal to sell to informed buyers, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally by targeting the high valuations of both mass and niche segments (at price equal to $H _ { 1 } ) . ^ { 3 }$ This occurs when Segment 1 consumers' high valuation is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high or very likely:

$$
H _ {1} > \frac {\pi^ {u}}{\alpha_ {1} N + \alpha_ {2}} = \frac {\max \left\{v _ {2} , v _ {1} (1 + N) \right\}}{\alpha_ {1} N + \alpha_ {2}}.
$$

From the perspective of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, $\nu _ { i }$ is the maximum price she can charge if she chooses selling to uninformed segment i. De<sup>fi</sup>ne $U _ { i } = H _ { i } - \nu _ { i }$ as segment i buyer's upside potential, representing the revenue gain the <sup>fi</sup>rm can possibly obtain if he endows additional information and the buyer realizes his high valuation. After algebraic simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation, the condition above can be re-written as

$$
U _ {1} > U _ {1} ^ {T} = \max \left\{\frac {N (1 - \alpha_ {1}) + (1 - \alpha_ {2})}{\alpha_ {1} N + \alpha_ {2}} v _ {1}, \left(\frac {v _ {2}}{\alpha_ {1} N + \alpha_ {2}} - v _ {1}\right) \right\},\tag{3}
$$

indicating that if Segment 1 consumers have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant upside potential $U _ { 1 }$ or a high probability $\alpha _ { 1 }$ of realizing it, then it is optimal to target the high valuations of both segments after they become privately informed. This leads to the following result.

Proposition 1. Selling to informed buyers can benefit the firm by enabling it to implement a premium pricing policy that targets only consumers who realize high valuations with additional private information. This happens as long as Segment 1 consumers' upside potential is sufficiently high or very likely, satisfying Condition 3.

Why is selling to an informed market attractive, even though it is not advantageous with respect to each segment? The intuition behind this result parallels the well-known “statistical reversal paradox” (popularly described as the “baseball batting averages” paradox). The counterintuitive aspect of this paradox is that one population can have a lower average than the other population in each of several scenarios, yet the second population has a higher average under a linear combination of scenarios. In our example, selling to uninformed buyers achieves higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t for Segment 1 or 2 alone, but selling to informed buyers can be more advantageous when the two segments are combined. Thus, despite the more dispersed valuations, selling to informed buyers can be advantageous because it gives the <sup>fi</sup>rm greater <sup>fl</sup>exibility in partitioning the market.

To illustrate the result, consider the online distribution of independent music of different genres (e.g., www.CDbaby.com) to a market which consists of a group of music a<sup>fi</sup>cionados and a group of general listeners. While general listeners have a lower expected valuation than a<sup>fi</sup>cionados, some of them might realize a high valuation ex post because of their individual preferences. Suppose that the demand pro<sup>fi</sup>les for uninformed and informed buyers are as given in Table 1 (Case 1). Then the optimal price for uninformed market is $p ^ { u } = \$ 7.50$ with a pro<sup>fi</sup>t $\bar { \pi ^ { u } } = \mathbb { S } 7 5 0 ^ { \mathrm { ~ ~ } }$ , whereas the optimal price for informed buyers is $p ^ { i } = \$ 14$ , yielding a pro<sup>fi</sup>t of $\scriptstyle \pi ^ { i } = \$ 14\times$ $( 0 . 5 \times 8 0 + 0 . 7 \times 2 0 ) = \ S 7 5 6$ . Supplying buyers with additional information is advantageous because the <sup>fi</sup>rm can opportunistically sell to the “premium” – both a<sup>fi</sup>cionados and general listeners who realize their high valuations – while discarding those consumers who realize low valuations. This result will reverse, however, if the upside potential of general listeners is not high enough (e.g., low and high valuations of \$3 and \$12), as shown in Case 2 of Table 1.

## 3.2.2. Serving the expanded middle class

In this section, we present a second scenario where selling to informed buyers might be attractive. As discussed in Section 1, the <sup>fi</sup>rm faces greater dispersion in buyer valuations when buyers become privately informed. When consumers are homogeneous ex ante, such dispersion makes it harder to extract buyer surplus, rendering informed selling less attractive [12,26,30,36]. However when buyers are heterogeneous ex ante, comparing Eqs. (1) and (2) yields the condition under which informed selling is more pro<sup>fi</sup>table,

$$
\min \{L _ {2}, H _ {1} \} > \frac {\max \left\{v _ {2} , v _ {1} (1 + N) \right\}}{\alpha_ {1} N + 1}.
$$

This condition corresponds to a scenario where the informed market demand contains an “expanded middle class” that combines the “niche” Segment 2 consumers with a fraction of “mass” Segment 1 consumers who realize their high valuations after being privately informed. De<sup>fi</sup>ne $R _ { i } = \nu _ { i } - L _ { i }$ as segment i buyer's downside risk, or the revenue loss the <sup>fi</sup>rm might incur by selling to a buyer of segment $L _ { i \cdot }$ Recall that $U _ { i } = H _ { i } - \nu _ { i } ,$ , and we can rewrite the above inequality as

$$

The impetus behind Proposition 2 is that the informed market demand curve – although dispersed over a wider range of consumer valuations – becomes “<sup>fl</sup>atter in the middle”.<sup>4</sup> This dichotomy between dispersion and the overall transformation of the demand curve is analogous to Erdem et al. [15]'s insight about how advertising impacts consumers' price sensitivity. They argue that merely focusing on advertising's impact on elasticity can yield incorrect conclusions; what matters is whether advertising <sup>fl</sup>attens the demand curve. In our setting, the demand curve is <sup>fl</sup>atter in a relatively high-price region, making the <sup>fi</sup>rm's margin-vs-volume tradeoff less stark than under selling to uninformed buyers (where the <sup>fi</sup>rm must either price high at $\nu _ { 2 }$ and sell to Segment 2 only, or cover the whole market but at a much lower price $\nu _ { 1 } )$ . This feature of informed selling is similar to the demand-smoothing effect that makes product bundling attractive. It is well known in the bundling literature that bundles usually have a <sup>fl</sup>atter demand curve than individual components, which leads to a narrower dispersion of valuations and enables the <sup>fi</sup>rm to extract a greater fraction of the potential surplus [1,3,27,28]. When selling to informed buyers, the demand curve also becomes <sup>fl</sup>atter in the middle, nevertheless the dispersion in valuations is actually higher ex post.

Proposition 2 suggests that Segments 1 and 2 buyers' valuation uncertainty has an asymmetric effect on the choice of selling to informed vs. uninformed buyers. Intuitively, to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from providing additional information to buyers, the <sup>fi</sup>rm must combine Segment 1 consumers who realize high valuations with the Segment 2 consumers: thus, for Segment 1, both the upside potential and probability of high valuation matter. In contrast, for Segment 2 the likelihood of achieving the high and low valuations does not matter, because the <sup>fi</sup>rm sells to entire Segment 2 when selling to informed buyers. The only relevant factor is the downside risk: too much risk will push $L _ { 2 }$ well below $H _ { 1 } ,$ resulting in low price and pro<sup>fi</sup>t.

The above analysis highlights practical circumstances for selling to the “expanded middle class.” Consider the sale of a product that serves two distinct segments: a small group of heavy users who highly value the product, and a large group of light users who have relatively low valuations. For many such products (e.g., movie-making software, photo-editing software), the high-value users are aware of product features and requirements, hence have low downside risk. On the other hand, occasional users are highly uncertain about the suitability of the product to their tasks. However, some might be delighted with the product once they experience it, implying a high upside potential. In this situation, the <sup>fi</sup>rm can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from selling to informed buyers, i.e., allowing a trial period or adopting a generous return policy. This would enable the <sup>fi</sup>rm to sell – at reasonably high price – to both the heavy users and the light user segment that discovers an af<sup>fi</sup>nity for the product.<sup>5</sup>

We have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two possible scenarios under which it is optimal to provide buyers additional information before their purchase. In fact, it is easy to verify that these two scenarios, combined together, are not only suf<sup>fi</sup>cient but also necessary for informed selling to be superior in our model. Fig. 3 depicts the <sup>fi</sup>rm's optimal strategy as a function of the upside potential of Segment $1 \ ( U _ { 1 } )$ and the downside risk of Segment $2 \ ( R _ { 2 } )$ . When $U _ { 1 }$ is small, selling to uninformed buyers is clearly the optimal strategy. However, when $U _ { 1 } \geq U _ { 1 } ^ { E }$ and $R _ { 2 } \leq R _ { 2 } ^ { E } ,$ , serving the “expanded middle class” becomes optimal and selling to informed buyers becomes advantageous. When $H _ { 1 } > L _ { 2 }$ and $U _ { 1 }$ further increases to exceed $U _ { 1 } ^ { T } ,$ selling to informed buyers is preferable even when $R _ { 2 }$ is high. This gives another region where it is optimal to “target the premium buyers” after they become privately informed.

## 4. Sale timing problem

The previous section examined the <sup>fi</sup>rm's strategic decision of selling to uninformed buyers, or providing product previews so that buyers become informed before their purchase. A similar problem exists when buyers can purchase at a time preceding consumption. In this case, buyers' valuations depend on their consumption state that is usually unknown at the time of purchase [36]. By controlling the sale timing, the <sup>fi</sup>rm essentially controls the amount of information buyers have before their purchase.

Despite the similarity, the sale timing problem has two distinctive features related to temporal pricing. Because products are sold over time, uninformed selling can only happen in advance, and consumers may strategically delay their purchasing decisions. As a result, it can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to implement selling in advance only to uninformed buyers. Second, it is possible to sell in both advance and spot periods at different prices, which cannot be implemented under product preview mechanisms. This section examines the sale timing problem with a focus on these two features.

Following prior literature [8,12,36] we develop a model of two periods, referred to as the advance and spot periods, respectively. All consumers arrive in the advance period, while consumption occurs only in the spot period, during which they learn their consumption state privately.<sup>6</sup> Thus, selling to buyers in the advance and spot periods corresponds to selling to uninformed and informed buyers, respectively. Similar to the product preview problem, the market consists of two segments $( i = 1 , 2 )$ in the advance period, when consumers are uninformed of their valuations. For example, for World Series Cricket, there can be a segment of general viewers, and a segment of sports fanatics who follow the games closely. Segment i consumers have probability $\alpha _ { i }$ to realize a favorable state with valuation $H _ { i } ,$ and probability $1 - \alpha _ { i }$ of unfavorable state with valuation $L _ { i \cdot }$ In the advance period, consumers (and the <sup>fi</sup>rm) know their statecontingent valuations and the probability of each consumption state. Let $( q ^ { a } , q ^ { s } )$ denote the sales when the <sup>fi</sup>rm sells in advance and spot periods, respectively. Overall the <sup>fi</sup>rm faces three possible strategies:

Advance-only selling: Firm sells in the advance period only, at price $p ^ { a } .$ . All consumers make their purchase decisions with uncertain valuations, either purchase in the advance period or not at all $( q ^ { a } > 0 , q ^ { s } = 0 )$

Spot-only selling: Firm sells in the spot period only, at price $p ^ { s } .$ . All consumers make their purchase decisions after they privately learn their true valuations $( q ^ { a } = 0 , q ^ { s } > 0 )$

Advance+spot selling: Firm sets prices $( p ^ { a } , p ^ { s } )$ that induce some buyers to purchase in the advance period, while motivating others to wait and purchase during the spot period. There are positive sales in both periods $( q ^ { a } > 0 , q ^ { s } > 0 )$

Let $\pi ^ { a } , \pi ^ { s }$ and $\pi ^ { a + s }$ denote the optimal pro<sup>fi</sup>t under the three selling strategies, respectively. Depending on the optimal strategy, there are three possible outcomes. We refer to the two outcomes with $q ^ { s } > 0$ as “it is optimal for the <sup>fi</sup>rm to endure information disadvantage”, or equivalently, “spot selling is optimal,” because at least some buyers make the purchase after privately learning their valuations. Similar as before, we take the conservative perspective and require pro<sup>fi</sup>t $\pi ^ { s }$ or $\pi ^ { a + s }$ be strictly greater than $\pi ^ { a }$ for spot selling to be optimal.

## 4.1. Advance-only and spot-only selling: the role of strategic buyers

Under advance-only selling, the <sup>fi</sup>rm <sup>fi</sup>rst announces its advance price $p ^ { a } .$ Consumers arrive to the market, form their expected valuations for future consumption, and decide whether to buy now at $p ^ { a }$ or wait.<sup>7</sup> Mathematically, advance-only selling is equivalent to that of selling to buyers without any previews. However, for products or services with zero marginal cost (e.g., information goods, airline travels, hotels, cruises, etc.), the <sup>fi</sup>rm has the incentive to sell in the spot period to any remaining consumers if any of them are available in the market. As a result, buyers may strategically avoid purchasing in advance, by reasoning that the resulting second period demand might cause the <sup>fi</sup>rm to sell at a lower spot price. In other words, there can be potential problem in implementing advance-only selling.

Nevertheless, we do observe the practice of selling in the advance period only, when the <sup>fi</sup>rm can make a credible commitment, on account of previous experience, public announcements, or reputation effects. For example, broadcasters of national or international sports events routinely sell advertising slots well in advance of the event, even though advertisers' valuations depend heavily on factors (such as weather, teams' performance, set of teams in the <sup>fi</sup>nals, etc.) that are unknown at the moment of purchase. For the 2009 Twenty20 World Cup cricket tournament, ESPN Star Sports (ESS) sold advertising spots several weeks before the tournament starts, without knowing the composition of the audience for each match. Since advance-only is a well-established practice for selling advertising spots, advertisers do not have the option to wait and buy, despite the possibility of a sharp change in viewership pro<sup>fi</sup>le due to winning or losing of some teams or players. Xie and Shugan [36] provide additional discussion of credible commitment for advance selling. If the <sup>fi</sup>rm can credibly commit to selling in advance only, the optimal pro<sup>fi</sup>t, $\pi ^ { a } .$ , is mathematically equivalent to $\pi ^ { u }$ , the pro<sup>fi</sup>t of selling to uninformed buyers in the product preview problem. Otherwise, we have $\pi ^ { a } < \pi ^ { u } . ^ { 8 }$ In the rest of the analysis, we take the conservative perspective by allowing $\pi ^ { a } = \pi ^ { u }$

Under spot-only selling, the <sup>fi</sup>rm sells in the spot period only at price $p ^ { s } .$ . Or, alternatively, the <sup>fi</sup>rm sells in both periods at the same price, i.e., $p ^ { a } = p ^ { s }$ . With no capacity constraint, there is no incentive to purchase early, i.e., every buyer will prefer to purchase after they are sure about their valuations. Thus, the pro<sup>fi</sup>t of spot-only selling is the same as that under informed selling, i.e., $\pi ^ { s } = \pi ^ { i } .$ . Comparing to $\pi ^ { a }$ leads to similar results as in Propositions 1 and 2, where targeting the premium or selling to an expanded middle class can bene<sup>fi</sup>t the <sup>fi</sup>rm who employs spot-only selling.

## 4.2. Advance+spot selling: intertemporal price discrimination

As discussed in the Introduction, simultaneously offering of informed and uninformed selling at different prices is meaningless for product preview mechanisms. For products sold over time, however, it is possible to have advance+spot selling, where a subset of buyers make the purchase after learning their consumption state. Since selling at the same price over time (with no capacity constraint) is essentially spot-only selling, advance+spot selling refers to the strategy under which the <sup>fi</sup>rm sells in both periods at different prices.

Under advance+spot selling, the <sup>fi</sup>rm <sup>fi</sup>rst announces its advance price $p ^ { a } .$ . Consumers arrive and observe $p ^ { a } ,$ , form expectations about their future valuations and the spot price $p ^ { s } ,$ and decide to purchase now or wait until their valuation uncertainty is resolved. The demand pro<sup>fi</sup>le in the advance period consists of Segments 1 and 2, with valuations $\nu _ { 1 }$ and $\nu _ { 2 } ,$ respectively. In the spot period, the <sup>fi</sup>rm announces its spot price $p ^ { s } ,$ which is optimal with respect to the remaining demand. Those consumers who haven't yet purchased in the advance period comprise the <sup>fi</sup>rm's spot period demand pro<sup>fi</sup>le, at which time they learn their true valuations and choose between purchasing at $p ^ { s }$ and not at all. The problem is solved using backward induction. We <sup>fi</sup>rst analyze buyer behavior and solve for the optimal spot price under each of the remaining demand scenarios. The optimal advance price is then solved by anticipating buyers' strategic behavior.

In the advance period, Segment i consumers delay their purchase decisions to the spot period only when the expected surplus from waiting exceeds that from advance purchase, $\nu _ { i } - p ^ { a } .$ For a type i buyer who waits till the spot period, the expected surplus of waiting is $\nu _ { i } - p ^ { s } ~ { \mathrm { i f } } ~ p ^ { s } \leq L _ { i } , ~ \alpha _ { i } ( H _ { i } - p ^ { s } ) ~ { \mathrm { i f } } ~ H _ { i } \geq p ^ { s } > L _ { i } ,$ and 0 otherwise. An advance+spot selling solution requires $q ^ { s } > 0$ and $q ^ { a } > 0 ,$ , i.e., at least one segment delays the purchase decision and some consumers within this segment make a purchase in the spot period. ## 5. Discussion: how to model consumer heterogeneity?

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings offer new insights about why <sup>fi</sup>rms might prefer selling to informed buyers despite the information disadvantage. These results challenge the prevalent understanding – obtained in two separate research streams – that when marginal costs are negligible, it is optimal to sell to uninformed buyers.<sup>10</sup> One stream of prior work [12,20,26,30,36] analyzes the problem with no heterogeneity ex ante, i.e., there is a mass of consumers with the same expected valuation v before they obtain additional information. A second stream analyzes the case where consumer preferences ex ante are continuously distributed along a single type dimension [19]. Below, we discuss and explain the contrasts between these prior results and ours, and examine the relevance and applicability of our discrete-segments formalism of consumer heterogeneity.

Comparing our results with the <sup>fi</sup>rst stream (homogeneous consumers ex ante), we can conclude that selling to informed buyers can be useful when the market has moderate heterogeneity ex ante, but not when the two segments are too close (the <sup>fi</sup>rm essentially faces homogeneous consumers) or too far apart (wherein the low segment is unattractive to serve). Comparing with the second stream calls for a closer examination of the nature of consumer heterogeneity. In this second stream, the adoption of continuously distributed valuations facilitates the use of calculus in the analysis but requires speci<sup>fi</sup>c assumptions on the demand curves such as decreasing marginal revenue or non-decreasing demand elasticity [18,22], satis<sup>fi</sup>ed when the demand function is log-concave [2]. Building on these assumptions, Johnson and Myatt [19] require that the <sup>fi</sup>rm's transformed demand curve (after consumers become informed) crosses the original curve exactly once (i.e., the original demand curve rotates around a point).

While the above assumptions are widely employed, they are also restrictive because log-concavity implies that “preferences are not too diverse” [9]. Empirical researchers have noted that these assumptions fail in, for instance, markets that have two (or more) distinct segments where the cross-segment differences are much sharper than within-segment variations [35]. As illustrated in [18], these assumptions are violated when the (continuous) demand distribution is bimodal, which is often the case when the potential market can be divided into two segments such as loyal fans and general customers, or novice and advanced users. For such applications, our discrete-segments formalization is a closer approximation of reality, than both the homogeneous-consumers and continuous distribution formalisms, and our results offer a more plausible explanation of the commonly used practice of previews and trials for information goods.

A more general model would consider a continuous bimodal demand distribution, which is even more compatible with the market characteristics discussed above. Fig. 5 depicts the density function for such a demand structure. The ex ante distribution, shown by the dashed curve, is essentially the same as the density function in Fig. 1(a) of [18], where the two modes correspond to the low-value segment (e.g., occasional users) and the high-value segment (e.g., frequent users), respectively. The corresponding pro<sup>fi</sup>t function, shown in Fig. 5, is also bimodal, violating the decreasing marginal revenue assumption as discussed in [18]. Moreover, the demand curves for the uninformed and informed buyers cross multiple times (see Fig. 5), rather than just once as required in [19]. Consequently, the existing models with the decreasing marginal revenue assumption no longer apply. At the same time, the analysis based on a continuous bimodal demand distribution loses tractability quickly, and cannot provide a characterization of the optimal strategy.

However, building on the insights from our discrete-segments formalism, we can examine whether the <sup>fi</sup>rm would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from enabling consumers to become privately informed. First, note that the ex ante demand curve (the dashed curve in Fig. 5) has a steep middle segment, forcing the <sup>fi</sup>rm to trade off between a high-margin lowvolume (sell to the high segment only) and a low-margin highvolume (sell to both segments) selling strategy. Alternatively, if the <sup>fi</sup>rm enables consumers to become informed, it faces more dispersed valuations, but the transformed demand curve is <sup>fl</sup>atter in the middle. If this <sup>fl</sup>attening occurs in a relatively high price range, then it becomes possible to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from selling to informed customers, as demonstrated in the adjacent Fig. 5.

## 6. Conclusion

Our work focuses on valuation uncertainty which is a common feature of information goods. Firms can take actions such as informative advertising, trial periods, limited previews, money-back guarantees, spot selling, etc., that help buyers to resolve their uncertainty before purchase. Prior literature suggests that such actions are not advantageous when they are mean-preserving in the aggregate, because they simply widen dispersion in valuations, making it harder to extract surplus. On the contrary, we <sup>fi</sup>nd it can be advantageous to sell to privately informed buyers even if the <sup>fi</sup>rm has to face an information disadvantage. Our results highlight the importance of understanding how consumer heterogeneity and buyer valuation uncertainty combine to transform the demand curve. We identify three possible motives for <sup>fi</sup>rms to pro<sup>fi</sup>t from allowing buyers to purchase after resolving uncertainty in their valuations: (i) targeting a set of “premium” consumers who realize high valuations, (ii) serving an “expanded middle class,” and (iii) intertemporal price discrimination between informed and uninformed buyers when the product can be sold over time.

Given the prevalence of valuation uncertainty for information goods, we argue that <sup>fi</sup>rms could and should leverage mechanisms that enable consumers to become informed prior to their purchase. Although our results are derived under mean-preserving information, a natural implication is that the <sup>fi</sup>rm can be better off even if the information lowered the mean across the market. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are directly applicable to many consumer-oriented information and service goods such as software, information services (e.g., real-time traf<sup>fi</sup>c updates), and online streaming.

To best convey our results, we considered a simple model of a non-differentiated product with zero marginal cost, no capacity constraint, and single period of consumer arrivals. However, our insights continue to hold when consumers arrive in multiple periods, when the marginal cost is not trivial, or when capacity is constrained. Note that for the latter two, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will be more willing to sell to informed buyers, a result that has been established in the literature (e.g., [36]). Our model considers indivisible goods. For divisible goods, Clay et al. [7] examine a <sup>fi</sup>rm that decides whether to offer a menu of two-part tariffs ex ante (uninformed) or ex post (informed). They show that when buyers have small demand uncertainty, ex ante pricing induces consumer choices that are suboptimal ex post, which yields higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t than spot selling. This logic is consistent with the Shugan–Xie model where advance selling induces consumer choices that yield negative surplus ex post. Clay et al. [7] also provide a numerical example to show that when uncertainty is high, ex post pricing is better at excluding low ex post types that would otherwise limit pro<sup>fi</sup>t from higher types. Miravete [23] demonstrates that ex post twopart tariffs are better under high ex post consumer heterogeneity, which may arise either due to substantial ex ante heterogeneity or a large demand shock. These results are consistent with our insights.

In practice, the <sup>fi</sup>rm (or buyers) may incur some cost in providing (or obtaining) product information. For instance, it can be costly for the <sup>fi</sup>rm to hold seminars to educate users, or to provide trials and samples. In this case, informed selling remains advantageous if the cost for revealing information is not too high. When the <sup>fi</sup>rm offers generous return policies, it is often the buyer who bears the cost of getting informed. In this case, the seller needs to lower the price under informed selling, again making informed selling less attractive. Different information revealing process, especially initiated by different parties (seller or buyer), will have different impacts on the seller's pro<sup>fi</sup>t. A promising future research direction is to identify conditions under which different strategies for revealing information are adopted by the seller.

There has been a vast literature on intertemporal pricing to account for the observation of mark-up or mark-down pricing in reality. However, the majority of the literature does not involve valuation uncertainty. For instance, for fashion goods, buyers' valuation decreases over time and mark-down pricing is used to price discriminate customers. For consumer electronic products, newer generations keep coming to the market and mark-down pricing is often used to liquidate inventory of older generation products. If low valuation consumers arrive early and high valuation consumers arrive late, then <sup>fi</sup>rms can also exercise mark-up pricing when capacity is tight. Our paper captures one driving force (valuation uncertainty) and its interplay with consumer heterogeneity. This driving force, however, sometimes can be combined with other models to explain the reality. For instance, it is common to see software products of single or multiple versions that offer free trials. Future research can also examine the role of valuation uncertainty when the <sup>fi</sup>rm sells vertically differentiated products.
