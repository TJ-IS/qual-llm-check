# Understanding risk-taking behavior of groups: A “decision analysis” perspective

Joseph S. Valacich <sup>a,1</sup>, Saonee Sarker <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Jamie Pratt <sup>b,2</sup>, Mike Groomer <sup>c,3</sup>

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Keywords: Group risk-taking behavior Computer-mediated groups Decision analysis Firm risk preference Hidden pro<sup>fi</sup>le tasks Types of anonymity

## a b s t r a c t

Past research has extensively investigated the effect of media, especially focusing on how anonymity increases risk-related behaviors of groups when using computer-mediated communication (CMC). This study extends prior research by examining the differences in group risk-taking behaviors between face-toface groups and completely non-anonymous CMC groups (i.e., groups working in a fully identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, synchronous CMC environment similar to popular instant messaging systems utilized widely within organizations). Drawing on the “decision analysis” perspective, a key framework for understanding organizational decision-making, the study also examines the effects of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's risk preferences as well as the type of information distribution among group members (i.e., full information known to all group members versus partial information know by only some of the members) on the groups' risk-taking behaviors. Results from a laboratory experiment using student subjects found no differences in risk-taking behaviors between CMC and face-to-face groups; additionally, no differences were found related to how information was distributed among group members. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect was found, however, for the risk preference of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, showing that risk-neutral <sup>fi</sup>rms in<sup>fl</sup>uenced groups to make riskier decisions than groups from risk-averse <sup>fi</sup>rms. Finally, groups within risk-neutral <sup>fi</sup>rms receiving partial information made riskier decisions than groups receiving full information. The implications of these results for future research and practice are examined.

## 1. Introduction

Today, due to the imperatives of enhancing ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and <sup>fl</sup>exibility, organizational tasks are increasingly being performed by groups [8,17]. It is also an acknowledged fact that virtually every organizational group makes decisions involving risk (e.g., [1]). Further, group-based decision making is increasingly occurring at the top management level within organizations [8]. Therefore, examining the risk-taking behaviors of groups in general and of upper echelon teams in particular has been viewed as important by both researchers and practitioners (e.g., [8]).

Ongoing developments in communication technologies, combined with <sup>fl</sup>atter organizational structures and geographically-dispersed employees, are also changing the way in which today's groups interact [20]. Thus, it is important to examine risk-taking behaviors of groups using not only face-to-face communication, but also those using computer-mediated communication (CMC). As such, given this importance, considerable prior research has examined group risktaking behaviors, focusing primarily on the differences between individual members' pre-group-discussion positions and the <sup>fi</sup>nal group decision, termed the “risky-shift” paradigm (cf., [39]) or group polarization (cf., [8,38]). Only a few of the above-mentioned studies, however, have speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examined the differences between CMC and face-to-face groups in the overall riskiness of their decisions.

El-Shinnawy and Vinze [8] argue that one of the primary features of computer-mediated communication that can affect group risktaking behaviors is “anonymity.” As a result, much of the prior research has focused on examining the differences between face-toface and anonymous CMC groups or between anonymous and nonanonymous CMC groups on their risk-taking behaviors. However, it is important to note that anonymity is multifaceted. A review of the literature suggests that prior research has typically focused on groups that had (or did not have) procedural and content-based anonymity, but almost always had environmental anonymity [11]. Given that many group decision-making scenarios (especially those involving the top management or board of directors) typically lack in all forms of anonymity, even though such groups may often revert to the use of CMC technologies due to the geographical distribution of the team members (e.g., [37]), it is important to examine the risktaking behaviors of CMC groups that lack in all forms of anonymity, and compare them with face-to-face groups. Our study attempts to address this issue.

The decision-making literature, especially the “decision analysis” perspective points to certain other important factors that need to be considered when examining risk-taking behaviors. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, proponents of the “decision analysis” perspective argue that decision-makers typically make (or should make) decisions that are “logically consistent” with the components of the “decision basis” [16]. One of the key components of the “decision basis” includes the risk preferences of the firm in which the decision-maker is embedded (e.g., [16]). Thus, our study also sought to examine the effect of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's risk preferences on a group's risk-taking behavior.

Finally, decision analysis theorists also argue that the type of information provided to the decision-maker is a key component of the “decision basis,” and has a strong effect on the <sup>fi</sup>nal decision ([16]). A similar emphasis on the “type of information” can also be found in the group decision-making literature (e.g., [5,42]). Consequently, our study examines the effect of the type of information provided to the groups on their risk-taking behaviors.

The rest of the manuscript is organized as follows: Next, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y review relevant literature on the “decision analysis” perspective, risk, group risk-taking behaviors, type of information, and anonymity in CMC groups, followed by a discussion of the study's conceptual model and hypotheses. Next, we describe our research methodology, followed by a presentation of the results. The manuscript concludes with a discussion of the study's results, limitations, and implications.

## 2. Review of prior literature

## 2.1. The “decision analysis” perspective and risk-related decision-making

Decision-making researchers de<sup>fi</sup>ne risky decisions or “risky behavior” as decisions where the “expected outcomes are more uncertain,” and where the “decision goals are more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to achieve” than other decision-making related behaviors [40 pg. 11]. Much of the research on risk-related decision making has been guided by the paradigm of “decision analysis” (e.g., [16]). The objective of the “decision analysis” process is to transform an opaque problem into a “transparent decision” problem [16 pg. 608], such that it will prompt the decision-maker to take a particular action. The path from the problem to the decision is mediated by the “decision basis” which itself can be multi-dimensional [16]. In the context of risky decision-making, for example, the “decision basis” is composed of 1) the decision-maker's risk preferences and propensities, and 2) the type of information provided to the decision-maker [16 pg. 681].

It has been often acknowledged that what affects the risk-taking behavior within organizational settings is not the risk preference of the individual decision-maker, but the risk preference of the organization [40,41]. There have been numerous debates about the appropriate way in which to assess the organization's or <sup>fi</sup>rm's risk preference [41]. Some scholars suggest that one should assess the risk preferences of the particular manager or departmental unit, using this as a surrogate for the <sup>fi</sup>rm's risk preferences. Others have advocated the assessment of the average risk preferences of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's top management, using this as a proxy for the <sup>fi</sup>rm's risk preference (e.g., [16]). Finance theorists on the other hand have argued that a <sup>fi</sup>rm's risk preferences should be assessed by examining the risk preferences of its shareholder [41]. This approach has emerged as the dominant method for assessing a <sup>fi</sup>rm's risk preferences [41 pg. 115]; consequently, it is adopted here.

Much of the risk-preferences literature has drawn on the expected utility theory [46], which proposes three types of risk preference: risk aversion, risk neutrality, and risk-seeking. Among these three, risk-neutrality and risk aversion are most dominant (e.g., [14,15,46]). In the context of a single shareholder, Pablo, Sitkin, and Jemison [31] also argue that their risk preference will most likely be either risk-averse or neutral. Risk aversion refers to attitudes where the shareholder does not like risk, and prefers “lower risk options at the expense of gains” [47 pg. 136]. Whereas, risk neutrality refers to a state where the shareholder does not really care about risk, and prefers “options with the highest expected value and in which the risk is fully compensated” [47 pg. 136]. Consistent with this view, we view the risk preferences of the shareholder (and consequently, of the <sup>fi</sup>rm) to be either risk-averse or risk-neutral.<sup>4</sup>

## 2.2. Information distribution in groups

Howard [16], a proponent of the “decision analysis” perspective, argues that the type of information provided to the decision-maker (in terms of probability assignments, or any other pertinent information that may aid the assessment of the relationship between the decision and outcomes) affect the <sup>fi</sup>nal decision. Organizational groups are often composed of members from different functional areas, who possess different types of information (e.g., [5]). The members typically bring with them some common information (i.e., information that is known to all participants), and some unique information (i.e., information that is known to only one participant prior to the interaction) to the group process [5]. In such situations, decision outcomes depend on how effectively members share unique or partial information. To simulate the above contexts, studies have been conducted where the full set of information required to complete the exercise was hidden from some of the group members [42]. Existing studies on risk also seem to suggest that the nature of the distribution of information would affect a group's risk-taking behavior [21].

## 2.3. Risk-taking behavior of groups

Although a large number of prior studies have examined group risk-taking behaviors, this work has primarily focused on comparing the pre-discussion positions of individual group members to their post-discussion ultimate decision, or on comparing the group's ultimate decision with the individual members' pre-discussion preferences, termed as the group polarization or the risky-shift phenomenon [36 pg. 303,38,39]. Two primary theoretical approaches have guided prior research on the risky-shift or group polarization: the social comparison theory (e.g., [36]), and the persuasive arguments theory (e.g., [2]). While initially developed to understand risky-shift, these two theoretical perspectives may also be drawn upon to understand the overall risk-taking behavior of groups, and the differences between groups utilizing face-to-face versus computermediated communication.

The social comparison theory argues that individuals often fail to adopt extreme positions, fearing that they may be labeled extremists [36]. However, during a group discussion, members get exposed to the opinions of others, which often reveal that others are not as risk averse as they thought. Through this social comparison, moderate members may then move towards more extreme (or risky) positions [36,38]. Likewise, members initially holding extreme positions may not feel signi<sup>fi</sup>cant pressure to “moderate” their opinions [7]. As such, interaction can lead groups toward a more extreme (or risky) position [36].

The major proponents of the persuasive arguments theory argue that the strength and distribution of arguments for, or against, a particular option during a group discussion determines the amount of choice shift [2,8]. The basic idea behind this theory is that during any group process there is a given pool of arguments for, or against, each alternative being considered [8]. When a greater weight of “compelling arguments favors a position more extreme than that of the average group member” [36 pg. 304], polarization towards the alternative that “elicits more and/or better arguments” occurs [2 pg. 316]. Some researchers contend that the social comparison process and persuasive argumentation occur simultaneously within group interaction [36]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it has been argued that social comparison is the primary mechanism through which groups move toward an extreme decision, but the move is facilitated by the persuasive arguments that members engage in to in<sup>fl</sup>uence each other.

2.4. Anonymity in computer-mediated groups: implications on risktaking behaviors

El-Shinnawy and Vinze [8] argue that one of the key characteristics that affect the processes and outcomes of groups using CMC is the anonymity of the members. In a CMC context, anonymity results in an “inability of group members to identify the origin of messages they receive, and the destination of messages they send” [33 pg. 278]. Gavish and Gerdes, Jr. [11] argue that anonymity can be of three types: environmental, content-based, and procedural.

Environmental anonymity “deals more with the group makeup… than the actual communication process” [11 pg. 307]. One of the basic components of environmental anonymity is the extent to which there is intra-group familiarity; in other words, the extent to which participants have knowledge about each other and have had interacted with each other in the past. Content-based anonymity is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which “the source of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c contribution can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed through the contribution's content,” such as a signature or a particular phrase [11 pg. 306]. Finally, procedural anonymity refers to the extent to which the “communication protocol hides the source of a message” [11 pg. 309]. For example, a header attached to a communication easily identi<sup>fi</sup>es its source [11]. It is important to note that it is possible for groups to not have content and procedural anonymity, while still have environmental anonymity. Once environmental anonymity is also removed, groups engage in fully non-anonymous communication. It has been argued that different types of anonymity is appropriate in different contexts, with certain forms of organizational and group decision-making bene<sup>fi</sup>ting from a lack of some, or all forms of anonymity [37]. As mentioned earlier, current research suggests that many organizational groups (especially those at the top management levels) lack in any form of anonymity, and thus there is a need to examine risk in the context of groups that lack in all the three levels of anonymity.<sup>5</sup>

## 3. Study's model and hypotheses

In this section, we present our research model and hypotheses (see Fig. 1).

One of the key characteristics that affect the behavior of CMC groups is the anonymity of its members. However, in this study, our focus is to examine the differences between completely nonanonymous CMC groups with face-to-face groups. Due to the lack of anonymity in CMC groups, it may be argued that they are similar to face-to-face groups, and would exhibit similar risk-taking behaviors. However, we believe that even in the midst of the absence of anonymity, another key characteristic of CMC groups, the presence of “parallelism,” would create differences between such CMC and face-toface groups.

Due to the parallelism of the media, all group members using CMC can enter information simultaneously, while only one face-to-face member can effectively speak at a time [30]. As such, the parallelism of CMC assists in exposing each member to the thoughts and opinions of every other member; in contrast, within face-to-face discussions, “production blocking” hinders the ability of all members to share their views.

The social comparison theory argues that the greater the exposure to each other's ideas and preferences, the greater the social comparison among the group members (e.g., [36]). During this comparison process, members may realize that relatively extreme members “hold opinions closer to their most admired position.” This comparison may act to move moderate members toward a more extreme position. Further, in the absence of any pressure on the extreme members to become more moderate, the overall group tends to move toward a more extreme position.

Additionally, Kiesler and Sproull suggest that computer-mediated groups, due to reduced exchanges of social information, in general, tend to take “unconventional, risky viewpoints” [19 pg. 113]. From this, we propose:

H1. Completely non-anonymous CMC groups will make riskier decisions than face-to-face groups.

As discussed earlier, the <sup>fi</sup>rm's risk preference, assessed through the risk preference of the primary shareholder has a significant effect on the risk-taking behaviors of the organizational stakeholders. Decisionmakers (whether individuals or groups) who are risk-averse tend to avoid risks; they “attend to and weigh negative outcomes” more, overestimate the “probability of loss relative to the probability of gain” [40 pg. 19], and often require a much higher “probability of gain to tolerate exposure to failure” [40 pg.19]. Whereas, risk-neutral decision makers are more relaxed in their attitudes towards taking risks; they tend to weigh positive and negative outcomes more equally, and show more willingness in tolerating “exposure to failure with lower probability of gain” [40 pg. 19]. Consequently, groups that are embedded within organizations where the shareholder is risk-averse will tend to make less risky decisions than groups embedded in organizations where the primary shareholder has a risk-neutral preference. Thus, we propose:

H2. Groups in organizations that are risk-averse will make less riskier decisions than groups in organizations that are risk-neutral.

Our model also argues that the type of information distribution among group members will affect the groups' risk-taking behaviors. In any group decision-making setting, group members may either hold common information or unique information (known only to a subset of the group). In situations where group members possess unique information, it is important that they share this information during group discussion, such that they are able to “detect the decisional implication of the full information set,” and therefore make a suitable decision [13 pg. 322]. However, it has been demonstrated that groups do not always effectively share and examine uniquely held information [43]. As such, this lack of information sharing negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uences decision quality; i.e., when failing to share needed information, groups fail to detect the best alternative [13,43]. On the other hand, when all group members possess complete information, it is much more likely that this information is shared and examined, enabling groups to fully assess the depth and breadth of a problem, and therefore, make an improved (and more informed decision [5]).

Although no known research has examined the role of information distribution (partial versus full) on a group's risk-taking behaviors, it is likely that the pattern of results will parallel those found for decision making quality. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H3. Groups' risk-taking behaviors will be different when members possess unique information required to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll a task, versus when members possess the full information.

Our review of the literature also suggests that there will be an interactive effect between the type of information possessed by group members and the risk preference of the organization, especially in the context of the risk-neutral <sup>fi</sup>rms. While those that are risk-averse tend to avoid risks at all times, it has been argued that the behavior of riskneutral decision-makers often depends on the context (e.g., [32,47]). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it has been argued that the type of information provided to risk neutral decision-makers affects their risk-taking behaviors [45,47]. When members possess unique information, owing to the lack of pooling of this information across the groups, the group as a whole may not accurately perceive the amount of risk involved in a particular decision alternative (e.g., [32]), or assess the various advantages/ disadvantages of each choice, and therefore erroneously move towards a more risky decision (e.g., [13]). Thus, we propose;

H4. Groups in organizations that have a risk-neutral attitude will make riskier decisions when their members possess partial information required to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll a task, versus when their members possess full information.

## 4. Methods

## 4.1. Research design

An experimental study involving the manipulation of communication media (face-to-face versus computer-mediation), the <sup>fi</sup>rm's risk preference (i.e., risk averse or risk neutral), and the type of information distribution (partial vs. full) was used to test the hypotheses. Student subjects were randomly assigned to threeperson groups, and each group was randomly assigned to one of the different experimental conditions. Our choice of methodology and sample was driven by three primary reasons: First, laboratory experimentation with student subjects has been widely utilized in prior group decision-making and risk-taking research [9]. Second, numerous studies comparing decision-making by students with those of managers in real organizations have concluded that there are few differences in the costs, erraticism, or patterns of biases in decisions made by these two groups (e.g., [29]). Third, current research has also argued that it is often preferred to use student subjects (instead of “practicing managers”) in investigating issues related to decision-making, since managers are “in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their work environment and are likely to carry organizational or job-speci<sup>fi</sup>c perceptions,” making it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to identify and control “organizational in<sup>fl</sup>uences” that could taint the results [35 pg. 169].

## 4.2. Subjects

Two hundred and seventy-four students enrolled in the spring and summer terms of an intermediate <sup>fi</sup>nancial accounting course at a major US university were invited to participate in this experiment. A requirement of this course was either the participation in an experimental subject pool or the preparation of an un-graded one and a half page research paper. The <sup>fi</sup>nal sample used for the analysis consisted of two hundred and twenty two participants (seventy-four groups), after removing subjects who opted to complete the research paper, or for whom we did not have complete data. ## 4.3. Task

In selecting and deciding upon an appropriate task, we consulted prior research on group decision-making. McGrath's [25 pg. 62] Task Circumplex model suggests that group tasks can be categorized as generative, choice-oriented, negotiation-related, or execution-related. Within the choice-related category, tasks can be either intellective (i.e., those that have a right answer), or decision-making (i.e., those that do not have a demonstrably right answer, but where the “preferred or agreed upon answer [of the group] is the right answer”). McGrath [25] suggests that research investigating risk-taking behaviors, choice-shift, or group polarization should involve tasks that are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as “decision-making.” El-Shinnawy and Vinze [8] examined the effect of different types of tasks, and their interactive effect (with the group communication media) on group risk-taking behaviors. Consistent with their hypotheses, their results indicated that there are no differences between CMC and face-to-face groups on the risky-shift when groups worked on “intellective” tasks. However, results indicated that there are differences between these types of groups when they worked on “decision-making” or “judgmental” tasks. Owing to the fact that one of the key objectives of this study was to examine the differences between CMC and face-to-face groups in terms of their risk-taking behaviors, we used a “decision-making” (or “judgmental task”).

Each group was asked to serve as an executive compensation committee for a <sup>fi</sup>cticious organization. The task had two components to it. First, subjects were given a case that contained information about a growing retail company, its board of directors, and its executive compensation committee. Subjects were also informed that the company has a single shareholder and that the shareholder is either risk averse or risk neutral. Once they were presented with the information about the company and the shareholder, subjects were asked to refer to the information provided and make an individual choice about the type of compensation contract for the company's chief executive of<sup>fi</sup>cer (CEO). To make the choice, subjects were given a 6-point scale of executive compensation options that ranged from 100% salary/0% bonus to 0% salary/100% bonus, with the former being the most risky decision, and the latter being the least risky. The charge of the compensation committee was to choose a contract that maximized the wealth of the company's shareholder. The contract choice de<sup>fi</sup>ned the risk-sharing arrangement between the CEO and the shareholder — i.e., as the risk borne by the CEO increased, the risk borne by the shareholder decreased, and vice-versa. 0% salary/100% bonus was a type of employment contract where the CEO bears the most risk, since his/her income would depend on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's performance. Likewise, in such a situation, the shareholder bears the least risk since he/she would not need to pay anything unless the <sup>fi</sup>rm realizes a certain level of performance (e.g., [3,27]). Further details about the task are provided in Appendix A. By capturing the individual group members' recommendation prior to the group discussion, we were able to take members' average a priori preferences into consideration when assessing the overall group's risk-taking behaviors.

In the second part of the experiment, subjects worked on the same case scenario (discussed above) with their group members and provided a group recommendation using the same 6-point scale used to make the individual recommendation.

We believe that our choice of the task and our measure of decision riskiness is consistent with prior research, and makes our study more relevant to practice as well. Prior decision making research has utilized similar tasks to examine decision risk in a variety of different contexts [21]. Researchers also suggest that “pay for performance,” a type of compensation scheme used in this study, is an “effective contracting mechanism” [3 pg. 387], and has consequently, been a topic of great interest for management and accounting researchers [27]. From a practical standpoint, organizations often utilize <sup>fi</sup>xed, bonus-based, or some combination of <sup>fi</sup>xed and bonus-based contracts when setting top-level executive compensation [4], since it enables them to better control the top management's behavior and actions [27].

## 4.4. Setting

The individual aspect of the tasks and the face-to-face group sessions were conducted in a typical university classroom. At the beginning of the experimental sessions, we allowed each of the groups (whether face-to-face or CMC) to brie<sup>fl</sup>y meet (and interact with) each other face-to-face. This enabled us to remove environmental anonymity speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in the CMC groups.

CMC group participants interacted with other members of their groups using the chat feature of Microsoft NetMeeting. To also remove content and procedural anonymity, we asked students to log in using their <sup>fi</sup>rst name, last name, and email address. In each group, one member was selected as the anchor point. His/her IP address was shared with the other two members. These two members were then instructed to call the IP address of the member playing the anchor role. Once the calls were successful, each group was considered to be in an electronic meeting. They were then instructed to use the Chat feature of MS NetMeeting to communicate with each other, and reach a consensus surrounding the CEO's compensation package. Participants were strictly asked “not to verbally converse” once the meeting started. Additionally, a researcher also monitored the CMC sessions to ensure that participants did not engage in any verbal communication during the meeting.

The information type was manipulated by giving each group member either suf<sup>fi</sup>cient information to compute the expected value of each contract (full information) or only one of the three parameters (partial or unique information) necessary to compute the expected value of each contract.

Finally, approximately one-half of the groups were informed that their shareholder was risk-averse, while the others were informed that their shareholder was risk-neutral (see Appendix A).

## 4.5. Procedure

Each experimental session consisted of four different components/ phases. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase, subjects were briefed by the experimenter on the nature and scope of the experiment. Subjects then met with their group members introducing themselves and getting to know each other. Subjects were informed that they would each be provided with cash incentives ranging from \$5 to \$15, which would be based on the quality of their (and their group's) performance (e.g., how thoughtful/meaningful their answer/recommendations were). The average payment to subjects was \$10.00. We believe that the inclusion of this performance incentive helped ensure that the subjects gave their best while working on the exercise.

Subjects were then trained on risk assessment and on choosing an option from a set of alternative investments. The training session <sup>fi</sup>rst asked each participant to carefully read through three exercises (where the solution was provided) dealing with expected returns and risk assessment. Subjects then had to individually complete three similar exercises. Students who did not correctly complete all three exercises were then asked to complete three additional exercises. Only nineteen subjects needed to complete the additional tutorial, suggesting that most of the participants were comfortable with the task. CMC groups were also trained on MS NetMeeting. However, given that the study involved the use of only the chat feature of NetMeeting (which is similar to other chat programs (e.g., IM, Yahoo Chat) that subjects regularly use to interact with friends), we believe that participants' comfort level with it was quite high.

Next, all subjects were given the task, and were <sup>fi</sup>rst asked to provide an individual decision about the form of the compensation contract for the company's CEO. The subjects were also asked to answer four questionnaire items to validate their understanding of the issues contained in the case.

Upon completion of the individual part of the exercise, participants relocated to their respective experimental rooms to start working on their group exercise. Face-to-face group members relocated to a different meeting room. They were then asked to communicate with the other members of their compensation committee, and together make a group recommendation about the form of the CEO's compensation contract. Each group provided only one recommendation on the choice of the contract. Similarly, CMC groups relocated to a computer lab, and using the chat feature of MS NetMeeting, made a group recommendation. Finally, a post-experimental questionnaire (measuring subjects' perceptions about the media) was administered to all the participants.

## 4.6. Measures

The dependent variable used was the group recommendation regarding the type of compensation contract to be selected for the CEO. As discussed earlier, the recommendation ranged from 100% salary/0% bonus to 100% bonus/0% salary.

The independent variables were the type of communication media used by the group (coded as 0 or 1 depending on whether it was faceto-face or computer mediated), the risk preference of the <sup>fi</sup>rm in which the group was embedded (coded 0 or 1 based on whether <sup>fi</sup>rms were risk-averse or risk-neutral), and the type of information provided to the group members (coded as 1 or 2 depending on whether each member was provided partial/unique information or the full information).

The questionnaire for assessing the media manipulation was drawn from an existing media richness scale [6]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, eight items were used to measure subjects' perceptions about the media and four items were used to measure subjects' perceptions about the nature of the group interaction offered by the media (see Table 1). Items were recoded where necessary. For example, 4 items measuring the perceptions of media richness (#s 2, 3, 6, and 7) were negatively worded. These were recoded by subtracting each subject's response on the particular item from 8 (since we used a 7-point Likert scale), to match the other four positively worded items (see Table 1). Reliabilities of the measures were above the recommended range (see Table 2).

Impact of the media on subjects' perceptions about the nature of the communication environment and their perceptions about the group interaction were used as manipulation checks. ## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Interpretation of results

Overall, results of the study indicated that computer-mediated groups do not make riskier decisions than face-to-face groups. This empirical <sup>fi</sup>nding is likely a result of the lack of anonymity of the computer-mediated environment. Prior research investigating the risky-shift paradigm in anonymous CMC groups concluded that CMC group decisions tend to move towards an average of the individual members' pre-discussion preferences, as opposed to moving towards the more extreme risky option like the face-to-face groups (e.g., [8]). One of the reasons offered for such a difference between CMC and faceto-face groups is that in an anonymous CMC setting, group members “feel protected to a large extent from the social pressures of face-toface settings,” and thus feel less normative pressures to move towards an extreme decision. In our study, however, the groups were fully identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, lacking in all three forms of anonymity. Their only difference from the face-to-face groups was that they did not engage in verbal communication, and the members were seated in a non-proximate location from each other. This lack of difference between the CMC and the face-to-face groups (in terms of the environmental anonymity) could thus have resulted in a lack of difference in their risk-taking behaviors as well, thereby con<sup>fi</sup>rming the contention of prior research that anonymity in CMC groups is one of the key characteristics affecting risk-taking behaviors of such groups.

Further, as predicted, results indicate that the <sup>fi</sup>rms' risk preferences (enacted through the risk preference of the primary shareholder) have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on the risk-taking behaviors of groups. The broader risk literature argues that the <sup>fi</sup>rm's (and the shareholder's risk preferences) has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on the decision-maker's riskrelated decisions (e.g., [41]). Our study con<sup>fi</sup>rms that this effect is not only restricted to an individual decision-maker, but also to a group making a decision on behalf of the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

Contrary to the predictions in Hypothesis 3, the type of information provided to the group members (partial/unique versus full), did not have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on the group's risk-taking behaviors. While there has been no known prior research examining the role of the type of information provided to group members on their risk-taking behaviors, our review of the literature on hidden pro<sup>fi</sup>le tasks and the decision analysis perspective, seemed to suggest that there would be a difference. Future research needs to examine this relationship more closely.

While the type of information provided to group members failed to have a direct effect on the groups' risk-taking behaviors, it had an interactive effect with the <sup>fi</sup>rm's risk taking preferences on the groups risk-related decision (as predicted in H4). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our study con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that when the <sup>fi</sup>rm is risk-averse, groups tend to shy from risky options (irrespective of the type of information they are provided). However, when the <sup>fi</sup>rms are risk-neutral, their decisions depend a great deal on the extent of information that group members have regarding the decision scenario. Owing to the fact that groups within risk-neutral <sup>fi</sup>rms are always looking for the “highest expected value,” they have a propensity towards risk (though they do not actively seek it). When they do not have complete information regarding a decision scenario, the lack of pooling of the unique information across the group members resulted in an inability to assess the risks involved in the situation. This coupled with their nascent propensity towards risk, moves them (albeit erroneously) towards a more risky option.

## 6.2. Limitations

While this study makes a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution to our understanding of risk-taking behaviors of groups, like any other empirical study, it also has some limitations. The primary concern may be the use of an experimental study involving student subjects, which has been argued to lower the external validity of a study [12]. However, we argue that results obtained from student subjects are usually similar to results obtained from managers in organizations (e.g., [23]). In addition, prior CMC/FTF group studies in a variety of disciplines have routinely investigated risk-related behaviors of groups using student subjects (e.g., [38]). Another concern related to the use of student subjects in our study is the ability of the students to make appropriate risk-related decisions. To address this issue, we provided extensive training, followed by numerous exercises, and ensured that subjects had the capability to make appropriate decisions pertaining to the scenario provided to them during the study.

Another potential limitation of this study is that the experimental context and task are highly stylized, raising concerns about the applicability of the results to more complex real-world settings. While this concern is inevitable with a highly-controlled experiment, we attempted to minimize it by carefully constructing the experimental environment to include the essential elements associated with choosing an executive compensation package (<sup>fi</sup>xed vs. variable compensation and compensation for risk) as well as the information necessary to ascertain the risk-sharing arrangement between the CEO and the shareholder. Thus, consistent with prior literature (e.g., [22]), we believe that the use of stylized experimental tasks in studentsubject based experiments is reasonable, if not ideal.

A third limitation of this study stems from the use of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of communication media (i.e., synchronous chat), and the fact that group members were located in the same place (though not necessarily next to each other). Given that organizational work is conducted using both synchronous and asynchronous media, where group members may be collocated or distributed (e.g., [30]), questions may be raised regarding the generalizability of this study. However, several prior studies comparing distributed and co-located CMC groups have concluded that distributed CMC groups do not necessarily differ from co-located CMC groups (e.g., [18]). In sum, it may be argued that the results of our study, which used a synchronous communication media with all members co-located, may be generalized to other forms of CMC groups (e.g., different time/different place).

Another limitation of the study is that only two types of <sup>fi</sup>rm risk preferences were investigated. Although these two risk preferences are the most dominant in the literature, especially in the context of single shareholders of small organizations (e.g., [14,31]), there is clearly an opportunity to investigate the role of other risk preferences (e.g., riskseeking) on group risk-taking behaviors in different communication and information distribution contexts.

Finally, our task scenario described a relatively simple organization, which had a single shareholder. Thus, the results of this study need to be interpreted accordingly, and may not be generalizable to much larger and complex organizational contexts. However, given the current increase in the number of small and medium sized enterprises around the world (e.g., [24]), we believe that the results of the study are still applicable to a broad range of organizational contexts.

## 6.3. Implications for practice

We believe that this study has several implications for practice. One of its key conclusions is that completely non-anonymous CMC groups do not make riskier decisions than face-to-face groups. The use of computer-mediated communication environments is on the rise within virtually all types of organizations, and in different levels of the organization. Within the upper levels of the organizations (for example, among top management or members of the board), CMCenabled group meetings are fairly common, since members are often distributed (either because a large proportion of today's organizations are globalized with members hailing from different countries, or because top-management or members of the board travel frequently). Additionally, such organizational groups often make decisions that involve a great deal of risk. The results of this study suggest that the use of a certain communication media would not necessarily prompt groups to take (or not take) undue risks in their decisions, and that shareholders or owners of small <sup>fi</sup>rms can continue to encourage the use of CMC, without fear of it negatively affecting the groups' risktaking behaviors. Future researchers may attempt to extend this study by undertaking further examination of this phenomenon in globally distributed/virtual contexts.

The results of our study also suggest that organizational groups do adhere to the risk-taking preferences of the <sup>fi</sup>rm in which it is embedded in making a risk-related decision. For the primary shareholder, this result again brings a sense of comfort that the management's and board's “duty of loyalty” to the primary shareholder moves them towards making “decisions in the interests of the [primary] shareholders rather than their own interest or the interest of other constituencies” [41 pg. 115]. Future research will however need to examine the study's results in the context of large and complex organizations. In such contexts, the risk preference of the top management, or that of the manager/departmental unit in which the decision-making group is embedded, should be used as surrogates of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's risk preference.

Finally, future research should also examine the effect of “riskseeking” preferences of the <sup>fi</sup>rm on the upper echelon team's riskrelated decision-making. Given the large number of entrepreneurial ventures around the world, most of which are (and need to be) riskseekers to compete with large organizations [28], the results from such studies can have important implications for entrepreneurial organizations.

Overall, we would like to reiterate that risk-taking behaviors of groups are an important topic for organizations, owing to the increasing use of groups in organizational decision-making, and due to the fact that in today's turbulent environment, a majority of these decisions involve risk. This study, taking a “decision analysis” perspective, attempts to examine the role of three important factors on groups' risk-taking behaviors: 1) the role of the communication media used by the group, 2) the risk preference of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, and 3) the type of information distribution among group members. While the role of the communication media has been examined in prior literature on group risk-taking, this study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to examine the differences between completely non-anonymous CMC groups (which are increasingly growing in prominence in organizations) and face-to-face groups. Further, the study extends prior research on group risk-taking by also examining the role of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's risk preferences and the type of information distribution. In this regard, the study makes an important contribution to the existing research on both risk and group decision-making. Nevertheless, much research remains.
