# Does risk matter? Disengagement from risk management practices in information systems projects

## Abstract

Risk management and ‘routine-based reliability’ is considered fundamental to project performance. Existing theories of project risk management do not fully explain why project managers stop practicing risk management information systems (IS); however, constructs drawn from organisation theory offer insights into how and why such disengagement occurs. The study examines risk management practices in 21 IS projects within 10 organisations. By focusing on risks that resulted in significant events and mapping backwards over time the practices associated with those risks, we identify that in all but five projects the manager had disengaged from prescribed risk management before executing risk responses. In most projects, the majority of formally identified and assessed risks remained unallocated and untreated. A laddering technique was used to help explain why this transpired. We found five key underlying beliefs that governed project managers’ risk management attitudes and actions. ## Introduction

Despite established management processes, many Information Systems (IS) projects face challenges in terms of meeting the classic cost and time parameters, as well as falling short of expected organisational and user benefits (Charette, 1996; Gibson, 2003). Nelson (2007), in his metaretrospective study, established that in 47% of cases of troubled projects, risk management was perceived to be ineffective. Identifying and controlling risk impact, therefore, is regarded by both practitioners and researchers to be fundamental in ensuring success in IS projects (Barki et al, 1993).

Central to the issue of improving project performance is the application of risk management processes to reduce human error and promote reliability (Boehm, 1991). Professional institutions such as the Project Management Institute (PMI) and the Association of Project Management (APM) seek to establish ‘best practice’ standards and define risk management as the systematic process of identifying, analysing and responding to project-related events, or managerial behaviours that are not known in advance, but have the potential to affect a project negatively (Project Management Institute, 2008). Risk management practices have become well-established with prescribed standards and guidelines.

Organisations often endeavour to implement and follow the recognised risk management methodology in the belief that they can reduce scope creep from ambiguous requirements, ill-matched resource needs and availability, contractual and funding constraints and other unwanted risks. However, such ‘self-evidently correct’ processes (Williams, 2005) and ‘routine-based reliability’ (Butler & Gray, 2006) are not without limitation. Studies in other domains, such as the marketing and consumer literatures and safety research in hazardous industries, have long demonstrated that a singular preoccupation with directive risk management processes, control and compliance are often insufficient in achieving high levels of reliability (Weick & Sutcliffe, 2001). Studies of IS project management have also shown that ‘people-related’, ‘human-centred’ or ‘behaviour-based’ shortfalls such as ineffective stakeholder management, lack of commitment from business leaders and lack of cross-functional communication are also contributory factors to poor project performance and project failure (Nelson, 2007; Cerpa & Verner, 2009). According to Mitchell & Boustani (1994), the intended and unintended consequences of the failure to manage risk can be wide-ranging, painful and long-lasting, resulting in the general preference to avoid mistakes rather than maximise utility when engaging in risky decisions.

The literature suggests that risk management is often circumvented by IS project managers. This is consistent with other recent research:

Our reviews indicate that project managers rarely work their way through this process in its entirety. (Nelson, 2007, p. 75)

Despite the wealth of risk-based research, little is known about either why IS project managers disengage from prescribed risk management process of identification, analysis and response, or the reasons why departure occurs at some point during the project lifecycle (Mark et al, 1998). The primary aim of this study is to help address this gap in the literature.

In order to address the aim of this study is to examine risk management practices in 21 IS projects within 10 organisations. Our findings reveal that prescribed risk management processes were established in every project. However, significant differences were found in the way that individual projects accomplished risk identification, analysis and response. By focusing on risks that resulted in significant events and mapping backwards over time the practices associated with those risks, the data shows that in all but five projects the team had disengaged from prescribed risk management. In most projects, the majority of formally identified and assessed risks remained unallocated and untreated. In order to help explain why this transpired, a laddering technique was employed to elicit the underlying beliefs that govern project managers’ risk management attitudes and actions.

In what follows, we first consider the research on IS project risk management to help derive our four research questions. We next introduce the methods of data collection and analysis. We then demonstrate how the completion of risk identification, analysis and response varied considerably across the 21 projects in our sample and that most project managers had disengaged from prescribed risk management processes before executing risk responses. Finally, to help explain how and why such adherence or deviation occurs, we explore the underlying beliefs that govern project managers’ risk management attitudes and actions. In so doing, we aim to contribute to the on-going debate about IS project failure and to the theoretical development of IS project risk management.

## IS risk management and project reliability

IS projects have a high-risk profile, suffering from both generic risks common to all projects and IS-specific risks (Boehm & Ross, 1989). Aside from poor or inadequate software requirement specification (McKeen et al, 1994; Lamb & Kling, 2003; Markus & Mao, 2008), IS projects are also subject to market risk, financial risk and technical risk (Schwalbe, 2002). The need to achieve more with less as costs are squeezed threatens project quality and delivery (Shehu & Akintoye, 2010). Bannerman (2008) cites Chapman & Ward (2004) and Willcocks & Griffiths (1997, p. 2119) when suggesting that project dimensions which include ‘size, structure, complexity, composition, context, novelty, long planning and execution horizons and volatile changes’ can have an impact on project risk and ultimately, project success.

IS project risk is the product of uncertainty associated with risk factors and the potential loss or harm to the project. The endemic nature of risk in IS projects forces managers to look for ways to prevent risks from negatively influencing project performance. Boehm & Ross (1989) argue that IS project risk management can be used to avoid disasters, reduce rework, help balance work and effort, and stimulate win-win situations.

The need for high levels of reliability in project when having to work with complex and unreliable components in unpredictable contexts requires further investigation (Butler & Gray, 2006). Reliability shows itself only by the lack or low levels of risk occurrences (Hollnagel, 2006). In context of high hazard systems, Woods & Hollnagel (2006, p. 14) describe this resilience as the ‘intrinsic ability of an organisation entity (system) [such as a project] to maintain or regain a dynamically stable state, which allows it to continue operations after a major mishap and/or in the presence of a continuous stress’. Butler & Gray (2006) argue that there are two key perspectives on how this reliability can be achieved in projects: routine-based reliability and mindfulness-based reliability. The first seeks to provide systematic risk governance through structures and processes. The second is premised upon the mindfulness of project actors. It emphasises heedful anticipation, scanning, awareness and action.

‘Routine-based reliability’ is a systematic approach to risk management (e.g., Boehm, 1991), primarily emphasising conformance to prescribed standards and guidelines (e.g., Chapman, 1997; Project Management

Institute, 2008). Conventional wisdom suggests that project reliability can be achieved through step-wise risk identification, analysis, control and response (e.g., Chapman et al, 2000). This line of argument stresses risk identification as the first task for any risk manager as it assumes the ability to gauge the extent to which projects are under threat. Accordingly, much effort is given in the IS literature to defining ‘what is a risk?’ and ‘what are the typical risk factors for IS projects?’ (Boehm & Ross, 1989). Cule et al (2000) suggest classifying risks by their source (e.g., client, self, task, environment, contractual), whereas Lam (2004) views risks and their impact along the project life cycle. Boehm (1991), Johnson et al (2001) and Schmidt et al (2001) all sought, through their research, to establish a ‘definitive’ list of important IS risk factors that could be used as a checklist.

A checklist of the typical risks factors for each risk type is in common use by companies to assess a project and to decide the action strategies – namely: avoidance, reduction, transfer, mitigation and acceptance. Attempts to avoid risk imply adapting the project plan so that risks can be isolated and project activities re-scheduled. The probability of risk occurrence may be reduced or risk may be transferred to another party by shifting the impact, whereas risk mitigation involves efforts to reduce the likelihood of a risk occurring or its impact on the project outcome. Finally, project managers may decide that some residual risk should be tolerated.

On the basis of the prospect of an automated action to external stimulus (March & Simon, 1958), a routinebased risk strategy assumes that reliable risk action can be pre-programmed through standard operating procedures, decision rules and associated actions. The assumption that the creation of decision rules and repetition of associated responses are an effective way of generating reliable practice is grounded in the planning control model of cognitive psychology (Hopkins, 2009). Glover (2004) explains that planning using visual cues (e.g., risk dashboard and other visual tracking tools) is a process, which, through repetition, becomes less susceptible to conscious influence. This rational and empirical approach to risk management often appeals to IS project managers.

Many IS organisations employ risk process frameworks and standards provided by project professional bodies such as PMI (Project Management Institute, 2008), APM (Association for Project Management, 2005) or national standards such as BSI (British Standards, 2000). These standards guide a company’s approach to risk evaluation, control and response. However, as observed by Johnson (2006) projects remain susceptible to risk and failure. Effective action depends on a match between the system and the situation. Importantly, research evidence indicates that a gap exists between actual risk management and nominal practice (March & Shapira, 1987, Ropponen & Lyytinen, 1997, Taylor, 2006), with some researchers drawing the conclusion that risk management is not well-applied in practice (Ropponen & Lyytinen, 1997; Ropponen, 1999; Kwak, 2000).

While most organisations do operate some form of risk management methodology, only a quarter of project managers reported that they performed risk management in any great detail (Ropponen, 1999). Whilst the practice of risk management situation may have improved in the intervening years, there is a paucity of research on why managers stop practicing risk management. Research suggests that risk managers may disengage from standard risk management because the ‘checklist method’ has the potential for bias and can have a limited scope in terms of context, cultural and control relevance for different stakeholder groups (de Camprieu et al, 2007). Keil et al (2000) have highlighted that a risk checklist, as an identification tool, offers little guidance on the magnitude of loss when a risk occurs and stakeholders tend to focus on the perceived potential impact or loss rather than the timing of the impact or when a loss might occur.

Our review of the literature on project risk management reveals that a gap exists in the understanding of the practice of risk management in IS projects with respect to the following questions:

RQ1: To what extent to do project managers adhere or disengage from prescribed risk management processes?

RQ2: Why do managers stop practicing risk management in projects that have significant risks that could affect project performance?

RQ3: To what extent do the core beliefs of project managers towards risk help to explain adherence or disengagement?

## Methods

In order to address our research questions, data were collected from companies that are major players within the IS development market: Capgemini U.K., Hewlett-Packard, Siemens IT Solutions and Services, and seven other major organisations who wished to remain anonymous. A selective sampling approach was applied to define projects that included a substantial level of repeatability proving a basis for applying risk manage ment. Roll-out projects that included the repeated deployment of standard infrastructure and software applications met this criterion. Twenty-one U.K. Rollout projects were included. Although standardised in their project approach, duration and budget varied from project to project.

Within these projects, managers were chosen who, first, had at least 5 years’ experience in Roll-out projects and, second, 3 years’ experience in actively managing risks (see Table 1). As a result, lack of familiarity with typical risks and risk management, which was cited in the literature as a reason to disengage from risk management (e.g., Akintoye & MacLeod, 1997; Jaafari, 2001), was excluded from the outset of the study and thus, unsurprisingly, did not emerge as a key factor of disengagement.

In addition, only managers fulfilling the previously stipulated criteria were interviewed. They all had indepth knowledge about the project, competence to manage risks and were close to incidents (as an indicator of reliability through risk management), which had a significant influence on performance.

It is worth highlighting that all interviewees were certified risk managers, hence a basic knowledge about risk management can be assumed. In all projects, a basic risk management standard was advocated. In essence, regardless of whether the nine-stage PRAM process (Chapman, 1997; Association for Project Management, 2005) was applied, all standards included the identification, probabilistic assessment and response to risk.

Two interrelated approaches to data collection and analysis were employed. First, visual mapping of how risk management practices evolve over time. Second, a laddering technique was used to elicit the underlying beliefs that govern project managers’ risk management attitudes and actions.

Mapping risk management practices This study employs a processual mapping strategy anchored on critical events that significantly influence project outcomes. An inverse line of enquiry was adopted in the mapping process (see Figure 1). This involved elaborating critical events and their adversity on project performance (see Flanagan, 1954) then tracing backwards in time from each event to identify whether or not risk management processes, such as planning, identification, analysis and response, had be adhered to.

An analysis of formalised risk management procedures provided explicit evidence on the level of attention given to each possible risk. Risk logs were used to locate at which stage managers deviated from the formal risk management. Practices (identification, assessment, response) were established and documented and consequently executed. This suggests that a risk management process phase had not been adopted if a third of the required actions were not completed (see Table 2).

The formal management of risk, although of importance, can disguise the fact that project managers also engage with risks in a less visible, more informal, tacit way. Therefore, a series of in-depth, one-to-one interviews were conducted to develop an understanding of whether managers disengage from managing significant risks that materialised and affected project performance.

Project managers were initially asked questions such as ‘can you tell me about significant risks that actually materialised?’ This led to the production of a chronology of events and an understanding of the context to which the actions of the project managers could be related. These events were further explored by enquiring about the event itself and the impact of the event on project outcome (e.g., What happened? Who was involved?).

Visual mapping is a valuable means of theorising from process data (Langley, 1999). A visual map is useful in creating a mental picture of the processes that managers employ in risk management. Mindful of Langley’s (1999) critique that the temporal process approach is a poor mechanism for understanding the ‘why’ of human actions and behaviours, a laddering process (see Figure 1) was also used to elicit the reasons for any disengagement.

## Eliciting espoused risk management beliefs

Laddering was used to reveal the espoused beliefs of project managers in order to help explain the reasons for adherence or disengagement from risk management processes. Laddering is an established technique in marketing and advertising to elicit information about people’s motivation and beliefs. It was developed to understand how consumers interpret the tangible attributes of a product and relate them to personal motivations (e.g., Gabriel, 1997). The technique of laddering has also received attention in management theory (e.g., Van Rekom et al, 2006) due to its potential to uncover personal beliefs, but has found relatively little application in the IS community (Lapinski et al, 2006).

It was possible, using the laddering technique, to relate hierarchy of attributes (A) to consequences of use (C) and ultimately to individuals’ beliefs (B) forming, effectively, a ladder (the sequence being A-C-B). In this study, the ‘attribute’ refers to elements of the actors’ attention to the entity of ‘risk’ and/or its ‘management’.

1. A (Attributes) refers to a response not taken to plan for risk management, and identify, analyse and respond to an event that had a significant impact on project performance. Questions were asked, such as why the decision to respond to risk is less desirable than identifying and analysing it.

2. C (Consequences) – From this stage, the line of questioning proceeded to the characteristics of project personnel who are associated with the second level of the sequence A-C-B. The ‘preference–(in)actiondifferences’ allow, for example, explanations that responses to risk are less desirable because of the consequence of the costs committed to a risk that has yet to materialise.

3. B (Beliefs) – Follow-up questions further moved the respondent up a ladder of abstraction, exploring the beliefs of respondents that underline their perception of not attending to a risk despite the normative view to do so. In order to explore the respondents’ general view about risk management, questions about the contribution of risk management to project delivery were also asked.

The A-C-B data were synthesised with the process data in a graphical form (Miles & Huberman, 1994), allowing comparisons between events, parallel processes of disengagement, the passage of time, as well as factors explaining why managers disengaged from risk. Such visual, graphical representations were completed on a project-by-project basis and, as an intermediary step, led to a more abstract conceptualisation of adherence and disengagement (see Figure 2).

## Data validation

The process flow chart developed from the data was sent to respondents for their comments. In addition, data from documents and insights from the respondents were juxtaposed for convergence and divergence. Both sets of data were used to probe each respondent’s view of inattention towards risk. Project documentation was particularly helpful to trace and substantiate what actually happened and how risks were managed.

## Results

Although all investigated projects reported the initiation of standardised risk management procedures and processes, actual approaches and levels of detail in risk identification, evaluation and response varied. The attention of project managers to the active management of risks was determined by establishing whether any response had been executed to engage with risks that might have adversely influenced the project outcome. Table 2 provides greater detail about the point at which the project team elected to formally disengage from managing risks that significantly influenced project performance.

In most projects the formal stages of a risk management process were followed up until the process was no longer inspected. At the point where some kind of surveillance of the risk management process ceased, so did the formal implementation of the risk management process. In most instances, once engagement with the risk management process stopped, it was not renewed (although this was not always so, as in Project 13). In 17 out of the 21 projects, over two-thirds of identified and assessed risks were not responded to when they materialised.

In some projects, although risks were assessed (i.e., the probability of occurrence and impact defined), interventions were not carried out to counter the threat. In the words of some of the project managers, risk management was seen as an evaluation exercise:

Despite the provision of ‘self-evidently correct’ rules to manage risk, the evidence indicates a tendency for the attention on the management of risk to be restricted to the front-end (risk identification and administration) without follow-through to an adequate risk response process. In all but four of the projects examined, the project team had disengaged from proactive risk management before actually executing risk responses (see Table 2). In most projects, the majority of formally identified and assessed risks remained unallocated and untreated.

The findings reveal that IS project managers tended to deviate from acting upon significant risks that were assessed (identified and analysed). Therefore, the major obstacle in increasing risk management reliability was less the issue of not detecting changes in the environment (risk) (e.g., Clarke, 1993) but of actions taken to prevent risks from happening.

Respondents found little criticism for their tendency to assess risk while not actually managing it, despite the occurrence of significant events. For example, in project 10, the project suffered from a serious fault in an ‘unattended setup routine’, resulting in the roll-out of several hundred IS systems to be suspended. Although the risk of a fault installation routine had been identified, it was neither assessed nor responded to in advance of its occurrence.

These results would be less surprising if most risks had been ‘accepted’ because they were deemed to be insignificant. However, the results of the in-depth interviews show that the choice of disengagement from managing risks is driven predominantly by five beliefs: legitimacy, value, competence, fact and authority. Each of these is explained below.

## Legitimacy

Most respondents accepted that ‘best practice’ project risk management standards are important to promoting common understanding of and response to project risk. Respondents reported that the pressure to comply to standard processes was ever present in each of the projects. In order to be recognised and accepted by customers and other stakeholders, there was an expectation and pressure to conform to the prescribed routine of risk management:

The company has a risk manager and it has a formal risk management process at a high level. This is roughly what we are trving to deliver to the customer. This is the value of the contract and it is a high risk because of A, B, C and D. (Project 7)

The accepted and approved process was described by one respondent,

Yes, we use a standard method, which is a risk strategy based on avoidance, acceptance and mitigation for reduction of probability, reduction of the impact, or to deflect or share that risk with a third party. Using that model we tend to look at each strategy according to those main headings. We then look at the advantages and disadvantages of each strategy and then we will come up with an order of preference in terms of which strategy may be the most appropriate. (Project 10)

Over time, project risk management practices had become embedded, normalised and generally accepted as customary practice. In all projects, the individual’s performance evaluation was linked in some way to the extent to which a project manager actually assesses (identifies and analyses) risk:

Oh yes, we used the standard process of [Company X], which is really common sense, which basically allows everybody to identify risks. We have brainstorming sessions, and we say ‘Okay! What can go wrong?’ We have assessed the impact and probability and developed a risk management process or risk management plan based on the perceived risk, and obviously some risks you say ‘Well, the chance that it happens is that small and the impact is that small, we are not going to do anything’, because obviously you could carry out risk management endlessly and be doing risk management or playing risk management techniques for months and months before you actually start delivering. You have actually to find a happy medium, and that is something that comes with experience. (Project 9)

Projects are embedded in an on-going network of interorganisational relations (Granovetter, 1973). Within this environment, the prevailing values, beliefs and practices often become institutionalized and the adoption of risk management processes practices can be driven by coercive, normative and mimetic influences (DiMaggio & Powell, 1991). Further, within a field, such as IS project management, practices can established as authoritative guidelines and there was a strong belief held among project managers that demonstrating legitimacy within the domain is contingent upon implementing standardised risk management procedures and processes.

## Value

The findings suggest that project managers believe that risk management must be demonstrably useful, and where this was not apparent they were likely to disengage from the process. In some projects, IS project managers argued that although the investment of time and resources in risk management was clearly visible, the benefits of its application remain hidden. In the light of not knowing whether risk management will deliver the benefits, project managers were doubtful about engaging with risk management at the planning stage. Some examples include:

It would not have helped anyway. (Project 6)

It [risk management] did not matter. (Project 8)

In projects where risk management was perceived to deliver/enable the controll of risks managers were more willing to commit resources to plan, identify and analyse possible risks:

Project risk is one of a number of project control mechanisms I believe were important. (Project 9)

The project client, owner or sponsor was reluctant to spend money or energy on a management process without knowing that it has definite benefits (Lanza, 2000). Raz & Michael (2001, p. 14) say: ‘y we met many project managers who claimed that risk management was an unnecessary activity, and that the resources it required could be put to better use elsewhere in the project. For example in project 6, incompatible software and hardware led to a significant delay in initiating a nationwide roll-out. Yet, such a risk was not planned for, despite the fact that the respondents mentioned that the risk of incompatibility between software and hardware is not at all uncommon.

## Competence

Doubt in one’s ability to control risk was a key driver of disengagement from risk management. The data revealed instances where project managers felt the need to be positive and were concerned that the suggestion of anything untoward happening would not only upset the client, but indicate inadequacy or a lack of control over the project. As a result, the project managers were unwilling to expose stakeholders to any identified risks for fear of raising anxiety and doubt about the competence of the service provider to deliver the project as planned and promised.

It (risk) was as visible for the client as it was for us, when they wanted it to be. We obviously didn<sup>0</sup>t broadcast it. We weren’t there to raise doubts and the feeling was that we had everything under control and could solve everything. If that<sup>0</sup>s the arrangement the client wants, that’s what we’ll do. No problem at all. We presented a united front. (Project 5)

The research participants described how they avoided admitting that things might go awry with the project. They suggested that the act of admitting problems with the project was unmentionable. One project manager remarked:

y but they [risks] could never have been brought to the client’s attention, because the priority was simply the successful completion of the project. (Project 6)

In this particular case, the risk was not actively managed because it was considered that in raising the very subject of risk, the customer would become aware of it and this awareness would then jeopardise the relationship between the customer and the project management team. In some cases this led to identified risks being deliberately ignored:

But they should not have been deleted. They should have been managed internally, not just excluded or even ignored. They did not go even in the internal risk register. (Project 6)

In the presence of discomforting risks, the relationship between knowledge and perceived risk (Wildavsky & Dake, 2002) may result in a ‘calculated cautiousness’ where actors elect to preserve the freedom to choose whether or not to expose themselves (and others) to the dangers that lie in the activity (of risk management) (Hale et al, 1987, p. 76). The findings suggest that rather than confront the unknown and its implications, managers often preferred to adopt a ‘head-in-the-sand’ stance. The result was that they failed to engage with the identification and evaluation of risks (White et al, 2003). This unwillingness relates to the desire to give stakeholders the answers they want to hear and, in so doing, provide the impression of apparent certainty and a safe and predictable world (Slovic et al, 1980; Fischhoff et al, 1981; Beierle, 2004). Risk is not a ‘glamorous’ topic, being seen in a rather gloomy and mainly negative light (Raftery, 1994). Further, as the bearer of bad news runs the risk of their own competence coming under close scrutiny, the temptation can be the downgrading of the identified risk (Machlis & Rosa, 1990). The level of risk can then be safely dealt with through standardised risk management without the side effects of fear or the scrutinizing of project management competence. Just as risks that may have an influence on the project outcome are suppressed, they are not managed for the sake of avoiding discomfort among stakeholders:

The reason being that to highlight the risk to the stakeholders would potentially damage the resources available to the project. (Project 11)

## Fact

Project managers thought that risks needed to be tangible, perceptible and real. Therefore, they disengaged with risk management where risks were thought to be fictitious or imaginary or where information about risks was unreliable. Project managers often suspended management of potential risks until they materialised:

y if it did [risk materialised], we could get round it somehow. (Project 1)

Deferring the responsibility for managing risks that had not yet materialised can lead to a degree of inertia as ‘people can stop repeating their actions, for example, when their actions do not lead to intended outcomes or lead to unintended outcomes’ (Peltokorpi, 2008, p. 18). Despite knowing about specific potential risks and acknowledging the need to manage risks from a purely economic standpoint, responses were deficient:

y, and because nobody wanted to make a decision there and then, even on a project management level to resolve the issues. (Proiect 7)

For a couple of problems, which ultimately caused the project to falter, no, there was no adequate response because the risk was not dealt with. It is a response issue. (Project 11)

Whereas on the one hand the preference of a project manager often lies in identifying, analysing and responding in advance, a reason for disengagement can be the benefit to oneself of keeping options open; to wait until ‘fictional’ potential risk turns into facts that require immediate attention (Bobbitt & Ford, 1980; Yang et al, 2004). As Smallman (1996) found, project managers appear to distrust potentially erroneous, misleading or fictitious information that has yet to be confirmed as true or false. Hence, project managers may also defer commitment to a predestined outcome for as long as possible and keep any options open (Shafir et al, 1993; Bown et al, 2003).

## Authority

Project managers were often more likely to disengage with risk management if they thought that they were powerless and had limited authority to act. Many respondents felt that they were not empowered to execute risk mitigation actions. In some projects, resources such as manpower were not available to actively mitigate risk (e.g., by building a test environment). In other projects, although responsibility for risks was delegated, the ability to deal with them was not given.

There was also the fact that we were unable to implement counter-measures, because we didn’t have the client’s approval. Internally we had no problems solving anything, because we had all the available means and resources. For the client, it was a matter of successfully completing the project. Each risk that affected him could possibly have meant that his goals would not be achieved. He wanted to avoid that. (Project 1)

In this study, the belief of authority was often driven by external stakeholders, who opposed any proactive management of risks on political grounds. Once a risk becomes an issue, the responsibility automatically lies with the provider and at the cost of the provider.

In another instance, risk response actions were not carried out because of a lack of authority:

y because the risk management process should have initiated measures, countermeasures to correct the problems but that was not allowed to happen for the reason that I did not have the authority. (Project 12)

The lack of flexibility and the inability of project managers to act suggests a perception of powerlessness. Previous studies have found that power activates a person’s behavioural approach system and underlies our motivation to act, while powerlessness inhibits our behaviour and restricts response and risk-taking (Lammers et al, 2008). This degree of powerlessness manifested itself for some project managers as a resignation to fate:

There is no risk management as far as I was concerned in the implementation phase. All we had was a list of risks. So what? We have got a list. We can not do anything with it. (Project 7)

Beyond that the senior [company name] management are interested in revenue and taking on revenue, but don’t really engage at all in the success of [the] project per se and when risks are flagged up the general approach is to push them back down again. (Project 19)

Overall, the analysis provided a picture of stepwise disengagement from risk management by project managers (see Figure 2).

According to each process stage of risk management, there was a gradual uncoupling between prescribed procedure and action. The most significant factor in uncoupling process from practice related to defining and executing a response to risks that were identified and analysed. Hence, many significant events were known in advance, but this knowledge, in the form of assessed risk, was partially not responded to. Although the need to demonstrate legitimacy by following the systematic, normative view of risk management provided an incentive to manage risks, strong beliefs of value, competence, facts, and in particular the beliefs of authority and the freedom to act led to the disengagement from routinebased reliability.

## Discussion: the practical drift away from risk management

Given that existing theories of project risk management do not fully explain how and why adherence or deviation from prescribed risk management practices occurs, we draw on the broader literature on organisation theory, particularly the constructs of routines, practical drift and mindfulness. The project risk management literature has labelled systematic approaches to risk management and associated standard operating procedures and decision rules ‘routine-based’ risk management (e.g., Boehm, 1991; Butler & Gray, 2006). However, this literature has not fully explored the construct of routines. Organisation theorists have defined routines as ‘repetitive, recognisable patterns of interdependent actions, carried out by multiple actors’ (Feldman & Pentland, 2003, p. 95). The distinction between the ostensive and performative is an important basis for understanding routines (Feldman & Pentland, 2003). Routines comprise both an abstract pattern that guides action (the ostensive aspect) and actual performances (the performative aspect) that may vary every time the routine is enacted (Feldman & Pentland, 2003). Drawing on Latour (1986), Feldman & Pentland (2003) argue that the performative and ostensive aspects are a duality, whereby repetitive performances constitute, reproduce or alter the abstract pattern while the shared understanding of the routine shapes action. Therefore, while the concept of routines suggests stability, conformity and compliance, routines are actually continuously changing (Weick & Quinn, 1999; Tsoukas & Chia, 2002) and are generative (Pentland & Reuter, 1994).

The data shows that project managers in our study shared a common understanding of the risk management routine: project planning, identify threat, attach probability, determine effects, evaluate response, determine response owner and execute actions. The ostensive aspect of the risk management routine was also reinforced by a five key beliefs about risk management. First, that conforming to mainstream, prescribed risk management processes and procedures would enhance the legitimacy. Second, that risk management delivers value and has utility. Third, risk managers are concerned with appearing competent and being in control of risks. Fourth, project managers were concerned with facts and would adhere to risk management where there was strong evidence of a real threat. Fifth, project managers thought that it was critical to have authority and the power to act on risk information. When the performing of the routine over time revealed that these core beliefs about the routine were not met, risk managers were likely to disengage from risk management.

Snook (2000, p. 24) labels “the slow uncoupling of practice from procedure”, practical drift. Snook’s study of a friendly fire incident that led to the accidental shoot down of two Army Black Hawk helicopters over northern Iraq, which caused 26 deaths, shows that globally encoded written rules and procedures often transform into locally generated task-based routines. Such ‘practical drift’ or ‘fine tuning’ (Starbuck & Milliken, 1988) results from the need for individuals to organise around the immediate demands of work. In situations where rules are over-designed and do not match actual work most of the time there is a pressure for change (Snook, 2000). Over time, deviance (from the original procedure) becomes normalised (Vaughan, 1996) and ‘departures from the routine become routine’ (Dekker, 2005, p. 149). People within the system often believe that they are,

ycompliant with the emerging, local ways to accommodate multiple goals important to the organization. They are compliant, also, with a complex of peer pressures and professional expectations in which unofficial action yields better, quicker ways to do the job; in which unofficial action is a sign of competence and expertise; where unofficial action can override our outsmart hierarchical control and compensate for higher levels of organizational deficiencies or ignorance. (Dekker, 2005, p. 149)

Snook (2000) argues that the typical response to practical drift is enforcing more rules, compliance and greater inspection. However, as shown in this study, applying risk management did not prevent IS project managers from uncoupling practice from process.

This research specifically investigated IS projects where risks had been realised – that is, resulted in significant events. Future research should explore IS projects where managers disengage from risk management, yet risks are not realised. In principle, the lack of response to risk may leave project managers in an ‘y overly reactive and vulnerable position’ (Nelson, 2007, p. 75) and the practical drift away from prescribed processes may lead to increased vulnerability to risk and thus reduced reliable performance in IS projects. However, research in other fields suggests that risks are often avoided, trapped and mitigated. It has been argued that significant adverse events can be managed through mindfulness-based reliability (e.g., Langer, 1997; Weick & Sutcliffe, 2001). In principle, mindfulness involves the ability to detect changes in the environment and also take timely action. A mindful response ‘y is not an attempt to make the best choice from among available options but to create options’ (Langer, 1997, p. 114).

The literatures on high reliability (Weick & Roberts, 1993) and resilience engineering (Woods & Hollnagel, 2006) suggest that risks are often realised due to people’s inability to react rapidly and flexibly to unexpected variations in the context. In IS projects, it is impossible to plan with certainty (Packendorff, 1995) as they are likely to unfold in unique, unpredictable and unexpected ways (Cleden, 2009). Rather than enforce compliance with procedures, to enhance reliability it is necessary to empower those with operational expertise to make quick decisions and take corrective action when unexpected situations arise (Weick & Sutcliffe, 2001).

Mindfulness involves members of the project team anticipating threats, remaining open and alert to new information, willing to take on board different perspectives and understanding adverse events and learning from them (Langer, 1989; Weick & Roberts, 1993; Weick & Sutcliffe, 2001). For mindfulness-based risk management to work, individuals must be willing to remain open to new information and ideas while retaining the capacity for critical judgement (Langer, 1989),

As with quality assurance, it is beneficial to have one person whose job is to play devil’s advocate – to look for the reason that a project might fail and keep managers and developers from ignoring risks in the planning and execution. (Nelson, 2007, p. 75)

Such form of inspection may involve the informal use of peer reviews, walkthroughs and scenarios (e.g., Hofmann & Lehner, 2001; Lavy & Yadin, 2010) to prevent IS project managers becoming narrowly focussed and acting only in their own interest. Mindfulness-based reliability is about managers being encouraged, trained and given sufficient leeway to supplement the prescribed risk processes with situational assessment and to decide on the appropriate risk response.

## Conclusions

IS project failure has increasingly been associated with the failure to actively manage risk (e.g., Whittaker, 1999; Nelson, 2007). It is not surprising, therefore, that rules, procedures and processes have been created to ‘y focus on reducing or eliminating situated human cognition as the cause of errors’ (Butler & Gray, 2006, p. 214), increasing the dependence on routine-based reliability. Conventional wisdom suggests that in order to obtain the maximum benefit from risk management, it needs to be implemented in a systematic and disciplined manner. However, this study shows that despite implementing prescribed risk management practices and identifying significant risks, IS project managers often disengage from risk management. Given that this practical drift is a likely outcome in environments, such as IS projects, that are often rulebased and over-designed, we need to take seriously the promise of mindfulness-based reliability as a complementary approach. A caveat is that an over-emphasis on mindfulness may provide project actors too much latitude to develop a repertoire of spontaneous and improvised responses that could have unpredictable or suboptimal outcomes, which could be grounds for escalation in a crisis. Therefore, the key challenge for IS project risk management lies in finding an appropriate balance between routine-based and mindfulness-based reliability.
