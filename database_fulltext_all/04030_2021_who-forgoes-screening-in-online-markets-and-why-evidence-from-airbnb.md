# WHO FORGOES SCREENING IN ONLINE MARKETS AND WHY? EVIDENCE FROM AIRBNB<sup>1</sup>

Raveesh Mayya Leonard N. Stern School of Business, New York University, New York, NY, U.S.A. {raveesh@stern.nyu.edu}

Shun Ye School of Business, George Mason University, Fairfax, VA, U.S.A. {sye2@gmu.edu}

Siva Viswanathan and Rajshree Agarwal Robert H Smith School of Business, University of Maryland College Park College Park, MD, U.S.A. {sviswan1@umd.edu} {rajshree@umd.edu}

Screening is considered a necessary mechanism for alleviating information asymmetry but has also raised concerns about increased discrimination in online peer-to-peer market platforms. Paradoxically, providers of goods and services may also voluntarily forgo screening, even though it increases the risks and costs associated with poor matches. We examine who may choose to forgo screening and why, and its impact on their performance outcomes. Our study’s empirical context is the Airbnb platform, wherein the “Instant Book” feature enables hosts to forgo the screening of guests. Utilizing a unique panel dataset of all listings in New York City during August 2015-February 2017, we first explore the antecedents of voluntarily switching to instant booking and then causally identify the impacts of switching. Our study provides evidence of the economic benefits of forgoing screening from increased occupancy even as review ratings decline; these effects are stronger for Black and female hosts. We discuss the strategic and social welfare implications of these findings within the context of current conversations on discrimination and bias in the sharing economy.

Keywords: Online market, information asymmetry, screening, sharing economy, discrimination

## Introduction

Screening—the verification and vetting of potential trade partners—is a key mechanism to reduce information asymmetry frictions and alleviate the adverse selection problem (Akerlof 1970) in online peer-to-peer (P2P) markets (e.g., Horton 2017; Iyer et al. 2009; Lin et al. 2013). Screening helps determine the trade-worthiness of potential transactional partners by examining pertinent information (e.g., profiles, past transaction histories) or through direct communication, and to reject transaction requests from questionable parties. Two-sided screening is ubiquitously used in online platforms such as Uber (a ridesharing platform), EatWith (a dining experience platform) and Upwork (an online labor market platform). While screening increases market efficiency by ensuring better matches (Barach 2015; Iyer et al. 2009), recent work provides cautionary evidence of discriminatory/ excessive screening of one party by the other (Cui et al. 2020; Fradkin 2015; Pallais 2014; Romanyuk 2017). For example, Cui et al. (2020) find that Airbnb hosts engage in discriminatory screening of guests based on Black vs. White names. Similarly, Chan and Wang (2018) show that clients in online labor platforms screen workers by gender.

Left unexamined, however, is the recent phenomenon of one side voluntarily forgoing the option to screen even as the other side continues to screen. For example, car owners in the automobile sharing platform Turo and dog sitters in the dog boarding platform Dogvacay can enable the “Instant Book” feature to directly confirm booking requests. Instant booking implies that providers forsake the benefits from verification of match quality and increase the risk of encountering poorly matched buyers. Despite its increasing usage, we lack systematic studies of the phenomenon. Who may choose to forgo screening and why, and what are the resultant outcomes of forgoing this option?

We address this research gap in the setting of Airbnb—the online lodging marketplace platform—which expanded on its traditional mechanisms requiring hosts’ approval of guests’ booking requests (i.e., screening is enabled) by popularizing the “Instant Book” feature that allows hosts to automatically accept every booking request (i.e., screening is forgone) in 2014. The setting enables us to examine the voluntary decision of hosts to switch from traditional to instant booking, relate characteristics of market listings and hosts to such behavior, and investigate the market performance of the hosts’ listings based on their behavior. Given that most online P2P markets, and particularly our research context—Airbnb—initially offered only the traditional booking option, we treat traditional booking as the default norm (i.e., the nonswitchers). We ask and answer two research questions: First, we explore the antecedents of forgoing screening for a listing and ask, what characteristics of market listings (and the hosts) are associated with the likelihood of forgoing screening? Second, we causally identify the consequences of forgoing screening for a listing and ask, how do the market outcomes—occupancy rate, listing price, and review ratings—for a listing change after it forgoes screening as compared to a listing that does not do so? Answers to these questions will help us understand why some are more likely to forgo screening than others. Figure 1 depicts our research framework and questions.

Based on a unique panel dataset of all Airbnb listings in New York City between August 2015 and February 2017, we conduct various listing-level analyses to answer the proposed questions. Our context represents a self-selected intervention of forgoing screening. We begin with a binomial logit model to explore what listing conditions and host characteristics are associated with the likelihood of a listing forgoing screening. Next, we employ propensity score matching (PSM) in conjunction with difference-indifferences (DiD) analysis (e.g., Liu and Lynch 2011; Smith and Todd 2005) to causally identify the impact of forgoing screening. Specifically, we create a one-to-one match of listings that switch to instant booking (i.e., the treated group) and listings that do not (i.e., the control group) based on propensity scores across both groups prior to the “treatment.” We then employ a DiD model to infer the causal impact of forgoing screening on a listing’s occupancy rate, price, and review ratings, using a three-equation simultaneous estimation system.

We find that forgoing screening is more likely when listings have dwindling demand, i.e., when listings have midlevel occupancies and face greater recent declines in occupancies, and/or price. This provides evidence that improving occupancy is one of the major drivers for voluntarily forgoing screening. Switching to instant booking seems to pay off economically—occupancy levels increase by an average of 13.52% (or 1.79 nights per month) with no change in listing price, translating to an average of roughly \$300 in increased revenue per month. However, this is at the cost of decreased review ratings by about 1.20% (or 1.078 points).

Underlying the above “average effects” based on demand conditions, we find significant heterogeneity in race/ethnicity and gender regarding who is more likely to switch, and their associated outcomes. Black hosts are more likely to forgo screening than White hosts and also reap higher benefits from doing so. Black hosts experience a higher percentage improvement in occupancy (18.37% as compared to 14.62% for White hosts), with lower declines in review ratings (a 0.28% drop as compared to 1.56% drop for White hosts). Turning to gender differences, female hosts are more likely to forgo screening than male hosts. Moreover, while forgoing screening causes male hosts to decrease their listing price by \$4.28, we do not see such decreases for female hosts, indicating that they gain more (\$56.62 in monthly revenue) than male hosts.

Our main analyses are robust to various robustness checks, including falsification tests that analyze the reenabling of screening for some listings. These analyses also yield important additional insights, largely consistent with the underlying drivers for forgoing screening. First, switching back to screening is more likely for listings that experience greater drops in ratings from forgoing screening. Second, we find no differences based on gender in the likelihood of continuing to forgo screening. Finally, Black hosts are more likely to continue to forgo screening compared to White host; this is true regardless of the gender of hosts.

Overall, our study documents who forgoes screening and why. In examining these understudied effects of forgoing screening, our study makes important contributions. First, we revisit the traditional view on mechanism design in online P2P markets that screening is a necessity for information asymmetry mitigation. We show that providing online market participants the choice to forgo screening can be beneficial, as it enables each participant to engage in their individual cost-benefit analyses and customize their choices based on their own characteristics and local demand conditions.

Second, we contribute to the emerging literature on the online sharing economy by showing how changes in mechanism design have important social consequences. While prior studies have noted that hosts (sellers) screen and discriminate against guests (buyers) in online platforms, our study shows that policy changes that give voluntary choices to hosts regarding screening behavior actually reduce the likelihood that they will themselves encounter discrimination in online markets. In doing so, we highlight that policy designs of online markets need to pay careful attention to customization and flexibility not only for increasing economic efficiency but also for improving social outcomes related to race/ethnicity and gender.

## Theoretical Background

As backdrop and motivation for our study, we first provide a focused literature review related to two key features of online P2P markets: screening mechanisms for addressing information asymmetry, and within-heterogeneity in demand conditions facing individual listings (products or services). We then discuss the antecedents and consequences of forgoing screening.

## Screening as a Mechanism for Addressing Information Asymmetry in Online Markets

Most transactions on P2P markets are one-time and decentralized transactions, with little opportunity for direct contact or extended communication. Accordingly, information asymmetry is a particularly thorny issue, as both sellers and buyers encounter problems of adverse selection and moral hazard (Akerlof 1970), and screening is one of the key mechanisms to alleviate it.<sup>2</sup>

Screening permits an uninformed agent to take actions to ascertain the quality or type of the potential trade partner involved in a transaction (Stiglitz 1977). Two mechanisms of screening under imperfect information are identified in previous studies: indirect screening and direct screening (Buchmueller 1995; Hoff and Stiglitz 1990). Indirect screening refers to the uninformed agent’s practice of offering a menu of contracts or options to the trade partner to make the agent self-reveal their type (Bastani et al. 2015). In direct screening, the uninformed agent exerts efforts to actively gather, investigate and evaluate information about the trade partner to infer their type (Cornell and Welch 1996). In this study, we focus on direct screening which is frequently observed in online platforms. For example, the online lodging sharing platform Airbnb provides various tools for hosts to know about potential guests: reviews left by other hosts, identity verification such as Facebook profile or Government ID, <sup>3</sup> and private messaging that allows hosts to infer the quality of the guest based on textual communication. From the host’s perspective, these direct screening tools help the host avoid poor matches that could lead to various forms of bad consequences and damages, such as the guest leaving a mess that requires extra cleaning from the host, the guest ignoring the housing rule and causing discomfort to the host, and the guest leaving unexpected negative reviews that could hurt future occupancies, etc. Direct screening also helps hosts avoid inconvenience and intangible costs that some guests may cause.<sup>4</sup> For the sake of brevity, hereinafter we refer to direct screening as screening.

Researchers have documented the benefits of screening mechanisms in online labor markets where client screening results in higher-quality workers and output at lower prices (Barach 2015), and in online lending markets where lenders’ screening of borrowers based on peer group comparisons mitigates adverse selection (Weiss et al. 2010) and reduces interest rates for borrowers with lower credit risks (Iyer et al. 2009). However, scholars have cautioned about screening also leading to excessive vetting, resulting in a reduction in efficiency and/or welfare. Fradkin (2015) documents an increase in market frictions on Airbnb because almost a third of buyer requests are potentially rejected due to the hosts’ screening. Pallais (2014) notes a decrease in inefficient matches in the online labor market Upwork (previously called oDesk) when clients disregard screening and offer inexperienced workers their first job. Analytically, both Romanyuk (2017) and Kanoria and Saban (2017) show welfare gains from platforms’ policies that limit information disclosure, which reduces excessive screening. On a related note, scholars have also documented the excessive use of screening mechanisms to discriminate against specific groups. For example, researchers have documented discrimination against travelers with disabilities (Ameri et al. 2017), younger guests, and guests with children (Karlsson et al. 2017).

Thus, existing research depicts mixed findings for the use of screening mechanisms; while scholars note the beneficial effects of screening for the agent engaging in this mechanism, there is evidence on its welfare reducing role, particularly when taking into account the increased discrimination against the party being screened.

## Heterogeneity in Demand Conditions for Listings in P2P Markets

Even under conditions of perfect information, there is significant heterogeneity in price and quality combinations in transactions in P2P markets. Accordingly, in addition to overall demand conditions facing all providers of an online listing (product or service), individual listings specific conditions that relate to real or perceived differences in quality and price may also lead to heterogeneity in outcomes (Gunter and Onder 2018; Jin and Kato 2006). For example, in lodging-sharing platforms such as Airbnb—our research context—although “average” demand for rentals is city and time specific, “heterogeneous” demand stems from characteristics of the individual listings— the listing’s price, amenities, geographical location, etc.

In addition to listing characteristics, host characteristics such as race/ethnicity and gender also influence individual listing demand. Specifically, discrimination (statistical or taste-based) based on such characteristics has been well documented in the literature. Studies on the labor market have documented a preference for hiring White applicants over Black applicants (e.g., Pager et al. 2009; Reimers 1983) resulting in persistent racial wage gaps (e.g., Black et al. 2006). Research also indicates that Black employees receive less favorable treatment—fewer rewards for the same level of “good” but more punishments for the same level of “bad”—than White employees (e.g., DeSante 2013; Steffensmeier and Demuth 2006). An underlying driving factor of such discrimination in markets with information asymmetry stems from racialized bias that White individuals are perceived as more trustworthy and honest than their Black counterparts (Doleac and Stein 2013; Mays et al. 2007).

Even in P2P markets, studies of baseball card trades note that Black agents receive inferior offers relative to White agents (List 2004), with 20% reduced prices observed in a field experiment on eBay (Ayres et al. 2015). Studies of online lodging platforms reveal similar lower prices for equivalent listings by Black hosts relative to White hosts (Edelman et al. 2017; Kakar et al. 2017). On P2P lending platforms, Black borrowers are less likely to receive funding (Pope and Sydnor 2011). Discrimination can also occur in the other direction (sellers discriminating against buyers). For example, Cui et al. (2020) conduct two randomized field experiments on Airbnb and observe racial discrimination by hosts based on guest race/ethnicity: absent alternative quality information, requests from guests with Black names are accepted significantly less often than requests from guests with White names.

Gender-based prejudice has also been similarly reported in a variety of contexts. Gender gaps in economic outcomes, including wage differentials, are consistently reported, despite the increasing participation of women in the labor force and improvements in women’s educational background and economic decision-making experience (e.g., Altonji and Blank 1999). Prior field studies have also found evidence for price discrimination against women, as they may be perceived to have lower reservation prices (Ayres and Siegelman 1995).

In P2P markets specifically, Kricheli-Katz and Regev (2016) analyzed over a million transactions on eBay, finding that equally qualified female sellers receive fewer bids, about 80 cents for every dollar a male seller receives when selling identical new products. Chan and Wang (2018) find that while female workers are more likely to be shortlisted and hired by employers in P2P labor market platforms, such differences are mainly based on employers without much experience on such platforms. Similarly, Stroube (2021)’s study of online lending platforms utilizes an exogenous policy shock to reveal tastebased bias against female borrowers.

Thus, in addition to information asymmetry, another important characteristic of online P2P markets is the heterogeneity of demand conditions for listings, whether driven by listing or provider characteristics.

## Antecedents and Consequences of Forgoing Screening

Several gaps emerge from the above literature review. One, the literature on screening mechanisms leaves uncontested the implicit assumption that screening is always beneficial from the perspective of the party who engages in screening. This runs counter to the growing popularity of voluntary willingness to forgo screening in online markets. Many online markets, as noted in the introduction, enable an “Instant Book” feature, where sellers can select not to engage in the screening of buyers. Two, the literature on mitigating information asymmetry is not fully integrated with the literature on heterogeneity in providers and consumers. These studies do not account for the impact of within-heterogeneity of listings/providers on the differential use of screening mechanisms. Accordingly, studies examining screening mechanisms in P2P markets focus on their costs and benefits on overall market efficiency or for the other party subject to screening from the focal party. To the best of our knowledge, there are no studies that examine whether forgoing screening can benefit the party choosing to forgo screening or which party realizes greater benefits.

In this study, we seek to answer when and whom we are likely to observe forgoing screening, and what the outcomes of doing so are. In doing so, we seek to understand why certain types of hosts are more likely to forgo screening than others. At the outset, we note several boundary conditions. First, we are interested in markets where providers have the option of either retaining screening (traditional booking) or forgoing it (instant booking) with low switching costs, meaning that providers can switch between maintaining and forgoing screening relatively easily. Second, we focus on the focal provider’s perspective rather than the perspective of market efficiency or the other party’s perspective. Third, we focus on online markets where the other party can continue to screen, i.e., the focal provider is still subject to potential buyers’ screening and ascertaining their own tradeworthiness.

Our first research question examines the antecedents of forgoing screening in an exploratory manner, seeking to uncover the factors associated with the likelihood of forgoing screening. From the focal provider’s perspective, forgoing screening offers the benefits of a potential increase in the demand for their listings, because the “Instant Book” feature accommodates guests’ time and convenience preferences and may also appeal to new guests on the platform, the ones with limited transaction histories. However, forgoing screening implies an increased risk and thus the cost associated with of having more lower-quality matches. Signaling theory also suggests that the status of forgoing screening can act as a signal to prospective guests (Jian et al. 2019; Spence 1973). Prospective guests may perceive listing providers that choose to forgo screening as lower-quality hosts in need of renting their underutilized capacities. Especially if the proportion of “Instant Book” listings is small, the negative perception conveyed by the choice of “Instant Book” adds to the cost of forgoing screening for sellers. Additionally, lower-quality matches absent screening may result in lower ratings or reviews, which comes with the potential cost of lower demand in the future. Thus, when deciding whether to forgo screening, sellers must assess the benefits and costs relative to retaining the option. It stands to reason then, that the use of screening mechanisms will have different associated costs and benefits for different listings. For listings in demand, the benefits of market expansion from forgoing screening are limited. However, for listings with overcapacity or excess holdings, the benefits of increased demand from a larger pool of guests as a consequence of forgoing screening could outweigh the potential costs. Based on this cost-benefit calculus logic, we expect listings that experience lower (or declining) individual demand to have higher excess capacity and thus be more likely to forgo screening.

Equipped with the knowledge of when a listing is more likely to forgo screening, our second research question causally examines the market outcomes associated with forgoing screening. Again, consistent with the cost-benefit logic, we expect outcomes to be consistent with a “separating equilibrium.” The market outcomes for listings and providers will reflect the commensurate benefits and costs, given endogenous decision-making by providers. We expect that immediate returns—in terms of higher occupancy rates—would be higher for switchers relative to comparable nonswitchers, but this would be accompanied by lower review ratings and/or lower listing prices, which are reflective of higher costs.

As the characteristics of the providers also contribute to the differences in demand conditions they face, the cost-benefit calculus will differ based on these factors. For example, considering that Black hosts and female hosts face discrimination and consequently lower demand in online markets (e.g., Edelman et al. 2017; Kakar et al. 2017), they may have more incentives to forgo screening to benefit from a larger pool of guests. Therefore, in answering the two research questions, we also examine the roles that host gender and race/ethnicity may play.

## Research Context and Data

We examined the above research questions on Airbnb, the world’s largest online marketplace for short-term rentals (Barker 2020). The platform allows homeowners (i.e., hosts) to rent unused space out to guests seeking accommodations. Launched in 2008 in the U.S., Airbnb now operates in more than 100,000 cities in over 190 countries, with over seven million active listings.<sup>5</sup> While initially limited to traditional booking, Airbnb introduced the “Instant Book” option in 2014. The traditional booking option represents two-way screening: the guest screens across listings and submits a request to the host in the first step; the host screens and either accepts or denies the request in the second step. In the instant booking mode, hosts voluntarily forgo their own screening of the guest in the second step and automatically accept every booking request (see Appendix Figure A1 for a comparison between traditional booking and instant booking).

Several elements of this research context make it ideal for our study. First, Airbnb is a decentralized P2P platform with participants (hosts and guests) making their own choices. This allows us to study the impact of market mechanisms such as instant booking on individual outcomes. Further, switching to instant booking is relatively simple and seamless on Airbnb, as hosts can set house rules, guest requirements, and length of stay when enabling the “Instant Book” feature. Third, it is worth noting that the “Instant Book” feature requires only hosts to waive their rights to screen their guests but allows the guests to continue to search through the listings, screen them, and shortlist the ones they prefer. Finally, the context suits our data needs: as described in greater detail below, the analysis requires data about listing and host characteristics, which not only determine their screening choice, but are also visible to potential guests for screening. Additionally, it requires data on outcomes associated with choosing to implement or forgo screening, such as subsequent occupancy, price, and ratings.

We compiled our data by triangulating across publicly available data on Airbnb and proprietary data procured from a business intelligence firm that publishes market intelligence reports on Airbnb for about 80,000 cities. Our sample consists of all Airbnb listings in New York City (NYC) between August 2015 and February 2017. To ensure generalizable implications for a typical and active listing on Airbnb, we excluded listings priced above \$1,000 a night in any month during the observation window, as well as listings appearing for less than a year, <sup>6</sup> resulting in 13,757 listings in our panel. It is pertinent to note that most listings in our sample (about 96%) switched to instant booking at most once; less than 4% of all listings switched to instant booking twice or more. This suggests that forgoing screening is not a period-by-period decision for most hosts. Instead, hosts preferred to either stay with traditional booking or instant booking, whichever worked better for them based on their individual cost-benefit calculus. Consistent with our research questions, the data permit us to differentiate across two groups of listings: (1) listings that always used the traditional booking status; (2) listings that were initially in traditional booking but later switched to instant booking (i.e., switchers). These two categories constituted 96% of all listings; traditional booking represented the dominant choice, and instant booking represented 16% of the listings in our sample.<sup>7</sup>

While our definition of switchers includes listings that switched back to traditional booking later on, we only included their observations prior to switching back in the main analyses and ensured robustness of our results through additional tests without such listings. Our final data as described above resulted in 196,155 listing-month observations in the main analyses. For each listing, we had time-varying information about its characteristics (e.g., cancellation policy, room type, number of photos, etc.), as well as characteristics of the host (gender/race, Superhost designation,<sup>8</sup> year of entry into Airbnb, displayed name, displayed picture, etc.). We also had information on the local competition (e.g., number of competing listings in the same neighborhood, number of Superhosts in the same neighborhood, etc.), and data on the listing’s monthly outcomes (listing price, occupancy rate, review ratings).

## Variable Definitions

Table 1 provides a detailed description of the variables used in the major analysis. The summary statistics and the correlation matrix are provided in Appendix Tables A1 and A2.

Dependent Variables: Our first dependent variable—the choice of forgoing screening is coded as a dummy variable, which takes the value of 1 if the listing was available for instant booking, and 0 if the listing had a traditional booking status. The other three variables measuring outcomes relate to the occupancy rate, price, and review rating of the listing. We measured the occupancy of a listing for each month as the fraction of days the listing is booked (occupied) relative to the number of days the listing is made available in the month. The price was based on the listing price in US dollars for the listing-month observation, and review ratings were obtained for each month for each of the listings. On average, listings were made available for booking 24.5 days per month, its listing price was \$167.87, and its monthly occupancy rate was 0.54 (or 0.54\*24.5=13.23 nights).

Independent Variables: Our key independent variables include listing-specific variables representing the listing’s demand conditions, and host-specific variables. In particular, prior levels and changes in occupancy rates, price, and rating reflect the demand conditions specific to the listing, after accounting for many controls, as listed below and in Table 1. The host-specific variables include dummies that indicate whether the host is a Superhost or whether the host is a professional.<sup>8</sup> We measured host tenure as the number of days since the host registered on Airbnb. For each host, we determined the race/ethnicity and gender as described in the section below. We included variables that capture local competition by leveraging the similar listings panel displayed for each listing (12 similar listings per panel). Finally, listingspecific control variables include review count, cancellation policy, type of listing (shared room, private room, or entire property), minimum number of nights required for a booking, number of photos, and listing popularity (measured as the number of times the focal listing appears in the similar listings panel of competing listings).

Chief among the host variables are race/ethnicity and gender. We classified race/ethnicity and gender of the hosts based on inspection of profiles displayed on Airbnb. The race/ethnicity categories include White, Black, Asian, Hispanic, and uncertain (e.g., ambiguous name and obscure profile picture is obscure, showing only the back of a person, for example). The three gender categories include male, female, and uncertain (e.g., the host name is an ambiguous name such as AJ and/or the profile picture does not show the host’s face). We painstakingly coded the data manually because of the advantages of the manual coding approach over the algorithmic approach for identifying stylized names (e.g., @m@nd@ instead of Amanda) and lowquality photos (e.g., caricatured/tilted/side profile photos).

Further, we validated the accuracy of the manually coded host race/ethnicity and gender labels by randomly selecting 20% of the sample and cross-validating their labels with those predicted by computer algorithms (Ambekar et al. 2009; Chan and Wang 2018). We combined two algorithmic approaches: the first relies on predictive models based on host names<sup>9</sup> and the second relies on predictions using host pictures through facial recognition algorithms.<sup>10</sup> Neither algorithm is necessarily superior to the other for revealing information about race/ethnicity and gender, which is why we combined both and designated the race/ethnicity and gender of a host as algorithmically identified if the labels generated by the two algorithms matched and marked them as uncertain otherwise. Our cross-validation between manually coded labels and algorithmically generated labels yielded an agreement of 93.75% for host race/ethnicity and an agreement of 99.46% for host gender. These high levels of agreement increased confidence in our use of manual coding; in additional robustness tests, we confirmed the consistency of the findings across each type of approach (manual coding; name-based inference, and picture-based inference) as well as their combinations.

## Antecedents of Forgoing Screening

To examine the factors that influence the voluntary choice of forgoing screening—our first research question—we use the following logit model:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Logit(instant\_ {b} okable} _ {i t}) = c _ {_ 0} + c _ {_ 1} * o c c u p a n c y _ {i t - 1} + \\ c _ {_ 2} * o c c u p a n c y _ {i t - 1} ^ {2} + c _ {_ 3} * \Delta o c c u p a n c y _ {i t - 1} + c _ {_ 4} * p r i c e _ {i t - 1} \\ + c _ {_ 5} * r a t i n g _ {i t - 1} + c _ {_ 6} * h o s t \_ c o n t r o l s _ {i t} + \\ c _ {_ 7} * l i s t i n g \_ c o n t r o l s _ {i t} + c _ {_ 8} * c o m p e t i t o r \_ c o n t r o l s _ {i t} + \\ c _ {_ 9} * m o n t h \_ d u m m y _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where the subscript i indexes the listings and the subscript t indexes the months. Behaviorally, Equation (1) models a listing’s decision of switching from the initial traditional booking status to instant booking status during our observation period. As noted earlier, despite that in theory a host could forgo screening at any given time, in practice most listings do not frequently switch back and forth between screening and forgoing screening. Instead, in our sample, they switch to instant booking at most once. In this case, the choice of forgoing screening is irrelevant once a listing has already switched. Therefore, following prior research (e.g., Gopal and Gao 2009; King et al. 2005), we drop observations related to a listing from the analysis after the listing has switched to instant booking. As argued earlier, the costbenefit calculus of forgoing screening for a listing is related to the listing’s demand situation, such that lower (or declining) demand is associated with a higher likelihood of forgoing screening because the perceived benefits of increased occupancy are perceived to outweigh the associated cost of doing so. Therefore, we model a listing’s decision to switch to instant booking in month t as affected by its occupancy in the previous month t-1 and its recent change in occupancy from month t-2 to month t-1. We include a quadratic form of occupancy<sub>it-1</sub> to detect a possible curvilinear relationship between occupancy<sub>it-1</sub> and the propensity to switch to instant booking. In addition, we control for the listing’s other market performance indicators such as price and review rating in the previous month and their recent changes. We note that while our main analysis focuses on measures in the prior month, additional robustness checks confirm that results are qualitatively similar when the levels and changes are computed in the prior periods ranging from three to six months. Our choice of the prior month is to minimize the loss of observations due to differencing.

We control for several variables that might also drive a host’s decision to forgo screening, such as: (1) host tenure— novice hosts may use instant booking to learn to evaluate the quality of guests; (2) whether the host is a professional host—professional hosts, like hotels, are accustomed to the practice of not screening guests; (3) number of competitors in the neighborhood that have forgone screening— competitive pressure may force a host to mimic competitors; and (4) minimum length of stay—a listing with a higher threshold for the minimum length of stay may be more concerned about the potential damages resulting from a poor match. In addition to controlling for factors such as the number of listing photos, the host’s Superhost status and the like, we also control for seasonality by including monthfixed effects in the model. Given our interest in exploring how host gender and race/ethnicity may be associated with the decision to forgo screening, we do not include host-fixed effects in our exploratory models. The coefficient of occupancy<sub>it-1</sub> is significant and positive, and the coefficient of the quadratic form of occupancy<sub>it-1</sub> is significant and negative. These results suggest that having midlevel occupancy levels is positively associated with the likelihood of forgoing screening for a listing.

## Impacts of Forgoing Screening

## Difference-in-Differences Estimation Model

To answer our second research question examining the impact of forgoing screening on a listing’s market performance, we adopted a difference-in-differences (DiD) model, which is commonly used to infer the causal effect of a “treatment” on the treated (Meyer 1995). In our case, the treatment is the switch to instant booking, and the treated are the listings that switched to instant booking (as compared to the control group of listings that stayed with the traditional booking mode). Given that the treatment in our context is staggered, as each treated listing may switch at different times, we followed prior research (e.g., Autor 2003; Gao and Zhang 2016) and normalized the time dimension: for each treated listing, we set the month it received the treatment to time 0, with months in the pre-treatment period in reverse chronological order denoted as month -1, -2, etc. and months in the post-treatment period sequentially denoted as month 1, 2, etc.

There are three key threats to inference to address in our DiD estimation approach. First, who receives the treatment is nonrandom, as discussed above in our first research question; thus, we needed to address the selection bias regarding who receives the treatment. Approaches to addressing this issue include matching a control group with the treated group (Abadie 2005), using matching methods such as propensity score matching, which has been employed in various nonexperimental settings when the assignment of treatment is not controlled by the researcher (Dehejia and Wahba 2002). Propensity score matching (PSM) has been frequently used in combination with DiD analysis for causal inference of the effect of a nonexogenous intervention (e.g., Liu and Lynch 2011; Smith and Todd 2005). Therefore, following the PSM method and taking into account the dynamic timing of treatment across listings, we first predicted the propensity score of a listing receiving the treatment at the observed treatment month (e.g., June 2016) using a logit regression on key observable covariates (i.e., time-variant covariates such as occupancy level, listing price, review rating, host tenure and number of reviews, and time-invariant covariates such as listing type and listing location) in the month prior to the treatment (e.g., May 2016).<sup>12</sup> We also ensured that each matched listing is within a three-mile radius of the treated listing (calculated based on the zip codes of listings) to further eliminate any difference caused by geographic areas. To minimize the bias in the estimated treatment effect, for every treated listing we applied one-to-one nearest neighbor matching without replacement on the propensity score to identify a matched control listing that is used as a match only once (Austin 2010).<sup>13</sup> A covariate balance check shown in Appendix Table A4 provides evidence that a treated listing and a control listing are similar in all covariates after PSM despite their significant differences before matching.

We further checked whether the distributions of host race/ethnicity and of host gender remained consistent after the propensity score matching. As shown in Table 4, the prematching distribution of host race/ethnicity is comparable to the post-matching distribution of host race/ethnicity. Similarly, the distribution of host gender remained qualitatively unchanged after the matching. These results suggest that the sample after matching is representative of hosts of various races/ethnicities and genders in the overall sample. Furthermore, treated and control listings are similar in terms of host race/ethnicity distribution and host gender distribution, providing further confidence on the validity of the sample used in the analysis.

Finally, a third challenge to our estimation is simultaneity is the determination of occupancy, price, and review ratings. Given that endogenous dependent variables such as occupancy<sub>it</sub> and price<sub>it</sub> also enter the estimation equations on the right-hand side and the three error terms may be correlated with each other because of unobservable common factors related to a listing, we simultaneously estimated the three equations by employing the three-stage least squares (3SLS)

approach which has been commonly used in estimating a system of equations in similar situations (e.g., Aral et al. 2018; Chang and Gurbaxani 2013; Roberts and Schlenkera 2013). In economic theory, it is reasonable to assume that competing products’ characteristics have no direct impact on a consumer’s utility for a product but may directly affect the characteristics of the product that are endogenously decided based on competitions (Berry et al. 1995). For instance, Hausman (1996) argues that the variations in the equivalent characteristics of competitors or neighbors should correlate with the focal player’s characteristics but would only affect other characteristics of the focal player indirectly. In a similar vein, Reynaert and Verboven (2014) discuss and establish why competitors’ characteristics are valid instruments for the focal player’s price. Following these studies, a number of studies have used the characteristics of competing products as instrument variables, sometimes referred to as Hausman type instruments, for a product’s endogenous characteristics (e.g., Aral et al. 2018; Cachon et al. 2019; Ghose and Han 2014). In keeping with this, we use the average price of a listing’s 12 local competitors as an instrument for price<sub>it</sub>. Similarly, we use the average occupancy of a listing’s closest local competitors as an instrument for occupancy<sub>it</sub>.<sup>16</sup>

## Robustness Checks

To ensure the robustness of our DiD estimations to a potential serial correlation in the residuals that could lead to inconsistent standard errors, we follow the recommended approach by Bertrand et al. (2004). We collapse and average the time serials data into a panel of length 2—a pre-treatment time point and a post-treatment time point—which circumvents the autocorrelation issue without making any specific assumption about its form. The results are shown in Appendix Table A7. Consistent with earlier results, we observe a positive and significant coefficient for inst\_book\_group<sub>i</sub>\*post\_switch<sub>t</sub> in the equation for occupancy and a negative and significant coefficient for inst\_book\_group<sub>i</sub>\*post\_switch<sub>t</sub> in the equation for rating. Results are also consistent for the moderating effects of host race/ethnicity and gender.

To further ensure that our results are not biased by the choice of the matching method, we conducted two additional robustness checks. First, we employed coarsened exact matching (CEM) and then replicated the DiD analysis. Unlike PSM that requires a parametric estimation of propensity scores, CEM is nonparametric and does not make assumptions on the data generation process. Appendix Table A8 outlines the bins for CEM and the associated DiD estimation results. We obtained qualitatively similar results. Second, to address the issue of any remaining unobservable factors that may affect a listing’s likelihood to forgo screening, we employed look-ahead propensity score matching (LA-PSM), which has been used in prior studies (e.g., Bapna and Umyarov 2015). In this approach, we considered listings that switched to instant booking at a later time as the control listings. As a result, unobservable predictors of the treatment affecting both treated and control listings were controlled as much as possible. Again, we obtained consistent results in the DiD analysis based on this new matching method, which yielded 1018 treated and 1018 control listings. These additional results, shown in Appendix Table A9, increase confidence in the robustness of our findings. In an additional robustness check, we tightened the definition of switchers to only include listings that switched to instant booking and stayed with it while excluding listings that switched back to traditional booking later. All the major results still hold.

Finally, we tested the robustness of our findings against alternative measures of the dependent variables. First, instead of examining listing occupancy and listing price individually, we multiplied them as listing revenue, a performance indicator that the host also cares about. This reduced our system of estimations into two equations. We obtained qualitatively consistent results. For example, we found that forgoing screening increases a listing’s revenue but decreases the listing’s rating. Second, while our ratio measure of occupancy helps mitigate the issue that a listing’s availability is endogenously decided by the host, we performed two additional analyses to further alleviate the concern. In the first analysis, we used the total number of nights a listing is booked in a month as an alternative measure of occupancy and obtained largely consistent results. In the second analysis, we examined whether a listing’s availability may be endogenously influenced by the host’s decision to forgo screening. We found that the number of days marked as unavailable drops for both treated and control listings after the time of treatment. However, the difference in the magnitude of drop between the treated and control listings is statistically insignificant. These analyses, available upon request, help rule out the endogeneity concern of listing availability being affected by the decision to forgo screening and offer further support for the robustness of our findings.

## Falsification Tests

Overall, our analyses suggest that: (1) one major reason hosts may voluntarily forgo screening is to improve listing occupancy, albeit at the cost of lower review ratings; and (2) White hosts incur higher costs and improve the occupancy levels of their listings less than Black hosts after forgoing screening. Following the same cost-benefit calculus logic, these results imply that listings associated with higher costs of forgoing screening and listings by White hosts are more likely to switch back to the screening state. Therefore, as a falsification test, we also examined which listings among switchers are more likely to switch back to traditional screening (whom we call the two-way switchers), relative to continuing with forgoing screening. The sample for this analysis includes observations for all switchers after they have forgone screening. To account for the fact that the value of the dependent variable (traditional\_bookable<sub>it</sub>) will always be 1 after a two-way switcher has switched back to traditional booking, we also only retained two-way switchers’ observations in the month of switching back while dropping their observations afterward. Formally, we model the decision to switch back a listing to traditional booking as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {logit(traditional\_ {b} ookable} _ {i t}) = \pi_ {0} + \\ \pi_ {1} * \text {occupancy\_change\_percent} _ {i t - 1} + \\ \pi_ {2} * \text {price\_change\_percent} _ {i t - 1} + \\ \pi_ {3} * \text {rating\_change\_percent} _ {i t - 1} + \pi_ {4} * \text {host\_controls} _ {i t} + \\ \pi_ {5} * \text {listing\_controls} _ {i t} + \pi_ {6} * \text {competitor\_controls} _ {i t} + \\ \pi_ {7} * \text {month\_dummy} _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

Similar to Equation (1), here we model a listing’s decision to switch back to traditional booking in month t as a function of variables measuring how much a listing has benefited from forgoing screening, including change in occupancy (occupancy\_change\_percent<sub>it-1</sub>), change in listing price (price\_change\_percent<sub>it-1</sub>), and change in cumulative review rating (rating\_change\_percent<sub>it-1</sub>), and other host-related and listing-related variables.

We report the estimation results in Column (1) of Appendix Table A10. We observe insignificant coefficients of occupancy\_change\_percent<sub>it-1</sub> and price\_change\_percent<sub>it-1</sub>. However, the coefficient of rating\_change\_percent<sub>it-1</sub> is significant and negative, suggesting that listings that experience greater drops in rating due to forgoing screening are more likely to re-enable screening. Overall, our results suggest no discernable differences effects of occupancy and price changes, but listings that experience a higher drop in review ratings and thus incur a higher cost from forgoing screening are more likely to switch back to screening.

Black hosts, we find, have a lower propensity to re-enable screening as compared to White hosts. This result, along with our finding on rating\_change\_percent<sub>it-1</sub> (that listings with higher drops in ratings are more likely to re-enable screening), confirms our conjecture and provides further support for our main findings regarding the antecedents and consequences of forgoing screening. We found no significant difference between female hosts and male hosts in their propensities to re-enable screening. However, in additional subsample analyses (see columns (2) and (3) of Appendix Table A10) we determined that, among women, Black women are more likely to continue to forgo screening than White women. Similarly, among men, Black men are more likely to stick with forgoing screening than White men.

## Discussion and Conclusion

## Key Findings and Discussion

Motivated by the dearth of research examining an increased prevalence of forgoing screening in online P2P markets, we systematically investigated who chooses to forgo screening and why and what the outcomes of doing so are in the context of Airbnb, a popular P2P sharing platform. Our findings are summarized in Table 9. We find that the probability of forgoing screening increases when the expected benefits outweigh the expected “costs” of doing so in the decisionmaking calculus specific to the listing. Specifically, forgoing screening is driven by dwindling demand conditions facing individual listings, i.e., a recent decline in occupancies and/or price, and higher competitive pressure, i.e., screening forgone by a majority of local competition listings.

Host characteristics also matter, in two different ways. One, supply characteristics such as being a new entrant or a professional host increases the likelihood of forgoing screening. Thus, just as inexperienced hosts leverage instant booking to increase demand for their listings, so do professional hosts, perhaps because their prior experience makes them more comfortable with the practice of not screening guests. Second and importantly, noneconomic characteristics of hosts such as race/ethnicity and gender are associated with forgoing screening. Consistent with the literature on discrimination and bias, we find that Black hosts and female hosts are more likely to enable instant booking to increase demand for their listings.

The outcomes associated with the choice to forgo screening are consistent with the expected costs and benefits. Occupancy rates indeed increase for listings with no associated decrease in price, thereby increasing the total revenue for the listing. However, as would be expected from renting to unknown (potentially ill-matched) guests, forgoing screening also results in a decline in review ratings due to lower match quality. Importantly, relative to White hosts, while Black hosts benefit in terms of greater percentage increases in occupancy rates, they also suffer lower drops in review ratings. We show that this can be attributed mainly to Black switchers being of higher relative quality (vis-à-vis an average Black host) than White switchers (vis-à-vis an average White host). Similarly, as a whole, female hosts that forgo screening have better-quality listings than male hosts. As a result, male hosts who forgo screening decrease their listing prices by an average of \$4.28, leading to smaller increases in monthly revenue by \$56.62 (on average), as compared to female switchers. In essence, the benefits of forgoing screening accrue disproportionally more for Black and female hosts. Given that guests are most likely aware of hosts’ ethnicity/gender based on their profile photos, this suggests that guests seem to tradeoff their taste-based bias for the added convenience of instant booking.

## Limitations and Future Research

We acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, similar to prior research on Airbnb (e.g., Edelman and Luca 2014), our sample consists of all Airbnb listings within a specific time frame (i.e., from August 2015 to February 2017) in New York City, the largest Airbnb market in the U.S. These findings may not be generalizable to other locations, particularly in international cities where there may be significant differences in socioeconomic characteristics. Thus, additional studies across locations would help verify the consistency of the findings or identify contingency factors affecting the documented relationships. Similar concerns apply when generalizing findings to other online P2P platforms. In our context, even though the majority of providers did not switch back and forth, exercising this option was relatively easy. For platforms where participants face high costs of switching or lack the autonomy to switch, the cost-benefit calculus may be more complex and therefore needs further investigation.

Second, while we focus on heterogeneity in listings and host characteristics in forgoing screening, our study abstracts away from demand heterogeneity. Data constraints preclude the examination of whether different screening choices represent shifting compositions in guests over time—for example, whether the instant booking feature attracts guests that find nonscreening desirable. Particularly worthy of attention is the link between instant booking choices and guests’ use of Airbnb in place of traditional hotels where guests are not screened when making a reservation. Future studies could also incorporate data on Airbnb guests to examine how heterogeneity in their characteristics maps to provider screening choices. This would enable future research to discern the impact of one side forgoing screening from the other side’s perspective. Given that policy changes focusing on one side of the platform may induce strategic behavior on the other side (e.g., Klein et al. 2009; Mayya and Viswanathan 2019; Ye et al. 2014), studies could also examine how guests and other providers may opportunistically respond to the focal provider’s screening choices.

Third, a key reason Airbnb introduced the forgoing screening mechanism was to combat observed accounts of racial discrimination by hosts against guests.<sup>19</sup> Our study finds that instant booking offers benefits to Black and women hosts as well because guests trade taste-based bias for convenience. While lack of data on individual interactions between hosts and guests precludes us from examining the exact mechanisms underlying these choices and outcomes, future work could engage in a deeper investigation of such mechanisms.

Fourth, while we have examined what listings are more likely to forgo screening and eliminated several alternative explanations, more studies leveraging causal designs could help to clearly identify the mechanisms that drive decisions to forgo screening and their outcomes. In addition to hosts’ choices being driven by underlying heterogeneity in individual demand as demonstrated above, such mechanisms may include differences in risk aversion between Black and White hosts (e.g., Gutter and Hatcher 2008; Halek and Eisenhauer 2001). Similarly, future work could investigate whether differences in outcomes associated with forgoing screening by hosts of different races/ethnicities may be driven by statistical or taste-based discrimination. For example, it may be worthwhile to examine whether the differential impact on ratings is driven by differences in listing quality or differences in expectation of quality by guests (Doleac and Stein 2013). These studies are also critical for a deeper understanding of the social consequences of platform policy changes. For example, in cities with a large supply of listings, Airbnb automatically prioritizes instantly bookable listings in the search results. Such a policy may reduce the welfare of White hosts, who may gain less from forgoing screening. A careful identification of tradeoffs is needed, as they relate not only to economic but also to social consequences in the form of discrimination based on race/ethnicity or gender.

Finally, while we have used different matching methods in the DiD estimation to account for the endogeneity issue of a listing’s forgoing screening status in a quasiexperimental setting, it is plausible that the self-selected status of a listing is influenced by other unobservable factors. Future research could leverage exogenous shocks to the Airbnb platform to further examine a listing’s decision to forgo screening and its impacts in a natural experiment setting.

## Contributions to Theory and Practice

Burgeoning literature on online sharing marketplaces has increasingly attended to their social and economic impacts (e.g., Park et al. 2021; Zervas et al. 2014) and examined how transactions are consummated in such markets (e.g., Ye et al. 2018). However, less is known about how market mechanisms should be designed to support collaborative consumption activities in such markets. Online sharing platforms are not fully regulated and rely on a variety of self-designed mechanisms to facilitate and govern market transactions (Koopman et al. 2014). Accordingly, they represent an opportunity to critically assess the impacts of market mechanisms and identify those that best serve P2P sharing markets (Luca 2017). Our study addresses this research gap and, in doing so, contributes to the literature on market mechanism design and its socioeconomic implications.

So far, few studies have focused on examining screening mechanism design in online markets. In part, this may be because screening mechanisms have traditionally been considered a vital part of P2P online markets for information asymmetry mitigation (e.g., Stiglitz 1975). Indeed, even in our study’s research context, two-way screening was part of the default market design. Despite acknowledging the role of screening in discrimination by providers and reducing successful matches between agents (e.g., Cornell and Welch 1996; Fradkin 2015; Pallais 2014; Romanyuk 2017), scholars have largely assumed that screening is beneficial from the focal agent’s perspective. Our study questions this assumption and also relaxes the implicit assumption that providers within local areas have homogeneous demand characteristics. Accordingly, our empirical examination of who forgoes screening and when they do so may result in better outcomes reveals that individual agents assess their own relative benefits and costs across implementing and forgoing screening. We thereby provide evidence that providing choice to agents allows them to perform their individual cost-benefit calculus when determining whether forgoing screening is optimal for them, given their local demand conditions and their own characteristics.

This study also contributes to the literature on online markets by highlighting the roles of race/ethnicity and gender in platform policy adoption. Prior studies have shown the presence of racial discrimination against Black hosts on Airbnb (e.g., Edelman et al. 2017; Kakar et al. 2017). Our results suggest that the increased convenience provided through their voluntary forgoing of screening offsets tastebased bias in guests: Black hosts are more likely to forgo screening and can thus benefit through higher occupancy rates at lower costs (i.e., decreased ratings), relative to White hosts. Similarly, women are more likely to forgo screening than men, and their greater benefits relative to men largely relate to their ability to maintain price, rather than to increased occupancy rates. In essence, our study documents higher economic benefits to Black and female hosts when they choose to forgo screening. As such, our findings not only complement existing literature on how online market participants respond to mechanism design (e.g., Bapna et al. 2004; Ye et al. 2014) but also add to the growing literature on the roles of race/ethnicity and gender in online markets (e.g., Chan and Wang 2018; Edelman et al. 2017).

Practically, our findings provide important implications for online market platform design. Within our focal context, Airbnb now has the highest market share in temporary rentals, eroding the dominance of traditional hotel chains (e.g., Zervas et al. 2014). While initially operating under the assumption that two-way screening was critical for information asymmetry reduction, Airbnb’s market design precluded the “instant booking” norm in hotel chains. In so doing, it failed to cater to the convenience and time preferences of guests and also increased concerns regarding discrimination against guests by hosts. Our systematic investigation of Airbnb’s recent policy change to enable voluntary forgoing of screening provides evidence that guests react positively to instant booking and that this may even offset taste-based biases. We also show that hosts themselves benefit from having more choices, particularly when they are new entrants, have yet to build their reputation and quality signals, or face lower or declining demand. The option to forgo screening provides them with more degrees of freedom in customizing their choices: they can assess their own trade-offs between increased occupancy rates/prices and reduced review ratings, thereby increasing overall benefits. Of course, critical to such outcomes are other features of market design; it may well be that signaling and insurance mechanisms to offset information asymmetry in Airbnb permit screening to be an option. Moving beyond Airbnb, other online platforms may also benefit from our study in assessing their own overall design and providing flexibility and customization to different sides of the platform. Such mechanism designs not only increase economic efficiency but also social outcomes (in our case, related to race/ethnicity and gender).

In summary, our study sheds light on critical choices made in market design, and their subsequent impacts on market participants’ choices and outcomes. We show that a “one size fits all” policy under the assumption that market participants equally desire the same protection against information asymmetry may be misguided. Instead, providing options that enable the individual assessment of benefits and costs associated with screening allows for more decentralized and heterogeneous choices, with associated increases in market outcomes.
