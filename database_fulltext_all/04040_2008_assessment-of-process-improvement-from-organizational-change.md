## A B S T R A C T

In order to enhance their performance, many organizations have initiated change projects. However, management is reluctant to initiate them due to their enterprise-wide impact and costs that are higher than those of traditional system development projects. Thus, there is a need to assess the value of the redesigned process of a successfully implemented organizational change projects. The purpose of this study was therefore to assess process improvement from organizational change in the areas of resource utilization and allocation and cycle time and cost reduction. The candidate process and design alternatives were identified from organizational requirements analysis. The variables and their relations were defined to perform task activity analysis, bottleneck analysis, cycle cost analysis, and resource utilization analysis. A case study of a manufacturing company indicated that the assessment method was a promising approach for identifying alternative processes that leads to better organizational performance.

## 1. Introduction

Companies redesign business processes to achieve improvements in their performance (such as better service and quality). Practitioners and researchers have suggested organizational change using information technology (IT) to alter organizational structures and boundaries [16,21] and help redefine industry structure and competition [4]. Organizational change or related concepts such as business process redesign or reengineering (BPR) and organizational transformation have been the most important issue faced by IS executives since the early 1990s [27]; in fact, in 1994 nearly two-thirds of IS executives indicated that their firms were undertaking organizational change projects [12]. Many firms have redesigned existing business processes to achieve significant performance improvement.

Successful organizational change, which is not always radical, requires effective formulation of process alternatives, their evaluation, and implementation of the selected process. Many companies, however, are quick to attempt to ‘‘radically design’’ business process without a comprehensive analysis of its impact [15]. This often leads to irrevocable process change that yields little improvement in organizational performance. Hence, it is necessary to assess the value of the change by examining the expected performance of the alternatives. The most common reasons that people resist change include a belief that it does not make sense for the organization or there is a misunderstanding of its implications.

Hence, the issue of concern is to understand the multifaceted nature of the improvement before its implementation. In an effort to categorize and interpret the potential organizational outcomes of organizational change, this study suggested an assessment model of process improvement that views the organizational outcomes of change initiatives. The required variables and their functional relations were defined to conduct task activity, bottleneck, cycle cost, and resource utilization analyses of the alternatives. These were defined to gain a detailed understanding of the old and future process and to provide an objective basis for the redesign decisionmaking before firms undertake reengineering. The descriptions of outputs from these analyses are given in Table 1.

Human labor and intermediaries from business processes are eliminated and monitoring business process status and analysis of information and decision-making are improved through its use. Traditional hierarchical or matrix-based organizations are increasingly replaced with team-based organizations, where IT is used to support management coordination of work and organization by division of knowledge.

## 3. Assessment of process improvement

Identification of the right measures for a process is not straightforward. Process modeling or management methods may be adapted from total quality, industrial engineering, and IS practices and are suited to unstructured decision-making inherent in the assessment of organizational change [11]. The notion of going beyond typical financial indicators to collecting process information and metrics is critical in process measurement [5]. There are various process modeling methods that test the performance and feasibility of the process alternatives: systems analysis and design technique (SADT) [18], Jackson systems development (JSD) [13], dynamic systems, analysis, design, and evaluation (DSADE) [7], a cognitive approach to manage complex business activities [25], work flow management systems for data flow analysis [20], a composite stage–activity (S–A) framework for business process change (BPC) methodologies which has been recently applied to public sector [19], and data flow diagrams through fisheye views [22]. These are used for process modeling or simulation. Critical is the notion of process measurement and metrics. Information about process characteristics, outputs, and performance is obviously critical for process management, which provides the direction of potential process improvement and supports problem diagnosis with processes. The process performance information, however, is not fully provided by these models, making the definition of variables and their relationships elusive and difficult. Firms have given less attention to the virtues of measurement and analysis and ongoing process management than to the creative side of process change [6]. Measure selection and development should be carefully conducted to identify the specific output requirements and a detailed model to produce them, allowing a reliable analysis of predicted process performance.

Business process improvement is often measured in terms of lead time, service time, wait time, and resource utilization [17]. The purpose of process improvement assessment in organizational change projects is to select the alternative process that has the greatest organizational impact. That process will be used by the design team to decide whether to go through another redesign and simulate the process or to finalize the design and move on to its implementation. In the absence of a rich, consistent measurement approach, it should not be surprising that disparate and incomplete views have been made regarding the organizational impacts of change.

## 4. Assessment model of process improvement

## 4.1. Description of four analyses

For the evaluation of organizational impact, a multifaceted approach using four factors (cycle time, process bottleneck, cycle cost, and resource utilization) was chosen.

Cycle time is the total time needed to complete a business process; it is one measure of process efficiency. Completing each task activity involves delay and processing time. The reduction of cycle time for those processes critical to the firm, such as time-tomarket and time-from-order-to-delivery, is the major thrust of reengineering. At Hallmark Corp., for example, the development of a new greeting card took 2 years due to a long list of serial procedures in sketches, approvals, and cost estimates. After process reengineering efforts, the cycle time was reduced by half. After a series of reforms to improve collaboration between functional departments, Modicon Inc. brings products to market in one-third of the time it would formerly have taken.

Process bottleneck represents the extent to which each task in a process is likely to limit its output. While jobs wait for resources, they are bottlenecked. Design effort, no matter how ingenious, can hardly improve system throughput if this lowers system performance [24,26]. In a manufacturing environment, a bottlenecked process increases work-in-process inventory costs. A bottleneck occurs whenever the excess capacity of a task is less than that of the preceding task. Excess capacity is found by dividing the difference between the capacity and the load by capacity. Capacity is calculated by multiplying the number of employees in the position at which a task is performed by the time required to perform the task. A process bottleneck becomes apparent when excess capacity for each task is present. Fig. 1 illustrates an example of bottleneck analysis. A bottleneck occurs in Task B in the process stream; one also occurs in Task D, but to a lesser extent. The organizational change project team can eliminate bottlenecks by adding additional employees to the positions responsible for these tasks.

Cycle cost is the total spent for completing tasks in a process. It is the sum of overhead and labor costs. If they are assigned to specific task activities, the process in which they are elements incurs the aggregate costs. Those tasks which entail large labor costs and were not truly value added are targets for improvement. Cycle cost is closely related to cycle time; for example, IBM Credit Corporation reduced turnaround time for quotations to customers attempting to cut costs by cutting time per quotation. CIGNA, a leading provider of insurance and related financial services, saved more than \$100 million after it began business reengineering, which represents \$2–3 in returned benefits for each \$1 invested in reengineering, and it reduced operating expenses by 42% [2].

Resource utilization indicates the extent to which resources are over- or under-utilized, given process flow and processing requirements. It indicates how much work is performed relative to capacity; that is, the total available resources. Resource contention occurs when the total amount of time spent is greater than the amount of time normally available. For instance, if an assistant manager normally has 160 h available for work and is responsible for performing work that requires more than 160 h in a month, resource contentions occur. Any increase in workload for this resource can produce delays or extended cycle times, and should be avoided. It may also be possible to transfer some work from the overloaded positions to balance the work of employees. The capacity of under-utilized processes, however, may be reduced until they are highly utilized. This reduction should not be excessive, as processes with over-reduced capacity can become bottlenecks.

The performance of a business process must be improved to maximize customer satisfaction. The improvement of the four factors will substantially increase customer satisfaction.

## 4.2. Description of variables

For our research, task was defined as a set of operations commonly carried out by a single employee type; employee type represented a class of employees who performed the same tasks, possibly from different departments. In this paper, tasks, departments, and types of employees are represented by the indices, i, j, and k respectively.

Once the objectives of each analysis are defined, specific variables should be given as the input or output of each analysis. Some variables were estimated by interviewing staff members or observing the process; others were produced from estimated variables using defined relationships among the variables. That is, the estimated values of P(i, j, k), PH(i, k), DH(i, k), IA(i), L(k), O(k), and M(k) were used to perform the four analyses. The results were used to perform the three other analyses. ## 5. Case description

The assessment model of process improvement was applied to Paentec Co. This company manufactures pagers and cellular phones, and was founded in 1991. Radical shifts in the telecommunication industry have fundamentally changed this industry in recent years. Product life cycles have been dramatically shortened, accelerating the demand for accuracy and timeliness in manufacturing and purchasing plans. The entrepreneurial nature of corporate culture and the high level of competition in the telecommunication industry have forced the company’s manufacturing function dramatically or incrementally to improve its production processes. An organizational change initiative was started in the interdepartmental processes that were unsuccessful in producing coordinated and organized manufacturing processes. Capturing the structure and dynamics of the business as it operated in the real world demanded standardized systems analysis practices including interviewing users, observing business processes, and reviewing forms and files. All these standard methods were used to analyze the business processes of Paentec Co. The patterns of activities that were conducted in the performance of business process were examined first. The resources (e.g., staff members and managers) needed to carry out each processing task were then assessed by asking such questions as: How many staff hours are needed to process Task A? How many staff hours are actually used to process Task A?

The results of interviews with top management indicated that the company had four short-term goals; cost reduction, quality improvement, service improvement, and process time reduction.

The initial objective of an organizational change analysis was to obtain an understanding of the situation with sufficient accuracy to determine the best target areas for redesign consideration. To accomplish the goals and objectives presented in Table 3, engineering change order (ECO) and material purchasing process were selected as the core target processes to be improved. The organizational change in this process was ultimately intended to accomplish four short-term goals. The tasks in this process included a change in engineering design and production process after errors had been identified or market demands changed, as well as a change in material purchasing. This required coordination and cooperation among R&D, administrative, production, production planning, inventory, and accounting departments. The need for organizational change was developed based on senior management’s vision and the knowledge of what other organizations had been able to accomplish as a result of refining their processes.

Interviews showed that several problems had arisen in the old ECO and material purchasing process, as outlined in Table 4. The manual handling of order documents and the reporting procedures of engineering design problems increased inefficiency in the old ECO and material purchasing process. An organizational change in the process was expected to: overcome inefficiency in work and reduce the rate of production errors, provide rapid and satisfactory service to customers, and compress lead times for product development, these were ultimately to accomplish the objectives in Table 3.

## 6. Design of process alternatives

One or more candidate alternatives may be developed with the initial decision to undertake an organizational change project. Further alternatives can later be generated, and original ones refined, based on the results of a detailed analysis of existing business processes. An understanding of existing business process provides a baseline, a point from which future process improve ments can be explored and measured. Any operating improvements that management wishes to recommend can be described using this baseline; tasks that have no real value to the customer should be removed. Further, the new process should reduce process delays and synchronize activity flow to minimize the number of tasks where bottleneck or resource contention occurs.

Our study proposed two process alternatives: process innovation using IT alone or structuring of process performance by teams, which included use of IT. The effectiveness of a team-based structuring process could be examined by comparing these two alternatives. The case manager or team combined multiple functions into one unit: there could be a way to cut the cycle times of the process by employing a case manager that introduced a greater degree of concurrency among the activities in the process. The team consisted of representatives from all the functions involved in the process being redesigned (i.e., the ECO process), and they were entirely responsible for the performance of the process according to the goals that the process was intended to accomplish. A broad set of skills and perspectives increased the likelihood that the output would satisfy the cross-functional requirements.

The effect of IT on the success of organizational change, however, became greater when IT was used to facilitate teamwork. For instance, groupware software supported various activities including group communication, brainstorming, and scheduling of meetings and facilities. These groupware functions helped reduce the frequency of face-to-face meetings as well as meeting time, and enabled faster flow of informal decision-making. IT in the form of communication networks and shared databases, played the role of BPRs ‘‘enabler’’ and was an important factor preceding the successful implementation of BPR [1,23]. Firms use IT to reduce organizational complexity by eliminating redundant steps in transactions, delays, and administrative intermediaries. IT is usually a necessary but not insufficient factor in successful organizational change. The conscious alignment of business strategy with IT must be practiced to leverage its immense potential as a means of organizational transformation [9]. Organizational change is a multifaceted process change encompassing organizational structure and human resource management. Although IT is a powerful enabler of BPR, it involves much more than implementing IT to streamline complicated procedures. It should be accompanied by critical changes in process strategy to have maximum potential for significant improvement in performance. This alternative was the same as the first, except that it was not structured using a team or case manager. Material replenishment and order plans were made flexibly through the MRP system. ## 7. Analysis results

The activities for process modeling of organizational change establish a database of procedural structures, processing and delay time of task activities, resources allowable for each task, etc. The ECO and material purchasing process were made up of nine task activities. The first five pertained to the ECO process, while the remainder dealt with material purchasing processes.

Task 1: Release of request for solution for engineering problems (R&D department).

Task 2: Delivery of problem report (administrative department).

Task 3: Creating a draft of ECO (production department).

Task 4: Test of ECO (R&D department).

Task 5: Finalization of ECO (production department).

Task 6: Replenishment and purchasing of planning material (or parts) (production planning department).

Task 7: Creation of purchase orders (production planning department).

Task 8: Receipt of ordered materials (inventory department).

Task 9: Payment for materials (accounting department).

Three types of employees existed for the old process; staff members, assistant managers, and managers. Six departments were involved in the process. Fig. 2 depicts the old process and the portion that was to be changed with using either Alternative 1 or 2 processes. The Alternative 1 process changed Tasks 3–5, which involved making, testing, and finalizing ECO (in Area A), as the team members from three departments performed these tasks. This reduced the movement time of necessary documents through R&D, production, production planning, and purchasing departments. Task 2 was eliminated in Alternative 1. The functional background, skills, and experiences of team members requisite to the ECO process were expected to contribute much to organizational performance.

## 7.1. Task activity analysis

Table 9 represents the task activity analysis results using the values of the percentage of the number of work hours in Table 6. The total percentage of the number of work hours spent by employees to perform each task (LA(i)) and the number of work hours spent by employees to perform each task (HA(i, k)), the processing time (PA(i)), the delay time (DA(i)), and the cycle time (CYA(i)) for each task were provided. In the old process, the total percentage of work hours spent by employees to perform Task 1 (i.e., 0.2) was the sum of the percentages of the number of work hours (5 + 10 + 5) multiplied by 0.01. The amount of processing and delay time required to perform Task 1 (i.e., 2 and 40) in the old process was given by the sum of the processing and delay time for the employees of all types (0.5 + 1 + 0.5 and 15 + 20 + 10). The number of work hours spent by staff members to perform Task 1 (i.e., 10) was given by multiplying the total number of work hours in a month (i.e., 200) by the percentage of the number of work hours (i.e., 5%) in the old process.

Cycle time was given by the sum of process and delay times. Delay time (and cycle time) was generally much longer than processing time, indicating that one of the important criteria of successful organizational change lay in the extent to which delay time was reduced. The cycle time of the Alternative 1 process (i.e., 358.5 h) was significantly less than that of the old process (i.e., 678.5 h) and the Alternative 2 process (i.e., 636 h). This showed that the Alternative 1 process outperformed the others in terms of cycle time reduction.

## 7.2. Bottleneck analysis

Bottleneck analysis results are shown in Table 10. Bottlenecks occurred in Tasks 3, 6, and 8 in all processes. The Alternative 1 and 2 processes showed another bottleneck in Task 7. Those in which bottlenecks existed required more work hours from employees to reduce the extent to which the bottlenecks occurred. The process with the minimal number of bottlenecks (i.e., the old process) was the most desirable, but the small difference in this number among the three processes makes it difficult to select one according to this criterion.

The extent of bottleneck occurrence in each process is the sum of B(i) for all tasks. This sum for the old process is 72.5 h, which is much smaller than that of the Alternative 1 (i.e., 164.14) and Alternative 2 processes (i.e., 150). This indicated that, in terms of bottleneck analysis results, the Alternative 1 process was superior to the others. The excess capacity of the tasks in the Alternative 1 process was more level than in the other processes, minimizing the chance for the tasks to limit the overall input of the process.

The excess capacity for Task 3 had a negative value in all processes, probably because overnight excess labor hours were not considered; this made the number of total work hours (the available resources) smaller than the minimum number of total work hours required for the task to be processed.

This smaller excess capacity meant more efficient use of resources in performing tasks. The Alternative 1 process had a smaller total excess capacity (i.e., 459 h) than the old (i.e., 486 h) and Alternative 2 processes (i.e., 507.5 h). Thus, resources were most efficiently used in the Alternative 1.

## 7.3. Cycle cost analysis results

The results of the cycle cost analysis are presented in Table 11. Cycle cost is the sum of labor and overhead costs. Labor cost was calculated by multiplying the number of work hours by labor cost per hour. Overhead cost can be calculated in a similar manner.As shown in Table 11, the cycle cost for the Alternative 1 process is 2320 dollars, which is lower than that of both the old process (2930 dollars) and the Alternative 2 process (2410 dollars). Hence, Alternative 1 is superior to the other process for this criterion.

## 7.4. Resource utilization analysis results

Table 12 presents the results of the resource utilization analysis. Resource contention is greatest for assistant managers, as they had the highest total value of resource utilization. Resource contention occurred when RA(i, k) was greater than HA(i, k) (i.e., RC(i, k) > 0); this was the case, for instance, for assistant managers that performed Task 3 of the old process. Here, the resource utilization of assistance managers was 30 multiplying by 3 the amount of processing time required for assistant managers to perform Task 3. The result was larger than the number of work hours spent by assistant managers to perform Task 3 in a month; the difference between these values (i.e., 20) is the extent to which resource contention occurred for assistant managers who perform Task 3. Resource contention should be reduced, as it indicated that resources were not evenly utilized across tasks; this was probably the reason for delays or extended cycle times.

The number of tasks in which resource contention occurred was three for the Alternative 1 process, which was slightly larger than that for the old and Alternative 2 processes. However, the Alternative 1 process had the smallest total extent of resource contention (13.5 h). This generally also lent support for the superiority of the Alternative 1 process in terms of resource utilization analysis.

## 8. Conclusions and implications

The measurement and evaluation of the overall performance of a process before business redesign could contribute to better overall process understanding, lower implementation costs, less organizational resistance, and faster implementation with fewer complications during organizational change. Our study suggested an executable model for the assessment of process improvement from change. The four analyses – Task Activity, Bottleneck, Cycle Cost, and Resource Utilization – were performed by structuring spreadsheets that accommodated the required data sequenced according to the assessment framework. Each analysis emphasized its own set of considerations and importance, and this provided a broader view of process improvement as well as an enriched understanding of why, where, and how to apply organizational change.

The results provide companies interested in redesigning their business processes with a mechanism for producing information related to future performance. The reluctance that makes firms unwilling to make investments in organizational change often stems from a lack of a consistent and effective ways to document and track the nature and extent of its impact. Our analysis can partially overcome such challenges by numerically assessing the relative utility of each alternative in process improvement.
