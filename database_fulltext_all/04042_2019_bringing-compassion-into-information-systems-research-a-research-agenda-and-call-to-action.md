# Bringing compassion into information systems research: A research agenda and call to action

Roopa Raman<sup>1</sup> and Laura McClelland<sup>2</sup>

Journal of Information Technolog 2019, Vol. 34(1) 2–21 © Association for Information Technology Trust 2019 Article reuse guidelines: sagepub.com/journals-permissions https://doi.org/10.1177/026839621881598DOI: 10.1177/0268396218815989 Journals.sagepub.com/jinf SAGE

## Abstract

In this article, we assert that compassion-driven approaches are the sustainable way for information and communication technologies to contribute to economic value. We urge future information systems research to emphasize, with equal vigor, the joint goals of compassion and financial gains from information and communication technologies. We present a broad agenda for future information systems research based on this premise. We also discuss how certain core assumptions underlying traditional information systems research—so far, driven primarily by economic value as outcome—would need to change in order to support this new agenda emphasizing compassion and economic value as complementary and synergistic outcomes. We provide a brief concrete illustration of this proposed agenda, and its underlying revised assumptions, by drawing on the example of a prominent field of study in information systems research, namely health information systems research.

## Keywords

Compassion, compassion-driven information systems, information systems research agenda, health information systems, information technology, “blue ocean” research, health care

## Introduction

The one thing [that all companies should be doing] is promoting the awareness that compassion can and will be good for success and profits . . . If I can turn the most powerful part of the world [corporate world] into a land of wisdom and compassion, it’s going to change the rest of the world. (Chade-Meng Tan, Google)

In traditional organizational settings, decisions driven by compassion—such as caring, connecting with others, lifting others out of pain, and helping them achieve their purpose (Dutton, Workman, & Hardin, 2014)—are often viewed as being at odds with those driven by financial/profit incentives (Dutton et al., 2014; Lilius et al., 2008; Tsui, 2013). This is not surprising since traditional notions of financial gains versus compassion draw on contrasting resources. Financial value—aka profit maximization—is built on notions of performance efficiency and standardization (Cool & Dierickx, 1993; Porter, 1979). The expression of compassion, however, requires customization of response, investment of financial and other resources into nurturing people, and emphasis on emotional intelligence (Dutton et al., 2014;

Goleman, Boyatzis, & McKee, 2002; Lilius, Worline, Dutton, Kanov, & Maitlis, 2011). As such, when building the case for pursuing compassion-driven outcomes, people tend to emphasize the “money is not everything” argument, appealing to people’s sense of philanthropy rather than to their business sense (Frost, 1999; Gallagher, 2009).

In this article, we present the following claim: Emphasizing compassion-based values, such as caring and human connection, is necessary for the sustainable creation of financial value in contemporary and future business enterprises. For information systems (IS) research, this implies that—contrary to its historical focus on efficiency and standardization (e.g. Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996;

Melville, Kraemer, & Gurbaxani, 2004; Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003; Wade & Hulland, 2004)—the future direction of our field needs to focus on compassion-driven approaches through which information and communication technologies (ICTs) would impact individual and firm performance, profitability, and strategy (Dutton, Frost, Worline, Lilius, & Kanov, 2002; Dutton, Worline, Frost, & Lilius, 2006; Worline, Wrzesniewski, & Rafaeli, 2002). In this new worldview, traditional concepts of efficiency and standardization will still have value, but in a more nuanced manner.

Industry has already caught on to this idea. We live in an era when, now more than ever, people are driven by a need to feel needed, connected with others, and empowered to help others out of pain and toward their life’s purpose (Bacq, Hartog, & Hoogendoorn, 2016; T. L. Miller, Grimes, McMullen, & Vogus, 2012; Ruskin, Seymour, & Webster, 2016). Many successful companies today—such as, Kickstarter, Change.org, and GoFundMe, among others— tap into this basic human need as their core business model (Andre, Bureau, Gautier, & Rubel, 2017; Belleflamme, Lambert, & Schwienbacher, 2014; Gleasure & Feller, 2016; Mollick, 2014; Park, Chung, Hall-Phillips, & Anaza, 2016; Snyder, Mathers, & Crooks, 2016). All of these companies use IS as the necessary engine to drive and implement human connection and caring. Companies that are at the forefront of industry today have found creative ways of using ICTs to enable decision-making that is driven, in this manner, by compassion rather than by just trying to make money, even though the latter is implicit and consequential to the former.

Academic IS research, on the whole, currently lags behind industry in its acceptance of compassion-based— rather than efficiency- and standardization-based— approaches as a means to IS-enabled performance and profitability. Recent calls for more inclusive IS research agendas, driven by social value creation, provide a great start in this direction; however, here too, social value creation is viewed as an alternative to financial value creation rather than as a direct path toward financial value creation (Majchrzak, Markus, & Wareham, 2016; Walsham, 2012). We would like to join this emerging call for a “betterment” orientation in future IS research, but present our own— arguably, more radical—vision for what such a future of the field may look like. We submit the following vision for the future of IS research: The direction for future IS research needs to be about IS that emphasize compassion-centered approaches to financial gains by focusing on building human connectedness and care through ICTs, rather than focusing on improving efficiency and standardization through ICTs at the cost of compassion. Ignoring compassion as a core business strategic goal—as IS practice and research has tended to do in the past—may yield short-term financial gains. However, we expect such gains to inevitably erode as these firms become misfits in modern-day markets where, increasingly, decision-making is driven by a moral obligation than simply by short-term material gains in efficiency and standardization (Minor & Morgan, 2011; Moizer, 2018).

In this article, we present how certain traditional assumptions underlying current IS research would need to change to adopt this future vision. We also lay out a new future IS research agenda that embraces the notion that focus on profit with compassion will pay off better in the long run than profit without compassion. This future IS research agenda would, therefore, emphasize both compassion goals and profit-maximizing goals for ICTs. Finally, we illustrate the viability of our future vision for IS research by applying it to a popular existing domain of IS research, namely health IS research.

We begin with a brief background that situates the present work within the broader context of what is already known.

## Background

Merriam-Webster dictionary defines compassion as the “sympathetic consciousness of others’ distress together with a desire to alleviate it.” Popular quotes on compassion assembled on the Internet (goodreads.com) emphasize love, lack of judgment, a sense of belonging or feeling connected, reaching out to or touching others, lifting others up, helping others reach their potential, kindness, sympathy, and empathy as compassion. In an everyman’s sense, therefore, compassion is defined rather broadly to represent an assortment of ideas that relate to caring and connecting with others in order to raise them up, either by reducing their suffering or by enabling their potential.

Compassion in the workplace is also a growing topic of research in Positive Organizational Scholarship (Dutton et al., 2014; Dutton et al., 2006; Frost, 1999). This literature takes a more focused and precise view of “compassion”; this view could be considered a subset of the broader view of compassion promulgated in popular literature. According to this view, compassion is an interpersonal process in response to suffering, involving the noticing, feeling, sense-making of suffering, and the taking of action to alleviate this suffering (Dutton et al., 2014). The ubiquity of suffering in the workplace has motivated researchers in this discipline to study compassion as a legitimate workplace behavior with tangible consequences to employee well-being and organizational performance (Baker & McNulty, 2011; Dewar, Pullin, & Tocheris, 2011; Dutton et al., 2006; Gallagher, 2009; Lilius et al., 2011; Lloyd & Carson, 2011; van Kleef et al., 2008). This literature also discusses self-compassion as occurring when people acknowledge their own shortcomings and deal with them objectively, with a view toward learning, rather than blaming (Leary, Tate, Allen, Adams, & Hancock, 2007; Shepherd & Cardon, 2009). Self-compassionate people are likely to keep their calm and equanimity in the face of challenges. At the organizational level, individual acts of compassion can be organized into coordinated expressions of compassion using existing organizational structures and practices and drawing on agency behaviors of individuals involved (Dutton et al., 2006; Lilius et al., 2011). This is known as “compassion organizing.”

This Positive Organizational Scholarship literature on workplace compassion recognizes both financial value creation and well-being-oriented human value creation as legitimate outcomes from workplace compassion (Coombs, 1999; Dutton et al., 2014; Lilius et al., 2008; McClelland, Gabriel, & DePuccio, 2018; McClelland & Vogus, 2014; Oveis, Horberg, & Keltner, 2010; Peus, 2011). In doing so, this literature supports the viability of our earlier claim in this article about the importance of compassion-based approaches to ICT-enabled financial value creation in organizations. For example, according to this literature, compassion can alleviate suffering (J. L. Goetz, Keltner, & Simon-Thomas, 2010; Margolis & Molinsky, 2008; Peus, 2011), improve perceived self-worth and the worth of others (Dutton et al., 2014), and create a higher prosocial identity, greater self-esteem, and overall positive attitudes (Coombs, 1999; Lilius et al., 2008; Oveis et al., 2010). Simultaneously, the improvement in employee morale enabled by workplace compassion (Lilius et al., 2008; McClelland et al., 2018; Waldman, Carmeli, & Halevi, 2011) has been found, in empirical studies, to translate to greater employee productivity and positive economic outcomes for the firm (Barsade & Gibson, 2007; Dutton et al., 2006; Greenberg, 1990; Lind, Greenberg, Scott, & Welchans, 2000; Martin et al., 2015; Worline & Dutton, 2017).

IS research has not contributed specifically to the association between IS and workplace compassion. However, more recently, IS research has started to recognize that, beyond financial gains, IS can generate value by improving wellness, “betterment,” and social value (Ganju, Pavlou, & Banker, 2016; Majchrzak et al., 2016; Walsham, 2012). The current article draws upon this emergent direction in IS research. For example, in 2012, Goeff Walsham called to refocus IS academic scholarship on the central question of how ICTs were making the world a better place (Walsham, 2012). Walsham introduced a four-pronged agenda for his future vision for IS research, anchored on the need for a “flexible/adaptable identity” for the field, a penchant for collaboration across disciplines, inclusion of multiple methodologies, and driven by the core purpose of investigating how ICTs can create a better world. What it means to be a “better world” is, according to this article, open to the interpretation of the researcher; this makes Walsham’s vision for future IS research reconcilable with present-day IS research, if we consider that the latter may construe “better” as the achievement of economic goals. Walsham, of course, urged for a more challenging interpretation of “better,” centered on social, rather than simply financial, well-being; this is where this article aligns well with the spirit of Walsham’s call.

More recently, an entire special issue of MIS Quarterly (Vol. 40, No. 2) was dedicated to papers investigating varied ways in which ICTs can tackle major societal problems like infant mortality, human migration, unemployment, and so on (Majchrzak et al., 2016). The editors of this special issue explained, moreover, that certain overarching insights for the future of all IS research—not just research focused on societal challenges—emerged from these studies. For example, novel conceptualizations of theory were revealed, namely, theories of the problem and theories of the solution. The study of ICTs and societal challenges also highlighted more creative and fluid ways of envisioning the IT artifact, often in terms of affordances and constraints provided to the user, rather than the typical enterprise systems view of most traditional IS research (Faraj & Azad, 2012; Leonardi, 2013; Majchrzak & Markus, 2014). The study of ICTs in societal challenges broadens the types of organizations that are worthy of study in future IS research beyond the traditional emphasis on Western for-profit corporations. From the standpoint of our article, the articles in this special issue are significant in showing that well-being-oriented perspectives are extensible to the whole of IS research, and capable of broadening the field as a whole, in terms of the theories, research questions, and research sites that would deserve attention.

The present article is nestled at the intersection of the works mentioned earlier in this section. Inspired by real life examples of compassion as a key driving force underlying many contemporary business models (e.g. Kedves, 2016; Manzoor, 2017), in this article, we develop the case that, in order to remain relevant, future IS research needs to embrace this compassion-driven approach toward financial value maximization. Our call in this article integrates well with related calls made by other authors in emerging IS research (Majchrzak et al., 2016; Walsham, 2012). As such, where possible, we build on structures and frameworks provided by these authors to present our own ideas in this article. In doing so, we hope to contribute to a growing cohesive integrated “voice” in favor of investigating IS as promoting workplace well-being on more dimensions than financial well-being.

In the next section, we present how certain traditional assumptions underlying current IS research would need to change in order to accommodate our compassion-driven vision for the future of IS research.

## Traditional Assumption 1: Efficiency is an absolute virtue

Traditionally, IS research has emphasized how IS can increase efficiencies by reducing costs, streamlining processes, and reducing slack (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996; Chen, Su, & Hiele, 2017; Pang, Tafti, & Krishnan, 2014; Pinsonneault & Rivard, 1998). The focus on efficiency has also meant that employees would be rewarded primarily for contributions to the bottom-line. Training programs would typically be designed to provide employees with the skills to do their jobs, rather than to pursue their passions and aspirations.

Our vision is based on the notion that compassion is a necessary pre-requisite to profit, since profits without compassion would be unsustainable in the long run. This new vision calls for IS research to investigate how IS can enable compassion in the workplace, and in doing so, can sustainably spur firm profitability. Traditional notions of efficiency and its importance are incompatible with this vision. A compassionate workplace is one in which people care about each other’s suffering and aspirations, take time to listen for these, and then implement actions to alleviate suffering or facilitate aspirations (Dutton et al., 2014; Lilius, Kanov, Dutton, Worline, & Maitlis, 2012; Worline & Dutton, 2017). These things take time and effort, for good reason. Moreover, a compassion perspective that is pervasive across the entire workplace needs additional structural/institutional resources, such as the backing of senior management, and more generally, a certain degree of slack in individuals’ workload (Dutton et al., 2006; McClelland & Vogus, 2014). Traditional IS research, focused on wringing out inefficiencies in organizational workflows, is ill-aligned to such a vision.

Our emphasis on compassion in the workplace leads us to envision the future of work as being organized into what we call “compassion-supported workflows.” These would consist of the primary outcome-oriented workflows (e.g. “order fulfillment” or “hiring employees”) scaffolded by supporting compassion pathways. Compassion pathways would lay out the specific processes and decision trees through which people can systematically engage in active listening, monitoring, and on-demand intervening to uphold the care and connectedness of those involved in the primary workflows.

This compassion-supported view of the future of work necessitates future IS research to undertake a more nuanced approach toward efficiency. This approach needs to focus on using IS to selectively allow slack (i.e. decrease efficiencies) in the primary outcome-oriented workflows while enabling efficiencies in the supporting compassion pathways. In this approach, for example, information technologies would have a two-pronged objective. The first would be to automate tasks in the primary workflow as a means to “free up” human cognitive resources, which could then be engaged in providing compassionate support to others in the course of performing this work. The second would be to use IT to provide greater streamlining and structure for the compassion pathways so that people can have clear guidelines, and perhaps even receive technology prompts, on when to initiate these pathways and how to use them. This way, people could use the slack resources shaved off of the digitized primary workflows more efficiently toward compassion, without having to waste valuable time and energy in just figuring out who may need help and/or how they can provide this help. Note that such automation actually introduces inefficiency in “primary outcome-driven workflows,” at least from a financial standpoint, since the technology is not actually replacing people but enabling them to rise to a more compassioncentric call during their workday.

This nuanced assumption about the connection between ICTs and efficiency opens up several possibilities for future IS research. For example, how will this revised assumption force us to rethink employee hiring/firing policies and reward structures (Barclay & Barclay, 2011; Masli, Richardson, Watson, & Zmud, 2016)? How will the future of employee training need to be changed to include self-development/continuing education/pursuit of personal passions and aspirations as an integral aspect (Connellan, 1972; Raghavendra & Raju, 2016; Useem, 1993; Weisser, 2012)? IS research can also investigate the social, behavioral, and technical issues associated with the use of specific tools, such as MOOCs (Massive Open Online

Courses), in the systematic delivery of such aspiration-driven (rather than job requirement-driven) continuing education in the workplace (Mackness, Mak, & Williams, 2010). Companies would need to actually reward helpful caring behaviors in the workplace and ICTs may play a critical role in carefully identi fying, tracking, and documenting such behaviors. Finally, future IS research can also investigate the financial and productivity implications of this nuanced approach toward efficiency in the workplace. Despite adding to the organization’s cost, it is likely that using IS to design compassion-supported workflows would lead to a workforce that feels valued and is, therefore, more dedicated, energized, and loyal toward the firm. This can alleviate the widespread problems of employee burnout and turnover that all organizations face today, which would save them money and make them more productive. The external visibility of such actions is likely to set into motion a positive cycle of productivity and financial value by attracting a more qualified workforce and more loyal customers. We hope that entire sub-fields of future IS research could be dedicated to testing these expectations of financial value flowing from “compassion value.”

Key Takeaway: Efficiency should not be universally emphasized in all organizational processes. Processes through which work outcomes are accomplished, e.g. order fulfillment, can afford to incorporate some technology-enabled slack, in the form of freed human time and spare cognitive resources. These slack human resources should then be re-directed to pursue compassion-enabling processes, which should be very efficient, so that people are not fumbling to figure out toward whom or how to show compassion.

## Traditional Assumption 2: Standardization is an absolute virtue

In traditional organizations, driven by efficiency and productivity concerns, standardization—that is, compliance with one way of doing things—is held in high esteem (Han, Ada, Sharman, & Rao, 2015; Hofeditz, Nienaber, Dysvik, & Schewe, 2017; Kim & Kim, 2017). IS have typically been viewed as the great enabler of such standardization and enforcer of compliance (Puhakainen & Siponen, 2010; Smith, Winchester, Bunker, & Jamieson, 2010). Compassion, however, is customized and aims to stay that way. Compassion is the customized response to the suffering or aspirations of an individual, recognizing the uniqueness of the individual and of his or her circumstances (Dutton et al., 2014; Worline & Dutton, 2017).

We propose a nuanced view toward IT-enabled standardization in the workplace—a view that would support our vision of a new compassion-enabling role for ICTs. This view emphasizes standardization in the entry/capture of data into IS but customization in the business responses and solutions that these data would then drive. Digitized data entry needs to be standardized and detailed, with fewer opportunities for “free-hand” text entries, even if this comes at the expense of more time and perceived mundane data entry work. The resulting large volumes of electronic data that the organization would capture should then be mined, using business ana lytics capabilities, to create customized responses to customer needs and customized solutions to business problems. Although the ability to customize does not automatically imply compassion, we recognize that, in the absence of this ability, the expression of compassion would be weak, if not impossible. We, therefore, envision this ability to activate customized responses to individuals as a necessary pre-requisite—a starting point—for the expression of IS-enabled compassion in the workplace. For example, an organization could handle customer service on a case-by-case basis rather than apply blanket rules. As one instance of this, a compassioncentric organization’s customer relationship management (CRM) system could highlight a recently bereaved person as “qualifying” for a refund even without the receipt, and perhaps trigger the organization to send a “words of encouragement” style greeting card to the customer. Of course, in order to provide this level of customized compassionate service, customers and customer service agents would need to take the time to enter into the CRM system a high level of detail about the customer in a standardized format that can be readily mined. The resulting data ownership, privacy, control, and security issues, implied by customized compassionate responding, would need to be active areas of study in future IS research. ICTs have the potential to provide large-scale and systematic customized compassionate responding in a way that perhaps human cognitive limitations would preclude. We would like to see more of these capabilities of ICTs, and their resulting managerial, behavioral, and ethical implications, specified and delineated in future IS research.

Speaking broadly, IS that emphasize compassion would necessarily need to seek out the things that make individuals and situations unique, rather than trying to collapse these individuals and situations into categories, as has been done in the past. In the past, there were understandable difficulties in providing unique customized responses to individuals, due to limitations in technology and data availability. However, with the present-day explosion of data available through social media and other technologies, along with sophisticated business analytics capabilities, we are poised better than ever to provide mass customized responses that can treat each person as the unique individual that they are (Andriole, 2006; Gandhi, Magar, & Roberts, 2014; Tien, 2013). These technologies of today, are therefore, wellequipped to pursue compassion goals in the workplace. For example, instead of using business analytics to identify more ways of categorizing people and generalizing insights from specific cases, perhaps the focus of business intelligence could be redirected toward de-categorizing people and events, and identifying more of what makes them unique. This fine-grained insight could then be used to provide customized responses aimed at reducing individual suffering and enabling individual aspirations in the workplace.

The design, implementation, and use of such IS, the customized responses that can be generated through them, and the characteristics of productive versus unproductive customizations, could be some additional topics for future IS research, based off of this revised assumption.

Key Takeaway: ICTs should standardize electronic entry/capture of data but customize (i.e. personalize) the business responses and solutions that these electronic data would drive.

## Traditional Assumption 3: Firm valuation implies financial valuation

That organizational value is more than simply material financial value of tangible resources is well-recognized for many years (e.g. Billiot & Glandon, 2005; Srinivasan, 2006; Wang, Zhang, & Ouyang, 2009). Even from a purely monetary viewpoint, accountants have long known the importance of valuating intangible assets, such as goodwill and social capital. More recently, with social media ventures like Kickstarter and GoFundMe, intangible asset value is becoming even more prominent as it forms a basis of the company’s value proposition.

Our vision of a compassion-driven focus in future IS research calls for a sharper and bolder variant of this assumption. We suggest that, in order to consistently achieve the joint goals of compassion and profits, future IS research needs to reject direct focus on material financial gains. Although financial gains are obviously important, directly focusing on them could potentially cause future IS research to slip back into older efficiencyfocused ways of thinking. This could produce financial gains in the short term but also be unsustainable for society and business on a longer-term. To be sustainable, organizations must stay true to the higher goal of not sacrificing the well-being of customers and employees on the path to achieving financial gains. To this end, future IS research would need to focus on gains in compassion and related outcomes from ICTs in organizations, trusting that with these mechanisms in place, financial value would follow. Future IS research should not assume that an increase in financial value is unconditionally desirable for organizations; rather, an increase in financial value, gained through increases in caring, compassion, and well-being in the workplace, needs to be the assumed purpose for IS research and practice.

Key Takeaway: To achieve financial gains, do not focus directly on financial gains. Focus, instead, on improving compassion, promoting employee and customer well-being, and reducing burnout in the workplace, and trust that financial gains will follow.

Overall, we do not claim that efficiency, standardization, and financial value are unimportant within our new vision for the future of IS research. Rather, we submit that these concepts are not unconditionally virtuous in organizations. We present a more nuanced conceptualization of when these should be regarded as value-adding. We present this nuanced conceptualization as the revised set of assumptions underlying our compassion-driven vision for future IS research.

## A future agenda for IS research

To structure our research agenda comprehensively, yet parsimoniously, we draw significantly on the discussions presented in recent well-being-oriented publications in the IS literature, including Walsham (2012) and Majchrzak et al. (2016). To a large extent, we see our vision for future IS research as furthering the vision put forward in Walsham (2012). Walsham’s call was to consider the role that ICTs could play in building a better world. Our call in this article is to focus on compassiondriven approaches as one specific way in which ICTs could build a better world. We undertake this approach so that related works emphasizing a well-being-oriented mission for IS research may develop in an integrated connected manner, rather than as isolated piecemeal contributions.

In Table 2, we present a summary of our vision for the future of IS research and show how our vision compares to and builds upon the ideas presented by Walsham (2012).

Inspired by Walsham (2012), we organize our discussion of the future agenda for IS research into four key themes, namely (1) the core focus of the IS field, (2) view of the IT artifact, (3) methodology, and (4) inter-disciplinarity.

## The core focus of the IS field

We submit that future IS research should incorporate compassion as an outcome of interest following ICT implementation and use, alongside other rational/economic outcomes, like productivity and firm performance. With this new expanded focus on compassion as a business goal, future IS research would be driven by this fundamental question: How can ICTs create a workplace driven by compassion as the means to achieving profit? Or, stated differently: How can ICTs create a compassionate and profitable/sustainable workplace? In its broadest sense, then, we envision the future core of the IS field to be concerned with technology management that embodies compassion as a key component of sound management principles and practice.

Given that much (though, not all) IS research comes out of business schools, it would behoove future IS research to continue focusing on the management of digital enterprises along with its usual goals of running profitably<sup>1</sup> and productively (e.g. Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996). We encourage future IS research to extend this conversation, though, by adopting the notion that running profitably need not take away from running compassionately (Worline & Dutton, 2017). It is easy to see profits and compassion as being goals that are at odds with each other, at least in commercial for-profit organizations. However, this perception has generally harmed, rather than helped, the cause of well-being-driven agendas in IS research. So, the goal of future IS research should not be to pursue compassion goals as opposed to profit goals or vice versa, but instead to pursue compassion goals in concert with or as a path toward profit goals.

Under our future vision, compassion goals may be viewed as an intermediate outcome from designing, implementing, and using ICTs that would then impact firm productivity and performance as more distal ICT-enabled outcomes. Viewed differently, compassion goals may be viewed as the pathway through which ICTs would impact economic goals, like profit and performance. In the future, we encourage IS research to focus on building up this particular pathway toward ICT-driven performance and profits as opposed to the myriad other pathways (e.g. increasing efficiency, downsizing, controlling costs, streamlining supply chains, etc.) through which ICTs have traditionally been known to usher in performance and profits. Future IS research can identify sustainable ways in which organiza tions could choose compassion-driven or compassion-sensitive paths to IT-enabled productivity and performance over paths that ignore compassion. Conceptually, this point of view has parallels to current discourse on sustainability in business (Bansal & DesJardine, 2014; Colbert & Kurucz, 2007). If we want business profits to be sustainable, we cannot expect to get there by burning out and trampling on the happiness of the very employees who run these businesses (Worline & Dutton, 2017)—this is the core motivation for why IS research should hold compassion as a central goal en route to profits, performance, and productivity.

We intend for our aforementioned future core question to not constrain the breadth of IS research, but rather to inform it. Future IS research needs to demonstrate awareness of compassion-related consequences that arise from IS as these systems are used to achieve performance goals in organizations. Some studies in future IS research will express this awareness by explicitly investigating compassion-mediated pathways to ICT-enabled performance. Other studies may express this awareness by simply including compassion in a discussion of the research and practical implications of their otherwise performance-oriented study. Still other studies may choose to investigate compassion as the sole outcome, seeking to either refine the concept in an IT-enabled setting or to investigate antecedents and circumstances that help or hinder IS from engendering a compassionate workplace. So, our anticipated goal for future IS research, summarized in the overall question above, is a trajectory that we envision cumulatively for the entire field in the future, rather than for each individual study in this field.

Finally, in response to recent calls for an expanded view of “theory” in IS research, theories of the problem as well as theories of the solution could be formulated within this future domain of compassion-driven IS research (Majchrzak et al., 2016).

Theories of the problem could focus on conceptualizing the varied challenges associated with the involvement of ICTs in compassion-driven approaches toward firm economic gains. For example, theories of the problem could investigate how ICTs can get in the way of compassion in the workplace and the factors that contribute to this. They could also investigate how ICT-enabled suffering lays the groundwork for subsequent expression of compassion, or lack thereof. These theories could, for example, link to the existing literature on technostress and on the interruptions that ICTs cause in the workplace (Ayyagari, Grover, & Purvis, 2011; Galluch, Grover, & Thatcher, 2015). Theories of the problem may investigate the likelihood that ICTs may, sometimes unfairly, be perceived as being non-compassionate due to their stoic mechanical nature, even when they are working to alleviate suffering in the workplace. For example, health care information technologies, like electronic charting, save lives but are seldom perceived as agents of compassion (Agarwal, Gao, DesRoches, & Jha, 2010; Goh, Gao, & Agarwal, 2011).

Theories of the solution may explore how ICTs can facilitate compassionate responses by/to individuals, groups, and organizations. These theories could also investigate the extent to which ICTs can be perceived as agents of compassion despite their machine-like interface. This research may draw on design science to redesign the technology to appear more “human” as well as draw on psychology research to influence changes in user perceptions of the technology (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Peffers, Tuunanen, Rothenberger, & Chatterjee, 2007/2008; Sergeeva, Huysman, Soekijad, & van den Hooff, 2017; Sun, Fang, & Zou, 2016).

Key Takeaway: Future IS research needs to be informed by the following core question: How can ICTs create a workplace driven by compassion as the means to achieving profit?

## View of the IT artifact

Most traditional conceptualizations of the IT artifact are based on rational profit-maximizing objectives (Ning & Tanriverdi, 2017; Oh, Yang, & Kim, 2014; Sabherwal & Jeyaraj, 2015). A revised focus of future IS research on compassion goals in concert with economic goals, or on compassion goals as the path toward economic goals, would necessitate a new conceptualization of the IT artifact. This new conceptualization would need to incorporate capabilities for scanning, detecting, alerting, and responding to suffering or to unfulfilled aspirations, as well as for connecting these to appropriate emotional support and remedial action (Dutton et al., 2014; Lilius et al., 2012). Such a conceptualization of compassion-driven IS would draw on and integrate IS fields, such as virtual agents, business analytics, and the Internet of Things. We see preliminary evidence of such artifacts from companies like Apple, advertising a smart watch that has mobilized compassionate care and help in the form of rescuers to the site of a car crash, and remote monitoring and care of a young child’s Type I diabetes (Gilbert, 2018; Turner, 2017).

Our future view of the IT artifact, driven by the joint goals of compassion and profit, would lend itself readily to the concepts outlined within technology affordances and constraints theory, which has been proposed and used for the study of ICTs and societal challenges (Faraj & Azad, 2012; Gibson, 1977; Leonardi, 2013; Majchrzak et al., 2016). This theory encourages us to think of technology, as not just a set of stand-alone features and functionalities but as a set of opportunities and constraints on action. Applying this theory, future IS research can investigate what features of the technology provide affordances for noticing and responding to suffering or unfulfilled aspirations and what affordances enable/inspire compassionate responses. Similarly, what technology features serve as constraints on noticing, alerting to, and acting compassionately toward suffering or unfulfilled aspirations? How can these constraining features be either removed or transformed into affordances? These are some of the many questions that could be pursued within our future vision of an IT artifact that is aligned with the goal of compassion as the pathway leading to firm profit.

Key Takeaway: A network-based conceptualization of the IT artifact is encouraged, in which people, tasks, and technologies (including wearable technologies) would be the nodes. A Compassion-Driven Information System (CDIS)—bringing together various technological capabilities, such as business analytics, virtual agents, and the Internet of Things—is detailed as a hypothetical, but realistic, example of such an IT artifact. Technology–people and technology–task interconnections, as well as the overall structure of these interconnections, need to be emphasized as relevant elements of the IT artifact, beyond simply the individual technologies, people, and tasks that constitute the artifact. This networked IT artifact could be viewed as a set of affordances and constraints on compassionate action in the course of task performance.

## Methodology

We join other researchers in emphatically supporting methodological pluralism in future compassion-driven IS research (Majchrzak et al., 2016; Walsham, 2012). A future focus on compassion outcomes and economic outcomes as joint goals from ICTs would result in studies that outline factors and processes connecting ICTs, compassion, and profits/performance. As such, multimethod research designs, involving qualitative and quantitative data collection, would be the most effective. In future IS research, design science approaches are likely to gain prominence in the study and design of compassion-driven IS. IS researchers would need to collaborate with colleagues from other disciplines, such as psychology, positive organizational scholarship, and leadership, in order to pursue compassion-based goals in their work (e.g. Boyatzis, Smith, & Blaize, 2006; Dutton et al., 2014). This would further necessitate willingness and present opportunity to embrace multiple methodologies.

The pursuit of compassion-based goals in IS research may also create opportunities to develop new research methodologies. Since notions of compassion, especially within an ICT-enabled space, are likely to be subtle tacit concepts, drawing respondents out to share the appropriate data may not be as forthcoming through traditional methodologies, like surveys, interviews, and observation sessions. Other creative methods involving self-reflection (e.g. journaling) or role-playing (e.g. scenario-based quasi-experiments) may need to be devised to capture rich data on the connections between ICTs, compassion, and economic outcomes.

Key Takeaway: Methodological pluralism is a must in future IS research. This would require combining traditional qualitative and quantitative methods with other creative methods, which are especially apt for drawing out data on subtle concepts, like compassion.

## Inter-disciplinarity

We anticipate a heightened need for inter-disciplinarity in a future IS research agenda that is driven by the goal of pursuing compassion outcomes as the means to economic outcomes. IS research needs to continue collaborating with other fields within the school of business, such as Positive Organizational Scholarship, Organizational Studies Marketing, Organizational Behavior, Human Resource Management, and others. Positive Organizational Scholarship offers us a great starting point for incorporating compassion goals in IS research through its extensive and focused study of compassion in the workplace (Dutton et al., 2014; Frost, 1999; Lilius et al., 2008). In addition, the broader and more flexible conceptualizations of compassion found in popular literature would create opportunities for new research questions to be asked at the intersection of IS research and research in other business fields. For example, what compassion-driven goals would fit within organizations’ digital marketing strategies? Or, IS collaborations with Organizational Behavior could focus on developing the concept of technology-enabled compassion-sensitized networks or teams, which aim to accomplish business outcomes while simultaneously pursuing the reduction of team member suffering and fulfillment of team member aspirations as explicit team outcomes. Or, IS research and HR research could join forces to develop targeted employee training and reward systems that would automatically identify candidates who need to be rewarded or those who need to be additionally trained on their ability to align compassion goals with economic goals.

Fields of study outside of the business school, such as Law, Religion, and Leadership, have defined compassion differently from Positive Organizational Scholarship literature (e.g. Bennis & Nanus, 1985; Bisesi & Lidman, 2009; Boyatzis et al., 2006). While the organizational literature has viewed compassion as necessarily targeted toward suffering, literatures from these other fields have taken a broader view of compassion as being able to help others to reach their dreams and aspirations (Boyatzis et al., 2006). These other fields, therefore, offer a scientific treatment of certain dimensions within the broader everyman’s conceptualization of compassion. Therefore, by collaborating with fields outside of schools of business, such as Psychology, Law, Religion, and Public Policy, we would be able to mesh newer theories, concepts, and methodologies into the study of ICT’s role relating to the everyman’s view of compassion. This would expand the rigor and relevance of compassion-driven IS research in the future.

These examples only scratch the surface of all the opportunities for new research at the intersection of ICTs, compassion, and economic goals that are likely to be made possible through an interdisciplinary mind-set.

Key Takeaway: Future IS research needs to collaborate extensively on theory, methods, and impact with other fields within and beyond the business school.

## An illustration: The future of health IS research

So far, in this article, we have theorized in favor of a compassion-driven approach in future IS research. We have focused on two things in particular: (1) how this approach would change the core assumptions underlying our field, and (2) a broad future agenda that we could follow, as an academic discipline, to incorporate this approach in our research. In this section, we would like to provide a concrete illustration of this future vision by drawing on a prominent domain of study within IS research, namely Health IS or “IS in Health care,” and exploring the extent to which our proposed vision applies to this chosen domain. Our choice of this domain for this illustration is motivated primarily by our familiarity with it, rather than by an unduly strong affinity of this domain toward our proposed vision.

As a field of research, Health IS or “IS in Health care,” focuses on the study of all things relating to information and communication systems in health care settings (Agarwal et al., 2010; Kane & Labianca, 2011; Mishra, Anderson, & Agarwal, 2012). Historically, the practice of medicine tended to be about the healing process, and the personal connection between the patient and the physician (Dranove, 2009). With the advent of medical and information technologies and complex networked landscapes of providers, hospitals, physician offices, insurance companies, and accountable care organizations, this personal touch has by and large become missing from health care practice (Lown, Rosen, & Marttila, 2011; Toll, 2012). Notions of efficiency and standardization have become central in many large hospital systems, and govern remuneration structures, much like they would in a traditional commercial setting, such as a manufacturing company (Starr, 1982).

More recently, though, industry and academia have prominently called for “re-humanizing health care” (Dugan, 2004; O’Connor & Lanning, 2001; Rathert, Vogus, & McClelland, 2016). This call challenges health care organizations to devise creative ways of using medical and information technologies to bring back the human connection and individual attention that is sorely felt missing in present-day health systems (Lown et al., 2011; Toll, 2012).

We will explore how our proposed revised underlying assumptions and our proposed future vision for IS research fit within this exemplar of Health IS research (see Table 3 for summary).

## IS research assumptions within the health IS research domain

Present-day hospitals increasingly emphasize efficiencies of process (e.g. throughput) and of outcome (e.g. reduced length of stay in the hospital) as measures of quality of patient care (Traditional Assumption 1 in IS research) (Chaudhry et al., 2006; Delone & McLean, 2003; Poissant, Pereira, Tamblyn, & Kawasumi, 2005). These efficiencies of process and of outcome for the hospital as a whole are, however, enabled through detailed real-time electronic documentation at the individual patient–provider level (Raman & Bharadwaj, 2012). Individual provider workflows are, therefore, purposefully made more inefficient—that is, requiring more provider time and energy—in order to realize efficiencies at the process-level and outcome-level for the hospital as a whole (Chaudhry et al., 2006; Poissant et al., 2005).

Health IS research and practice is, therefore, already attuned to a nuanced view of efficiency that is similar in spirit to what we have proposed as a future revised assumption underlying compassion-driven IS research. Thanks to the opportunities and limitations of electronic charting systems, health care organizations are well aware that efficiency is not a universally desirable quality to pursue; depending on the context, planned inefficiency on one level (individual provider) may be necessary in order to accomplish efficiency on another level (process-level and outcome-level for the hospital as a whole).

Future health IS research could build on this assumption to design networked systems that could efficiently detect suffering, fatigue, burnout, and unfulfilled aspirations in the workplace and direct resources to alleviate these. The efficient functioning of such a compassion-driven health IS would depend on providers documenting more detail regard ing themselves, personally and professionally, to fuel these systems, that is, it would demand more time, attention, and energies from individual employees. Hospital administration would need to think about ways to use other health IS to “free up” such human resources so that they could be redirected into fueling these compassion-driven IS. A variety of behavioral science and design science research opportunities for health IS research emanate from this simple future shift in assumptions regarding efficiency.

The health care industry also appears primed to adopt a nuanced view toward the role of standardization in the health care workplace, similar to what we have proposed in our second assumption supporting future IS research. Standardization of care through evidence-based medicine (EBM) is a long-standing, albeit debated, perspective in health care (Baxter & Baxter, 2002; Leach, 2006). EBM calls for standardized patient-care protocols, based in cumulative scientific evidence, which assure the same treatment regimen for all patients presenting the same condition, regardless of institution or provider (Davidoff, Haynes, Sackett, & Smith, 1995; Sackett, Rosenberg, Gray, Haynes, & Richardson, 1996). While this seems reasonable on the surface, EBM has its detractors because such a “cookie-cutter” approach to medicine does not allow for that customized personal touch between patient and provider (Feinstein & Horwitz, 1997; Grahame-Smith, 1995; Walshe & Rundall, 2001). For example, an evidence-based protocol may call for running a battery of tests on a vomiting child. However, taking the time to know the patient personally may lead to the provider’s intuition that the vomiting may just be a side-effect from having received too much to drink. This intuition, based on local context-specific knowledge, could save the patient from unnecessary trauma and pain. Health care organizations, therefore, understand—as we propose—that standardization of patient-care practice is not universally desirable.

A compassion focus in future health IS research would assume customization (and not standardization) of responses at the individual patient level. This is because compassionate responses are, by nature, personal and customized, recognizing the uniqueness of someone’s pain and trying to do something to respond (Dutton et al., 2014; Lilius et al., 2012; Worline & Dutton, 2017). With the human genome having been fully mapped, there has been a more recent clinical movement toward more individualized clinical care as specific DNA patterns have been unveiled for each patient through genetic testing (McDermott, Downing, & Stratton, 2011). The trend seems to be toward less standardization as a result of genetic mapping

Even studies that investigate patient-care outcomes often state their motivation as stemming from a productivity, cost control, and/or revenue maximization standpoint. A focus on compassion toward patients, toward colleagues, and self-compassion as explicit objectives of patient-care workflows, backed by hospital administration, could expand this research by providing another pathway through which to achieve the financial and productivity goals that are so important to health care organizations (Raab, Sogge, Parker, & Flament, 2015; Sinclair et al., 2016; Taylor, Hodgson, Gee, & Collins, 2017). This makes well-meaning patient-care initiatives and health IS difficult to implement (Garman, Leach, & Spector, 2006; Nembhard, Alexander, Hoff, & Ramanujam, 2009).

## Future IS research agenda illustrated in the health IS research domain

In our proposed research agenda, we have encouraged future IS research to be driven by the following research question: How can ICTs create a workplace driven by compassion as the means to achieving profit? In health IS research, theories of the problem and theories of the solution can be readily constructed in alignment with this core question. Health care settings represent high-stress work environments where providers burn out easily and regularly (McHugh, Kutney-Lee, Cimiotti, Sloane, & Aiken, 2011; Shanafelt et al., 2012). This often results from an inability to open up to their colleagues or to express their worries, fears, and anxieties, due to high societal pressures on physicians, nurses, and other health care professionals to always be in control and know the right thing to do (Brand et al., 2017; Dewa, Loong, Bonato, Trojanowski, & Rea, 2017; Heeter, Lehto, Allbritton, Day, & Wiseman, 2017; Johnson-Coyle et al., 2016; Wharne, 2017). As a result, compassion toward colleagues, compassion toward oneself, and even compassion toward patients (a possible numbing effect from constant exposure to patient problems) is severely lacking in these environments (Cocker & Joss, 2016; Sinclair, Raffin-Bouchal, Venturato, Mijovic-Kondejewski, & Smith-MacDonald, 2017).

Health IS allow detailed documentation and time-stamping of all activities performed by patient-care providers as part of their jobs (Raman & Bharadwaj, 2012). Historically, an emphasis on efficiency/resource utilization has resulted in the use of this electronic data for monitoring and enforcement, and to guide reward/punishment algorithms (Carmichael et al., 2017; Caron et al., 2017; Jones, 2008). Instead, embracing our proposed compassion-driven approach, future health IS research could investigate how this same information can be used to proactively identify stress points in the health care workplace and mobilize helping resources to support and dissipate the stress before it spirals into full-blown burnout, erroneous patient-care decisions, and employee turnover. Future health IS research could also investigate how the information captured within health IS can be used to systematically identify and address misaligned aspirations or unfulfilled potential among employees in ways that would benefit the individual as well as the health care organization. A compassionate process of addressing performance shortfalls in the health care workplace would involve unique considerations that take into account the hierarchical power differences across providers spanning different professions and work roles; these need to be the subject of future health IS research. In addition, the impact of these compassion-driven technology systems on provider morale, patientcare process performance, patient-care outcomes, and finally financial outcomes could be investigated as a vibrant stream within future health IS research.

The availability of large volumes of patient-care data through EMR systems, coupled with rapid advancements in health analytics capabilities, afford a new conceptualization of the IT artifact in health IS research. In this conceptualization, the IT artifact of interest to health IS research need not be a single technology, but rather could be a network of health information technologies, medical technologies, providers, patients, and patient-care practices. This networked IT artifact would have the potential to enable data-driven customization of responses to suffering in patients and providers as well as to unfulfilled aspirations, such as wellness aspirations of patients or career aspirations of providers. Before electronic documentation systems, identifying and helping stressed, fatigued, burned out colleagues—or, on the other end of the spectrum, underwhelmed, bored, and uninspired/under-inspired colleagues—may have been a daunting task that was forced to be left to random chance. However, health IS, conceptualized in this networked manner, have the potential to support systematic and reliable expressions of compassion within a network of colleagues, patients, and technologies in the health care organization. These technologies—as well as others, such as telemedicine, specially programmed “compassionate” virtual agents (see description accompanying Figure 1), and so on—can play an important role in answering the call to re-humanize medicine. Due to sheer growth in size and complexity, re-humanizing present-day medicine will obviously need to look very different from medicine that was organically humane in the past; technology enablement clearly has a role to play in the re-humanizing process. Delineating what this role is, and what the corresponding IT artifact(s) may need to look like, need to be core topics in future health IS research.

Finally, given the widespread impact of IS in all aspects of health care provision and management, meaningful research on health IS cannot just build on the knowledge of a single discipline (such as, Management Information Systems) or depend on a single dominant methodology. Researchers publishing health IS work hail from a variety of disciplines, including medicine, IS, organizational behavior, organization studies, public health, public policy, law, psychology, and others (e.g. Ford, Menachemi, Peterson, & Huerta, 2009; Gravely & Whaley, 2009; Kuperman et al., 2007; McKibbon & Fridsma, 2006; Murnane et al., 2016; Schmit, Wetter, & Kash, 2017). Another related trend in current health IS research that is well-aligned to our proposed future vision is that health IS research is frequently published through collaborative efforts between academia and practice (e.g. Kuperman et al., 2007; Payne, Hoey, Nichol, & Lovis, 2003). Health IS research includes a variety of qualitative and quantitative methodologies, like field studies, “big data” analytics, survey-based research, and narrative studies, to name a few (e.g. D. G. Goetz, Kuzel, Feng, DeShazo, & Love, 2012; McAlearney, Hefner, Sieck, & Huerta, 2015; R. H. Miller, West, Brown, Sim, & Ganchoff, 2005; Strack et al., 2014). It includes many innovative data collection techniques, such as journaling and social network analysis, alongside the more standard interviews, observations, and surveys.

This detailed illustration reveals that health IS research and practice is well-aligned to our proposed future vision, as well as its underlying assumptions, for all of IS research to maintain a compassion-driven approach in the study of economic gains from IS. We encourage and invite further such illustrations in other research streams within IS research.

## Discussion and conclusion

The field of IS research has been shaped thus far by a rich tradition of pursuing IS for profit-maximizing goals (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996; Jasperson et al., 2002; Kim &

Kim, 2017; Venkatesh & Morris, 2000; Venkatesh et al., 2000). In the relentless fight to establish the legitimacy of our academic discipline within larger business academia, we have perhaps forgotten to focus on our legitimacy in the world at large. All that is changing, though, amid burgeoning interest across academia and practice in how business in general, and IS in particular, can help improve wellbeing while also running economically viable establishments (Coombs, 1999; Majchrzak et al., 2016; Walsham, 2012; Worline & Dutton, 2017). For example, “sustainability” and “corporate social responsibility” are terms that are popular in almost all fields of business and in academic as well as practical settings (e.g. Kelly & Walker-Said, 2015; Lecuyer, Capelli, & Sabadie, 2017; McCarthy, 2017; Rahman, Rodriguez-Serrano, & Lambkin, 2017). Entire fields of research, under the umbrella of Positive Organizational Scholarship, have arisen whose explicit agenda is to help develop a stronger well-being orientation in managing organizations (e.g. Andrews & Whitney, 1976; Dutton et al., 2014; Schwartz & Bardi, 2001; Worline & Dutton, 2017). Recent research articles published in prominent IS journals have called on ICTs to make the world a better place and to solve major societal challenges (Majchrzak et al., 2016; Walsham, 2012).

In this article, we have presented a new agenda for future IS research that is driven by compassion goals as the means to profit/performance goals. We view compassion-based intermediate goals as the primary sustainable pathway for IS to impact the more distal economic goals of profit, performance, and productivity. Compassion toward the employees, customers, and partners that shape a business is necessary for these stakeholders to work effectively toward the business’ economic goals. IS have a potent, albeit thusfar neglected, role to play in all of this. We invite future IS research to tackle such complex questions as, How can ICTs maintain a compassion focus in organizations and how can such a focus enhance rational/economic goals of the organization? In making this call, therefore, we are implicitly, but consciously, discouraging the pursuit of economic goals at the expense of or without regard to compassion goals. Walking away from such “low-hanging fruit” will require considerable fortitude among IS researchers and practitioners, but will pave the way for greater sustainability and responsibility in technology-enabled business management. We illustrate the viability of our proposed future vision for the whole of IS research using a prominent domain of study within IS research, namely research on health IS. We invite similar applications/illustrations in other IS research domains.

As with any field of scientific investigation, the rigorous pursuit of compassion-focused IS research will need to address various challenges in conceptualization, boundarysetting, and design. For example, everyman’s views of compassion would need to be sharpened in ways that uphold rigor and relevance in future IS research. In compassion-focused IS research, “reducing suffering” deserves sharp distinction from such related concepts as reducing errors, reducing difficulty of interface, and so on, which have all remained established goals of IS research; sharpening these conceptual distinctions will prevent future IS research from falling in the trap of repackaging old classic concepts into new labels.

We hope that this article has impressed the importance of engaging in such rigorous ground-setting, and in some cases, ground-breaking work by demonstrating that the current gap in the research intersection between IS and Compassion matters to the richness, novelty, and meaningfulness of the insights that could be generated from future IS research.

## Appendix

## The vision of a “Compassion-Driven Information System”—The narrative accompanying Figure 1

Figure 1 represents our future vision of an IT artifact that can systematically and predictably express compassion using a network of technologies and people that already exist in the modern-day workplace. This type of networked IT artifact can help employees of a company go about their job responsibilities in a more compassion-sensitized manner. Such a compassion-driven information system (CDIS), as we call it, would be a good example of the kind of IT artifact that we propose as part of our future agenda for IS research.

The CDIS would include a group of people—for example, employees in an organization —connected to each other through smart wearable devices (e.g. smart watches) on a network. An analytics network would overlay the device network, such that each individual device would include embedded analytics functionalities that would be allowed to access information on any of the devices in the network. With appropriate user permission mechanisms in place,<sup>2</sup> these smart wearable devices would connect to users’ personal and professional information, such as information about members in their family, primary responsibilities at work and at home, likes and dislikes, things the user is most worried about/scared of/confident about, personal and professional aspirations, and so on. This could include information specifically entered by the user as part of the CDIS (e.g. when signing up to join the CDIS network) and/or information gleaned from curating the user’s social media presence. It would represent a collection of dynamic information updated in real-time (e.g. evolving information re: current projects or an upcoming vacation) as well as static background information that may not change as frequently (e.g. job title or work history). In addition, similar to smart wearable devices like FitBits®, or Spire (https://www.spire.io/), these devices would also be programmed to track specific biochemical markers of physical or psychological stress/distress. Finally, similar to most modern smartphones, these devices would come equipped with some sort of virtual agent presence, similar in concept to Siri or Cortana, but with added and more flexible compassion-sensitive capabilities (more on this later).

When a user in the CDIS network is stressed, this increase in stress level would be detected by his or her smart wearable device. This would cause the device’s analytics capabilities to kick in, assessing user-specific information available at the time, to identify plausible causes of the increase in stress levels at that time. Or, the user may also directly type in/speak their exact cause of feeling stressed at the time into the smart device. The device’s analytics functionality would then scan through the information of other members of the CDIS, available through their devices connected to the CDIS network, to locate other CDIS network members with similar/relatable circumstances, and with compatible personality/attitudinal profiles as the stressed individual. Armed with this preliminary information, the virtual agent embedded in the stressed individual’s smart device would send a notification to its owner informing him or her (1) that he or she is stressed, (2) about the likely causes of this stress at this time, and (3) about other people in the network with whom the user may want to connect to help alleviate this stress. The virtual agent would then ask the user if he or she would like the virtual agent to connect him or her with the suggested network contacts. For example, a virtual agent could send the following notification to a stressed user: “You may be feeling stressed right now due to child-rearing/child health issues; Mrs. Jenae in Accounting may understand because she went through a similar situation recently. Would you like me to connect you with Mrs. Jenae now, or later, or just anonymously check with her to see if she would be willing to talk with you?” Or, another example of a notification sent to a stressed individual could be one in which the agent identifies the top five possible causes of the increased stress, and the corresponding people in the network who may be able to help for each cause, allowing the user to select with whom, if anyone, they would like to connect. The virtual agent could be programmed to send this notifi cation either through an emotion-neutral text, or by assuming different compassion-sensitized voice avatars, like “kind and gentle,” “straight shooter,” and so on. The particular notification style would be user-adjustable to accommodate changing user preferences.

Clearly, the value of this CDIS would depend on how much information users are willing and able to share and update. The CDIS would also depend on an elaborate mechanism of security and privacy protections before it could be functional. Many of these would involve managerial, financial, behavioral, legal, and ethical choices, all of which would need to be subjected to rigorous IS-based research and policy-making. What we hope we have accomplished through this hypothetical example, though, is to demonstrate that the technological capabilities that would embody this type of a future CDIS are already in existence and well within the reach of scientific and commercial communities of interest. We also offer the CDIS as one example of what we envision an IT artifact may look like in future compassion-sensitized IS research.
