# Evaluation of knowledge management performance: An organic approach

## Abstract:

Knowledge management (KM) strategy is the planned or actual coordination of a firm's major goals and learning, in time, that continually co-align its knowledge-based resources with the environment. Based on the organic perspective of strategy, a KM performance evaluation approach should be able to 1) review the knowledge governance mechanisms and learning routines that underpin KM strategy, as well as the performance outcomes driven by the strategy, and 2) predict the evolution of performance drivers and outcomes into the future to facilitate strategic planning. This study combined a survey study and system dynamics (SD) simulation to demonstrate the transformation from a mechanistic to an organic perspective on KM strategy and performance evaluation. The survey study was conducted based on a sample of 143 construction contractors, and used structural equation modeling (SEM) techniques to develop a KM performance index for reviewing the key elements that underpin KM strategy. The SD simulation predicted the development of KM strategy configurations and the evolution of KM performance over time. The organic KM performance evaluation approach demonstrated by this study has significant potential to improve the alignment of KM strategy within an increasingly dynamic business environment.

Key words: Knowledge management, knowledge management capability, knowledge management strategy, learning routines, governance mechanisms, performance evaluation, system dynamics analysis.

## 2. Theory

## 2.1 Theoretical framework overview

From the perspective of the DCV, KMC is a dynamic capability that builds on the microfoundations of learning routines and governance mechanisms [3, 10, 21]. Learning routines reflect patterns of learning behavior that characterize a firm‘s reactions to various stimuli [7, 22]. The stimuli can be generated within the firm or from the external environment [7, 22]. Learning routines reconfigure the organizational knowledge base and match business operating routines with the needs of a dynamic market [3]. The implementation of learning routines is facilitated by the governance mechanisms of the firm [6]. In this vein, an organizational learning system is comprised of three essential elements: learning routines carry out learning and reflect the actionbased nature of KM implementation [3]; governance mechanisms can enable control of the implementation [6]; a constant co-evolution of learning and control drives the emergence and evolution of a capability-based KM strategy that achieves performance outcomes [10, 13, 23, 24].

According to the organic perspective on strategy [9], a firm's KM strategy is the planned or actual coordination of a firm's major goals and learning, in time, that continually co-align its knowledge resources with the environment. The DCV-based assertions [3, 10, 21] suggest that the coordination is achieved by developing appropriate micro-foundations of KMC: that is to reconfigure a firm's internal governance mechanisms and learning routines to not only respond to but also influence environmental conditions and development. The organic- and capability-based assertions have significant implications on the performance evaluation of KM strategy. In this vein, KM performance evaluation needs to assess how governance mechanisms and learning routines are deployed, and to develop KMC that produces desired performance outcomes. This implies that the evaluation framework should provide assessment of the three types of observable elements that constitute the learning system. Among them, governance mechanisms and learning routines are performance drivers, which produce performance outcomes. In addition, the evaluation should not only be able to assess the existing learning system, but also to predict its evolution for strategic planning. ## 2.2 Strategic implication of knowledge management capability

The KMC concept has been used specifically for evaluating KM performance from a capabilitybased perspective [e.g. 4, 28, 29, 30-33]. The initial conceptualization recognizes that knowledge has emerged as the most strategically significant resource of the firm due to its heterogeneity and immobility [1, 2, 17]; it perceives KMC as a dynamic capability underpinned by internal firm attributes that enable a firm to coordinate and exploit its knowledge-based resources [4, 5]. In line with the resource-based view [34, 35], the conceptualization posits that the potential for competitive advantage depends on the value, rarity, and inimitability of a firm‘s knowledgebased resources and KMC, and the degree to which the firm is organized to fully realize this potential – the VRIO framework [36]. The conceptualization drew primarily on KM assertions about the organizational capability in knowledge variation, generation, dissemination and application within organizational boundaries [7, 15, 17, 37]. Nevertheless, the positions underpinning the original conceptualization paid less attention to the traditional absorptive capacity theory on leveraging knowledge from external sources [18, 19, 25]. Further, the imperfect imitability of KMC was not explicitly explained, due to limited discussion on learning routines, which are the regular and persistent firm features that ―play the role that genes play in biological evolutionary theory‖ [38: p.14].

To enable the organic KM performance evaluation, this study re-conceptualizes KMC based on an integrated perspective, which emphasizes the learning routines that explore, transform, and exploit knowledge from both internal and external sources [3]. Furthermore, in line with the organic perspective on strategy [9], the reconceptualization asserts that there are interdependent and multiple reciprocal relationships among the key elements that constitute the micro-foundations of KMC [3, 6]. In addition, the relationships evolve with time in a non-linear

#

manner [8, 20]. Hence, compared to the positions asserted by the original conceptualization, the re-conceptualization provides an organic understanding of how strategic learning drives the emergence and evolution of KMC. Based on the reconceptualization, the theoretical framework provides much more fine-grained positions about strategic learning. These positions facilitate the study of the independence and dynamics of the learning system, which align KMC with market stimuli.

Recent advancements in the research areas of KM [1, 6, 15, 39], absorptive capacity [e.g. 18, 19, 25], and dynamic capabilities [e.g. 7, 12, 40] lead to an integrated view of a knowledgebased dynamic capability [3]. This view combines internal and external aspects of organizational learning [3]. Internal learning focuses on internal knowledge creation, dissemination and application, which are emphasized by KM [15, 17] and DCV scholars [7]. External learning is addressed by the conventional absorptive capacity concept [18, 19, 25], which represents a firm‘s capability for the identification, acquisition, assimilation and exploitation of knowledge from external sources. The integrated view argues that externally acquired knowledge needs to be integrated through internal knowledge creation and exploitation routines, so as to achieve the reconfiguration of a knowledge-based resource that helps to sustain competitive advantage over time [3]. This view further considers the complex interdependence and complementarities between internal and external learning routines as important sources of the imperfect imitability of knowledge-based dynamic capability [3].

In line with the integrative view, this study defines KMC as a knowledge-based dynamic capability that a firm deliberately develops to deploy a KM strategy that aligns its knowledge resource with a changing environment. The alignment extends, modifies and creates knowledgebased resources for the purpose of enhancing future profit [14, 22]. This resource reconfiguration

#

is fulfilled by developing conducive governance mechanisms to facilitate internal and external learning that respectively explores, transforms and exploits knowledge from inside and outside its boundaries [3, 6]. In this vein, the learning by nature is a strategic one [11]. Knowledge governance mechanisms facilitate learning and guide the reconfiguration of learning routines [10, 12]. The micro-foundations of KMC and the relationships between them are idiosyncratic and path-dependent, hence conferring the imperfect imitability and dynamic nature of KMC [3, 6, 7, 25]. Therefore, according to the VRIO framework [36], KMC is a valuable firm resource in its own right, and a source of performance heterogeneity. This conceptualization leads to the first hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: Knowledge management capability is positively associated with the business performance of firms.

## 2.3 Learning routines

## 2.3.1 Learning routines: micro-foundations of KMC

According to capability-based assertions, learning routines are the building blocks of KMC [3, 12, 33]. Further, in the dynamic capabilities literature, learning routines can be classified into four essential categories, which carry out four sequential learning phases [3, 7, 15, 19, 25]. Knowledge identification (KI) routines identify and respond to the stimuli of the external environment, recognize essential external knowledge that is needed for resource configuration [21], and combine external stimuli with internally generated knowledge derived from firms‘ existing routines [7]. Knowledge acquisition (KA) routines acquire essential external knowledge [18, 25], and create new knowledge internally [15]. Knowledge dissemination (KD) routines assimilate, share and retain new knowledge from both internal and external sources [3], and codify new tacit

#

knowledge into procedure manuals for the reconfiguration of operating routines [7]. Knowledge utilization (KU) routines apply and exploit the newly transformed knowledge to modify and create operating routines that improve performance outcomes [3].

In general, the classification asserts two broad learning approaches. Exploratory learning identifies and acquires both internal and external new knowledge, allowing firms to experiment with alternatives and generate technological changes that are necessary for managing challenges in a turbulent environment [7, 19, 25]. Exploitative learning disseminates and utilizes knowledge to create new commercial outputs and refines existing routines, thereby increasing efficiency in adaptive processes [7, 19, 25]. The heterogeneity of firms‘ KMC, to a certain degree, stems from their idiosyncratic and complex combination of learning routines within specific business contexts [3, 28, 41, 42], in particular their imperfectly imitable strategies to balance exploratory and exploitative learning [43, 44]. Therefore, this study proposes that the four types of learning routines are micro-foundations of KMC.

## 2.3.2 Performance implications

The capability literature proposes two broad assertions about the performance implications of learning routines. First, learning routines influence business performance outcomes [3, 19]. Second, strategic learning is conducted in sequential phases to influence performance outcomes [3, 7, 25], implying an indirect impact of some learning routines on performance. Previous empirical studies [4, 26] found that learning routines are associated with performance outcomes, and that the degrees of their associations vary. Their findings provide evidence for the first assertion. However, the second assertion has received limited attention in empirical investigations.

#

In order to explore the specific roles of the four learning routines in affecting business performance, this study develops seven hypotheses for testing.

The capability literature suggests that learning routines interact in sequential phases to explore and exploit both internally generated and externally acquired knowledge [3]. The learning phases chain in a recursive cycle, carrying out continual learning that drives the evolution of KMC for knowledge resource reconfiguration [7, 21]. The point of departure for each learning cycle lies in the knowledge identification phase, which helps to manage adaptive tension through modifying and creating goals that stimulate changes [3, 25]. Variations in a firm‘s competitive position revealed by its performance outcomes trigger learning, through which managers test the values and assumptions that led them to adopt certain knowledge or routines in the first place [13]. Knowledge identification routines carry out both external and internal learning. External learning identifies and responds to new challenges through scanning external stimuli (e.g., competitors‘ initiatives), and also recognizes external knowledge that is essential for resource configuration [21]. Internal learning combines external stimuli with internally generated information derived from a firm‘s existing routines [7]. The higher the variance in performance level, the stronger the external stimuli, and the higher the intensity required of the knowledge identification routines [13, 24]. Therefore,

Hypothesis 2: Business performance level is positively associated with the intensity of knowledge identification routines.

In order to tackle new challenges, the subsequent learning phase is mainly carried out by knowledge acquisition routines. During this phase, external learning acquires new knowledge from external sources [18, 25], while internal learning creates new knowledge internally [15].

#

Both internally generated and externally acquired knowledge are subsequently evaluated in terms of their potential for enhancing the effectiveness of existing routines or the opportunity to create new ones [7, 44]. In this vein, the higher the intensity of knowledge identification routines, the stronger the demand for new knowledge, and the higher the intensity of knowledge acquisition routines. Hence,

Hypothesis 3: The intensity of knowledge identification routines is positively associated with that of knowledge acquisition routines.

The new knowledge that survives the internal selection processes is then disseminated within the firm through a knowledge dissemination phase. During this phase, the firm assimilates the new knowledge that largely fits its existing cognitive structures, or transforms existing cognitive structures to adapt to new ideas and situations [21]. The transformation of the cognitive structure allows the firm to deal with path dependency and overcome inertia in the reconfiguration of a knowledge-based resource [21]. To facilitate the transformation and assimilation, knowledge dissemination routines share and retain new knowledge from both internal and external sources [19]. These dissemination routines also codify new tacit knowledge into knowledge artifacts, such as guidelines and manuals [7, 44]. The more knowledge that is acquired and generated by the knowledge acquisition routines, the more intensive the knowledge dissemination routines that are needed [41, 42]. Thus,

Hypothesis 4: The intensity of knowledge acquisition routines is positively associated with that of knowledge dissemination routines.

The disseminated knowledge is subsequently incorporated into business operations through knowledge utilization routines [3]. The primary purpose of knowledge utilization is to increase the efficiency in adaptive processes to match the operating routines with the needs in the changing market [19]. The more knowledge is disseminated, the more it is likely to be used, thus the higher the intensity of knowledge utilization [7, 42]. Therefore,

Hypothesis 5: The intensity of knowledge dissemination routines is positively associated with that of knowledge utilization routines.

The application and exploitation of the newly transformed knowledge to a dynamic competitive context leads to the modification and creation of new operating routines that improve performance outcomes [7, 44]. In other words, the new knowledge from both internal and external sources can only have an impact on performance outcomes through knowledge utilization routines [3, 7]. Therefore,

Hypothesis 6: The intensity of knowledge utilization routines is positively associated with business performance level.

The application of assimilated knowledge into business operations leads to the creation of new knowledge [15]. These novel approaches refine and extend existing routines and technologies, in particular generating more understanding of the performance implications of the operating routines employed [7]. Therefore, knowledge utilization triggers the knowledge identification phase in a new learning cycle [21]. The larger the variance in performance outcome, the stronger the stimuli, and the more intensive the prompted knowledge identification routines [13, 24]. Hence,

Hypothesis 7: The intensity of knowledge utilization routines is positively associated with that of knowledge identification routines.

In the meantime, performance outcomes as new knowledge that reveals the competitive position of the firm, are normally directly disseminated into different organizational levels for analysis and reflection [11]. The knowledge dissemination routines select knowledge that needs to be applied for improving business operations and refine knowledge resources such as manuals and guidelines [7]. The larger the difference between the performance objectives and the actual outcomes, the more intensive the analysis and reflection. Hence,

Hypothesis 8: Business performance level is positively associated with the intensity of knowledge dissemination routines.

## 2.4 Governance mechanisms

## 2.4.1 Governance mechanisms: micro-foundations of KMC

Governance mechanisms are micro-foundations of KMC due to their salient roles in facilitating strategic learning [12]. A firm‘s governance mechanisms determine the conduciveness of its organizational conditions that engage employees in learning [21]. There are two broad types of governance mechanisms. Informal governance mechanisms such as management style and organizational culture reflect a firm‘s value system [5]. Formal governance mechanisms, such as planning, reward schemes, regulations, and the development of information systems, are directly influenced by managerial interventions [5]. Both formal and informal mechanisms are applied simultaneously to support learning [5]. The conduciveness of informal mechanisms is reflected by the degree to which conscious management efforts are applied to develop formal mechanisms for improving learning [6]. Therefore, this study focuses on the impact of observable formal mechanisms on learning routines.

#

It has been empirically established that both organizational and technological mechanisms are essential formal governance mechanisms that constitute the foundation of a firm‘s capability in KM implementation [4, 26-28]. Organizational mechanisms provide leadership and strategic guidance for KM implementation and learning [4, 26, 27, 42, 45]. Technological mechanisms enable the development and application of ICT systems to facilitate KM implementation and learning [4, 26, 27, 45, 46]. Based on the evidence, this study proposes that organizational and technological mechanisms are essential attributes of KMC. In addition, organizational mechanisms such as incentive schemes enable organizational culture change, and thus have a positive impact on information and communications technology (ICT) implementation and the adoption of emerging technology [47, 48]. Thus,

Hypothesis 9: The strength of organizational mechanisms is positively associated with the strength of technological mechanisms.

## 2.4.2 Impact on learning routines

The KM literature suggests that the implementation strength of governance mechanisms has a positive impact on the application of learning routines. However, empirical evidence on the impact of different kinds of mechanisms on various types of learning routines is limited [6]. From the organic perspective [9], the two mechanisms may influence the four learning routines to different degrees, and the impact could be indirect. This study attempts to explore the different degrees of impact of the two governance mechanisms on the four learning routines, and confirms the pathways of indirect impact. In order to facilitate the investigation, two hypotheses are proposed to assert various degrees of impact of the governance mechanisms on the learning routines.

Hypothesis 10: The strength of organizational mechanisms is positively associated with the intensity of the four learning routines, i.e., knowledge identification, acquisition, dissemination and utilization, to different degrees.

Hypothesis 11: The strength of technological mechanisms is positively associated with the intensity of the four learning routines, i.e., knowledge identification, acquisition, dissemination and utilization, to different degrees.

In addition, the relationship between organizational mechanisms and knowledge identification could be reciprocal. At the variation stage of the learning cycle, the knowledge identification routines analyze internal organizational mechanisms against both opportunities and challenges of the external business environment [3]. The outputs of the opportunity identification and analysis provide direct guidance for modification and formulation of organizational strategies and policies directed at innovation and ICT application [11, 13]. The higher the intensity of knowledge identification routines, the stronger the organizational mechanisms might become. Thus,

Hypothesis 12: The intensity of knowledge identification routines is positively associated with the strength of organizational mechanisms.

## 2.4.3 Performance implications

The combinations and complementarities of organizational and technological mechanisms are socially complex and idiosyncratic [26, 33, 45]. The unique configuration of governance mechanisms is dependent on the historical evolution of a firm [36]. Employees are also often unclear about how various governance mechanisms, especially the complementarities between them, provide different degrees of incentives for different types of learning routines [6]. The unique mechanism configurations are even harder for competitors to observe and imitate [3]. The path dependence and causal ambiguity of their configurations make governance mechanisms a VRIO resource, and hence a source of performance heterogeneity [26, 33].

Further, the positive impact of the governance mechanisms on firm-level performance outcomes, identified in previous empirical studies [e.g. 4], is a shorthand way of representing more complex underlying firm-level organizational phenomena [6]. The deployment of these governance mechanisms, in particular the formal aspects of organization that can be manipulated by management, directly influence the conduciveness of the firm‘s cognitive context to facilitating organizational learning [6]. This is the learning that directly modifies the operating routines to realize performance outcomes [3, 7, 22, 27]. Therefore, two hypotheses are proposed to facilitate the test of the mediating effects of learning routines on the relationships between the strengths of governance mechanisms and business performance level.

Hypothesis 14: The strength of technological mechanisms is positively associated with business performance level.

## 2.5 Organic KM performance evaluation framework

According to the organic and capability-based assertions, the essence of a firm‘s KM strategy is to develop conducive governance mechanisms to facilitate strategic learning that constantly aligns its knowledge-based resources to the needs of a dynamic and evolving market. In this vein, any evaluation of the performance of KM strategy should assess the degree to which the micro-

1 foundations of KMC are configured and the performance outcomes achieved over time. More 2 specifically, the organic evaluation framework should have two functions. The first should assess 3 the existing performance drivers (i.e. governance mechanisms and learning routines) and 4 performance outcomes of a firm‘s learning system. This assessment helps to review a firm‘s 5 existing learning system and the underlying configurations of its KMC. The second should 6 predict the future evolution of the performance drivers and outcomes. This prediction provides 7 more accurate information to assist the planning of KM strategy. The KM performance 8 evaluation approaches reported by the current literature focus primarily on the review function, 9 whereas research on the prediction approach is limited.

Most KM performance evaluation frameworks in the literature follow the mechanistic perspective on strategy [9] by adopting a synchronic view of time and a directional view of flow [24]. The evaluation approaches focus on a single occurrence of a set of given variables at a particular time, and evaluate cross-sectional data based on a linear view of events. For example, Lee et al. [49] constructed a KM performance index (KMPI) through the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) of knowledge process measures. Tseng [50] measured the governance mechanisms of KM strategies and developed a KM system performance index (KMSPI) through EFA. Chen et al. [51] developed an analytical network process model to compare a firm‘s KM performance with that of its rivals. Ngai and Chan [52] used the analytic hierarchy process to select the most appropriate tool to support KM implementation. Fan et al. [28] investigated a fuzzy multiple attributes decision-making method for evaluating KMC.

A wide range of KM maturity models (KMMMs) are designed to overcome the static nature of KM performance evaluation frameworks, and to capture the KM development process by assessing the extent to which KM is defined, managed, and controlled [e.g. 31, 53-59].

#

Recently, Chen and Fong [29] employed a survey study design and adopted hypothesis-testing approaches to evaluate KM performance and maturity from the DVC perspective. Even though their study recognizes the evolutionary nature of KMC, the research design is still built on the mechanistic perspective on strategy [9], like other KMMMs. Chen and Fong‘s capability-based model [29] evaluates a set of cross-sectional data. It presents a linear and sequential view of causality between the micro-foundations of KMC and the performance outcomes, but pays limited attention to the feedback and multiple reciprocal influences among them.

The mechanistic KM performance evaluation approaches in the literature have provided significant evidence of the performance implications of learning system elements. However, these evaluation approaches only review the elements of an implemented learning system and its performance outcomes, thus providing insufficient guidance for the formulation of KM strategy within a dynamic market environment. To bridge this knowledge gap, this study seeks to develop an organic KM performance evaluation approach that performs both review and prediction functions.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1 Research design overview

This study adopted a mixed-methods design to fulfil the transformation from mechanistic to organic epistemological assumptions for studying the complexity of KM strategy and the evaluation of KM performance. The research design maintains continuity: it builds on, rather than rejects the mechanistic concepts, approaches and evidence [9]. This study first developed significant empirical evidence about the values of learning system constructs and the relationships between them through a mechanistic approach, which involves a cross-sectional

#

design, variance models and hypothesis-testing procedures. The analysis identified what underpins KM strategy and performance [8, 23]. The evidence laid a foundation for the subsequent SD simulation, an organic analysis that visualized how KM strategy and performance evolve [8, 23].

As experimentation in real learning systems is not feasible within organizational environments, SD simulation provides a practical way to study the feedback, accumulations, and nonlinearities of the complex system [20, 60]. The SD model design is able to incorporate evidence derived from the mechanistic study to model specifications as parameters, functional forms and initial values [20]. The simulation thus has the advantage of showing the initial state of the learning system at a specific time, thereby reflecting the solid evidence of the mechanistic study. Another advantage of simulation lies in its capacity to visualize and measure the potential evolution of the system, thereby demonstrating that the management of KM strategy is a continuous process rather than a selection among static configurations [8, 9, 20].

## 3.3 Sample and procedures of the survey study

Large and medium-sized construction contracting firms operating in Hong Kong were targeted as the research population. These firms are important sources and adopters of innovations. In addition, they work within a very dynamic and competitive market, where regulatory and competitive conditions are subject to rapid changes [66, 67]. Among 260 firms in the sampling frame, 109 (42%) were Group C contractors for public works with contract values exceeding HK\$50 million [69], and 125 (48%) were Group B contractors for public works with contract values of up to \$50 million. ## 3.4.1 Learning routine constructs

The literature review highlights four types of learning routine that underpin the KMC: knowledge identification, acquisition, dissemination and utilization. The four types of learning routine are defined as learning routine constructs, which are both dependent and independent variables in the and measured the learning routines within a construction context. ## 3.4.2 Governance mechanism constructs

In line with the propositions of many KM frameworks [4, 72], two broad categories of mechanisms have been empirically identified as facilitators of learning routines within the construction context [73]: ‗people-centered approaches‘, which center on the human and organizational aspects, and ‗technological approaches‘, which focus on the development of information and communication technology (ICT) tools. Based on the evidence derived from the literature review, this study defines two governance mechanism constructs. Organizational mechanisms (OM) include both formal and informal governance mechanisms that provide leadership and strategic guidance for KM implementation and learning [4, 27, 28, 42, 45]. Technological mechanisms (TM) enable the development and application of ICT systems [4, 26-

28, 45, 46]. These two constructs are both dependent and independent variables during the test of inter-dependent relationships within a learning system. This study used the scales developed by Chen and Fong [29] to measure the two constructs. A five-point Likert-type scale (from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) was applied to indicate the strength of organizational and technological governance mechanisms, as presented in Appendix A.

## 3.4.3 Business performance (BP) construct

The balanced scorecard (BSC) [74] has been adopted by construction firms for strategic review and planning and, in particular, for the evaluation of KM performance [75]. In this study, the performance outcome construct (i.e., BP) was operationalized by scales developed by Chen and Fong [29]. The scales measured performance from the four perspectives of the BSC [74]. As presented in Appendix A, the 5-point scale (1 = much less, 5 = much more) allowed respondents to indicate firms‘ performance level related to the average level of their immediate competitors. Relative measures have been widely used in empirical studies [e.g., 76]. Following Richard et al.‘s suggestion [77], this study used fully subjective self-reported measures to address latent performance constructs directly; in the meantime, selected well-informed respondents reduced the psychological biases faced by the measures.

## 3.5 Dynamic hypotheses and simulation assumptions of the SD analysis

The SD model was built in congruence with the theoretical framework, which is in line with the organic perspective on strategy [9]. The model seeks to reflect the strategic change process in organizational learning [13, 23]. It posits that 1) an organizational learning system is a nonlinear, network feedback system; 2) the system uses both positive feedback that produces instability, and negative feedback that produces stability; 3) long-term outcomes of the system emerge from detailed interactions between elements (i.e. governance mechanisms and learning routines) of the system; 4) as the system adapts to its environment, the outcomes are in effect prescribed within given limits by the environment; 5) the emergent state of the elements within the system is predictable – knowing the environment change and knowing the resource endowments for

Building upon the evidence derived by the hypothesis-testing approaches in the survey study described in Section 4, the dynamic hypotheses specifically propose that governance mechanisms, learning routines and performance outcomes form a learning system that drives the evolution of KMC. Within the system, learning routines not only mediate in themselves to form learning cycles in sequential phases, but also mediate between internal governance mechanisms and performance outcomes that reflect the stimuli of external forces. Organizational learning is derived and enabled by governance mechanisms, and in turn shapes the configuration of governance mechanisms. In line with the evolutionary perspective of DCV [3, 7, 21, 25] learning routines carry out learning in time, respond to and create changes, and continually co-align a firm‘s knowledge resources and KMC with the environment. Performance outcomes indicate the quality of the co-alignment with the external environment, affecting learning routines (directly or indirectly) on a continual basis, or through unique historical events [3, 7, 8, 21, 25]. Therefore, performance evaluation needs to capture development and change over time.

The SD analysis attempted to simulate how the high and low KMC firms identified by the survey study reach their full KMC and BP potential, and to reveal differences in the evolutionary path of the KMC and BP of the two types of firms. With the primary objective of identifying the basic structure of the learning system, the SD simulation was carried out under the following assumed conditions: 1) firms with different KMC levels (e.g., ‗high‘ or ‗low‘ as defined by this study) continue to operate in the same market environment; 2) their governance mechanisms and learning routines are driven only by the organizational desire to improve performance through interactive relations of learning, in other words, re-investment into the governance mechanisms and learning routines is entirely dependent on the stimuli of performance enhancement; 3) there is no delay in policy formulation and implementation; and 4) performance outcomes are reviewed annually, thus the simulation time unit was set as one financial year. Clear identification of the basic structure of the learning system is the first step in simulating a learning system. With evidence of the basic structure, future studies will enable the testing of delays and shorter-term review cycles in the strategic review process [23], based on further insights derived from qualitative research approaches.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusion

## 6.1 Theoretical implications

This study fulfilled the four research objectives and answered the five research questions. The

analysis has derived empirical evidence that supports the theoretical framework. Based on the

integrated view of knowledge-based dynamic capability, the study re-conceptualized the KMC

concept to facilitate the evaluation of KM performance from an organic perspective on strategy [9]. The survey study findings endorse learning routines and governance mechanisms as microfoundations of KMC. KMC has a positive impact on performance outcomes, thus requiring strategic attention and conscious management. The KM performance index was developed to measure the state of KMC, its underlying micro-foundations, and the performance outcomes of the capability-based KM strategy at a specific time. The analysis quantified the key elements of the learning system and provided evidence of the essential influence of organizational governance mechanisms and knowledge dissemination on the heterogeneity of KMC. The proposition and testing of the fine-grained relationships among the micro-foundations of KMC and performance stimuli confirmed the existence of feedback loops in the learning system. The findings offer insights into how the micro-foundations of KMC coordinate a firm‘s major goals and learning to develop a KM strategy that responds to environmental changes.

Building upon the evidence derived from the mechanistic KM performance evaluation approaches, the SD model was designed and simulated to incorporate organic epistemological assumptions on time, flow, and construct coupling in the KM strategy. The simulation of the basic structure of the learning system visualized the non-linear relationships among key elements of the system, when time is taken into consideration. The simulation demonstrated the feasibility

Organizational mechanisms and learning routines are firm-specific and depend on the unique

historical evolution of firms [3]. The complex interdependence of formal and informal

mechanisms and the complementarities of learning routines are usually ambiguous [33, 41, 42],

and their performance implications are equivocal, even for internal employees [36]. In addition,

the causal ambiguity arises partially because organizational mechanisms often influence learning

behaviors in a tacit and subtle way [3], and strategic learning is cognitively complex [43].

In line with these assertions, the analysis of the KM performance index found that

heterogeneity of KMC can, to a large degree, be attributed to the variance in organizational

mechanisms and knowledge dissemination, which are stronger cognitive components of KMC

according to Chow and Chan [92]. The path dependence, causal ambiguity and tacitness of

governance mechanisms and learning routines are essential sources of the imperfect imitability of

KMC. In sum, the resource constraints and imperfect imitability of KMC provide an explanation,

to a certain degree, of the persistent gaps in KMCI, BPI and KMI between high and low KMC

## 6.2 Managerial implications

This study developed an organic approach for reviewing and planning KM strategy. The organic evaluation approach has the capacity to 1) review the key elements of the learning system that underpin the KM strategy and performance achieved at a specific historical time, and 2) predict the change and emergence of the learning system and its potential effect in responding to external stimuli in a future timeframe. The organic evaluation approach thus helps firms to deal with sensing, evaluating and planning the changes required by strategy formulation [9]. The analysis demonstrated the methods to evaluate KM performance at a specific time and predict the performance implications of KMC over a certain period. The measurement scale developed by this study can be used to measure the KMI of any large or medium-sized construction contracting firm operating in Hong Kong, through a firm-based survey. The assessment helps individual firms to identify the pros and cons of governance mechanisms and learning routines, and make strategic modifications. The index also helps the construction sector to benchmark the KM performance of firms through a survey conducted in this sector. As a dynamic evaluation tool, the SD model enables firms to predict KMC evolution based on the KMI assessment at any particular historical time. The SD simulation realizes and visualizes the potential change and emergence of the governance mechanisms and routines, as well as likely effects of the change and emergence within the timeframe of strategic planning. The simulation analysis thus enables firms to formulate a realizable KM strategy, which helps to achieve a sustainable capability alignment with its dynamic market environment. In general, the organic KM performance evaluation approach encourages firms to adopt an evolutionary perspective in implementing KM, and to align their KMC with the needs of the evolving market more effectively, rather than making choices narrowly based on information at a specific historical time.

## 6.3 Research limitation

This study has two primary limitations. First, the study was undertaken within the empirical context of one type of firm (construction contractors) operating in one geographical location (Hong Kong). Thus contextual factors, such as degree of environmental turbulence, are controlled. Second, the study only confirmed the basic structure of a learning system.

## 6.4 Future research

Future studies are needed to validate the theoretical framework with broader empirical contexts and fine-tune the structures of the learning system. Future research may study the emergence and evolution of KMC with a larger sample composed of different types of firms from multiple industries. The sample could facilitate research on the variance in contextual factors and their impact on the dynamics of the learning system. A study of larger scope would yield more insights into the KM strategy evolution and evaluation in diverse business environments.

The authors have designed future studies to acquire further evidence from the real-life contexts of construction firms. A multiple case study design will be adopted to obtain insights about resource availability, decision-making delays, and meso-level review cycles. The investigation requires a mixed-methods case study design that combines documentation review, interviews, focus group discussion and survey, as well as SD modeling. The authors expect that the case study approach will provide further evidence from a sample of high and low KMC firms.
