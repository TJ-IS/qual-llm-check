# User engagement in e-government systems implementation: A comparative case study of two Singaporean e-government initiatives

Calvin M.L. Chan <sup>\*</sup>, Shan L. Pan

c/o: Dr. S.L. Pan, Dept. of Information System, School of Computing, National University of Singapore, Singapore 117543, Singapore Available online 29 January 2008

## Abstract

Noting that user engagement is a key issue in e-government systems implementation and that the actual form and strategies of user engagement, especially within the context of e-government, is poorly defined, this paper intends to contribute to the research and practice of e-government systems implementation by conducting a comparative case study of two egovernment systems implementation within a single government agency in Singapore. The comparative analysis of these two e-government systems implementation was accomplished through using stakeholder theory as a sense-making theoretical lens. This generated four findings pertaining to the form and strategies of user engagement in e-government systems implementation.

## 1. Introduction

The need to engage users is among the key issues raised in e-government systems implementation (Carter and Belanger, 2005; Evans and Yen, 2006; Tan and Pan, 2003; Tan et al., 2005). User engagement in information systems (IS) implementation has been a significant area of research since the 1960s (Markus and Mao, 2004). While most research acknowledge that user engagement is often associated with successful implementation (e.g. Hunton and Beeler, 1997; Jiang et al., 2006), the actual form and strategies of user engagement remains poorly defined (Cavaye, 1995; Markus and Mao, 2004). Indeed, IS implementation failure continues to be rampant (Pan, 2005; Wastell, 1999), with billions of dollars being lost annually (Genus and Dalcher, 2003). Peculiarly, deficiency in user engagement is still a key contributor of failure (Hornik et al., 2003; Lyytinen and Robey, 1999; Pan, 2005). With recent development in the practice of IS implementation such as outsourcing (Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003), global IS implementation (Kirsch, 2004; Kirsch and Haney, 2006) and e-government (Chan et al., forthcoming; Heeks, 2006; Pan et al., 2006) indelibly reshaping traditional IS implementation context and processes, Markus and Mao (2004) has issued a call for more in-depth exploration of user engagement in IS implementation. This research can be perceived as a respond to this call as it aspires to examine the actual form and strategies of user engagement in e-government systems implementation. More importantly, this research intends to inform the research and practice of e-government systems implementation by drawing lessons on user engagement in e-government systems implementation through a comparative case study of two e-government systems implementation at a government agency in Singapore. This focus on Singapore is given further relevance as Singapore has been consistently ranked among the best for its e-government eforts by a variety of league tables (Accenture, 2006; EIU, 2004; UN, 2005). Thus, there is ground to believe that insightful lessons on user engagement in e-government systems implementation can be gained through this Singapore based research.

Stakeholder theory was employed as the theoretical lens to further structure this research. Stakeholder theory has been upheld by a number of e-government researchers as a promising theoretical lens for developing the nascent e-government phenomenon (Flak and Rose, 2005; Scholl, 2001) as governments essentially operate within a complex stakeholder environment where their performance are often scrutinised and held accountable by various stakeholders. Furthermore, e-government initiatives are often a result of public sector reform where government agencies are increasingly run like private businesses with the emphasis on treating citizens as ‘customers’ (Flak and Rose, 2005; Gronlund and Horan, 2004; Heeks, 2006). These accentuations in the implementation of e-government system tally well with stakeholder theory, which is essentially about the management of stakeholders. Stakeholder theory has also been proven to be a fruitful analytical lens for the study of e-government systems implementation (Tan et al., 2005).

The next section will first ofer an elaboration on e-government systems implementation with a focus on user engagement. This is followed by a brief review on the stakeholder theory. The research approach will then be described. Before discussing the findings, the two cases of e-government systems implementation will be presented. Finally, the conclusion and implications of this research will then be drawn.

## 2. e-Government systems implementation and user engagement

e-Government can be simply defined as the use of information technology (IT) to facilitate the business of government (Moon, 2002; UN and ASPA, 2002). Although the term e-government only emerged in the late 1990s, governments are one of the earliest adopters of IT (Bozeman and Bretschneider, 1986; Kraemer and Perry, 1979). This can be traced to the genesis in the history of IT (Agar, 2003). The focus of these early applications of IT was on the automation of internal backend operations (Gronlund and Horan, 2004). Nevertheless, with the emerging popularity of the Internet, governments also began to experiment with it to transform their external dealings with the citizens (Davison et al., 2005; Ho, 2002; Tan and Pan, 2003). Through the implementation of e-government systems, it is posited that governments can transform their stereotypical image of being bureaucratic and inflexible to a new image of being customer oriented and eficiency conscious (Devadoss et al., 2002; Mecella and Batini, 2001).

Even as the research of e-government systems implementation is considered to be nascent (Chan et al., forthcoming; Gronlund and Horan, 2004), with most study being rhetorical rather than empirical (Hinnant and O’Looney, 2003; West, 2004), governments across the world have already committed billions of tax payers’ dollars to the implementation of various e-government systems (AFP, 2003; UN and ASPA, 2002). Such hefty investments are often inspired by the promised benefits of greater customer orientation, better eficiency, improved efectiveness (Devadoss et al., 2002; Watson and Mundy, 2001) and reduced bureaucracy (Fountain, 2001). Nevertheless, these promised benefits remained persistently elusive in most e-government systems implementation (Ke and Wei, 2004; Heeks, 2006).

In the literature on e-government systems implementation, one of the pertinent issues that has been raised is the need to engage users of e-government systems (Carter and Belanger, 2005; Evans and Yen, 2006; Tan and Pan, 2003; Tan et al., 2005). This can be understood at both a strategic as well as an operational level. At a strategic level, most e-government systems implementation is instigated by the New Public Management movement which emphasis the need to reform governments towards being customer oriented (Box et al., 2001). As e-government systems are implemented to ofer enhanced customer orientation, it will be imprudent to implement these systems without conducting any genuine user engagement activities. At an operational level, the success and institutionalisation of any e-government systems is ultimately contingent upon the willingness of its users to adopt and use the systems (Evans and Yen, 2006). Carter and Belanger (2005) have found that users’ intention to utilise an e-government systems is dependent on the level users’ trust, the compatibility between the systems and the users’ values, beliefs, experiences and needs, and the perceived ease of use of the systems. Research has also suggested that all these conditions can be achieved through proper user engagement during e-government systems implementation (Tan and Pan, 2003; Tan et al., 2005). Moreover, unlike most conventional information systems, e-government systems usually boast a much larger user base (Chan et al., forthcoming). It is also generally harder to control the participation of these users as compared to the organizational based users of conventional information systems. This is especially relevant in countries where distrust exists between the citizen and their government (Tolbert and Mossberger, 2006). Notwithstanding, the actual form and strategies of user engagement in e-government systems implementation remain to be conceptualized.

## 3. Stakeholder theory

Stakeholder theory deals with the managerial behaviour in response to stakeholders, who are defined as anyone who can afect or are afected by the actions of an organization. Its development can be traced to the seminal tome of Freeman (1984), where strategies and processes for handling stakeholders are specified. Over the past two decades since Freeman (1984), stakeholder theory has been further developed to encompass the twin focus of stakeholder identification (Frooman, 1999; Jawahar and McLaughlin, 2001; Mitchell et al., 1997; Savage et al., 1991) and stakeholder management (Friedman and Miles, 2002; Frooman, 1999; Rowley, 1997; Savage et al., 1991).

The need to identify a key group of stakeholders is due to the limitation of resources. As such, not all stakeholders can be attended. An eficient allocation of resources to cater to the requirements and concerns of the most salient stakeholders can only be attained through stakeholder identification. Early research into stakeholder identification generally centred on dividing stakeholders into primary and secondary categories (Nutt and Backof, 1992). Primary stakeholders are those crucial for survival where a failure to retain their participation would result in dire consequences (Clarkson, 1995). Reflecting the strategic significance of primary stakeholders, terms like ‘critical stakeholders’ and ‘strategic stakeholders’ are often used synonymously (Turnbull, 1997). On the other hand, secondary stakeholders are those who can influence or afect, or are influenced or afected by the actions of the organization. However, there is an absence of direct transactions and thus they are not essential for survival.

To further develop the concept of stakeholder identification, Mitchell et al. (1997) focused on the attributes of stakeholders and proposed a framework where the identification of salient stakeholders is based on facets such as the moral legitimacy of a stakeholder’s claim, the stakeholder’s power to influence the organization, and the urgency of the stakeholder’s claim. Thus, any stakeholder who scores highly on all three facets possesses higher saliency than those with lower scores. In addition, Mitchell et al. (1997) also suggested that stake holder saliency is a dynamic, time-sensitive social construct.

Frooman (1999) and Jawahar and McLaughlin (2001) have also contributed to the development of the stakeholder identification concept by fusing it with resource dependence principles. Resource dependency theory postulates that organizations resemble open systems where survival is dependent on other entities within the same environment (Yuchtman and Seashore, 1967). The extent of this dependency is directly related to the importance and degree of control the external entities have over the required resource (Pfefer and Salancik, 1978). Extending this resource dependency theory to stakeholder identification, it is suggested that more attention should be paid to stakeholders who control resources that are critical for survival (Alge et al., 1999).

Once the stakeholders are identified, stakeholder management will set in. Diferent approaches can be adopted to deal with each group of salient stakeholders. Some of the stakeholder management approaches noted in the literature are proactive, accommodative, defensive and reactive strategies (Clarkson. 1995: Jawa: har and McLaughlin, 2001; Wartick and Cochran, 1985). Among these four strategies, proactive and accommodative strategies are more resource intensive as more eforts must be expended to attend to the stakeholders’ concerns. In a sense, proactive and accommodative strategies are particularly tantalizing to stakeholders, whilst defensive and reactive are least attractive. Savage et al. (1991) however took a diferent approach by proposing that the management of stakeholders should be based on their capacity and willingness to threaten the focal organization. This is done through the assessment of the likelihood that stakeholders will be adversarial or cooperative in their behaviours toward the focal organization. The authors then introduced four diferent management strategies of collaborate, involve, defend and monitor. The first two strate gies are to be deployed when the potential for cooperation with stakeholders is high and the remaining two strategies are to be used when there is a high potential threat of adversarial behaviour from the stakeholders.

Several scholars have also pointed out that stakeholder relationships do not actually occur in a vacuum of dyadic ties between an entity and each of its stakeholders, but rather in a network of influence, i.e., stakeholders are likely to have direct relationships with one another (Freeman and Evan, 1990; Rowley, 1997; Wolfe and Putler, 2002). As a result, organizations should not respond to each stakeholder individually, but should be prepared to deal with multiple inter-related stakeholders concurrently.

## 4. Research approach

## 4.1. Data collection

Data collection occurred in a Singapore government agency that has implemented two e-government systems with varying degree of adoption rate. For reason of confidentiality, the identity of this agency and the two e-government systems are assigned the pseudonyms of SINGA, SINGA-ALPHA and SINGA-BETA, respectively. An advantage of this comparative study is that the implementation of the two e-government systems occurred within the same organization and therefore minimised possible contextual and organizational diferences which might have influenced the dynamics of the implementation.

Interviews were conducted with members of the development team (who are responsible for systems development) and the operational team (who took over the ownership of the systems upon the launch and are responsible for day-to-day operations) to elicit an understanding of the actions and processes they applied throughout the implementation. Key interviewees were identified based on their involvement in the design, development or maintenance of the systems. During the semi-structured interviews, questions on the rationales behind the conceptualization of the e-services were first raised, followed by specific questions on how the interviewees attempted to identify and engage the relevant stakeholders. Lastly, the interviewees were asked to appraise the overall success of the system. Information on the dificulties faced during the implementation, such as engagement of stakeholders, benefits attained from the implementation and future enhancement of the systems, was also obtained.

A series of meetings and formal interviews were also conducted with senior managers and directors of different branches and divisions in SINGA to solicit additional data on the strategic considerations in implementing the two e-government systems. In addition, interviews with external users were also conducted to complement the interviews with SINGA personnel. There were a total of 24 interviewees and all interviews were transcribed. The afiliation of the interviewees is summarised in Table 1. Additional data were also collated from trade magazines, newspapers and newsletters to further supplement the interview data.

## 4.2. Data analysis

The data on the two implementations were analysed for similar ideas on user engagement by using the stakeholder theory as a theoretical sense making lens (Langley, 1999; Strauss and Corbin, 1998) coupled with literatures on e-government systems implementation and user engagement. Conceptual labels were assigned to describe similar ideas. There was no plan to generate any specific number of these conceptual labels although there was conscious attempt to avoid generating too many similar conceptual labels. When such similarity arises, they were rationalised and merged into one unifying conceptual label. This resulted in four conceptual labels, i.e. ‘Engagement Timing/Duration/Frequency’, ‘Inducing Participation’, ‘Soliciting Inputs’ and ‘Testing/Training Participation’. Following comparative research design (Miles and Huberman, 1994; Yin, 1994), cross-case analysis were then carried out by comparing and contrasting the two e-government systems implementation to examine the similarities and diferences between the user engagement approaches adopted in each case to generate the findings. The analysis result was also sent to the case agency for review and comments.

## 5. Case description

## 5.1. SINGA-ALPHA implementation

The targeted users of SINGA-ALPHA are essentially foreigners intending to study in Singapore as they need to register themselves with SINGA and get the necessary legal permit to stay in Singapore. Prior to SINGA-ALPHA, prospective foreign students would usually need to make multiple trips to SINGA to submit his/her application and numerous other supporting documents. During the annual peak period, the processing of these applications may take as long as five weeks. Although In-Principle Approval letters (IPA) would be issued to the students when their applications are being processed, delays in the application process would still lead to problems for the institutions of learning (IL) as foreign students would have to withdraw their candidature if their application is subsequently rejected. An admission oficer from an IL highlighted the situation: ‘‘During peak periods, they could not issue permits on time. Sometimes, I even had to visit [SINGA] to plead with them to issue the permits on time.”

In view of the government strategic plan government to develop Singapore into an education hub and increase the population of foreign students by another 10%, the SINGA-ALPHA project manager explained the rationale for the system: ‘‘We needed to improve our service and portray ourselves as a well-organized agency ready to move forward and meet or even exceed customers’ expectations.” It was hoped that through SINGA-ALPHA, SINGA would be able to trim manpower, reduce the number of trips make to SINGA by applicants, and reduce the processing time for the permits.

Instead of directly engaging foreign students in implementing SINGA-ALPHA, SINGA decided to concentrate on the ILs. An oficer from SINGA shared: ‘‘It will be dificult and unwieldy to involve the foreign students . . . New applicants are stationed overseas. They are also unfamiliar with the process. Those who have applied before and are in Singapore may not be interested since they’ve already gotten their permits and won be using the system again soon.” Consequently, SINGA roped in the ILs as a surrogate to get the user require ments. The project manager mentioned: ‘‘ILs were the one liaising directly with the students. So they can rep resent the feedback and requirements of students . . . If we can meet the requirements of the ILs, we can meet the requirements of the students.” Moreover, a manager in SINGA also revealed a perception that: ‘‘Foreign stu dents are a more captive audience so we just leave it to the ILs to inform them of [SINGA-ALPHA] . . . No training or publicity was done for the students . . . Furthermore, it makes a lot of sense for these students to submit via [SINGA-ALPHA] as it’s simpler and faster.”

As soon as it was decided that ILs will be included in implementing SINGA-ALPHA, SINGA immediately got in touch with them. A member of the development team recalled: ‘‘We informed the ILs that we’ve decided to go ahead even before we really started the project . . . we talked to them regarding the development of [SINGA-ALPHA] and the cooperation needed to build a common interface.”

Through consultation with the ILs, SINGA discovered that much repetition and redundancy existed in the traditional application process. For instance, a student’s particulars had to be entered twice: once into the IL’s system and then again into SINGA’s backend system. The project manager remarked: ‘‘If I were to register

Besides merely automating existing processes, the reengineering of the application process was proposed. Instead of having applicants visit SINGA, it was suggested that the ILs’ student admission process should be integrated with SINGA’s permit application process. In the proposed new workflow, the task of printing and issuing IPAs, as well as the verification of applicants’ documents, would be handled by the ILs. Therefore, a foreign student who succeeded in his/her application to one of the ILs would now receive the IPA together with the acceptance letter from the IL. Meantime, the particulars of successful applicants would be automatically uploaded onto SINGA-ALPHA. The actual processing of an application will only begin after the applicant had accepted an ofer for a place in the IL by login onto and indi cating this on SINGA-ALPHA. The entire application process would be completed and the permit issued when the applicant finally arrives in Singapore and presents an endorsed medical report to SINGA. This new workflow would slash processing time from five weeks to between 3 and 10 days. In addition, the applicant would only need to make one visit to SINGA. A SINGA manager detailed the benefits of SINGA-ALPHA: ‘‘It could handle a high workload, and reduce processing time and crowd volume at [SINGA]. More importantly, ILs and foreign students can all experience a straightforward, convenient and eficient system.”

However, the ILs were initially wary about taking over some of the work traditionally done by SINGA. For example a senior oficer at an IL shared: ‘‘What will happen if a prospective student didn’t turn up and pass the IPA to a friend? Passport can be fabricated to match the details on the IPA so as to gain illegal entry to Singapore. Since I’m the one issuing the IPA, will I be held responsible?”

Thus, efort was made to address the ILs’ concerns and measures were made to seek their commitment to SINGA-ALPHA. SINGA had to convince the ILs of the possible benefits by participating in the implementation of SINGA-ALPHA. A member of the operational team explained: ‘‘It’s really a matter of coaxing and enticing ILs with the prospect that their process would be speeded up and students would have fewer problems with their applications.” Besides highlighting the possible operational benefits, SINGA also stressed on developing a strategic convergence of interest with the ILs by emphasising the common interest that they shared in advancing the government’s long term strategic plan to develop Singapore into a premier education hub. The project manager commented: ‘‘Both [SINGA] and ILs shared the common interest in making Singapore into an education hub . . . They were helping us, helping the government by attracting foreign students to Singapore . . . They will also be benefiting from this.”

SINGA also adopted a give-and-take approach when dealing with ILs during the implementation. For instance, when some of the ILs requested for printers to print IPA letters, SINGA agreed and gave each IL new sets of computers, printers and even reams of paper. The project manager explained: ‘‘We cannot just do a system and then impose it on them. We didn’t want this type of working relationship . . . We discussed a lot with the ILs, to work out and formulate how best everyone can work together and meet everybody needs . . . There’s a lot of give-and-take, like we are providing the PCs for them to do the registration because we are actually getting them to help us with screening applications and verifying documents.”

An admission oficer with an IL also disclosed: ‘‘Initially, they wanted the ILs to collect application fees and students’ passport . . . Then we said, ‘‘We can’t be doing this.” I think all the ILs raised the concern that we don’t want to be the custodian for hundreds of passports and be responsible for it. This should be [SINGA]’s job. [So] eventually it was narrowed down and we don’t have to collect money nor passports.”

The project manager of SINGA-ALPHA felt that such a give-and-take approach has helped in developing the ILs’ commitment to SINGA-ALPHA and ofered this as evidence: ‘‘We did not demand that the ILs participate in all progress meetings. But most of the time, they were present, sometimes even bringing in three or four people to the meetings!”

The ILs were also involved in the testing of the prototype prior to the launch of SINGA-ALPHA. The purpose of this was to ensure that the final system matched the requirements and expectations of the ILs. In addition, classroom style user training sessions were conducted for the ILs’ admission oficers who will actually be using the system. User manuals were also prepared by SINGA and given to the ILs for their future reference. The project manager commented: ‘‘We trained them on how to check if an application has been uploaded successfully . . . We explained various issues to them.”

However, the foreign students were never included in any of the training. Instead, the task of training the foreign students was delegated to the ILs. A number of ILs did so by preparing a step-by-step guide for students.

Since its launch, SINGA-ALPHA has consistently handled more than 90% of the overall applications. The system is also well recognised to be a successful implementation by various parties involved. A SINGA Director noted: ‘‘SINGA-ALPHA has been a very good and successful story. I think it’s because it is so convenient to use . . . It’s a win-win situation for all three parties (SINGA, ILs and students).” An admission oficer from an IL concurred: ‘‘From the customer service point of view, SINGA-ALPHA is something I would say is really good in the sense that it has shortened processing time . . . the foreign students are happy with the application process and with us.”

Even after the initial launch of SINGA-ALPHA, the ILs continued to raise new requirements to SINGA. An admission oficer in an IL described one such instance: ‘‘My staf noticed that the system couldn’t sort the results in certain orders. We felt that the sorting function would be very useful for us. So we asked them to add in the function so that we can sort the results, etc. They agreed with no fuss.”

Furthermore, SINGA also conduct post-mortem exercises with the ILs at the end of each admission period so as to cross-examine problems and issues that may arise in an admission exercise. These feedbacks would form the basis of improvements to be incorporated into the system for the next year. A senior oficer from an IL commented: ‘‘The post-mortem exercises are really good because we could then present the problems we have faced, and more importantly, there is proper follow-up after that . . . They send the minutes listing the things that we raised and what they have done. So we know exactly whether our suggestions are implemented or not and the reasons behind it. This convinces me that for any project, users must feel that they are moving along with the project . . . This convinces me that for any project, users must feel that they are moving along with the project. For the past two years, I have had very fruitful post-mortem meetings with them.”

## 5.2. SINGA-BETA implementation

The targeted users of SINGA-BETA are Singapore citizens who need to apply for certain oficial identification documents from SINGA. An operation manager at SINGA recollected the situation prior to SINGA-BETA: ‘‘In the past, one has to come over to [SINGA] to make an application and then a week later, he/she has to come down again, to collect the identification document.”

The idea for SINGA-BETA can be traced to a Business Process Reengineering (BPR) study conducted in 1998 to identify areas for improvements and boost its overall productivity and service levels. The project manager of SINGA-BETA related the motivation then: ‘‘At [SINGA], we have a broad strategy to reduce, as far as possible, the number of public interfaces. This means reducing the time needed to deal with the public face to face. We also wanted to reduce the number of trips that people need to make to complete a transaction with us to just one. This translates into better convenience for the public.”

External consultants were brought in to lead in the BPR study. Among the recommendations made were for an online system to handle the public applications of the identity documents. For various reasons, the recommended system was not implemented.

Two years after the BPR study, the Singapore Government initiated the e-Government Action Plan to make all government services to be available online. Through this e-Government Action Plan, agencies can apply for central government funding to support their eforts to implement e-government systems. An operational manager of SINGA-BETA described the climate at that time: ‘‘It was during the dot.com boom. Dot.- com was a very popular and sexy word. Everyone was saying that we should try to push everything online.”

It was under such a climate that the decision to implement SINGA-BETA was made. A senior manager expressed that SINGA-BETA: ‘‘is meant to enhance service standards . . . we aimed to give applicants greater flexibility and convenience.” The project manager further elaborated on the intended benefits of SINGA-BETA: ‘‘The amount of attention needed is definitely a lot lower than that of counter-based [application]. You can probably handle 20 plus cases on SINGA-BETA in an hour, but it’s quite unlikely that you can do that over the counter. We wanted to boost up online usage because if we push the bulk of the applications to the internet, then we could have some costsavings such as closing down the counter or cutting back on staf to process the transactions.”

During the design of SINGA-BETA, one of the key challenges was the online submission of a digital photograph. The photograph needs to be of a certain resolution, which may not be easy for a lay-person to produce. The operational manager explained: ‘‘. . . if you take a digital photo of yourself, you may have to crop it yourself and then do touch-up to it. [However,] if you go to a photo studio to take a photo, it can be exactly the size that we require.” Moreover, it was thought that the photo studios can also act as an intermediary for those citizens who may not be IT-savvy to transact using SINGA-BETA. A SINGA oficer remarked: ‘‘Not everyone uses the Internet or owns a PC . . . There are groups of people you cannot reach if you ofer only e-services.”

Another key challenge was overcoming the security concerns in allowing online submission of files attachments as malicious software virus can easily be uploaded into SINGA-BETA and infiltrate SINGA’s IT infrastructure. Understandably, this became a more urgent challenge than the digital photograph and thus became a central focus throughout the implementation of SINGA-BETA. Comparatively, the digital photograph issue was sidelined and eforts to engage photo studios and the citizens only occurred in the later stages of implementation, when the technical solution to counter the security concerns was found. The operational manager of SINGA-BETA described the process of engaging the photo studios: ‘‘We invited members of the [photo] studio association for briefing before the project launch . . . We wanted to alert them that it could afect their business . . . We wanted to let them know our digital specifications so that they could consider rolling out digital photos and helping customers go on board [SINGA-BETA.]”

To further entice the photo studios to participate, SINGA also arranged for participating photo studios to be on a reward scheme that was administered by another government agency. Through this scheme, photo studios would be paid a token fee for every successful transaction on SINGA-BETA. However, the response from the studios was lukewarm and only a few studios registered for the scheme. The SINGA-BETA project manager explained: ‘‘Some of them are still not into digital photos. They don’t have digital cameras, personal computers or broadband connection. So they are not receptive to [SINGA-BETA]. . . Today, I would say that we have only managed to operate on a very small scale.”

None of the photo studios or the citizens was involved in the testing of SINGA-BETA as well. The operational manager shared: ‘‘It was our staf that actually tried out the system and gave their assessments as to which features were missing. We assessed how well they used the system in terms of user-friendliness.”

SINGA-BETA was finally launched after several rounds of testing. Training for photo studios was only limited to a briefing session and a self-help CD from SINGA. Although there was no training conducted for the citizens, considerable eforts was invested in publicising SINGA-BETA to the citizens. SINGA oficers were sent to relevant community fairs and road-shows to promote SINGA-BETA to potential users. A member of the development team explained: ‘‘We wanted to create awareness among the people. They may not need the documents straightaway, but at least we can tell them that they can actually get it online when they need it later.”

As more citizens transact on SINGA-BETA, some of them also voluntarily sent their feedback to SINGA. Some of these have led to subsequent enhancement of the system. The project manager commented: ‘‘People who transacted on [SINGA-BETA] gave their feedback, and much of it has proven useful. Many service requests were raised in response to the feedback.”

An example of such enhancements was the inclusion of a photograph editing function so that SINGA oficers could edit the submitted photographs to the required quality. Even though SINGA had already predicted possible issue pertaining to the photograph attachment, it certainly did not expect an overwhelming percentage of submitted digital photographs to fail the requirement. Through feedback from the public, SINGA realised the shortcoming and took immediate actions to rectify it. A member of the development team described the remedial action: ‘‘With the photo-editing software installed onto our processing system, our oficers would try to salvage the photos that may not meet our requirements. In this way, we could improve the acceptance rate.”

Although the implementation of SINGA-BETA was considered to be a success in SINGA, a manager noted that: ‘‘The usage rate is still not ideal”.

## 6. Discussion of findings

## 6.1. Engagement of salient intermediary in e-government systems implementation

The end users of SINGA-ALPHA and SINGA-BETA were prospective foreign students and local citizens, respectively. Although the relationships between SINGA and each of these respective end users mirrors a dyadic tie, SINGA was seen to have attempted the engagement of other intermediaries during the respective egovernment systems implementation. This corresponded with stakeholder theory, which noted that stakeholder relationships do not actually occur in exclusive dyadic ties between an entity and its stakeholders but occur within a web of other stakeholders (Freeman and Evan, 1990; Rowley, 1997; Wolfe and Putler, 2002). More significantly, it is found that the choice of the respective intermediaries can be explained through stakeholder identification (Frooman, 1999; Jawahar and McLaughlin, 2001; Mitchell et al., 1997; Savage et al., 1991).

In the case of SINGA-ALPHA, the success of SINGA-ALPHA implementation was considered to be dependent on the cooperation of ILs. As prospective student users were all stationed overseas it was almost impossible to get them to participate in the implementation. Even if it was possible to recruit their participation, it is unlikely to be fruitful as they lack experience on the application process. It was also unlikely to get students who have applied for the permits to participate as they already obtained their permit and do not need to use the system again. In contrast, the ILs would have been providing support to their foreign students in applying for the permits over the years and would thus possess a better understanding of the application process. This would render them as more suitable candidate to participate in the implementation process. Moreover, with SINGA-ALPHA, ILs are now required to handle most of the paperwork. These include verifying the authenticity of the applicant’s supporting document and the issuance of the IPA. ILs are also required to train new students to use SINGA-ALPHA. As such, it would be impossible to successfully implement SINGA-ALPHA without the active participation of the ILs.

In the case of SINGA-BETA, the success of SINGA-BETA appeared to be dependent on the participation of the photo studios. Although the participation of the citizenry end users could have aforded a better understanding of their requirements, the complexity involved in uploading a digital photograph of acceptable quality coupled with the fact that not all the citizens (i.e. prospective users) are IT-savvy would have rendered the active participation of the photo studios to be beneficial in boosting the ultimate take up rate of SINGA-BETA.

Based on such an analysis, a finding on user engagement in e-government systems implementation is the engagement of salient intermediary. As observed in the implementation of SINGA-ALPHA, it may not be possible to directly engage the end users in every e-government system implementation. The presence of a digital divide may also accentuate the dificulty to directly engage all relevant end users in every e-government system. Even in cases when it is possible to directly engage the participation of the end users, the engagement of salient intermediaries may help in further boosting the take up rate of e-government system implementation. This can be seen in the case of SINGA-BETA.

However, e-government systems typically boast a highly varied user base (Chan et al., forthcoming) and identifying relevant intermediary may not necessarily be a straight forward afair as many user groups may all lay claim as potential intermediary. Stakeholder identification posits that attention should be assigned to the most salient stakeholders, who can be identified based on the degree of dependency between the entity and the stakeholders (Alge et al., 1999; Frooman, 1999; Jawahar and McLaughlin, 2001). In the case of SINGA-ALPHA, the success of SINGA-ALPHA was directly dependent on the participation of the ILs. Although the dependency between the success of SINGA-BETA and the photo studios was not as striking as that between the success of SINGA-ALPHA and the ILs, the participation of photo studios would nonetheless have helped to alleviate the take up rate of SINGA-BETA to some degree. Hence, it is suggested that salient intermediary can be identified from among the varied user base by conducting a stakeholder analysis based on the principles of dependency.

## 6.2. Inculcating strategic convergence of interest in e-government systems implementation

Stakeholder theory has traditionally emphasised the need to actively engage salient stakeholders and obtain their commitment (Friedman and Miles, 2002; Frooman, 1999; Rowley, 1997; Savage et al., 1991). Studies on IS implementation have also found that user participation can help to generate user satisfaction and thereby leading to successful implementations (Ives and Olson, 1984; McKeen et al., 1994; McManus, 2004; Tait and Vessey, 1988). Nevertheless, studies examining the actual form and possible strategies for engaging users during IS implementation have seldom been attempted, much less within the context of e-government systems implementation where end users do not necessarily see or identify with the profered values of the e-government systems.

Analysing the implementation of both SINGA-ALPHA and SINGA-BETA, it was found that user engagement in e-government systems implementation is not merely about attracting user participation but inculcating strategic convergence of interest. In the implementation of SINGA-BETA, the financial incentives introduced to attract the participation of photo studios only resulted in lukewarm response. This is not surprising as photo studios do not have a direct vested interest in SINGA-BETA, especially since the earnings from such ad-hoc digital photograph taking jobs only accounted for a very small fraction of their revenue. Moreover, some photo studios have yet to catch up with digital technology and do not possess the requisite capability even if they had wished to participate. Although SINGA tried to attract the participation of the photo studios, the absence of any strong convergence of interest may have led to the lukewarm response.

In contrast, eforts were expended in the implementation of SINGA-ALPHA not only in attracting the participation of the ILs, but also in convincing them to embrace and take ownership of the system. For instance, it was emphasised that both the ILs and SINGA shared the common goal in advancing the government long term strategic plan to establish Singapore as a premier education hub. Moreover, it was also highlighted to the ILs that the implementation of SINGA-ALPHA would stand to benefit them as there will be shorter turnaround time and a reduction in bottlenecks. Concessions were also made to the ILs in addressing some of their concerns and requests such as providing them with printers and papers in printing the IPA. It is thus suggested that the strategic convergence of interest between SINGA and the ILs enabled the ILs to embrace SINGA-ALPHA and even take on ownership of the system as they assumed some of the responsibility previously administered by SINGA.

## 6.3. Coalescing coercion and conviction in e-government systems implementation

A common assertion in stakeholder theory is that the presence of power vested in either the entity or its stakeholders largely will determine the management approach taken to engage stakeholders (Frooman, 1999; Mitchell et al., 1997). In more exact terms, the party that possesses power can impose its will on the other parties through coercive measures. Within the governmental context, government authorities and agencies are usually recognised as the party that possesses power and thus should be able to impose its will on its citizenry subjects. However, systems implementation studies have widely cited that coercive approaches to obligate user participation is a contributing factor to dismal system acceptance rates, and in some cases even resulting in implementation failures (Ives and Olson, 1984). Examples include the London Ambulance Computer Aided Dispatch project which was launched in 1991 and aborted less than a year later (Beynon-Davies, 1995; Wastell and Newman, 1996). The heavy-handed approach undertaken by the authorities to compel user participation and usage, coupled with a lack of consultation, resulted in users feeling alienated, ultimately causing user rejection of the system. It thus appeared that the assertion of stakeholder theory holds contrary to the empirical findings from the systems implementation literature. Nevertheless, the comparative analysis of the SINGA-ALPHA and SINGA-BETA implementations, revealed a possible synthesis of this ostensible contradiction.

The implementation of both SINGA-ALPHA and SINGA-BETA were imposed upon the users and the intermediaries as the decision to implement the respective e-government system was made without prior consultation with these stakeholders. Yet, as illustrated in the analysis of Finding 2, SINGA-ALPHA attained comprehensive acceptance and usage while SINGA-BETA was very well received. Even the conciliatory approach of ofering cash incentives did not translate into satisfactory usage or acceptance rates. Further analysis of the cases revealed a possible explanation for this anomaly. Comparing with SINGA-BETA, more efort was invested in convincing the intermediary in the implementation of SINGA-ALPHA. After coercing the ILs into accepting the implementation of SINGA-ALPHA, SINGA subsequently adopted a more obliging approach through negotiation and making compromise to convince them into embracing the implementation of SINGA-ALPHA and assuming a more active role in the implementation. Such earnest conviction was indistinct in the implementation of SINGA-BETA. Even in establishing the financial incentive scheme, much less persuasive efort was employed in convincing the photo studios to embrace the implementation of SINGA-BETA.

This analysis afirms the assertion of stakeholder theory in that the party possessing power can impose its will on the other parties through coercive measures. However, it also acknowledged the notion that user participation does not necessarily mean user satisfaction and acceptance (McKeen et al., 1994) as has been demonstrated in Finding 2. Change management efort, such as what is suggested in Finding 2, is still subsequently required to convince users into embracing the need for change and drive adoption of new system implementation (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Therefore, government agencies vested with power to coerce user participation must be mindful to temper the authoritarian approach with accommodative measures to convince and mitigate resistance and obtain genuine acceptance of the system. In other words, there must be a coalescing of coer cion and conviction in e-government systems implementation.

## 6.4. Sustained engagement of users in e-government systems implementation

The development life cycle of any information system typically consists of three phases: design, development and maintenance. Of these three phases, maintenance is often given the least attention (Zagal et al., 2002). However, with the increase in system complexity, size and lifespan of information systems, the importance of the maintenance phase is increasingly being emphasised (Gross et al., 1999; Xia and Lee, 2005). Nevertheless, research into the importance of user engagement in the maintenance phase is still lacking (Guinan et al., 1998; Pahl, 2004). In stakeholder theory, although the sustained and continuous engagement of stakeholders has seldom been highlighted, Freeman (1984, p. 171), has recorded the need to ‘constantly evaluate and monitor progress with respect to the strategies that have been developed’ to manage the stakeholders in his seminal tome.

The analysis of SINGA-ALPHA and SINGA-BETA showed that there was sustained engagement of users in the implementation of these e-government systems. In the implementation of SINGA-ALPHA, the ILs continued to raise new requirements which were subsequently incorporated into the system. Post-mortem exercises were held after every admission period to solicit the feedbacks of the ILs. Moreover, it was also felt that these post-mortem meetings have helped the users to strengthen their identification with the system. Although the implementation of SINGA-BETA was not as well received as SINGA-ALPHA, subsequent user engagement eforts during the maintenance phase have nonetheless helped to enhance the situation. For instance, on receiving public feedbacks on the dificulty they encountered with submitting an acceptable digital photograph, the remedial actions taken to address this issue has helped to enhance the acceptance rate of submitted digital photograph.

Thus, it is suggested that sustained engagement of users in e-government systems implementation can help to enhance user acceptance and contribute to its success. This may be especially relevant in the context of e-government as it is often dificult to be exhaustive in directly engaging a considerable proportion of the user base during the earlier stages of system implementation as the user base can at times be as large as the entire population of the country. The presence of sustained user engagement will therefore enable the e-government system to be further fine-tuned to the needs and expectations of the users.

## 7. Conclusion

The engagement of users has often been raised as a key issue in the implementation of e-government systems (Carter and Belanger, 2005; Evans and Yen, 2006; Tan and Pan, 2003; Tan et al., 2005). Sensing that the stakeholder theory is a promising theoretical lens for developing the nascent e-government phenomenon (Flak and Rose, 2005; Scholl, 2001), this study has employed stakeholder theory in conducting a comparative case study of the implementation of SINGA-ALPHA and SINGA-BETA in Singapore. Altogether, four findings were generated to inform the research and practice of e-government systems implementation. A summary of these four findings is provided in Table 3.

The four findings can be viewed as making a novel contribution to the nascent research on e-government, especially on the issue of user engagement in e-government systems implementation. As it may not be possible to directly engage the end users in every e-government system implementation, Finding 1 profers the alternative to engage salient intermediary. Even in cases where the end user can be engaged directly it may still be beneficial to engage salient intermediary. Finding 2 suggests that user engagement in e-government systems implementation is not merely about attracting user participation but inculcating strategic convergence of interest. This is especially relevant in the context of e-government systems implementation where end users do not necessary see value in the e-government systems. Extending Finding 2 and appreciating that participation in the implementation is not equivalent to a genuine acceptance of the system, Finding 3 highlights the need to coalesce coercion and conviction in engaging stakeholder in e-government systems implementation. This is especially applicable in instances where the government agencies are vested with the authority to coerce user participation. Lastly, Finding 4 posited that sustained engagement of users in e-government systems implementation can help to enhance user acceptance and contribute to its success. Given the large user base of most e-government systems, it is usually impossible to engage a considerable proportion of the user base during the earlier implementation stages. Finding 4 thus provide a means to assure that the implemented e-government system will still be fine-tuned to meet the needs of the broader user base.
