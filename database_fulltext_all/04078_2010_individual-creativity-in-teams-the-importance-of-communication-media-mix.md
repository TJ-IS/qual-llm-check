# Individual creativity in teams: The importance of communication media mix

Sherry M.B. Thatcher <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Susan A. Brown

<sup>a</sup> University of Louisville, United States

<sup>b</sup> University of Arizona, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 13 February 2009 Received in revised form 1 December 2009 Accepted 21 March 2010 Available online 27 March 2010

Keywords: Communication media Creativity Demographic differences

## a b s t r a c t

We use compensatory adaptation and dual coding theories to explore the effects of communication media use on creativity. Our <sup>fi</sup>eld study results show that high levels of self-esteem and information-based demographic differences positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence creativity. Social category differences negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uence creativity. Communication media mix is an important moderator, improving the relationship of self-esteem and social category demographic differences with creativity when individuals have proportionally more mediated communication. The relationship between information-based demographic differences and creativity is attenuated when individuals use proportionally more mediated communication. The results have implications for managers encouraging creativity among a diverse workforce using multiple communication media

## 1. Introduction

Creativity is increasingly important to organizations as they attempt to not only earn short-term pro<sup>fi</sup>ts but also develop new and interesting products and services that enable them to survive over the long term [29,63]. One way to foster creativity is through the use of teams. Organizations use teams to facilitate effectiveness and empowerment [58,91] and to realize the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of diversity [91] that can contribute to creativity [69]. Technology advances have made the use of teams even more advantageous as organizations are able to bring together the appropriate people to work on a project, regardless of their locations [47]. The increased use of technology presents interesting opportunities and challenges for supporting creativity in organizations [15]. However, little research has examined the role of mediated communication in enhancing (or hindering) creativity.

Mediated communication refers to communication that takes place using some sort of communication technology. Examples include email, telephone, instant messaging, and video conferencing. Research has demonstrated that mediated communication offers both challenges and opportunities for organizations. On one hand, communication technology enables diverse groups of people to collaborate and make decisions, regardless of their geographic locations [53]. On the other hand, some research suggests that the potential for process losses exists when using mediated communication, particularly for certain types of individuals [21,72]. However, process losses that occur because of mediated communication challenges may actually be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial when the end result is a creative product or process. In addition to creativity stemming from differential mediated communication usage, we explore how differences in demographic characteristics and self-esteem impact individuals working in a team.

Research has suggested that certain personality characteristics such as con<sup>fi</sup>dence and high levels of self-esteem may be correlated with individual creativity [37,55]. Other studies have shown that environmental factors such as empathetic and supportive supervisors [63] and workgroup support [3,83] positively affect individual creativity. Czikszentmihalyi [16] argues that individual creativity does not occur in isolation, but is a result of interactions among people; this argument has found support from organizational researchers studying networks, team-level creativity, and team learning behavior [41,67,73,82] Other research argues for the importance of demographic differences among individuals as a means of enhancing creativity [42,43]. These individual characteristics and demographic differences highlight the important contribution that the organizational environment makes to individual creativity. We extend this line of research by focusing on the role of the mediated communication on individual creativity.

Although the current study investigates the effect of mediated communication, demographic differences, and self-esteem on individual creativity, our main contribution is to examine the direct and indirect roles that communication media mix (i.e., the proportion of mediated and non-mediated communication) has on creativity. We do this by examining how multi-modal communication (i.e., the mix of communication media use) [31] directly and indirectly affects creativity based on compensation adaptation and dual coding theories. Second, we investigate how demographic differences in<sup>fl</sup>uence creativity by distinguishing among different types of diversity using social identity and cognitive resource theories. While previous research has found support for the idea that diversity is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for creativity [10], we believe that not all forms of diversity will affect creativity in the same way, particularly when examined in light of communication media use. These contributions will enable us to extend theory in the areas of multi-modal communication, diversity, and creativity as well as provide speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidance for managers who wish to increase creativity among employees who have access to other employees through both mediated and non-mediated communication.

## 2. Creativity

Creativity is the generation of novel and appropriate ideas, products, processes, or solutions that are useful or appropriate to the situation [3,63,78]. Creativity often involves bringing together pieces of information from different places, recognizing previously unnoticed connections and combining them into something useful to the organization [2]. Despite the importance of creativity for organizations, creativity is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to assess and measure; thus it tends to be understudied, underexplored, and understated.

Creativity is important to study because it is necessary to acknowledge that acts of creativity can and do occur in any workplace environment. One's job or one's occupation does not always determine the extent to which one can be creative. In addition, because creativity is not always externally-driven (e.g., not formally rewarded) and outcomes of the creative process may differ, the drivers of creativity within organizations may come about in surprising ways. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we focus on how individual-level creativity is affected by the mix of communication media use, demographic diversity, and self-esteem. ## 3. Theoretical background

We use a number of theoretical frameworks to develop our hypotheses. Since there is no unifying theoretical framework that explains creativity, it is important to use theories relevant in other domains and apply them to the area of creativity. In addition to the main theories of compensatory adaptation and dual coding that we explain in more detail below, we draw from social identity, social categorization, and cognitive resource theories. Because the theories of social identity, social categorization, and cognitive resources are central only to speci<sup>fi</sup>c hypotheses, we discuss them in the hypothesis development section.

## 3.1. Compensatory adaptation theory

Compensatory adaptation theory (CAT) is based on the idea that human brains are designed for face-to-face communication because this was the only means of communication among humans for thousands of years [50]. The result is that newer forms of communication, such as the telephone, email, instant messaging, and video conferencing, all have limitations in effectively conveying a message. Other theories such as social presence theory [79], media richness theory [17], and adaptive structuration theory [24] have recognized that mediated forms of communication not only hinder the way that people communicate but can also be catalysts for changes in the way that people communicate [51].

Kock, the main proponent of CAT, has developed and tested a number of components of the theory [48–51]. In a recent study, Kock [50] found that users of mediated communication exerted greater cognitive effort and experienced more communication ambiguity than dyads engaging in face-to-face communication. As a result, users of mediated communication experienced low levels of communication <sup>fl</sup>uency. Senders of information spent more time and effort crafting a carefully-worded message in an attempt to counteract the communication ambiguity presented by using mediated communication [50]. Another study investigating the effects of mediated communication on new product development teams found that when teams engaged in effective compensatory adaptation, operationalized as procedural structuring, team effectiveness increased [51]. Thus, as people become more accustomed to the idea of using alternative forms of communication, they devise ways to adapt to the technology so that there are fewer miscommunications. It is interesting to note that the receivers of information sent via mediated communication do not exert more effort in trying to accurately understand messages [50].

Despite the myriad of problems and obstacles that have been associated with mediated communication [19,44] we argue that the ambiguous communications or inaccurate communication <sup>fl</sup>uency that results from using mediated communication may actually lead to increases in individual creativity. Creative solutions to problems have sometimes developed as the result of accidents or misunderstandings [1]. Moreover, studies in cognitive psychology have shown that when individuals do not have all of the information they need to make a conclusion, they “<sup>fi</sup>ll in the blanks” based on their individual knowledge base (c.f., [50]); “<sup>fi</sup>lling in the blanks” may be a crucial way that employees develop creative ideas.

CAT complements traditional understanding of the drivers of creativity. As receivers of information do not spend signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more time compensating for the obstacles presented by mediated communication [50], they have more time to interpret presented information and combine it with existing knowledge which may help in the development of creativity. Some researchers have found that production blocking of new ideas may occur more in face-to-face groups relative to communication-mediated groups precisely because individuals in face-to-face groups spend so much time and attention on visual details [78]. Production blocking occurs when individuals are unable to develop or express their thoughts because they are distracted by activities occurring in their group such as other individuals talking. Since too many social and nonverbal cues may provide irrelevant information and interfere with message content, too much face-to-face communication (as a rich medium) may be detrimental to creativity [89]. Thus, the value of applying CAT to creativity is that the ambiguity and lack of communication <sup>fl</sup>uency inherent in the move away from continual face-to-face interactions provides individuals with opportunities to perceive information in ways different than that in which it was intended.

## 3.2. Dual coding theory

A second main theoretical perspective we draw upon to understand the relationship between communication media use and creativity is dual coding theory. Dual coding theory posits that individuals learn and retain information both through verbal systems and through nonverbal systems [13,64]. While there is an individual preference for how one generally wishes to present and receive information (c.f. [54]), the reality is that individuals present and receive information in a multitude of ways. The use of multiple media allows us to process different facets of information in a variety of ways [56,88]. Thus, receiving information through communication media and in face-to-face situations not only in<sup>fl</sup>uences how one receives information but affects how one perceives information and in<sup>fl</sup>uences the amount of time one engages in communication-related activities.

Using one form of media over another affects the amount of time one spends interacting with others. Creativity is enhanced through both convergent processes (de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an approach that brings together diverse points of view [3]) requiring individual thought, as well as divergent processes (de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a process whereby one analyzes situations from new angles [29]) requiring active communication with others [77]. Individuals in organizational teams interact personally with team members and generally have frequent access to their team members' thoughts and opinions through face-to-face interaction. However, individuals also communicate with one another using mediated communication. Use of non-face-to-face communication media provides individuals more time to ponder the information contained in a mediated interaction [22]. The time not spent in lengthy face-to-face interactions may be used to develop new ideas [77]. What varies between individuals is the amount of time spent in non-face-to-face (i.e., mediated) communications relative to face-toface interactions. Thus, dual coding theory would suggest that because creativity requires that an individual eventually bring divergent ideas together, having a mix of mediated communications and face-to-face interactions is important.

## 4. Hypotheses

## 4.1. Communication media mix

Researchers have investigated the relationship between mediated communication and creativity and have found that communication tools can provide an environment conducive for sharing new and novel ideas [32,62]. Too many social and nonverbal cues found in faceto face communication (as a rich medium) may provide irrelevant information, distract from the task at hand, and be detrimental to creativity [89]. In contrast, a mediated communication environment is conducive to creativity because it prevents production blocking (i.e., inability to develop or express thoughts because of distractions) that often occurs in face-to-face groups [78]. CAT predicts that more miscommunications may arise when using mediated communication. As stated earlier, this communication ambiguity may actually induce creative thinking.

In addition to the environmental effects of media, the use of one form of media more often than another form of media affects the amount of time one spends interacting with others. Most research examines the effects of using one communication medium (e.g., email, group support systems, and audio conferencing) compared to another medium or compared to face-to-face interactions [28]; however, researchers have begun to acknowledge that communication tools (including face-to-face meetings) are rarely, if ever, used in isolation [5,56]. Exploring the effects of using more than one form of communication is important because, as explained by the dual coding theory, humans process information differently when it arrives in diverse forms [64]. Processing information differently is a prerequisite for individual creativity. When multiple communication media are used, the total amount of possible communication activity expands [56], allowing all participants more air time. The increase in possible communication combined with the potential for creativity to arise from mediated communication ambiguity, suggests that more mediated communication is better for enhancing creativity.

H1. Individuals who engage in proportionally more mediated communication with their team members will have higher levels of creativity than individuals who engage in proportionally more face-to-face communication with their team members.

## 4.2. Demographic differences

Team members often use physical and social cues such as race, gender, age or educational background to categorize people, organize information about them, and develop expectations about their behavior [76]. Research on demographic differences (or relational demography) has found that individuals who are different from their colleagues tend to have lower levels of organizational commitment, higher turnover, lower frequency of communication, lower levels of satisfaction, and higher levels of absenteeism [42,86,92].

Recent work in the area of demographic differences and diversity has stressed the value in differentiating between forms of diversity—those based on social categories and those based on information categories [42,43]. Social category characteristics (also described as relationsoriented, visible or surface-level) [40,85] are observable attributes such as race/ethnic background, nationality, sex, and age that are likely to induce responses such as in-group biases and con<sup>fl</sup>ict [42]. Informational characteristics (also described as non-visible or deeplevel) [40,85] are underlying attributes of individuals (such as work experience and education) which, although not immediately detectable, are important in the completion of a task [42,43]. Researchers have suggested that these two types of diversity differentially impact group processes; social category differences tend to trigger group processes that hinder social interaction (e.g., decreased trust, increased con<sup>fl</sup>ict) whereas informational differences can foster task-related interaction (e.g., sharing different viewpoints) [42,43,66].

Diverse groups may yield more creative products because they bene<sup>fi</sup>t from diverse member's views and may be exposed to more approaches with respect to an issue [39]. Heterogeneous work groups also tend to be less socially integrated and experience more group process concerns than homogeneous work groups [61,92]. Demographic differences based on social categories refers to the extent to which an individual differs from other members of a group on social category characteristics that are easily observed by others and used for categorization purposes. While social category differences may not be relevant to a given task, they do shape people's perceptions and behaviors through mechanisms of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, categorization, and prejudice [65]. The extent to which these categorizations are accurate may lead to con<sup>fl</sup>icting expectations between individuals. For example, if a female colleague is perceived by others to be insensitive or callous (traits that are not associated with being female), interpersonal con<sup>fl</sup>ict may develop from the violated expectations of coworkers. Thus, individuals who are more different from their team members on social category characteristics are less comfortable with the team, less attached to their employing organization, and less satis<sup>fi</sup>ed [42,86]. Low levels of social support and uncomfortable work environments reduce the likelihood of creativity [3]. When creativity is not a required part of the job, having an environment where one feels comfortable expressing one's views is likely to encourage creative thought.

H2a. Individuals who are very different from their team members on social category dimensions will have lower levels of creativity than individuals who are similar to their team members on social category dimensions.

DCT suggests that use of different forms of communication media would help individuals develop and express creative ideas [13]. The relative value of speci<sup>fi</sup>c communication media usage is likely to depend on the individual's demographic differences. For example, individuals who have many social category demographic differences may feel safer expressing and receiving ideas when using more mediated communication. Face-to-face communication provides richer information than mediated communication because face-to-face interaction provides social context cues and social information about the members of the group [18,75]. There has been debate in the literature as to the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of richer media. Some researchers [18,88] suggest that richer media enables individuals to change their messages in response to cues from their teammates and allows multiple nonverbal and paralinguistic cues to con<sup>fi</sup>rm that people communicate effectively. Other researchers [89] suggest that team members can be distracted by too many social and nonverbal cues that provide irrelevant information, thus interfering with the content of the message.

Work in the social identity and minority literature has found that individuals who are different from others on social category characteristics are less comfortable when expressing views that are different from others [27]. This discomfort may be lessened when communicating in ways that do not highlight differences, such as in a computer-mediated environment where the sender and receiver do not see each other [9,81]. Therefore, we expect that employees who are more different from other group members on social category characteristics will have more creative ideas when their communication medium makes these differences less salient (i.e., it is mediated).

H2b. Communication media mix will moderate the relationship between social category demographic differences and creativity. Individuals demographically different on social category characteristics and who engage in proportionally more mediated communication will have higher levels of creativity than individuals who engage in proportionally more face-to-face communication.

Informational demographic differences refer to the differences in knowledge bases and perspectives that members of a group possess [43]. A cognitive resource perspective suggests that informational diversity will have positive implications for group outcomes, since group members will have access to a wide array of views, skills, and information [39,69]. Exposure to a wide view of perspectives is especially important when one is interested in the development of creative products and ideas [34]. Educational background, functional background, and industry experience are all linked to the set of skills one employs when undertaking a task. For example, when there is a team of individuals with different functional backgrounds a resource pool exists based on non-overlapping knowledge and expertise from which individuals can draw to solve problems [7]. Thus, individuals who are more demographically different on information-based characteristics will be exposed to new ideas and will have more opportunities to develop creative views on how to conduct tasks or develop organizational products.

H3a. Individuals who are different from their team members on informational dimensions will have higher levels of creativity than individuals who are similar to their team members on informational dimensions.

Communication media choices in<sup>fl</sup>uence how team members interact, the type of information conveyed, and the amount of information conveyed [8,20,56]. Individuals who interact using non-face-to-face communication media may be more task-oriented and less likely to exchange relational information than those who communicate face-toface [57,74]. This decrease in the amount and type of information shared may hinder the ability of individuals who are demographically different on information-based characteristics to develop creative ideas. As CAT proposes, individuals who come from different informational backgrounds may be better able to share novel ideas and thoughts in face-toface encounters because it is important to see visual cues when explaining something novel using language from different functional or educational backgrounds [46]. Work on telecommuting has found that one of the most dif<sup>fi</sup>cult aspects of telecommuting is being able to express oneself clearly to others who are not familiar with the organizational or department language [52]. Although some confusion in expression of information may encourage creativity, if individuals are unable to communicate basic concepts, effective exchange of information is unlikely to take place. Thus, individuals who differ on informational characteristics will be more creative when they are able to clarify and understand thoughts and ideas of others. This will be more likely to occur when communication occurs in a face-to-face manner.

H3b. Communication media mix will moderate the relationship between informational demographic differences and creativity. Individuals who are demographically different on information-based characteristics and who engage in proportionally more mediated communication will have lower levels of creativity than individuals who engage in proportionally more face-to-face communication.

## 4.3. Self-esteem

Self-esteem is an individual personality component that has received very little attention in the organizational creativity literature. The essence of self-esteem is the favorableness of individuals’ characteristic self-evaluations [6]. Self-esteem has been associated with job satisfaction, organizational commitment, desire to stay in an organization, citizenship behavior, and performance [33,71,76]. Some research on the relationship between self-esteem and creativity in educational settings has found that self-esteem has a positive correlation with creative potential [80], creativity of young schoolchildren [45], and self-assessed creativity of undergraduate students [37]. The current study is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst to explore the relationship between self-esteem and creativity in an organizational setting. Furthermore, we look at the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of communication media use on the relationship between self-esteem and creativity.

When individuals engage in creativity there is some risk involved as new ideas and approaches are not always received warmly by others [84,87]. Thus, for individuals to engage in creativity, they must be secure about themselves in order to take the risk that others will <sup>fi</sup>nd their ideas nonsensical or inappropriate. High self-esteem individuals tend to like who and what they are and believe themselves to be capable and worthy [70]. These individuals are also less likely to censor their thoughts [90] and may be more willing to express views and ideas that differ from others. Since being creative requires that one express thoughts and ideas that are new and outside the current stream of thinking, it is expected that individuals with high levels of self-esteem would be more likely to share creative ideas. In addition, by virtue of feeling capable and worthy, one is more likely to engage in processes that foster creativity.

H4a. Individuals with high self-esteem will have higher levels of creativity than individuals with low self-esteem.

Individuals who have a high level of self-esteem are more likely to undertake risk and express their ideas when expressing ideas is not expected [90]. Since individuals who have high self-esteem feel that they are worthy and can contribute to the organization, they are more likely to express themselves when they have a creative idea in both face-to-face and mediated communications. However, CAT suggests that individuals are more likely to misinterpret information when it is received via computer mediation. For individuals with high levels of self-esteem receiving information that is slightly ambiguous may enable them to be even more creative than receiving accurate information as it challenges them to integrate seemingly irrelevant pieces of information [38]. Furthermore, DCT suggests that receiving information in more than one form helps individuals retain different pieces of information. In addition, individuals who engage in mediated communication have more time to engage in convergent thinking. In other words, these employees have time to re<sup>fl</sup>ect upon different ideas and thoughts from others and engage in a creative cognitive process [8,20]. To this end, high self-esteem individuals will be more creative when they communicate proportionally more in a non-face-to-face, or mediated, manner.

H4b. Communication media mix will moderate the relationship between self-esteem and creativity such that individuals with high self-esteem who engage in proportionally more mediated communication will have higher levels of creativity than individuals who engage in proportionally more face-to-face communication.

## 5. Research method

## 5.1. Study design

To test the research model, we conducted a four-month <sup>fi</sup>eld study consisting of both surveys and interviews. The 40-year old company (hereafter referred to as Asset) has approximately 2250 full-time employees in 21 of<sup>fi</sup>ces in over a dozen countries. Asset began as an investment technology <sup>fi</sup>rm and now is a provider of asset management and investment technology solutions. They earned \$1.369 billion in revenues and \$260 million in pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in the year 2007. Initial interviews were conducted with project supervisors to generate commitment to our research study, obtain general background information on the participants, and ensure that archival data was accurate (e.g., job descriptions were current).

We worked with Asset's management to develop a comprehensive database of all Asset employees and their primary project membership and conducted a preliminary survey to gather basic objective demographic data on approximately 500 individuals. Individuals were chosen to participate in this study based on the size of their project teams (teams with less than 3 members were omitted from the study) and location (teams based outside of the two main of<sup>fi</sup>ces were not included) and presented to Asset for <sup>fi</sup>nal selection. Two hundred ten employees were invited to participate in the study. We received 172 complete usable responses in 26 teams, resulting in an 85% response rate.

Employees worked in teams on tasks assigned both to the team and to individuals. In addition, all supervisors felt that their teams’ tasks were very interdependent, required effective working relationships with all team members, and were noticed for creativity in ways that were helpful to the organization. The selected employees <sup>fi</sup>lled out a survey that took approximately 30min to complete. Supervisors were given surveys to assess the creativity of the study's participants. Archival demographic data, calculated demographic difference scores, survey responses, calculated scores based on survey responses, and individual creativity data collected from the team supervisor were used to test the hypotheses.

## 5.2. Measures

## 5.2.1. Self-esteem

Self-esteem was measured using a three-item self-report scale adapted from the revised Janis–Field Self-Esteem Scale [6,26]. The items were measured on a 1–7 Likert scale with “1” anchored by “Never” and “7” anchored by “All the Time.” The items included in this scale were:

• How often do you have the feeling that there is nothing you can do well? (Reverse-coded)

• How often do you feel that you are a successful person?

• How often do you feel inferior to most people you know? (Reversecoded)

## 5.2.2. Demographic differences

Demographic information was collected from the organization's database and con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by the respondents. The demographic data include gender, age, race, educational background, functional area of work, and tenure in company. The variable “social category demographic differences” was calculated by using the relational demography formula of Tsui, Egan and O'Reilly [85] and summing the difference scores of social category characteristics of gender, race, and age [43]. The variable “informational demographic differences” was calculated in the same manner as social category demographic differences but the demographic differences included in the summed score were educational background, current functional area of work, and tenure in company. For categorical variables, such as race, the score was calculated by determining the number of times an individual was different from other team members on that particular characteristic. For example, an Asian person in a group with four other people who are Caucasian will be different from four people in terms of race. The other members of the group are only different from one other person (the Asian) in terms of race. Thus, the value of this construct will be higher for the Asian member than the Caucasian members. For both social and informational demographic differences, the more different an individual is from his or her team members, the higher the score on these dimensions.

## 5.2.3. Communication media mix

The intent of this measure is to determine how often individuals engage in mediated communications (e.g., email, voice mail) relative to face-to-face interactions. Although the employees at Asset were relatively tech-savvy, the company culture fostered an environment of frequent face-to-face communication. When individuals were moved from one team to another they often moved their desks to be situated near other team members. Therefore, there was often little “need” to communicate with team members via technology, yet many individuals employed voice mail and email to communicate with other team members. Video conferencing was available but was almost never used for communication with team members; listservs were also used but primarily to broadcast information required by the team. In addition to face-to-face communication, email and voice mail were the primary technologies used to communicate between individuals. Given the prevailing communication options at Asset, the communication media mix score was calculated by looking at the ratio of asynchronously mediated interaction to face-to-face interaction with team members for each individual. Each respondent was asked to rate on a 1–7 Likert scale the degree to which they interacted with each team member on a mediated basis and on a face-to-face basis. Since each individual reported this score for every other member in a team, the scores were averaged for each individual to create their communication media mix score. The range of communication media mix scores (ratio of mediated/face-to-face) was 0.14 to 1.67 with a score of 0.14 meaning that the individual communicated with team members almost exclusively in a face-to-face manner. Values greater than 1 indicate that the majority of communication with team members took place via mediated communication.

## 5.2.4. Creativity

Creativity was measured by three items in which supervisors were asked to evaluate each of their employees on the number of creative ideas they have, the usefulness of the ideas, and the overall creativity of the work done by the employee [63]. The supervisor used a 1–7 Likert scale to respond to each item.

## 5.2.5. Control variables

It is necessary to control for additional variables that may cause spurious correlations between the predictor variables and outcome variables. Team size, individual tenure on the team, and communication frequency were included as control variables. Twenty-six teams were represented in this study. Team size refers to the number of members who are active participants within a team. Individual tenure refers to the number of years an individual has belonged to their current team and was measured by asking each individual how long they had been a member of their team. Communication frequency refers to the extent to which an individual communicates with other team members both in a mediated and face-to-face manner. Each respondent was asked to rate on a 1–7 Likert scale the degree to which they communicated with every other team member. Communication frequency was measured by summing these scores for the focal employee.

## 6. Results

## 6.1. Data analysis techniques

The data were analyzed using PLS-Graph Version 3.00 (Build 1126). PLS is particularly well-suited for research involving theory development and when the goal is causal-predictive testing and explanation of variance [4,11]. Additionally, PLS can provide a robust analysis for our sample size of 172, due to its component-based estimation [11]. Finally, PLS is well-suited to handle both formative and re<sup>fl</sup>ective measures in a single model [11]. Next we describe the 2-step approach to model testing in which we <sup>fi</sup>rst assessed the measurement model, then the structural model. Prior to conducting any analysis, all measures were standardized. This is done to reduce the multi-collinearity associated with the use of interaction terms. Further, in the case of the formative constructs—social differences and informational differences—in the interaction terms, we followed the procedure set forth by Chin, Marcolin and Newstead [12] in which a new item is created by weighting the individual items that comprise the formative construct using the weights provided in PLS. This new item is used for the interaction calculations by multiplying it by each of the items comprising the relevant re<sup>fl</sup>ective construct, in our case there was only one item—communication media mix [See 12, Online Supplement A].

## 6.1.1. Measurement model

We assessed the measurement model by examining item reliability and discriminant validity. We calculated the internal consistency reliability (ICR), a measure similar to Cronbach's alpha, for self-esteem and creativity, the two re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs in the model. The measurement scales exhibit reliability of 0.832 and 0.947 respectively. These values are well above the 0.70 threshold of acceptability [25,60].

We assessed discriminant validity by determining if the constructs share more variance with their own measures than they share with the other constructs in the model. Table 1 presents the results of the correlation matrix for the research model. For self-esteem and creativity, we calculated the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE), a measure of the average variance shared between a construct and its measures. To demonstrate discriminant validity, the square root of the AVE must be greater than the correlation between that construct and the other constructs. For both self-esteem (Square Root of AVE=.744) and creativity (Square root of AVE=.925), the square root of the AVE is larger than the corresponding row and column correlations, suggesting adequate discriminant validity [30]. To further examine discriminant validity, we examined the factor loadings following the procedure set forth by Gefen and Straub [36] to ensure that each item loaded higher on its own construct than on any other construct. The items for self-esteem and creativity each loaded as expected on their own construct, with no cross loadings above 0.30 (see Table 2). Finally, we examined the factor loadings for the re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs and item weights for the formative constructs (see Table 3). ## 7. Discussion

In this study, we examined the relationships among a number of differences, including self-esteem, social demographic, informational demographic, and communication media mix, on creativity. The results clearly demonstrate the importance of looking at both the direct and indirect effects of communication media mix on individual creativity. When the ratio of mediated communication to face-to-face communication is high, there is an overall positive impact on creativity. There is a clear, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact of demographic diversity on individual creativity, both in a positive and a negative manner. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our results show that having a high number of social category differences relative to group members diminishes creativity while having a high number of informational-based demographic differences relative to group members enhances creativity. A communication mix that favors mediated communication alters the relationship between social category differences and creativity. The direct relationship between social category differences and creativity was negative but when individuals who are more different than others employ communication strategy that favors mediated communication, they can parlay their differences into higher levels of creativity. However, a communication media mix that favors mediated communication reduces the positive effect that information-based demographic differences have on creativity. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight the importance of distinguishing between different types of demographic differences. Finally, our results demonstrate that individuals with higher levels of self-esteem have higher levels of creativity than individuals with lower levels of self-esteem, and this effect was exacerbated by communicating in ways that favored mediated communication.

## 7.1. Theoretical implications

This study contributes to the literature in a number of important ways. First, it explores two theories—CAT and DCT—and their relevance to creativity and communication in groups. While CAT has been used to explain how individuals have adapted to the use of electronic mediated communication [48], this is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst studies to examine how CAT may in<sup>fl</sup>uence creativity in organizations. Kock [48] has argued that the evolution of humans has not yet prepared us for the dissemination and receipt of so much information in written form and delivered through mediated means causing much confusion, misinterpretation, and misunderstanding. We argue that the ambiguity of messages received via mediated exchanges may actually be helpful if the goal is to develop creative products or processes. Furthermore, DCT suggests that people learn and remember things differently depending on how the information was conveyed to them. While most of the research on DCT compares visual images with text-based images, we argue that it is important to consider that information presented orally may be received differently than information presented in a written fashion. The reality is that in today's world, individuals receive information through a number of mediated avenues as well as in face-to-face interactions. We must continue to explore the interactions between all forms of communication and to understand how they impact important organizational outcomes.

A second contribution of this study is the development of a communication media mix construct that better captures the reality of communication within organizations. Researchers have begun to explore this idea. Nardi and Whittaker [59] discuss the concept of “media ecologies” that examines how a given situation requires a speci<sup>fi</sup>c technology mix. Belanger and Watson-Manheim [5] explore how a combination of media is used to reach different communication goals. Likewise, Massey and Montoya-Weiss [56] posit that the use of different media communication has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive impact on knowledge transfer. In an attempt to account for the use of multiple media, we conceptualize communication media mix as a proportion of mediated communication to face-to-face communication as it affects an individual's ability to be creative. This mix is especially interesting in a study of creativity as creativity requires both convergent and divergent thinking [68,84]. Mediated communication may help with convergent thinking as individuals are able to think about ideas from others when it is convenient for them to do so and face-to-face communication helps with divergent thinking. Future research should delve more deeply into the mechanisms that connect media use with desired organizational outcomes.

In addition, we found that communication media mix has indirect effects on creativity. This suggests that the production gains that come from a higher mix of mediated rather than face-to-face communication help individuals who have a high level of self-esteem. A greater use of mediated communication may also facilitate autonomy, something that has been found to correlate highly with increased levels of creativity [2,68]. We found that a communication media mix favoring mediated communication helped individuals who had high levels of social category differences to be more creative. This is consistent with research on computer-mediated communication that shows mediated communication decreases the negative categorization processes that diversity may highlight [9,81]. What is particularly interesting here is that the individuals in the study know each other reasonably well and have many chances to communicate with each other in a face-to-face manner. Thus, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that even after individuals know each other quite well, the negative categorization processes that can hinder individuals from expressing their views can be diminished if a greater amount of work is done in a mediated fashion.

Furthermore, a communication media mix favoring mediated communication reverses the positive effect that information-based demographic differences have on creativity. We argued earlier that CAT helped to explain how the ambiguity in mediated communication might stimulate creativity. However, when individuals are different in terms of their information-based characteristics, this is not the case. There is a difference between ambiguity in a message and an inability to comprehend a message. When individuals come from different work or functional backgrounds, they often think and speak in languages representative of their discipline. This is especially true in an investment <sup>fi</sup>rm where employees are highly educated and have deep levels of expertise. It is likely to be even more pronounced in cross-disciplinary research teams. In these situations, communicating via mediated communication is more likely to lead to frustration or lack of comprehension than it is to lead to creativity.

The differential <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the current study provide additional evidence that the processes underlying social category differences are distinct from those underlying information-based differences. Thus, a third important contribution is that this study highlights the differing effects of two types of demographic differences: social category and information-based on individual-level creativity. While social category demographic differences often lead to incorrect categorizations, bias, and inaccurate expectations resulting in uncomfortable work environments, information-based demographic differences are often accepted and valued for the alternative views they provide. Research on creativity has often suggested, and found, that individuals who receive diverse points of view (e.g., through an extended network, weak social ties, or access to coworkers with heterogeneous backgrounds) are more creative [34,68,84]. However, these studies have primarily examined information-based demographic differences. Other research conducted at the group level also suggests that different forms of diversity have differential effects on creativity [9,35].Thus, our results indicate that future research on the relationship between demographic differences and individual-level creativity must also take into consideration the type of difference that is being investigated.

## 7.2. Limitations and directions for future research

As with all research, this study has limitations. Most importantly, the absence of longitudinal data means that conclusions regarding causality cannot be made [14]. The cross-sectional design limits our ability to untangle the causal relationships. For example, it may be that individuals who are rated as more creative by their superiors receive some sort of feedback that increases their level of self-esteem. Future research should collect similar data in a longitudinal manner in order to establish temporal precedence.

Measuring creativity is always a dif<sup>fi</sup>cult task. In this study, the creativity measurement is re<sup>fl</sup>ected by three items: number of creative ideas, usefulness of these ideas and overall creativity. Determining what was creative was left up to the supervisor. However, given the nature of creativity, the best way for it to be measured is by an expert; this person is often a manager in charge of a particular product or process [63].

Similar to other recent studies [e.g., 68,69], we do not claim to develop and test a complete model of creativity. Rather, we focus on a subset of possible relationships. Creativity, like other organizational outcomes, is impacted by a number of individual, team-level, and organizational factors. For example, research has shown that group processes such as con<sup>fl</sup>ict, cohesion, and trust may mediate the relationship between diversity and performance outcomes such as creativity [39,42,43]. Group processes are often a function of the amount of time that a group has interacted together, particularly in a mediated environment (e.g., [8]). Therefore, individuals working in newly-formed groups may be less able to reap the positive effects of having a greater proportion of mediated communications. We encourage future research to examine additional possible antecedents of creativity, as well as the elements that moderate and mediate the relationships—especially those that may be related to communication media choices.

Although a variety of studies have looked at the effect that mediated communication has on brainstorming [20], more research is needed to investigate how communication media mix in<sup>fl</sup>uences creativity in the organization. Work at both the individual and group levels is needed in this regard. Future research also needs to look at how various types of communication (voice mail, telephone, email, chat, messaging, GSS, video conferencing) are used in tandem and the effects they have on the satisfaction and performance of individuals and groups [56]. For example, the results of the current study suggest that individuals who engage in proportionally more mediated communication are more creative than those who mainly use faceto-face communication. But how much mediated communication is necessary to produce the positive effects of creativity? At what point is there too much mediated interaction? We did test for the possibility that there is a nonlinear effect of mediated communication on creativity in our data but found no support for this idea. There may also be differences in how the types of mediated communication in<sup>fl</sup>uence creativity. Do voice mail, email, group support systems, chat, messaging, and video conferencing in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals differently? Research has shown that voice-based communications are richer than text-based communications (see [23,93]). Therefore, future researchers in this area should seek to isolate the effects of different forms of communication to see whether there is some optimal pattern of faceto-face, text-based mediated communication, and speech-based mediated communication. Finally, more research needs to take place where individuals are encountering more diversity, more teamwork, more communication media use choices, and more emphasis placed on creativity—in organizations.

## 7.3. Practical implications

The results of this study have important implications for managers who want their employees to be more creative. The dif<sup>fi</sup>culty for managers is that creativity, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, must be motivated by the employees. Despite this, there are some takeaways from this research to help managers. First, people who are different enough, especially with regard to informational-based differences, and have an opportunity to communicate with diverse others are likely to be creative. Second, although past research has suggested that it is imperative that teams communicate in a face-to-face manner in order to encourage creativity, we suggest that mediated communication is equally important, and possibly more so. For teams made up of individuals with high self-esteem and high levels of social category diversity, managers should monitor team communications to ensure that there is not too much face-to-face communication. Finally, even if creativity is not formally encouraged or a job requirement, managers can provide an organizational environment that is open to new ideas.

## 7.4. Conclusion

In this study, we proposed a model of individual creativity that incorporated individual, demographic, and communication media mix differences. Our results indicate that informational and social demographic differences operate on creativity differently. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, social category differences in<sup>fl</sup>uence creativity negatively, while informational differences have a positive impact. Finally, the nature of communication (mediated in relation to face-to-face) has important moderating effects on the relationship between the differences and creativity, suggesting that mediated communication has an important role to play in enhancing creativity.

Susan A. Brown is an Associate Professor at the University of Arizona. She received her Ph.D. from the Management Information Systems Department at the University of Minnesota. Her research interests focus on the adoption and diffusion of information technology by organizations and individuals. She has published in a number of journals including MIS Quarterly, Communications of the ACM, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, IEEE Transactions for Engineering Management, and The European Journal of Information Systems.
