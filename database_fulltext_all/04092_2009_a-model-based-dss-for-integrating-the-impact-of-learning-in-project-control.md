# A model-based DSS for integrating the impact of learning in project control

Keywords: Project management Earned value method Decision support model Organizational learning

## a b s t r a c t

Earned Value Method (EVM) is a popular project control technique. In this paper, we discuss the extended version of EVM (EVM/LC) that addresses the effect of learning on the performance of project teams. These effects have so far been ignored in most EVM applications. We present a spreadsheet-based decision support tool that automates the calculations and analyses in EVM/LC. Using this tool would save the project manager from having to perform complicated calculations while still taking advantage of the relatively accurate estimates generated by EVM/LC. Consequently, this paper contributes to both research and practice in project management.

## 1. Introduction

Planning is an essential component of project management. Considerable effort and time goes into planning so that acceptable results are delivered within the constraints of time, budget, and other resources. Project plans are based on assumptions made about a number of external as well as internal factors such as the continuous availability of management support and performance of the project team. Inevitably, there is a degree of inaccuracy in these assumptions, therefore project management goes beyond planning, and requires the project manager to regularly monitor the execution and progress of the plan and take corrective action when needed. This aspect of project management is known as project control. In this study, we focus on the control of project duration for its important impact on the other aspects of project success such as cost [11]. This is a relevant and important problem to study, especially for projects such as software implementation where it has been reported that 18% of projects are prematurely canceled, and 53% of them exceed their cost, schedule, and scope constraints [28].

Earned Value Method (EVM) is a popular project control technique that has been used successfully for managing various types of projects since 1960 [19,25,30,36]. Most applications of EVM are based on the assumption that project team performance is a constant function of time. The problem with this assumption for cross-disciplinary teams is that a great deal of knowledge transfer and team integration occurs during the project's initial stages. The knowledge transfer and team integration affect team performance during project implementation [7,15] in a way that performance (per unit time) of the team gradually increases [23]. This causes dif<sup>fi</sup>culties when forecasting the project duration, and makes a linear project control method ineffective when tracking project progress. The effect of nonlinear factors such as learning have been considered by previous research on project management, and there have been efforts to extend EVM with nonlinear elements [28], but these efforts have not thoroughly addressed the typical nature of learning in cross-disciplinary teams. To <sup>fi</sup>ll that gap, we recently [35] developed a mathematical model that incorporates L-curves in the calculation of project duration estimates. The model reported in the original study [33] provides a range for project duration, which is generated by using approximations based on mathematical series. The research reported in this paper furthers the development of that model where point values instead of ranges for project duration are calculated. In addition, instead of using approximations, the model variables are calculated numerically hence improving accuracy. These improvements have been made possible by the development of an automated decision support tool that is described later in the paper.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 is a review of the important issues in project control especially as they relate to forecasting dif<sup>fi</sup>culties. A demonstration of these dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in a reallife case study is presented in Section 3. In Section 4, we present a method in which EVM is combined with learning curves to address these dif<sup>fi</sup>culties, and in Section 5 we describe a decision support tool that implements the resulting model, and discuss the implications of prospective users' reaction to this system. The paper concludes with a discussion of the contributions of the study and future research directions in Section 6.

## 2. Background

Previous research has suggested that organizations should adopt multi-dimensional performance measures and techniques for the management of ongoing operations [9], as well as projects with <sup>fi</sup>xed durations [11]. Earned Value Method (EVM) is one such multidimensional project control method that integrates cost, schedule, technical performance, and scope [6,14,24,25,41]. Various forms of EVM have been in use since the industrial revolution when managers started looking for ways to measure the performance of manufacturing processes. What is commonly known as EVM today was <sup>fi</sup>rst adopted by the US Department of Defense in the late 1960s [41].

The “Earned Value” of a project at a point in time is the amount of work done up to that point. This is essentially the number of the unit project tasks completed, but practitioners are also known to use monetary measurements of earned value. Earned Value Method involves measuring the earned value of a project and comparing that to the “planned value”, which is an estimation of where the project manager thinks the progress of the project should be at the time this assessment is made [41]. This comparison gives the project manager an indication as to whether the project is progressing as planned or there are deviations from the project plan. In case the deviations from the project plan are such that the successful ‘on time’ completion of the project is unlikely, the project manager may need to make arrangements to speed up the execution of the remaining tasks to bring to project back to schedule.

There is consensus on the usefulness of EVM [42] to a degree that both the Project Management Institute (PMI) and International Project Management Association (IPMA) recommend its use [13]. However, it has been argued that there is a lack of earned value practice in many organizations and industries [13,42], and according to Evensemo and Karlsen [13] the reason for this is that EVM techniques and tools are not very well understood. This is one of the motivations of the current study. We aimed to build an easy-to-use decision support tool so that the complexities of project control in general and EVM in particular, are for the most part hidden from the end-user. This would in effect address the understandability issues and hence lowering one of the barriers of adoption of our EVM-based model.

Nkasu and Leung [31] observed that although the use of decision support models and systems have been common in areas such as accounting, <sup>fi</sup>nance, marketing, and new product development, their use in project management has been relatively recent. Given the critical impact of decisions especially during the planning stages of projects, the authors proposed a methodology that will support project managers with decisions in the management of a portfolio of projects that are executed simultaneously. Donzelli [12] also observed the scarcity of DSS tools for project management in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c domain of software engineering, and prescribed an approach that will help software developers in various phases of a project such as staf<sup>fi</sup>ng and scheduling. Garcia et al. [16] describe another DSS application in the software development domain, which focuses particularly on software size estimation. Other researchers developed DSS for various problems in project management such as determination of quality costs in the construction industry [27], and assignment of managers to projects [32].

Recent research by Lee et al. [26] highlights the need for decision support speci<sup>fi</sup>cally for project control, and presents an ontologybased intelligent decision support agent. The goal of this system is to track the amount of work completed by each project member, compare this to the planned values, and report this information to the project manager for the evaluation of each project team member's performance. Although the conceptual framework used for this system is the capabilities maturity model integration (CMMI), the basic approach is very similar to what is done in EVM. However, the stated purpose of this system is evaluation of individuals rather than the evaluation of the performance of the team as a whole to determine what kind of corrective action is necessary for the successful completion of the project.

There are commercial products on the market for the latter goal, to which the system reported in this paper would be an alternative. For example, Decision Edge, a Microsoft certi<sup>fi</sup>ed partner that provides advanced reporting and graphics capabilities for project management software, offers Earned Value Manager [10] — a DSS for managing project costs, measuring earned value, and analyzing budget, actual and forecasted costs. Earned Value Manager can be seamlessly integrated with Microsoft Project, and when compared to the DSS discussed in this paper, offers more advanced reporting options and seamless integration with <sup>fi</sup>nancial systems. However, like other commercially available systems, Earned Value Manager does not integrate the impact of learning in the analyses it provides. This has serious implications for practice, since as discussed throughout this paper; accuracy of duration forecasting is strongly correlated with learning effects.

The failure to include learning effects in EVM is commonly reported in the literature. For example, the EVM applications surveyed in [28] are based on the assumption that the performance of the project team is constant during the execution of the project [2,14,40]. Although this simplifying assumption is a good approximate for many projects, for many others, it causes forecasting dif<sup>fi</sup>culties, because team performance is affected by learning and thus not constant. Especially in IT projects, the performance of the project team increases signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly (and nonlinearly) during the progression of the project [17,37]. In fact, it was shown [40] that although variations of the linear EVM metrics lead to comparable forecasts for project scenarios where the learning effects are negligible, the predictions using these metrics differ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly when there are nonlinear learning effects. Therefore, in order to improve the accuracy of project duration forecasting, EVM should be merged with learning curves [1,5] that model the nonlinear change in the performance of the project team.

Learning curves are usually related to production rates or costs [3,21], and are frequently used to forecast productivity in both manufacturing and service sectors. A learning curve is a quantitative measure of performance changes during not only the execution, but also the lifetime of a project. Learning curves have been used in a variety of contexts. The most frequently used functional forms of learning are an S-curve and an L-curve [4,8,20,22,38,39].

The S-curve conceptualizes performance improvement as a function of practice, with the most dramatic improvements taking place at the beginning of the learning process [33]. The L-curve is similar, to the S-curve but ignores the start up effect, and therefore more adequately describes the performance improvement during the experiential learning phase.

Plaza et al. [34] investigated the conditions under which a speci<sup>fi</sup>c model of learning (L-curve or S-curve) is a <sup>fi</sup>t for a project team's performance using data from four ERP implementation projects. The authors concluded that although both models can be satisfactorily used in a number of contexts, if the initial level of knowledge and experience is very low (for example, internal staff needs to get a basic understanding of the technology to be implemented, consultants need to learn the environment the system will be implemented in, etc.) an L-curve with the initial condition of 0 is a more suitable functional form representing the performance changes over time. This condition is an accurate representation of many real-life project situations such as large IT implementations. However, the scarce work in project management that incorporates learning effects in EVM have been limited to the use of S-curves [6,28,40].Therefore, to provide decision support for the management of projects where initial level of knowledge and experience is very low, we adopt L-curves to model learning. We developed a model that integrates L-curves into EVM, and implemented a simple decision support tool to automate the projected duration forecasts and the related analyses. Before the tenets of this model are introduced, a case study that illustrates the negative impact of ignoring the learning curve in an IT implementation project is presented.

## 3. The problem

## 3.1. Case background

As described in [33], the (Canadian) company discussed in this section started an SAP implementation in early 1999. The company had to transition to SAP from several legacy applications. The management expected the data conversion and report development to be extremely complex, therefore set up a separate 9-month project to run in parallel to the main SAP implementation. The main objective of this separate project was to standardize data elements, develop procedures for data extraction and conversion, set up the environment for backups of the data, and provide reporting capabilities. The project manager used Earned Value Method (EVM) to plan and track the progress of this high impact project as illustrated below.

The average experience of the team members was 1 to 2 years, therefore the project manager assumed that the performance (per unit time) of the project team $p _ { 0 }$ can be approximated by a constant performance function, and calculated planned duration as $T _ { 0 } = 9 \cdot$ months (Fig. 1).

However, when the Earned Value EV of the project was measured at time $t ^ { * } { = } 2$ months, it was found to be only 80% of the value that was planned to have been achieved at this point in time (Planned Value PV). Therefore the project manager determined the Schedule Performance Index as de<sup>fi</sup>ned in Eq. (1) as 0.8.

$$
S P I = \frac {E V}{P V}\tag{1}
$$

$E V { = } p _ { 1 } { ^ { * } t } ^ { * }$ represents the dollar value of work actually completed, and $P V { = } p _ { 0 } { ^ { * } t } ^ { * }$ represents the dollar value of work that was planned to have been completed at time t⁎. When these expressions for EV and PV are substituted into (Eq. (1)) actual performance (point A) and adiusted duration can be calculated as:

$$
p _ {1} = S P I ^ {*} p _ {0}\tag{2}
$$

$$
T _ {1} = \frac {T _ {0}}{S P I}\tag{3}
$$

Since $S P I = 0 . 8 ,$ , the project manager concluded that the actual performance was much lower than assumed during the planning phases, and forecasted the project duration as 11.25 months $( T _ { 1 } { = } 1 1 . 2 5 \mathrm { m o n t h s } )$

The forecasts, however, turned out to be inaccurate and the team ended up completing all tasks ahead of schedule.

The possible reasons for the problems illustrated in this simple case study can be found in the project management literature, which suggests that when nonlinear effects are present, the predictive performance of EVM techniques are questionable [40]. However, to date there have not been any prescriptive studies on the nature and impact of learning on project, and thus EVM, performance. For that reason, one of the authors of this paper who was an observer in the mentioned case study initiated a study where four ERP implementation projects were examined for the existence, type and impact of learning on project schedule. The study clearly demonstrated that learning effects and training must be included in calculation of project parameters [34]. In follow-up research, the relationship between project duration, cost, and time of initial training was modeled [35]. Finally, performance changes due to learning were isolated from other factors affecting project duration, and EVM was integrated with Lcurves [33]. The model in [33] provided ranges for the project duration, which were generated by using approximations based on mathematical series. We discuss the model further in Section 4 since it is the starting point for the more accurate formulas in our current paper, where point values instead of ranges for project duration are calculated. In addition, instead of using approximations, the model variables are calculated numerically hence signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improving accuracy. These improvements have been made possible by the development of an automated DSS tool, which is the main contribution of this paper, and is described in Section 5.

## 4. A remedy for the forecasting issues

## 4.1. Extended EVM (EVM/LC)

The extended method, EVM/LC, is based on the combination of EVM and learning effects, i.e., the L-curve. It was developed to improve the accuracy of forecasts generated by the EVM [33]. EVM/LC is based on the assumption that many projects (such as IT implementation) are affected by two types of factors: those that have to do with the “true potential” of the team, which determine its maximum performance level, and those that have to do with its learning. Although both types of factors are always present and in<sup>fl</sup>uential on the team's performance, one type dominates the other depending on the stage of the project. In EVM/LC, factors that de<sup>fi</sup>ne the long-term maximum performance of the team (such as skill levels, work ethic, etc.) are distinguished from those causing a performance change due to learning (LC). The learning curve has an especially strong negative impact on the project during its early stages, which forces the Earned Value to be less than in a scenario where LC factors are neglected. Let us assume that $E V _ { E M }$ represents the dollar value of work that would have been completed if the team's performance had not been impacted by a learning curve. In Fig. 2, EV is the area under p (the maximum possible performance or the asymptote to the learning curve) from 0 to t⁎.

Fig. 7. The input module.

When the project manager measures EV, he/she can calculate SPI, but cannot determine $\begin{array} { r } { S P I _ { E M } = \frac { E V _ { E M } } { P V } , } \end{array}$ which is the portion of the Schedule Performance Index not impacted by the learning curve, since he/she does not know $E V _ { E M } .$ . If the Performance Reduction Index, PRI, is introduced as the corrective coef<sup>fi</sup>cient, then $S P I _ { E M }$ can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned in relation to SPI.

$$
S P I _ {E M} = \frac {S P I}{P R I}\tag{5}
$$

The values of $p _ { 1 E M }$ and $T _ { 1 E M }$ can then be calculated as:

$$
p _ {1 E M} = S P I _ {E M} ^ {*} p _ {0}\tag{6}
$$

$$
T _ {1 E M} = \frac {T _ {0}}{S P I _ {E M}}\tag{7}
$$

Substituting (Eqs. (6) and (7)) into (Eq. (5)) we get:

$$
\frac {E V _ {E M}}{P V} = \frac {1}{P R I} \frac {E V}{P V}\tag{8}
$$

Since $E V _ { E M } = p _ { 1 E M } { } ^ { * } t ^ { * }$ (area under $p _ { 1 E M } )$ then Eq. (8) can be rearranged into:

$$
E V = E V _ {E M} ^ {*} P R I = p _ {1 E M} ^ {*} t ^ {*} P R I\tag{9}
$$

EV, which is the amount of work actually completed on the project up to time $t ^ { * } ,$ , can also be calculated as the area under the L-curve, so

$$
\int_ {0} ^ {t ^ {*}} p _ {1 E M} \left(1 - e ^ {- k t}\right) d t = P R I ^ {*} p _ {1 E M} ^ {*} t ^ {*},\tag{10}
$$

which, after the integration is calculated and both sides of the equation are divided by $p _ { 1 E M } ,$ , yields:

$$
k t ^ {*} + e ^ {- k t ^ {*}} - 1 = P R I ^ {*} k t ^ {*}\tag{11}
$$

From this:

$$
P R I = 1 + \frac {e ^ {- k t ^ {*}}}{k t ^ {*}} - \frac {1}{k t ^ {*}}\tag{12}
$$

This implies that if the learning parameter k can be estimated, the calculation of the rest of the parameters in the model will be straightforward. k can be assessed by a test, in which the time required to complete a certain (unit) task (e.g., conversion of a unit amount of data) is measured at two different points (t⁎) during the progression of the project [33]. Since the performance rate, i.e., P(t) is inversely proportional to the time it will take to <sup>fi</sup>nish a unit task Δt, we can de<sup>fi</sup>ne

$$
\alpha = \frac {\Delta t _ {2} ^ {*}}{\Delta t _ {1} ^ {*}} = \frac {P (t _ {1} ^ {*})}{P (t _ {2} ^ {*})}\tag{13}
$$

$$
\alpha = \frac {p _ {1} \left(1 - e ^ {- k t _ {1} ^ {*}}\right)}{p _ {1} \left(1 - e ^ {- k t _ {2} ^ {*}}\right)} = \frac {1 - e ^ {- k t _ {1} ^ {*}}}{1 - e ^ {- k t _ {2} ^ {*}}}\tag{14}
$$

From this

$$
\alpha e ^ {- k t _ {2} ^ {*}} - e ^ {- k t _ {1} ^ {*}} + 1 - \alpha = 0\tag{15}
$$

Before we discuss the calculation of k and the further development of the model in the next sections, it is worthwhile to examine the impact of what has been discussed so far on the performance of EVM (and EVM/LC). The best possible project duration $T _ { 1 E M }$ is calculated based on the assumption that performance is at $p _ { 1 E M }$ all the time. Its value is therefore too optimistic, and can be used only as a limit for the actual duration $T _ { 2 } ,$ which will be between $T _ { 1 E M }$ and $T _ { 1 } .$ Note from Fig. 2 that point B (actual performance) is always between point A (the performance determined from EV by EVM) and point C (maximum performance possible if learning happened instantaneously). It is then useful to graphically analyze the range for the actual duration as a function of model parameters to have insight into where the basic EVM calculations are close enough to those of EVM/LC and therefore acceptable.

As expressed in Eq. (12), PRI is directly related to the learning curve coef<sup>fi</sup>cient k and time, at which the test was performed, $t ^ { * } .$ Fig. 3 is a plot of the relationship between PRI and k for a given $t ^ { * } ( t ^ { * } { = } 2 )$ . The nature of the relationship between PRI and k is important, because it determines how $T _ { 1 E M }$ and $T _ { 1 }$ are related. Fig. 4 is a display of the relationship between $T _ { 1 E M }$ and $T _ { 1 }$ for a given $T _ { 0 } = 9 ,$ and differing values of SPI and k. The narrower the margin between $T _ { 1 E M }$ and $T _ { 1 }$ is, the closer the estimate for $T _ { 2 }$ will be to $T _ { 1 } .$ Therefore this <sup>fi</sup>gure reaf<sup>fi</sup>rms the fact that EVM is an acceptable approximation of EVM/LC only for very large values of k. Note that, when k approaches its theoretical maximum (a step function that represents instantaneous learning), EVM and EVM/LC are identical. Additionally, the <sup>fi</sup>gure suggests that EVM estimates will be close to those of EVM/LC for higher values of SPI. This implies that the better the original EVM forecasts are, i.e. the earned value is close to or even better than the planned value, the less severe the impact of ignoring learning will be. Finally, note that this <sup>fi</sup>gure is for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>xed value of t⁎. Since PRI is a function of kt⁎, it can also be concluded that for a given k, EVM estimates would be close to those of EVM/LC for higher values of t⁎. This observation is also intuitive as it suggests that as time goes by; the project team will complete or come close to completing its learning. Therefore the effect of learning will be less and less pronounced.

To summarize then, EVM/LC is most useful: (i) during the <sup>fi</sup>rst few weeks of the project, (ii) when learning is slower, and (iii) for the smaller earned value to planned value ratios.

Fig. 5 is an analysis of the differences between EVM/LC estimates $\left( T _ { 1 E M } \right)$ and EVM estimates under different values of SPI. As seen in the <sup>fi</sup>gure, for projects behind schedule at the time of measurement $( S P I { < } 1 )$ , EVM would suggest that more resources should be made available for the project in order to complete it according to schedule. However as the curves for different $T _ { 1 E M }$ values indicate, there is still a good chance that the team could complete the project on time since all the $T _ { 1 E M }$ values in this area are below $T _ { 0 } .$ Although this observation is useful, it is based on the most optimistic estimates of project completion times, therefore may be unrealistic. At this point, we can further the development of our model by calculating the estimates for the actual project completion duration.

## 4.2. Calculations of the actual duration based on the EVM/LC

To calculate the actual project duration, we make the observation that the total work required for project completion is the same regardless of how the project progress is modeled. Whether it is represented by the L-curve or by the constant performance function p<sub>1EM</sub>, the total work done to complete the project is:

![](/api/attachments/TJ4ZZY42/fulltext/images/64e357a65a2f474f2d028cde3c727eccae4a28ffdfe3e8ecea1f7c51958434d9.jpg)

$$
\int_ {0} ^ {T _ {1 E M}} p _ {1 E M} d t = \int_ {0} ^ {T _ {2}} p _ {1 E M} \left(1 - e ^ {- k t}\right) d t\tag{16}
$$

which can be calculated and divided by $p _ { 1 E M }$ to yield:

$$
k T _ {2} + e ^ {- k T _ {2}} = k T _ {1 E M} + 1\tag{17}
$$

As in the case of the calculation of the learning parameter k in Eq. (15), Eq. (17) can be solved numerically to estimate the actual project duration $T _ { 2 } .$ Fig. 6 displays values of $T _ { 2 }$ calculated accordingly. This <sup>fi</sup>gure adds to the observation made on Fig. 4 that for higher values of k (faster learning), the differences between the duration estimates $( T _ { 2 }$ versus $T _ { 1 E M }$ as well as $T _ { 1 E M }$ versus $T _ { 1 } )$ narrow down therefore the value of EVM/LC is more pronounced for slow learning (low k).

In order to be able to calculate k and $T _ { 2 }$ numerically, and to automate the calculation of the other variables using numeric or direct, i.e. closed-form methods, we developed a spreadsheet-based decision support tool. Past literature identi<sup>fi</sup>es spreadsheets as the dominant platform for the development of end-user applications [29], therefore our approach follows common practice in organizations. The next section describes this tool in more detail.

## 5. A DSS that implements EVM/LC

To implement the spreadsheet-based decision support tool, we used Microsoft Excel with macros written in VBA. The system is designed for a project manager that has basic understanding of project control and EVM, but not necessarily that of EVM/LC and its parameters described in this paper. Meanwhile, the system provides brief explanations of what the inputs, outputs, and system variables are, so it gives the user an opportunity to familiarize him or herself about the model(s) implemented thereof.

When the system is active, the <sup>fi</sup>rst module with which the user interacts is the input module (Fig. 7). This module prompts the user to enter the known project parameters such as the planned duration of the project (T<sub>0</sub>), the points in time the project progress was assessed (t⁎ and t⁎), the amount of time it took to complete a unit task at t⁎ and $t _ { 2 } ^ { * }$ as well as the planned (PV) and earned (measured) value of the project (EV) at $\mathrm { t } ^ { * } \left( > = t _ { 2 } ^ { * } \right)$ . We performed an informal evaluation of this tool by obtaining some quick feedback from 4 practicing project managers. At the time of the writing, three of these managers (Reviewers 1–3) worked for the Canadian division of a global company, which implemented SAP and developed several extensions to the system. The fourth one also had project management background, and was working as an independent consultant. The managers were all familiar with EVM and appreciated the importance of proper assessment of the skill level of the project staff. They expressed genuine interest in a system that would increase the accuracy of project tracking, especially when team learning is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor.

To analyze this feedback, we followed the design evaluation guidelines from [18], which require that IT artifacts are assessed based on their ability to provide:

(i) the promised bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (functionality): in our case it is the ability to calculate project duration under the condition of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant performance changes due to learning effects

(ii) all required bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (completeness), in our case it is the ability to answer all questions regarding project performance under the condition of uncertainty, like forecasting project duration and cost for various possible scenarios including variation in input parameters, assessment errors, etc.

(iii) results that are consistent and suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently accurate (reliability)

(iv) results that are provided within the tolerable timeframe (performance), and

(v) the artifact, which can be integrated within the technical infrastructure and business environment (<sup>fi</sup>t with the organization): in our case it is the potential to integrate our tool with a commonly used project management tool – MS Project, and technique – the project management methodology endorsed by PMI.

## 6. Discussion and conclusions

In this research, we aimed to improve the body of knowledge on estimating project durations. Projects (such as most IT implementations) that are impacted by learning and knowledge transfers in the early phases prove to be very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to control with linear control methods. We argued that an L-curve is a good representation of learning where the project team has little or no knowledge of the system at the beginning of the project, and a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of learning has to happen in the early stages. To address duration forecasting dif<sup>fi</sup>culties for such projects, we developed an extension to the linear Earned Value Method (EVM) by combining it with L-curves. We found in our previous work [33] that the resulting model, EVM/LC, is a more realistic model of what happens during projects where learning and team-building takes place in the early stages; however this model did not provide straightforward calculations for project duration estimates, therefore would be challenging to adopt by practicing project managers.

In our previous work, we addressed this challenge by using mathematical approximations to generate closed-form expressions for project parameters. The approach described in the current paper is an improvement over those approximations in that we developed a spreadsheet-based system that numerically solves the equations within EVM/LC to generate more accurate duration estimates. This way, we are also able to generate point estimates (rather than ranges) for these variables. This DSS also automates all the calculations within the model thus saving the practicing project manager from the complexities of the mathematical derivations and calculations. The system requires its users to only input the known values of measurable (or designed) project variables, and provides project duration estimates and related sensitivity analysis automatically. We believe such a tool is valuable since it incorporates the necessary complexities of duration forecasts without putting the burden on the project manager. The system interface also provides information about the underlying model therefore the user can learn about the model while using the DSS. The feedback we received from practitioners suggests that the simple yet effective proof of concept system we report in this study would be welcome by the project management community.

The managers from whom we received feedback indicated that a system such as ours would bene<sup>fi</sup>t practice in project management, because it is straightforward to understand, and fairly easy to use. The fact that rather than attempting to replace common tools such as MS Project that virtually every project manager uses, our system complements them was particularly attractive to practicing project managers. Their suggestions and our observations lead us to the conclusion that as a proof of concept, this system was successful; therefore further efforts to improve its functionality and usability are warranted. As such, we have already started working on the improvement of the reporting capabilities that will provide more indepth analyses that will ideally be supported by better visualizations. We also implemented a module to help the users of the system to understand its underlying models and assumptions.

As with any research, what is reported in this paper has certain limitations, some of which should be addressed by future research. From a conceptual point of view, although EVM/LC is an improvement over what is generally used for project duration control, further research is needed to intensively test this model to <sup>fi</sup>nd out its accuracy on a variety of projects. It is not unlikely that the model will then have to be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed further to incorporate the complexities of learning in different contexts. Another limitation of the model is that it is fairly sensitive to the learning rate parameter, k [33], which makes it essential that it must be estimated accurately. What was reported in this paper was the assessment of the group's performance on a unit task at two different points in time. The challenge with this approach is to <sup>fi</sup>nd those tasks that are repetitiously performed during the project so that the changes in the performance of the team can be observed. Furthermore, the accuracy of these estimates could be impacted by project team member differences such as differences in the initial levels of experience, knowledge about the system, and learning rates. Although organizations are likely to assess experience and knowledge levels of the individuals, estimating learning rates would be a challenge. Also, this approach does not take into account group dynamics since it does not consider the impact of each member on the other members of the team. This is an open research area that should be addressed in the future. Once there are techniques in place to estimate individual learning rates, future research should also focus on developing methods to combine individual learning rates to calculate a composite <sup>fi</sup>gure that represents group learning rates. In the mean time, what can be done with our current system to minimize possible inaccuracies in the estimation of k is the addition of lower and upper limits to the results depicted in Fig. 10, which can then be calculated for required tolerance levels. For example, if up to 10% inaccuracy is expected in the calculation of k, then the lower and upper levels of PRI and project duration can be calculated for k⁎0.9 and k⁎1.1 accordingly.

For future research, we are planning to collect actual project data to test and to extend our model and the resulting system. Our informal user study already provided some interesting directions for the enhancement of the usability of the system. An appropriate methodology for the further development of the system is action research where the research team can get actively involved in the use of the tool and help streamline the issues in the adoption of the system described in this paper.

We believe that our research has been an effort in the right direction for improving the control of projects in various domains as a <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to address project management complexities that stem from people involved in the project. Although the approach here is still relatively simple, it is more complex than the overly simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed assumptions on which most techniques are based today. Any effort spent in improving the proper use of resources allocated to projects, especially large and mission critical ones as exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed in this paper, would have serious impacts on the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of organizations, which is particularly relevant in today's economic environment where acquiring resources is becoming gradually more challenging.
