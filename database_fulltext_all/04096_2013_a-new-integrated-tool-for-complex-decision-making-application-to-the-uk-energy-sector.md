# A new integrated tool for complex decision making: Application to the UK energy sector

Julian David Hunt, René Bañares-Alcántara ⁎, David Hanbury

Department of Engineering Science, University of Oxford, Oxford OX1 3PJ, UK

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 5 June 2012 Received in revised form 15 November 2012 Accepted 9 December 2012 Available online 20 December 2012

Keywords: Decision Support System IBIS MCDA Energy and water

## a b s t r a c t

This paper presents a new integrated tool and decision support framework to approach complex problems resulting from the interaction of many multi-criteria issues. The framework is embedded in an integrated tool called OUTDO<sup>1</sup> (Oxford University Tool for Decision Organisation). OUTDO integrates Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA), decision rationale representation and management, and probabilistic forecasting to explore how changes in external parameters affect complicated and uncertain decision-making processes. OUTDO's features are showcased with a case study which recommends future energy sources for the Yorkshire and the Humber region in the UK, taking into consideration water consumption and the possibility of desalination of sea water.

## 1. Introduction

Current global trends are driving humanity to a more sustainable world. Countries are under political and economic pressure to restructure their energy sector due to the need to reduce $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ emissions, while still supplying reliable and affordable energy services. In the UK, a provisional solution for the reduction of ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emissions is under way with the implementation of Natural Gas Combined Cycle (NGCC<sup>2</sup>) power plants. However a more long term solution is under discussion, led by the Department of Energy and Climate Change (DECC<sup>3</sup>) [20]. This White Paper on electricity generation, released in the summer of 2011, proposes several energy policies to renovate the UK energy sector and focuses on nuclear power, coal with carbon capture and storage (CCS<sup>4</sup>), and renewable energy generation, especially wind.

However, for the UK to develop a robust and resilient solution for its energy sector, the importance of a variety of external factors, some of which are beyond the control of the UK government, have to be thoroughly analysed. These factors include, for example, changes in oil prices, CO emissions cost and the availability of water. These factors are used to analyse how different future scenarios might change the recommended electricity generation source together with the possible utilisation of the rejected heat from steam condensation.

On the resolution of such complex decisions, various different aspects of a problem must be taken into account to <sup>fi</sup>nd the most appropriate solution. Some of these aspects are trade-offs, for example, project costs against the environmental impact, long term climate changes against immediate social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Thus the choice of methodology to manage the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of these aspects in the decision process is of paramount importance. Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis was implemented in OUTDO with the intent to keep the decision process transparent and to simplify its resolution. No less important than the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation of criteria in a decision process is the capability to foresee how the world is going to behave after a decision has been made. It can be the case that with a change in the value of a parameter in time (e.g. oil price or CO emissions cost) the best solution to a problem may also change. OUTDO therefore includes probabilistic forecasting in the decision support process.

At the same time OUTDO supports decision making processes, the decision rationale (reasons behind the decisions and the justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for these reasons) is also documented. There are many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to document the decision rationale. In many cases, the reasons and concepts behind decisions are not formally recorded, and are forgotten. Frequently, if the same decision process used to <sup>fi</sup>nd a previous decision is reused in a different place, circumstance or time, the outcome of the decision could be very different from what was decided previously. In addition, the management of knowledge and information is not easy to achieve. Communication between different groups of people working on the same project can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult because each person has a perspective on the project that <sup>fi</sup>ts with their own activities in the project [52]. OUTDO addresses this issue by enabling dialogue mapping (a technique for recording meeting discussions) [17] and the linking of different sources of project information to further clarify the decision process.

The combination of MCDA, decision rationale capture and Probabilistic Forecasting makes OUTDO a tool appropriate to aid long term and complex decision making which takes into consideration the change in external variables and involve a variety of con<sup>fl</sup>icting criteria, interacting decisions, and different stakeholders. This is explained in further detail in the next section.

Several attempts have been made to create rationale support tools [10,21]. The potential for decision rationale capture tools will only improve the decision process, if they can <sup>fi</sup>t naturally with the working methods of the decision makers and not impede them [10,21,46]. Having this in mind, the software showcased in this paper not only records the decision rationale but aids decision making with Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis and Probabilistic Forecasting, allows the user to explore alternative decisions by changing key parameter values and is an integrated assessment tool where relevant results from different optimisation models and numerical simulation packages can be combined to <sup>fi</sup>nd the most appropriate course of action for a complex problem.

This paper is organised as follows. Section 2 introduces the reader to the three DSS methodologies that are incorporated in OUTDO. Section 3 details the decision support system framework to be used in OUTDO. Section 4 showcases OUTDO with a case study to <sup>fi</sup>nd the recommended electricity generation option for the Yorkshire and Humber region. This section is divided into several subsections where each subsection describes a part of the decision framework or a feature of OUTDO. Section 5 discusses and presents the limitations of the application of OUTDO to the case study. Section 6 presents the conclusions and future work recommended for OUTDO.

## 2. Introduction to MCDA, rationale management and probabilistic forecasting

This section introduces the three DSS methodologies that constitute OUTDO. The three techniques are Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA, used to make decisions involving multi-criteria issues), rationale management (used to record the decision rationale) and probabilistic forecasting (used to predict how parameters might vary in the future).

MCDA is a structured methodology for supporting decision making that is suitable for addressing complex problems, that is, problems featuring high uncertainty, different forms of incommensurable data, long term consequences, multiplicity of interests and con<sup>fl</sup>icting assessments [15,49,60,62]. As many decision makers might be involved in the decision process, MCDA allows the representation of multiple and possibly con<sup>fl</sup>icting views in the form of different criteria weight sets, thereby allowing the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of important con<sup>fl</sup>icts and/or opportunities for compromise [36]. MCDA may be applied using a variety of methods; some of them are reviewed in [9,60].

The Weighted Sum Model (WSM<sup>5</sup>) is probably the most commonly used MCDA approach [9] and is the most important method in OUTDO. The WSM expression as de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Fishburn [27] is shown in Eq. (1).

$$
A _ {W S M - S C O R E} = \max \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} a _ {i j} w _ {j} f o r i = 1, 2, 3, \dots m\tag{1}
$$

where n is the number of decision criteria, m is the number of options, $a _ { i j }$ is the performance (so called score) of the i-th option in terms of the j-th criterion and $w _ { j }$ is the weight of the j-th criterion. To make sure that all the criteria contribute in proportion to their weight, the scores have to be normalised. This is done by multiplying the score by the inverse of the highest score for a criterion.

The results from WSM can be misleading for several reasons. If the problem consists of multi-dimensional criteria, it might be of interest to the decision maker to control the extent to which a variation in a score would affect the decision. Another problem is that the introduction of a new option could affect the normalisation factor, destabilising the ranking of options. In these cases, and where the uncertainties in the weights of criteria and scores are high, other MCDM methods should be used, for example, ELECTRE III as proposed by Roy in 1978 [48]. ELECTRE is an outranking method. The outranking approach seeks to <sup>fi</sup>nd a compromise by balancing the option's poor and well-performing criteria, making the process similar to the way people normally make decisions [50]. ELECTRE III allows inaccurate, imprecise, uncertain and ill-determined data (pseudo-criteria) to be processed [26]. It is particularly appropriate when a small number of criteria are used to compare a large number of options [60]. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c ELECTRE III version implemented in OUTDO was taken from [9]. However, ELECTRE III can be complex to apply and counterintuitive, thus it is not explored in detail in this paper.

Decision rationale is concerned with the explicit representation of decisions and with the systematic presentation of the reasons why the decisions were made [39]. Among the existing decision rationale methodologies, Issue Based Information Systems (IBIS<sup>6</sup>) is the one with the strongest intellectual appeal in the research community due to its combination of simplicity and expressive power [10] and, thus, was included in the framework. IBIS graphically represents decision processes. Issues in the decision process are linked to possible solutions (called options). In order to <sup>fi</sup>nd the most appropriate solution, arguments are added to support or oppose each option. Once an option is recommended other issues might arise from the recommended option and these issues are then treated the same way. This process, therefore, results in a hierarchy of issues. A change in one decision may trigger a series of ripple effects to other parts of the decision space. With IBIS, it is possible to trace back an invalid decision and correct it [21].

Having a process for documenting the rationale for an important decision helps in the quality control of the decision process, keeps track of possible effects when requirements for an artefact change (an artefact is anything created by a human that does not exist in nature, including abstract things such as decision processes [61]), and provides useful information to people who may seek to reuse solutions [5,21,46,58]. It is still often the case that large amounts of information relevant to decision processes are never documented and are stored only in the heads of the decision makers [35]. Often, working groups repeatedly discuss the same issues that had been resolved earlier, as the justi<sup>fi</sup>cations for a given decision might not have been clearly understood by all members of the group, or possibly the project group changes over time. If no comprehensive record of these discussions exists, the context in which key decisions were made may be lost [46]. Lastly, a variety of stakeholders involved in decision processes often have different sets of objectives and priorities, capturing the decision rationale makes the process explicit and supports a consistent view among stakeholders [22,58]. A list of tools for the recording of decision rationale can be found in [47]. OUTDO is the continuation of the work originating KBDS [5] and DRAMA [11]. Other tools similar to OUTDO are AOOPA [21] and IVMM [59], however they do not allow for a dynamic adaptation of the rationale with varying parameters and probabilistic forecasting.

Forecasting is an essential skill in the majority of decision making processes because all actions take time to implement and uncontrol lable external variables inevitably change [38]. A decision taken today may not be the recommended decision in a month's time, and the ability to anticipate changes or analyse risks is key to empowering people to make better decisions in the present. Often, there will be great uncertainty in how a variable will behave in the future. The use of con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals opens a door for the creation of a range of predicted values and their probabilities. There is no guaranteed ‘correct’ method of computing con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals, although some methodologies are assumed appropriate for certain forecasting approaches. For OUTDO, Hanbury [28] has established a method for the creation of con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals (see Appendix A). This method has the advantage of being applicable to any forecasting technique, and to estimate con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals using the mean error when the forecast is compared with the actual data. The next section presents the decision framework to be used in OUTDO.

Other studies have been used to support decisions on electricity generation technologies for different countries. In the case of the UK, there is an emphasis on the MARKAL numerical models [51,55]. MARKAL, however, only oversees the technical aspects of energy systems. There are other issues, which might be more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to model but which must be included in the decision, such as the extra infrastructure and professional labour required, the impact on the local communities and the environment, etc. [30]. Overall, there are various studies for electricity generation technology planning based on MCDA, such as [7,15,44,60], probabilistic forecasting, such as [23,37] and rationale management, such as [11]. However, to our knowledge, no study integrates all the methodologies as presented in this paper.

## 3. OUTDO (Oxford University Tool for Decision Organisation)

OUTDO (version 1.0) is a user-friendly, integrated decision support tool suitable for complex decision making. OUTDO provides tools to support and record the decision process allowing the decision maker to develop its own personalised system for decision support, suitable for his/her particular interests and context. Its use is suggested for long term, complex and interacting decisions that involve a number of different issues and con<sup>fl</sup>icting criteria.

The Compendium software (version 1.5.2) was used as the basis for the development of OUTDO as it is an open-source application in Java and has an active development community [16]. OUTDO is the result of more than 15 years of research [4,6,33] <sup>fi</sup>rstly in Edinburgh University and later at the University of Oxford in collaboration with Oxford Brookes University. The initial development of OUTDO was made by Skrzypczak (2007) [1,54]. The features of the current version are explained in this section.

## 3.1. Decision framework and OUTDO features

The decision support framework that is to be used within OUTDO is shown in Fig. 1.

This framework intends to support the creation and analysis of decision processes. At the same time, it keeps a record of decision rationale. An example of a decision process is shown in Fig. 3 in Section 4. The creation of the decision process starts with the Decision Configuration block. Its objective is to construct a hierarchy of issues describing in detail some possible outcomes to resolve a problem and present these results in a transparent and easy to follow manner. This block begins with the Definition of the Problem task [14,34] where the stakeholders concerned de<sup>fi</sup>ne the goals and the main problem to be resolved, called an Issue node (the node with a question mark in Fig. 3). The existing barriers to reach the speci<sup>fi</sup>ed goals are expressed as sub-issues of the main Issue node, during the Issues Group Brainstorming task. In order to keep track of the hierarchy of issues the Configuration of IBIS Structure task [10] is performed. Finally, options that address those issues are suggested and the most promising ones are listed and linked to the issue they relate to (Options Description and Selection task). Fig. 3 shows the results of this process when applied to the analysis of electricity generation sources. A window called “Goals vs. Criteria” allows the user to add new Criteria and Goals to the decision process. Criteria are used to quantitatively measure different aspects that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the comparison of options. They are the means by which Goals are expressed in the decision process. Check box columns are used to clarify which Criteria are related to which Goals in order to record the intent of the decision process, as shown in Fig. 2.

Once a variety of courses of action have been considered, each Issue node has to be analysed in detail with MCDA (especially WSM) in order to <sup>fi</sup>nd a recommended solution in the Solution Development block. The Solution Development block automatically adds the Option nodes linked to an Issue node as part of a Decision Matrix (based on the WSM method). Note, for example, that the Option nodes shown at the top of Fig. 3 correspond to the Options columns in the Decision Matrix of Fig. 5 in Section 4. The Solution Development block is de<sup>fi</sup>ned by <sup>fi</sup>lling the Decision Matrix cells: the criteria used to compare the potential options have to be carefully selected (Criteria Creation task) and weighted (Criteria Weighting task). Finally, the options are scored (Options vs. Criteria Scoring task) and the recommended option found (WSM Recommendation task). There are two views of the Decision

• Fixed manually by the user. This is the most straightforward way to set a Global Parameter value and should be used if a quick change in parameter is needed, without further analysis. An example is CO2\_E\_Cost (cost of ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emissions in GBP per kg $\mathbf { \left( 0 _ { 2 } \right) }$ in Fig. 7.

As the trader's personalised decision rationale is already recorded, he or she would only have to update the stock or commodity prices to get a new recommendation. • Imported from Excel. The user can extract a Global Parameter value from a cell in an Excel <sup>fi</sup>le. This allows the user to create his or her own models in Excel to improve the data used for comparing different options. An example is Desal\_Capital in Fig. 7. This is the capital cost for desalinating 100 million litres of water per day calculated with DEEP<sup>9</sup> 4, an Excel based application [31]. Some external models can be connected to OUTDO through Excel (e.g. Aspen Plus [3]). OUTDO offers a means by which scattered information from different sources can be brought together in a meaningful and organised way. • Varying with respect to time. A parameter t is available in the Global Parameters window to represent time. The user can create parameters that are time dependent expressions. An example is Gas\_Fuel in Fig. 7. It represents the fuel cost of a NGCC plant according to future predictions as shown in [42].

• Associated with the resolution of an issue. A change in recommendation for one Issue node might affect the resolution of another Issue node. An example is CCS\_Coal\_Yes in Fig. 7. This parameter will be equal to 1 if CCS is recommended for Coal based generation and 0 if not.

The user can then analyse how a change in the Global Parameters would affect the whole decision process. This is done by updating the Decision Matrix of every Issue node in the decision network (Propagate Change task). This will update the decision matrices of all Issue nodes that make reference to those Global Parameters. The Issue nodes whose recommendation changed as a result of the propagation (Change in Recommendation report) are reported in a table, such as the one shown in Fig. 8 in Section 4.

There are two general approaches for applying OUTDO, i.e. to standardise or to record decision processes. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is used if the decision process is going to be used to solve not one, but a number of similar decisions and even possibly be used to compare different decisions. This approach is time consuming as most options must be analysed further and varying parameters should be included in the analysis. The second approach is used when the decision is supposed to be used once or only for a few times. In this approach, only the selected options are analysed further. However, it is up to the user to decide the granularity at which the design rationale should be recorded and how to proceed with the MCDA.

Other examples of how the Global Parameters feature can be used are listed below:

• The feature can be used to create a decision process for one location and apply the same rationale for a different location. An example is when a company wishes to <sup>fi</sup>nd the best energy sources to supply clean and affordable heat and electricity services for a house. As parameters such as solar radiation, wind speed, house size, garden size (needed to install a heat pump) and, most importantly, the needs of each customer change, OUTDO can easily adapt the decision process to the new location and engage the customer in the decision process with his/her own inputs.

• External parameters relevant to a decision process inevitably change with time. If there is a problem with a solution previously decided using OUTDO, the decision maker can reuse the decision process, trace back to the decision that caused the problem, change the Global Parameters that varied since the decision was made, and <sup>fi</sup>nd the new recommended option. An example is when a reactor in a chemical plant needs to be replaced. Due to technical progress and changes in the market since the design and construction of the plant (e.g. cost of energy), there may be better options than to repeat the installation of the same type of reactor [58].

• Before making a <sup>fi</sup>nal decision, the user can study how different future scenarios would affect the decision process. For this reason the Sensitivity Analysis feature can be used to analyse how a given percentage variation in the value of a single Global Parameter would affect the decision process.

• Finally, the user can create probabilistic forecasts of how Global Parameters might behave in the future and, by importing these values into the decision matrix, a plot showing the probability that the options will be recommended as a function of time can be generated (Future Analysis block). This plot can then be used to evaluate the best options to be considered in the short, medium and long terms.

## 4. Case study: electricity generation in the UK

OUTDO's features are showcased with a study to <sup>fi</sup>nd the recommended sources of electricity generation in different locations in the UK, paying attention to water consumption and water puri<sup>fi</sup>cation with hybrid power and desalination plants. The UK's energy sector has embraced the challenge to reduce its CO emissions by 80% from 1990 levels by 2050 and still provide cheap and reliable energy. Decisions have to be made as soon as possible as a quarter of the UK's power stations are old and need replacing in the next <sup>fi</sup>ve years [13,53]. Interest in the production of electricity from coal and gas with CCS have been driving current policies but the technology is not mature yet, increases water consumption considerably and early investment is risky as the technology is under steep development. Furthermore, the UK's water supply sector needs essential infrastructure development as the frequency of dry summers is increasing and the population in water scarce regions is growing, especially in the South East. It may seem hard to believe but the South East of England has less water per person than countries such as Morocco and Egypt [12]. Desalination has already been implemented for part of the London water supply [57] and it has been considered for Brighton [32]. In 2007 electricity generation accounted for 30% of the fresh water abstraction in England and Wales [24], highlighting the urgent need to account for water availability when planning for future elec tricity generation.

## 4.1. Decision configuration

The above considerations represent part of the Definition of the Problem task in the Decision Configuration block (Fig. 1). Additional information on issues involving electricity generation in the UK were extracted from Jim Skea et al. [53] and the DECC White Paper [20]. The list of electricity generation technologies and their corresponding parameters such as cost, emissions, land and water use, etc., were taken from the $G \mathrm { E M I S } ^ { 1 1 }$ 4.6 database [42] and from the National Energy Technology Laboratory $( { \mathrm { N E T L } } ^ { 1 2 } )$ [41]. The GEMIS 4.6 database contains data from Germany and the NETL [41] publication has data from the US. The costs were converted from Euros and USD to GBP. Parameters such as solar radiation, wind speed, biomass plantation yield, were adapted to the UK. In the case of water, data for the cooling technologies was taken from [7,24,40,56]. Fig. 3 shows the results of this process. Firstly, the best source of electricity for a given location in the UK is analysed with the decision matrix shown in Fig. 5 (more details in the Solution Development section). The <sup>fi</sup>rst author has studied ten different locations in the UK for the construction of a new 700 MW power plant (or the equivalent to 700 MW capacity in solar PV,<sup>13</sup> onshore wind and geothermal projects) at each selected location. These locations are London, South West England, South East England, Yorkshire and the Humber, East England, North West England, South Wales, North Wales, Oxfordshire and Scotland. The Yorkshire and the Humber region was selected in this study because in the North Lincolnshire unitary authority area, water is over-abstracted (existing abstraction is causing unacceptable damage to the environment at low river <sup>fl</sup>ows) [24] and there is a demand for combined heat and power for district heating and industry. An example of a cogeneration (electricity and heating) plant in this area is the Immingham Power Station, which is the biggest cogeneration plant in Europe with a capacity of 730 MWe [45].

With the given weights of criteria and the Global Parameters values shown in Fig. 7, Coal with CCS was the recommended option, more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally IGCC with second stage Selexol for carbon capture and deep, saline aquifer for geological carbon storage, to be implemented in the Yorkshire and the Humber region in 2040. The inclusion or exclusion of CCS for Coal is decided in Fig. 4 (discussed later). Hydropower was not included in this study because its theoretical capacity would provide only 3% of the country's 2020 renewable energy electricity needs [25]. Map nodes (circles in purple) were used to better organise the decision process. The nodes contain further detail on the energy technologies that require cooling. For example, Fig. 4 shows the Coal Map node. Connecting to the Map nodes, the “How should the waste heat be consumed or dispersed?” Issue node, represents a detailed study of possible ways to use the waste heat from a power plant (usually 60% of the energy from a Coal power station [41]) with “Combined heat and power” or with the “Desalination of sea water”, or output the waste heat to the environment with “Dry cooling” (which might be expensive) or with “Wet Cooling” (which requires a large amounts of fresh water). More detail on this Issue node is shown in the External models section.

Most of the data and information in Fig. 4 were taken from [41]. It starts by comparing an Integrated Gasi<sup>fi</sup>cation Combined Cycle $( \mathrm { I G C C ^ { 1 4 } } )$ with Pulverised Coal $( \mathsf { P C } ^ { 1 5 } )$ and Conventional Steam turbine technologies. It <sup>fi</sup>nds IGCC as the recommended option, mainly because it is the option that requires the least money to include CCS.

Three different IGCC plant designs, (a) the General Electric Energy gasi<sup>fi</sup>er in the “radiant only” con<sup>fi</sup>guration $( \mathrm { G E E } ^ { 1 6 }$ Radiant Only), (b) the ConocoPhillips gasi<sup>fi</sup>er technology $( \mathsf { C o P \ E { - } } \mathsf { G a s } ) ^ { 1 7 }$ and (c) the Shell gasi<sup>fi</sup>er technology (Shell) are compared. GEE Radiant Only is the recommended option using the criteria weights, which are the same as in the main Issue Node to keep consistency throughout the decision process. Finally, the last issue is to decide whether CCS should be included or not. In the case of IGCC designs, which use precombustion technology for carbon capture and assuming a ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emissions cost of 0.035 £/kg, CCS is recommended for the GEE and CoP designs using a technology based on Selexol, but it is not recommended for the Shell design. Selexol is a solvent (mixture of dimethyl ethers) used to absorb ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ and $S 0 _ { 2 } .$ . Note that in the case of PC, which uses post-combustion technology for carbon capture (Amine Absorber), CCS is not recommended. According to the DECC [18] ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emission costs predictions, used in the Future Analysis section, the low ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emission costs estimate for 2040 is 0.068 £/kg. The ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emission costs of 0.035 £/kg was assumed for 2040 to exemplify the application of the Propagation feature. This exercise does not affect the <sup>fi</sup>nal conclusion of the paper, which is given in the Future Analysis and Discussion and limitations sections.

## 4.2. Solution development

All the Issue nodes in the decision process have an associated decision matrix used to compare the options. To maintain coherence throughout the decision process, the criteria and criteria weights chosen to compare the options were the same for energy related issues, with few exceptions such as the inclusion of the “Diversity of supply”, “Peak load response” [29] and “Risk aversion” [29] criteria that are only relevant for the most general Issue node “What is the best source of electricity for a given location in the UK?”. Fig. 5 shows the Values view of the decision matrix for this Issue node, each value being the result of the operations on the expressions in terms of the constants and Global Parameters in the Formula view. For some criteria (e.g. “CO equivalent (kg/MWh)”), the larger the score, the worse it is for the option being compared. In these cases, the weight of criteria has a negative value.

As it can be seen, for the case of the Yorkshire and the Humber region, Coal with CCS is the recommended option to be considered in 2040 assuming $\mathsf { a } \mathsf { C O } _ { 2 }$ emission cost of 0.035 £/kg, the Global Parameter values shown in Fig. 7 and the given weights of criteria. Another important thing to notice is that the “Water usage (kg/MWh)” criterion has some scores with negative values. This is because water is being produced as “Desalination of sea water” is the recommended option to the “How should the waste heat be consumed or dispersed?” Issue node. In addition, with the assumed ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emissions cost, Gas does not include CCS (this decision is made in the Gas Map node, which is not shown) and, therefore, it has higher ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emissions than Coal with CSS as shown in Fig. 5. Fig. 6 shows part of the three <sup>fi</sup>rst columns and rows from the decision matrix Formula view. The cost metrics utilised in this study provide an estimate for capturing, transporting, storing and monitoring carbon dioxide for a “favourable” sequestration project, and may vary signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly based on variables such as terrain to be crossed by the pipeline, reservoir characteristics, and the number of landowners from which sub-surface rights must be acquired. For more detail on the uncertainties regarding the cost of CCS see [41].

## 4.4. Propagation

Once the user changes the Global Parameters, the Propagation feature will update all Issue nodes with the new values. To simplify the analysis, only one Global Parameter, ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emissions cost (CO2\_E\_Cost), was changed from 0.035 £/kg to 0.203 £/kg in order to showcase this feature. The other Global Parameter values are the same as in Fig. 7. Fig. 8 shows the changes in the recommendation resulting from this change and its propagation. ## 4.6. External models

OUTDO is to be used as an integrated assessment tool where the analysis of different options may be strengthened with different numerical simulation packages and optimisation models. These models are not part of OUTDO, for this reason they are called External Modules. Three external models were used in the analysis of the Issue node “How should the waste heat be consumed or dispersed?” (in Fig. 3). The models calculate the capital costs, operation and management costs, water usage and net plant ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the different options attached to the Issue node in a NGCC plant. The net plant ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is calculated using Eq. (3).

$$
\eta = \frac {W _ {o u t} - W _ {i n}}{Q _ {H V}}\tag{3}
$$

where η is the net plant ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, $W _ { o u t }$ is the electricity generated from the turbines, $W _ { i n }$ is the electricity consumed in the process and $Q _ { H V }$ is the gas combustion heat (assuming High Heat Value $\mathrm { ( H H V ^ { 1 8 } ) } ;$ ).

The models are the Desalination Economic Evaluation Program (DEEP) [31] used to simulate a hybrid NGCC power and desalination plant, the Integrated Environmental Control Model (IECM<sup>19</sup>) [40] used to simulate cooling towers (wet cooling) and dry coolers for NGCC power plants, and a simulation model of an IGCC power plant in Aspen Plus taken from [2]. This model was adapted to represent a NGCC CHP plant.

Fig. 12 shows the flowsheet of a NGCC CHP plant in Aspen Plus. The simulation was used to calculate how a change in condensation temperature (stream $" 2 5 "$ in the diagram) affects the net plant ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciency. The lower the temperature of stream $" 2 5 "$ , the more electricity can be generated from the $\ " \mathrm { L P " }$ turbine in the diagram. However, if the condensation temperature is too low (close to ambient temperature), the heat from condensation would have to be wasted. If the condensation temperature is relatively high $( 7 0 – 1 8 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ , the heat can be used for district heating, for industrial purposes or to desalinate sea water. For desalination, the condensation temperature in DEEP was assumed to be $7 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and resulted in a net plant ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of 47.3%. Two condensation temperatures of $1 0 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and $1 2 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ were used in the simulation in Fig. 12 and their respective net plant ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies were found to be 44.6% and 43.4%.

Fig. 13 shows the results from the external models used in the decision matrix for the “How should the waste heat be consumed or dispersed?” Issue node. Two other criteria were created for this decision matrix. The “Proximity to sea” criterion is used to veto the “Desalination of sea water” and “Once through (sea water)” options if the plant is to be built in the countryside. And the “Low grade heat demand” criterion is used to quantify the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using the heat from the condensation of the steam. The data for “Once Through (sea water)” option was taken from [56].

## 5. Discussion and limitations

The Future Analysis window of OUTDO only allows the creation of con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals for one variable at a time. Other Global Parameters such as interest rate, gas prices, coal prices, capital costs, CCS costs, social risk aversion and availability of water are very uncertain and should be fully analysed with probabilistic forecasts in order to properly analyse the risks associated with the <sup>fi</sup>nal decision. Therefore, the possibility of creating probabilistic forecasts for more than one Global Parameter at a time and to propagate them to the decision matrices would greatly improve the analysis.

Another limitation is that the Future Analysis procedure only focuses on one Issue node. But the modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of a Global Parameter can change the recommendation of other Issue nodes in the decision process. The change in recommendation could, in turn, affect the Issue being analysed. This can clearly be seen in the horizontal bar chart in Fig. 16. After 10 years (in 2020), when the ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emission cost is 0.03 £/kg, the use of CCS will be recommended for Coal IGCC with a GEE technology. This should change the recommendation of Coal without CCS option to Coal with CCS in the main Issue node during Future Analysis, but this is not the case. The solution is to propagate the values from the probabilistic forecast to all the decision matrices for each Global Parameter value change. However, if this were to be included in the current version of OUTDO, each graph would take a long time to converge to a solution (the convergence is not scalable with the number of decisions in the project). If this desired functionality were to be implemented, the resulting Future Analysis graph would look similar to Fig. 15 for the <sup>fi</sup>rst 10 years, as CCS is not recommended, but will gradually become like Fig. 16 as ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emission costs increase and CCS is recommended. In other words, for the Yorkshire and the Humber region, Gas without CCS is recommended from 2010 until 2020, Coal with CCS is recommended from 2021 until 2031, Nuclear is recommended from 2032 until 2042 and Biomass with CCS is recommended from 2042 onwards. These recommendations seem sensible, but it should be stressed that they depend strongly on the data used for the case study, the particular selection of criteria and their corresponding weights (selected by the author).

Regarding the case study, there are some aspects of the electricity generation processes that could have received more attention such as geopolitical con<sup>fl</sup>icts, public opinion, political pressure, the environmental impact of coal mining, the disposal of solid waste from coal power plants and radioactive waste from nuclear power plants, etc. For example:

• The steam coal for power plants comes from Russia, a country that has cut off energy supplies to external countries in the past. In 2010, 45.5% of the steam coal consumed in the UK was imported, mainly from Russia [19]. The case study assumes that half of the steam coal consumed in the Yorkshire and the Humber region is from Russia and the other half is from coal mines in the Yorkshire and the Humber region.

• The disposal of solid and radioactive waste is included in the cost assessment, but the potential environmental impacts were not included in the decision process. The residue of the coal burning process is fairly toxic, and may contain signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amounts of uranium. Indeed, some coal contains so much uranium that some of its residual ash has been further processed in China to remove the uranium for the nuclear fuel cycle [43].

There are many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the use of the Weighted Sum Method to compare the options of each Issue node such as simplicity and transparency. However, OUTDO also inherits the limitations of WSM, such as the need to choose a normalisation factor and the lack of functions that control the way the options are scored as can be done with Multi-Attribute Utility Theory software.

Finally, an important limitation for OUTDO is that only one user can add information at a time. This limitation has been thoroughly discussed by the Compendium Institute community [16]. We plan to update OUTDO accordingly when a new version of Compendium which allows multiple users to work simultaneously becomes available.

## 6. Conclusions and future work

Documenting the decision process and applying MCDA instead of simpler assessments can be very resource-intensive. However, this paper foresees the importance of a <sup>fl</sup>exible framework that, in the context of uncertain technology deployment, records the essence of decision processes and aids decision making with in-depth multicriteria analysis and forecasts. This, results in robust and transparent solutions for complex decision processes, which can be effortlessly adjusted to varying parameters.

With this in mind, a new software called OUTDO was created to capture and reuse the decision rationale, using a framework based on MCDA with connectivity to external calculation models. In addition, the integration of probabilistic forecasting and MCDA provides insights for long term decision planning. These and other features were presented in a case study to <sup>fi</sup>nd the most appropriate electricity generation technology for the Yorkshire and the Humber region in the UK with particular attention to the availability of water.

Turning OUTDO's recommendations shown in the Discussion and limitations section into a <sup>fi</sup>nal decision must take the decision's time horizon into account. Every prediction has a risk, and the more into the future the recommendation is made, the higher the risk. There might be so many variables in<sup>fl</sup>uencing a forecast that it can be argued it is impossible to determine the magnitude of the risks involved. The decision maker could add further arguments to the OUTDO analysis and make a decision different from the OUTDO <sup>fi</sup>nal recommendation. For instance, the decision maker could decide to choose a more conventional decision such as Nuclear with the risk of it becoming obsolete in the future due to its high costs or choose a more uncertain decision that may become more robust in the future such as coal with CCS.

This paper concludes that, assuming the predictions for ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emissions cost from DECC [18], if a new 700 MW power plant is to be built in the Yorkshire and the Humber region from 2010 until 2050, the following technologies should be considered. From 2010 up to 2020, the selected technology should be Natural Gas Combined Cycle, without CCS but with sea water desalination. As ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emission costs increase and CCS becomes relevant after 2020, the recommended technology changes to Coal (Integrated Gasi<sup>fi</sup>cation Combined Cycle design) with CCS between 2021 and 2031. Nuclear power plants should perhaps not be considered for the Yorkshire and the Humber region, because nuclear power becomes advantageous only for a ten year span, and a Nuclear power plant has a much longer lifetime. However, as ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emissions cost predictions from DECC [18] might be too high, Biomass based generation with CCS should not be considered after 2042 and therefore Nuclear becomes the selected technology between 2031 and 2050.

Current developments in OUTDO explore the inclusion of semantics as an attempt to make the decision maker aware of aspects of the decision process that he or she might not have considered, and to run consistency checks. Ontologies (a technique for semantic representation) [8] will be used to represent the domain knowledge of different energy technologies and policies to support the decision process with consistency checks, inference of constraints and the provision of general information on the energy <sup>fi</sup>eld.

The method for the creation of con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals proposed by Hanbury [28] has the advantage of being applicable to any forecasting technique, and to derive projected con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals using the mean error when the forecast is compared with the actual data. The method is outlined below and the associated equations are shown in Fig. 17:

1) Load the time series data and create a forecast.

2) Assume that the error is likely to grow according to some de<sup>fi</sup>ned function with a forecast horizon, for example a power law: $e = a$ $f ^ { n } + b ,$ where e is the error, f is the discrete number of forecast points at that moment in time and a, b and n are all constants (the value of the rate of growth n and b could be determined from empirical results). The expected forecast Mean Absolute Deviation $( \mathrm { M A D } ^ { 2 0 }$ is the average difference between the data and the forecast) times the total forecast horizon (F, shown in the plot of Fig. 17) should be equal to the area underneath the curve, or

$$
\begin{array}{l} M A D _ {f o r e c a s t} * F = \int_ {0} ^ {F} e. d f \\ \Rightarrow M A D _ {f o r e c a s t} * F = \int_ {0} ^ {F} (a f ^ {n} + b) d f \\ \Rightarrow M A D _ {f o r e c a s t} = \frac {a F ^ {n}}{n + 1} + b \\ \Rightarrow a = \frac {n + 1}{F ^ {n}} (M A D _ {f o r e c a s t} - b) \end{array}

## Appendix B

Calculation of probabilities for future decision analysis

For each point in time in Fig. 14 the system propagates the value of the Global Parameter $( \mathsf { G P } ^ { 2 1 } )$ to the decision matrix of the selected issue starting from its lowest value (at the −90% line). The con<sup>fi</sup>dence interval range (from 90 to −90%) is divided into 80 intervals of equal size. This number of intervals can be higher than 80, but this would require more computation time. Each interval is assigned a GP value corresponding to the middle value of each interval. This value is then propagated to the decision matrix and a recommended option is found. All recommendations are gathered and a <sup>fi</sup>nal recommendation option probability is calculated.

The calculation of the probability for each recommended option is shown in Eq. (A.1).

$$
\begin{array}{r l} R O P _ {i} & = \left(\frac {x \times 1 5}{\left(G P _ {9 0 \%} - G P _ {7 5 \%}\right) \times 2} + \frac {y \times 2 5}{\left(G P _ {7 5 \%} - G P _ {5 0 \%}\right) \times 2} + \frac {z \times 5 0}{\left(G P _ {5 0 \%} - G P _ {- 5 0 \%}\right)}\right) \\ & \times \left(\frac {G P _ {9 0 \%} - G P _ {- 9 0 \%}}{8 0}\right) \end{array} \tag {A.1}
$$

$$
x + y + z = 8 0\tag{A.2}
$$

where, ROP is the set of recommended options probabilities and $G P _ { x \% }$ is the Global Parameter value in the 90%, $7 5 \% , 5 0 \% , - 5 0 \% , - 7 5 \%$ and 90% con<sup>fi</sup>dence interval curves. The number 15 is the number of percentage points in the section between the 90% and 75% lines and between the −75% and −90% lines. $( G P _ { 9 0 \% } - G P _ { 7 5 \% } ) x 2$ is the size of the section between the 90% and 75% lines plus the section between the 75% and 90% lines. The second and third terms in the <sup>fi</sup>rst parenthesis have a similar purpose. The expression inside the second parenthesis is the size of one interval. x is the number of intervals that result in the recommendation of option i in the sections between the 90% and 75% lines or between the $- 7 5 \%$ and −90% lines, y is the number of intervals that result in the recommendation of option i in the sections between the 75% and 50% lines or between the 50% and 75% lines and z is the number of intervals that result in the recommendation of option i in the sections between the 50% and −50% lines.

Eq. (A.3) shows an example calculation of the probability of recommendation in the year 2050.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Coal} = \left(\frac {7 \times 15}{(0.377 - 0.302) \times 2} + \frac {0 \times 25}{(0.302 - 0.254) \times 2} + \frac {0 \times 50}{(0.254 - 0.149)}\right) \\ \times \left(\frac {0.377 - 0.026}{80}\right) = 3.07 \% \\ \text {Nuclear} = \left(\frac {11 \times 15}{(0.377 - 0.302) \times 2} + \frac {11 \times 25}{(0.302 - 0.254) \times 2} + \frac {1 \times 50}{(0.254 - 0.149)}\right) \\ \times \left(\frac {0.377 - 0.026}{80}\right) = 19.48 \% \\ \text {Biomass} = \left(\frac {17 \times 15}{(0.377 - 0.302) \times 2} + \frac {11 \times 25}{(0.302 - 0.254) \times 2} + \frac {22 \times 50}{(0.254 - 0.149)}\right) \\ \times \left(\frac {0.377 - 0.026}{80}\right) = 65.99 \% \end{array} \tag{A.3}
$$

In Eq. (A.3), the sum of the probabilities of the recommended options is 88.54%. If the number of intervals were in<sup>fi</sup>nite, instead of 80, the sum of the probabilities would be exactly 90%.

Julian Hunt is interested in Decision Support Systems and their application to renewable energy policies, project selection and implementation. Currently, he is developing an extension module for the Compendium software tool with the objective of joining the IBIS (Issue Based Information Systems) methodology with Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis methods. Further research is also ongoing in the application of my extension module to real world problems and the addition of Semantics (ontologies) to the system. The goal of my research is to develop a system which will allow decision makers and stakeholders to achieve environmental targets, stimulate the renewable energy infrastructure and increase energy security through decision selection

Rene Banares-Alcantara has a Bachelors degree in Chemical Engineering from the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México (UNAM). He then studied in Carnegie Mellon University where he obtained both Master and PhD degrees in Chemical Engineering. Upon completion of his studies he worked for various academic institutions including the Instituto de Investigaciones Eléctricas, the Facultad de Química (UNAM) and the University of Edinburgh. He has been associated to the Department of Engineering Science (as a Reader) and to New College (as a Fellow) since 2003.

His general research interest since 1980 has been on the application of Computer Science techniques to Engineering, focusing on the use of Arti<sup>fi</sup>cial Intelligence techniques in Chemical Engineering design, synthesis and diagnosis. His current research interests are in the areas of development of computer-based support systems for the formulation of policies related to transport, energy and water.
