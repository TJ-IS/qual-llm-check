# The Consensus Effect in Online Health-Care Communities

LU (LUCY) YAN AND YONG TAN

LU (LUCY) YAN (yanlucy@indiana.edu) is an assistant professor of information systems at the Kelley School of Business, Indiana University. She holds a Ph.D. in business administration from the Foster School of Business, University of Washington. Her research interests include social media, social networking, and patient-centric health-care models. Her recent studies investigate the impact brought by social media to health care, especially for patients with social diseases. She has published in Information Systems Research.

YONG TAN (ytan@uw.edu; corresponding author) is the Neal and Jan Dempsey Professor of Information Systems at the Michael G. Foster School of Business, University of Washington, and Chang Jiang Scholar Chair Visiting Professor at the School of Economics and Management, Tsinghua University, China. His research interests include social media and networks, mobile and electronic commerce, big data analytics, and economics of information systems. He has published in Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Management Science, MIS Quarterly, and Operations Research, among others. He is a senior editor of Information Systems Research and a member of the Board of Editors of the Journal of Management Information Systems.

ABSTRACT: Online health-care communities have become increasingly popular among patients, enabling them to connect to a large population of patients who suffer from similar health problems and to access massive amounts of health-related information. We are interested in investigating how other patients’ consensus on treatment experiences affects patients’ perceived treatment effectiveness. In this regard, we use the cue diagnosticity framework to examine patients’ shared treatment reviews. By controlling individual heterogeneity and the inhomogeneous weighting function of social influence on patients, we find that consensus has a positive impact on patients’ perceived treatment effectiveness. This positive effect, however, is negatively moderated by the characteristics of the shared information, including volume and patients’ precommitment and social connectedness. Overall, we find that perceived treatment effectiveness is closely related to community participants’ perceptions about the treatment. These findings can be used to help health-care practitioners incorporate patients’ experiences into health-care systems and develop effective interventions to help patients better engage in their disease management.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: cue diagnosticity, health-information sharing, latent variable model, online health care, perceived treatment effectiveness, random coefficient model, social network.

As the number of patients who rely on online health information has steadily increased, the search for health information through online healthcare communities has drawn considerable attention. Over 100 million Americans are consumers of “Health 2.0” services [30], and about 80 percent of Internet users have looked online for information on health-related topics [15]. The results of a more recent survey reveal a similar trend, specifically, that 72 percent of U.S. adult Internet users search for health-related information online, 26 percent have read others’ comments on their experiences, and 18 percent have gone online to look for others with similar health concerns [16].

These changes in people’s health behaviors are driven by the broad adoption of social media technologies, which support communication and enable individuals to find and share health information, research rare conditions and new treatments, and exchange experiential and anecdotal knowledge. Further, the use of social media applications has increased the speed of the formation of health-care communities [1, 14]. Accordingly, patients are transforming themselves from passive information receivers to active participants who are building knowledge collaboratively [35], and peer-based health-care communities are becoming powerful members of the healthcare ecosystem, with an expanding role and potential influence in multiple sectors.

Despite the consensus that social media and online health-care communities can have profound implications [14, 35], we have yet to understand whether and how shared personal health information and medical experiences influence patients health knowledge and evaluations of health outcomes. Research in this arena is more likely to focus on the effectiveness of emotional and informational support that patients provide to each other in the virtual space [34, 35, 50], and little attention has been paid to understand the potential influence of patient-generated information on others’ health decisions. In this regard, Lefebvre and Bornkessel [25] find few experimental studies on the relationship between social networks and health behavior and health outcomes and, thus, conclude that the use of social media for health improvement is underexplored, particularly in the case of online health-care communities. This is an important topic because increased participation in online healthcare communities, as an extension of the traditional realm of health information technology (HIT), empowers patients to obtain relevant information more easily and act on it; it also strengthens the potential pathways for patients to influence one another’s decision making. Although patient-generated content is often valued, it is underused in health care and health promotion [25].

Although online consumers’ reviews have been extensively studied in prior literature, the findings are not applicable to health-care communities because considerable context-specific characteristics are associated with patient-generated content in such communities. First, prior studies on word-of mouth (WOM) effect focus primarily on cultural marketplaces, such as movies and books, and examine the impact of online reviews on sales. Extensive research has considered a range of review dimensions, including reviewers’ characteristics [45], the motivation for contributing online reviews [24], and the characteristics of the reviews [6, 8, 12, 26, 28, 29, 46]. The basic premise of these studies is that there is a purchase process through which users gather, combine, and integrate information to find a product that matches their preferences, thereby reducing the potential for purchase error [18, 41]. Note that this underpinning mechanism is markedly different from the case of treatment reviews generated by patients. Because health care is a highly regulated business, patients cannot make purchase decisions without a physician’s consultation or prescription. In addition, unlike previous research on WOM impact, prior studies in the health-care context find little evidence for a connection between the use of peer-to-peer support communities and improved health outcomes. For instance, Lefebvre and Bornkessel [25] document that health information obtained from social media is transformed into a social health experience rather than into individuals endeavors. This implies that there is lack of evidence of social influence in healthinformation processing in social media platforms.

Moreover, treatment reviews shared among patients can vary dramatically because the patient-generated content is, in fact, their firsthand experiences based on their unique health conditions and knowledge. Because patients may have a variety of medical conditions and be at different stages of a disease, the same treatment could demonstrate varying degrees of effectiveness among individual patients, leading to a variety of perceptions and ratings of the same treatment, each of which emphasizes a certain aspect. In other words, the treatment reviews are not a reflection of the quality of a certain treatment but, rather, the conditioned treatment quality for a medical condition of a focal patient who shared the experience. More important, most of the time, dissatisfaction with the treatment experience is not due to the treatment, per se, but is the result of patients’ poor self-management, for instance, not following prescription instructions. Finally, prior research also finds that the ability to interpret various forms of health information and to integrate such information varies widely across populations [33]. Given such heterogeneity, information- and experiential-based WOM may be weighted differently and given varying interpretations, depending on the patient [46]. Because the extent to which information from others matters in the context of health care, we are interested in exploring how patients use other patients’ shared medical treatment information and in verifying the potential social influence on their perceived treatment outcome.

In particular, we focus on patients’ shared treatment experiences and their reviews of prescription drugs, and examine the effect of patient consensus on treatment ratings. Consensus refers to the degree of agreement and dissension among patients in regard to perceived treatment effectiveness. In line with the cue diagnosticity framework [32], we consider review consensus as an extrinsic cue that affects patients’ treatment evaluations. According to prior research, review consensus is positively related to its persuasiveness [10], and a high level of agreement on product quality is found to be more persuasive [24, 46] and, thus, more influential. Therefore, we expect that treatment consensus will be a source of social influence on patients’ shared treatment evaluations. In addition, because cue diagnosticity is not static but, rather, depends on the existence of other cues [24, 32], we also investigate the interaction effect of consensus with other cues and expect that additional review characteristics (volume and valence) and patient characteristics will further alter the effect of treatment consensus.

Through our empirical analysis of a data set that contains 15 treatments reviewed by over 17,000 patients across 15 months, we find supporting evidence that treatment consensus has a positive impact on subsequent patients’ perceived treatment effectiveness. As expected, the diagnosticity of treatment consensus is altered when there are other cues. For instance, a high volume of reviews weakens the diagnosticity of consensus, whereas valence has no significant effect. In addition, patients who are experienced or more socially connected are less likely to be influenced by others’ treatment consensus.

We believe that our work contributes to the literature in several ways. First, given that patients’ shared treatment experiences are contingent on their health conditions and that the more reviews arrive the more heterogeneous the shared experiences are, the lack of evidence linking health-information seeking to health outcome suggests that social influence may not exist in the health-care context. Our work, which builds on the cue diagnosticity framework, provides evidence that supports the existence of social influence and illustrates the main and moderating effects of shared treatment experiences among patients.

Second, our results highlight the importance of treatment consensus on patients’ healthinformation processing and treatment evaluation. Despite the potential issues associated with treatment reviews contributed by patients (e.g., incomplete or inaccurate information), we find that patients focus more on the agreed experiences among fellow peers and less on a pure quality measure of the treatment. Thus, our findings provide quantitative evidence for the social influence of patient-generated treatment reviews, which supplement the qualitative results from prior survey studies.

Third, by focusing on the user-generated health content setting, we demonstrate additional insights brought by shared treatment reviews. Prior research on patients’ shared health information focuses on the value of treatment details shared in the reviews, such as medication dosage, or of individual patients’ stories of coping but ignores the effect of treatment experiences perceived by patients. Our findings suggest the value of patients perceived treatment effectiveness to other information seekers.

There are also practical incentives to understand how patients’ experiential expertise is being consumed by other patients and their families. Because health behavior is also affected by the outcome that patients expect their actions to produce [3], the knowledge created in learning from other patients’ experiences reduces the uncertainty that a patient may face and helps the patient to establish an appropriate expectation from a certain treatment. Online health-care communities thus have profound implications for how health care can be better facilitated. Understanding how shared health information and experiences are processed, therefore, becomes a necessary step in the design of peer support tools that meet patients’ needs. This is particularly true in view of a growing prevalence of largely unguided advice sharing through social media applications [19]. Thus, our work is beneficial for health-care providers and practitioners as well as systems designers and entrepreneurs behind these online health-care platforms.

## Theoretical Framework

Health-information seeking refers to the process by which patients search for and receive information that can be used to reduce uncertainty regarding their health status and to help them construct a social and personal cognitive sense of health [11]. In this regard, the virtual environment offers the potential for greater equity in accessing health information, for more individually tailored messages, and for patients’ behavioral changes. Among the varied forms of health information, treatment reviews posted by patients provide personal experiences from the patients perspectives. By reviewing and sharing personal experiences with others, patients learn how to interpret treatment outcomes and assess their effectiveness, adjust their expectations about a certain treatment, and compare self-progress with similar others. Of particular note in treatment reviews is that reading reviews does not necessarily influence one’s adoption decision, as evident in the fact that few studies investigate the effect of WOM of treatment reviews on sales. Hence, it remains unclear whether treatment reviews in the online health-care community will have the type of influence observed in other social contexts.

In this study, we propose that treatment reviews have value and can help patients to improve their health knowledge and to better engage in disease self-management. In the following, we first describe the cue diagnosticity framework as the theoretical foundation that we use to examine the existence of social influence. Then we explain online health-care communities and treatment reviews characteristics, followed by hypotheses development, focusing on the persuasiveness of treatment consensus and its interactions with review and patient characteristics.

## Cue Diagnosticity Framework

In many situations, people do not know the quality of a product before making their purchase decisions. In such cases, the attributes of a product (e.g., price, brand name) often serve as extrinsic cues that may affect people’s perceptions of product quality. The most commonly studied extrinsic cue is product price, and a number of studies have examined the relationship between price and perceived quality [32]. Other extrinsic cues that have been studied include brand name, advertising, retailer reputation, and manufacturer warranties [37]. Research has shown that not all cues will be used in an overall assessment, and some cues will be more important than others when they are combined or integrated [32, 37]. This idea has been conceptualized into the cue diagnosticity framework. In particular, this framework considers product quality assessment to be a categorization process in which available cues are used to help users assign a product to a specific category. In this concept, cues that can suggest one option over alternatives are more diagnostic, whereas cues that cannot discriminate between alternative categories are nondiagnostic. In the case in which multiple cues coexist, the cue diagnosticity framework further posits, based on cue utilization theory, that a cue’s utility varies with its perceived diagnosticity

Specifically, the perceived diagnosticity of some cues depends on the presence of other cues in the decision-making environment [24, 37].

Whereas social media offer convenient channels for users to share their experiences and evaluations, they also generate new forms of cues in addition to the traditional marketing matrix. These cues include WOM volume, valence, and variance, which are available to users simultaneously. Consistent with the cue diagnosticity framework, the bulk of research on the impact of online reviews have found that users formulate evaluations based on combining different cues. Accordingly, the extent to which a cue is used in making a judgment varies with its perceived diagnosticity. For instance, some researchers find that valence is diagnostic and that positive valence implies that a product is in a good category and increases sales [6], whereas others find valence to be nondiagnostic when volume is present because, while the former does not affect sales, the latter does [13, 29]. Sun [41] considers the diagnosticity of valence and variance and finds that review variance is a salient cue that becomes more diagnostic when the review valence is low. Because reviews offer multiple cues, and cue diagnosticity varies in different contexts, there is a need to develop an understanding of how users process multiple cues in making health-related judgments. Of particular importance is a consideration of both the cue diagnosticity and the interactions among the cues.

## Online Health-Care Community and Treatment Reviews

Online health-care communities are a popular venue in which massive information and emotional support can be exchanged among patients, especially among those who suffer from similar health problems [50]. The key characteristic of these communities is the social interaction that enables users to see what their peers and other fellow patients are doing. A field study shows that many participants in online health-care communities have been able to find another patient to help them understand how their specific condition will respond to a specific treatment [47]. Likewise, a recent survey finds that information about specific diseases and medical treatments are the dominant topics sought by those who have looked online [16].

Similar to what is seen in other social media contexts, health-care community users can write and post treatment reviews to share their firsthand experiences, including the symptoms from which they currently suffer and the treatment that they are undertaking. The convenient and interactive format allows patients to exchange experiences in an understanding environment with similar others and to have their validation needs met. This format thus attracts increasingly more patients and their caregivers to online healthcare communities [7, 35]. A recent study reveals that nonprofessionals, such as fellow patients and caregivers, are the preferred source for advice or reassurance on a personal health issue or to learn about coping mechanisms [16].

In contrast to physicians who learn about treatment quality from medical trials, patients have firsthand experiences with treatment quality and can write reviews to describe, in a concrete and pragmatic manner, how effective a certain treatment is for their specific medical conditions [5]. These evaluations are posted by patients in the hope that their experiences may be useful for others, but it does not necessarily lead to any medical endeavors. When reviews are posted in the health-care community, the WOM signals not the treatment quality, per se, but the consequent experience of the treatment contingent on a particular health condition. This provides a possible explanation of why online treatment reviews become part of the shared health experience, rather than influencing decisions.

This interpretation has been documented in several studies. Lefebvre and Bornkessel [25] explore the impact of social media network sites on individuals’ health-information seeking and health-related behaviors. They find one study on how people use social media for health-related purposes, which indicated that although 42 percent of participants looked up consumers’ reviews of health treatments via social media tools, and 45 percent used the obtained health information to seek a second opinion, only 34 percent considered the shared information in their decision to take certain medications. This suggests that patients do not rely on treatment reviews to make decisions but, rather, use the information to seek further advice. In fact, those who read shared treatment experiences indicated the need to determine both the credibility of the message and its applicability to their own circumstances. Despite the effort exerted to examine the impact of treatment reviews on patients’ health behaviors, there is a lack of systematic evidence on the diagnosticity of treatment reviews and the underlying mechanism for how cues in early reviews affect later patients’ treatment ratings.

## Hypotheses Development

Our effort to understand how patients’ reviews influence others’ ratings is based in the persuasiveness of cues, a well-established topic in social influence literature [31]. As noted, if patients use online information to supplement the information obtained from health specialists or to prompt further communications, it is conceivable that they would make use of the majority’s experiences or the experiences of similar others. In the following section, we first explain why consensus is the key cue for patients in the focal context and then hypothesize the influential role that consensus may have.

## Consensus

The consensus on treatment reviews is the level of agreement across reviewers’ opinions on the treatment. If patients have similar treatment experiences (good or bad), consensus is high. A typical example of consensus cases used in WOM studies [24, 46] is the following: high consensus (9.8 percent ratings on 1 star out of 5, 81.9 percent ratings on 2 stars, 7.1 percent on 3 stars, 1.1 percent on 4 stars, and 0.0 percent on 5 stars) and low consensus (65.2 percent ratings on 1 star out of 5, 7.7 percent ratings on 2 stars, 4.1 percent on 3 stars, 7.3 percent on 4 stars, and 15.6 percent on 5 stars). Although these two cases lead to comparable average ratings, the majority of users hold negative opinions in the high-consensus case, whereas in the low-consensus case, the majority of users hold contrasting opinions.

Learning from the experiences of fellow patients with similar health conditions can be powerful, as it helps patients to better understand their health. Notably, this knowledge is created through a collaborative feedback loop among a group of patients [14, 25]. This knowledge creation is termed the wisdom of patients [35]. Because online health-care communities allow enormous amounts of health information to be exchanged among patients, especially with very different health conditions, consensus or dissension may occur, depending on the extent to which patients’ opinions converge or diverge. When integrating information, the individual is challenged by divergence to arrive at a judgment [17]. For patients to make use of these shared experiences, consensus becomes an obvious cue or means for them to learn from others’ opinions while reducing cognitive effort. According to social influence theory, patients develop their own opinions on the basis of the group’s consensus [40]. Further, research has shown that the opinion of the majority is more influential than that of the minority [24] and that taking the majority’s opinions can increase the reliability of these shared experiences and reduce the influence of outliers [46]. As such, consensus is an important cue for patients to process and integrate shared treatment experiences and is possibly more useful than volume or valence in transforming information into benefits.

When consensus is high, the majority of patients who provided their personal treatment experiences have convergent opinions on treatment outcomes. According to cue consistency theory, reviews shared by various patients are more diagnostic when they provide corroborating rather than disparate conclusions [32]. That is, higher consensus leads to higher persuasiveness [46] and lower ambiguity and, therefore, is more diagnostic and influential. For instance, patients who compare their own experiences with a majority of others can find a common story and discuss it with their physicians in an effort to improve their own treatment outcome. Kane et al. [23] document the practical value of consensus on shared patients’ experiences. When consensus is low, the lack of agreement about treatment experiences increases the uncertainty of shared experiences and decreases patients’ confidence in processing information, as the inconsistent experiences are difficult to evaluate [18], and users respond negatively to such uncertainty [46]. Based on these findings, we propose that treatment consensus is a source of social influence on patients’ online ratings and that the higher the consensus, the more favorable the perceptions that will be formed.

Hypothesis 1: Treatment consensus positively influences a patient’s perceived treatment effectiveness rating.

## Consensus and Volume

Volume is the number of reviews for a particular treatment that are available in the online health-care community. This cue has been extensively studied in prior literature but with mixed findings. Some studies find that volume has a positive impact on sales [6], whereas other studies find no effect [13, 29]. Recent studies have documented a negative trend [18, 27]—that is, ratings decline as volume increases. This is counterintuitive because the fundamental value of WOM is to provide information to help users reduce uncertainty, ultimately leading to satisfactory or, at least, informed decisions. Wu and Huberman [49] proposes a motivationbased theory positing that, when there are more previously submitted reviews, users are motivated to provide reviews with lower ratings to increase the impact of their reviews. This cost–benefit explanation is consistent with, in a broader setting, social exchange theory, which indicates that posting reviews is a social behavior and that people engage in this activity with the goal of maximizing benefits while minimizing costs. Li and Hitt [27] find evidence to support this negative effect and offer an alternative explanation that increased volume is associated with increased dissimilarity among reviewers, which leads to lower ratings in new reviews.

Based on these findings, we believe that a high volume of early reviews will possess a negative effect on later ratings, as the increased volume of treatment reviews will increase the variety of opinions from different patients. Notably, research has documented that the increased heterogeneity in patients’ health conditions is welcomed and valued in the online health-information sharing environment, as it can lead to a better understanding of the underlying conditions [14, 35].

In addition, in line with the cue diagnosticity framework, the weights used for the evaluation of treatment ratings are determined by the cues’ usefulness and salience. In other words, the relatively more diagnostic cues will be used in determining the ratings when there are multiple cues [37]. We thus propose that the diagnosticity of consensus will be altered with the existence of volume cue. Prior literature has shown that volume is an influential extrinsic cue that can alter the persuasiveness of other WOM cues [24]. This is because volume is a “stand-alone” cue in the sense that its diagnosticity is relatively less dependent on the presence-valence of other cues. In a typical online environment, there is no restriction on the number of reviews that can be contributed by users. Therefore volume will increase over time, although with a decreasing rate [18]. On the other hand, consensus cue depends on the distribution of existing ratings and therefore its change is not “standalone” but relies on other associated cues. In addition, different from volume cue, consensus has the constraint that it may converge when there are a large number of ratings. As a result, with increased volume, consensus cue becomes less useful or salient, and the likelihood of its being used in assessing the perceived treatment effectiveness will be reduced. Therefore, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 2: Because volume is negatively associated with ratings, the higher the volume of other patients’ shared experiences, the weaker the influence of the consensus on a patient’s perceived treatment effectiveness rating.

## Consensus and Valence

Intuitively, higher valence, that is, higher average rating, will increase users’ preference for a product, which suggests a positive association between review valence and sales. This positive feedback loop is echoed as a reinforcement effect in which social influence is bidirectional and that, in the spread of influence, each individual serves as both a source and a target [31]. That is, users who write reviews are those whose opinions may influence others, while they also have been influenced by previously shared opinions. As a result, one would think that users who post later will already have read prior reviews, and thus make better decisions. Despite the prevalence of research on the cue diagnosticity of valence, consistent and conclusive findings are lacking. Some researchers find no influence of positive valence on sales and conclude that valence is not persuasive. Others, who take into consideration the temporal processes of reviews, argue that the observed decreasing trend in valence is due to the sequential effect in WOM, as users who hold positive opinions are likely to post first with higher ratings, and those with negative opinions will post later, which will result in lower ratings [27]. Godes and Silva [18], who studied the review creation process, taking into account both order and time aspects, offer an alternative explanation: on the macro level, reviewers tend to be more negative and critical. Once this effect is controlled, however, ratings increase positively over time.

Hence, we believe that the positive effect of valence will likewise be identified in the context of treatment reviews. The more that patients learn about a treatment and others’ experiences with it, the better the assessment that they can make when comparing their own experiences with those of others and adjusting their perceptions accordingly. Further, most unsatisfactory experiences—that is, lower ratings—result from patients’ failures to adhere to a prescription regimen [9].

We propose, similar to the interaction effect of consensus and volume, that there is an interaction effect between consensus and valence such that consensus diagnosticity will be altered by valence. The informational role of rating distribution has been examined in prior literature. In an analytical modeling setting, Sun [41] considers the rating variance as the mismatch cost and explores the interaction effect between the average rating and the variance of ratings. She finds, both analytically and empirically, that high variance influences subsequent sales if and only if the average rating is low. In other words, the distribution of ratings is less diagnostic when the average rating is high than when the average rating is low. Although Sun [41] uses book review data and thus differs from our research context, the intuition behind it is quite similar. When the average rating is high, patients are already confident about the overall outcome of the treatment. Since patients are more certain to process positive ratings than negative ones [10], high valence becomes more diagnostic and persuasive as the number of reasons for discontent are diminished with the increase of valence. On the other hand, when the average rating is low, there are many possible reasons leading to bad experiences. Consensus thus becomes an important cue in processing and assessing the effectiveness of the treatment. Said differently, when consensus level is held constant, the ambiguity in low valence will be reduced as valence increases, leading to patients’ increased certainty about the integration of their information. Putting these arguments together, we believe that in the presence of valence cue, consensus is more diagnostic when valence is low but less diagnostic when valence is high. Hence, we hypothesize the impact of consensus cue will be mitigated with increased valence. Accordingly, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 3: Because valence is positively associated with ratings, the higher the valence of other patients’ shared experiences, the weaker the influence of the consensus on a patient’s perceived treatment effectiveness rating.

In addition to review cues, social influence also varies among patients, as they are different in susceptibility to influence, given their individual characteristics. In the following, we consider precommitment and social connectedness as patients’ characteristics that can reshape the diagnostic ability of consensus for patients perceptions.

## Consensus and Precommitment

Precommitment means that patients are motivated to process a wealth of treatment experiences in a self-serving and favorable manner [24]. A recent study shows that 69 percent of U.S. adults keep track of their health, patients with chronic diseases take their tracking more seriously, and one-third of those who keep track of their health use others’ experiences to inform their decisions on how to treat an illness or health condition [16]. Therefore, information sharing among experienced patients is goal-oriented [45], and patients’ prior knowledge is a force that alters persuasion attempts and lowers the diagnostic of review consensus [4]. In other words, a patient with more experiences from prior trials would process others’ opinions in a goalsupporting manner and thus be less likely to be influenced by the review consensus [24]. In an examination of the relationship between the influence of information and recipients’ expertise, Bansal and Voyer [4] find that the influence of information on receivers is low when they have more experiences. In this regard, we believe that experienced patients, compared with less experienced patients, are less likely to be influenced by the agreement of other patients’ shared opinions because they are capable of filtering and processing treatment information in a more effective way. Therefore, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 4: The more experienced a patient, the weaker the influence of consensus on a patient’s perceived treatment effectiveness rating.

## Consensus and Social Connectedness

Another factor we take into consideration, which may change consensus diagnosticity, is a patient’s social integration into the community. Social integration refers to the overall involvement of a patient in a social environment, which includes the various types of social connections offered by formal and informal social relationships [39]. Social scientists have conducted extensive research on the influence of social connections on health and have found that social relationships have significant effects [39, 51].

We believe that the more socially connected a patient, the less influential the review consensus on his or her perceived treatment effectiveness. The diagnosticity of consensus is different for individuals who are well embedded in a social community, as compared to those who are less connected, because patients with high social connectedness receive benefits directly from their contacts through sharing of diverse experiences and information and through receiving support and encouragement to engage in disease self-management. For instance, a highly connected patient has peers to directly communicate with and to discuss issues related to treatment. The collaborative effort of direct contacts can help the patient evaluate the treatment experiences derived from review consensus and to establish a reasonable expectation of his or her own treatment outcome. A patient with fewer social connections, however, does not have the benefit of peer input, and thus needs to rely more on, and consequently be more influenced by, treatment consensus when evaluating perceived treatment effectiveness. As a result, patients who are socially embedded receive various supports and are less likely to be influenced by the consensus of others than those who are not well-connected. Therefore, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 5: The more socially connected a patient, the weaker the influence of consensus on the patient’s perceived treatment effectiveness rating.

## Data and Variables

## Research Context

We examine the social interactions among patients with mental problems because such problems can affect everything in one’s social life and because research has shown that such patients often access information about mental health in online health-care communities [7]. The focal social interactions are collected from a popular Health 2.0 website, whose online community provides patients with a platform to share firsthand experiences, discuss personal health conditions and treatments, and learn from each other. The site provides patients with a diseasebased community so that patients can meet other patients with similar medical conditions. Within a specific community, patients can create their own personal channels, called profiles, where they can upload their medical information (including their current symptoms, treatments, and dosages) and keep track of their own disease progress. The graphic presentation of a patient’s course of disease provides not only a picture of how his or her health conditions have changed over time but also an aggregated view for other patients who visit the profile page. If patients are attracted by others’ similar life experiences, they have the option to subscribe to these profiles, which facilitates further socialization opportunities among this smaller group of patients who are like-minded. A convenient channel for all members in the community to exchange information is also publicly available through the forum. Tags can be stamped on forum posts to prompt content such as drug features or useful supplements.

The most important aspect of the data for this study is the treatment venue, which allows patients to provide their treatment reviews, including prescription and overthe-counter drugs, supplements, physical therapy, and equipment. Of particular note, this venue is separated from patients’ profiles and can be considered a different channel for patients to exchange treatment experiences. For each treatment, community-based aggregated treatment experience and detailed individual-specific reviews can be accessed at two levels. At the community level, an overview of treatment includes the treatment effectiveness, side effects, dosages, and other related information, all of which are retrieved and combined from patients’ reviews. Individuals’ experiences and evaluations are also publicly available.

Our data collection starts with treatment reviews and expands to patients’ online activities. We first collected data on prescription drugs for 15 months, starting in October 2009. The number of drugs increased from a baseline of 42 to 639 by the end of the data collection period. Our data show that there is a long tail distribution for treatment reviews, while most treatment reviews are associated with only a few drugs, and the majority of reported drugs fall in the tail, which is a typical phenomenon observed in social media environments. Figure 1 shows the number of patients who participated in the community and the number of drugs reported in the focal community. We therefore focus on the top 15 prescription drugs (which correspond to 93 percent of patients in our data set) for model estimation. These drugs are categorized treatments that are carried by different brands. For example, Lamotrigine and Clonazepam are common medications for treating mood problems, and each is carried by several brands.

From the treatment reviews, we then collect patients’ information based on the shared review information that we retrieved. For every patient who wrote a review, we further collect his or her profile information and his or her cohesive social interactions. Figure 2 presents a comparison of the percentage of patients who report undergoing a certain type of treatment, writing reviews for the treatment, and providing multiple reviews that update the treatment experiences.

## Dependent Variable

The dependent variable is a patient’s self-reported treatment experience. Typically, patients report their evaluation of perceived treatment effectiveness in a numerical format. In our context, the observed rating is ordinal in terms of four categories: “not at all,” “mild,” “always,” and “major.” We operationalized the ratings as 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively.

## Focal Independent Variables

Our interest here is in the diagnostic role of consensus of other patients’ treatment experiences. We operationalize consensus as the variance among patients’ treatment reviews. This variance measure is consistent with that of West and Broniarczyk [46] and other studies on consensus-effect of WOM. In contrast to other online reviews sites, health-care communities differentiate treatment information by “patients who report undergoing a treatment but do not provide a review” and “patients who share a treatment experience but do not provide a perspective on the prevalence of the treatment,” as well as by “the number of patients who undergo a treatment and the number of reviews.” The treatment experience characteristics, in particular, volume and valence, are thus operationalized based on actual reviews by patients. Figure 3 shows the mean (valence) and variance (consensus) of perceived treatment effectiveness over 15 months for four randomly selected treatments. The dynamic evolution of review characteristics does not show apparent patterns.

In addition to community-based discussions of treatment characteristics, social influence can occur within a patient’s immediate social contacts in this virtual space. When a patient reviews another patient’s profile, he or she can construct his or her subscription network and maintain close contact with a small-size group of similar patients. Such a close-knit network includes patients who subscribe to other patients profiles or who “see” who visits their profile. This social filtering feature allows a patient to quickly engage in communication and socialize with those patients who are similar to him or her. Based on this egocentric network, we construct several measures to approximate the potential impact of frequent communications. The size of the network, captured by the number of subscriptions and subscribers (no. of social ties) indicates the strategic position of a patient in the online health-care community. This “important” position is critical for the patient, as it indicates the amount of experience from which he or she can learn by observing what others are doing before and after their condition changes [53]. Table 1 presents the measures, and Table 2, the descriptive statistics. We have calculated the correlations among the variables and taken steps to process the data. The average variance inflation factor (VIF) for the final data set is 2.07, and individual VIF is less than 10 (the maximum is 9.3). Table A1 in the online appendix presents the correlation matrix.

## Control Variables

To control for factors other than shared treatment reviews and negative concerns about treatment, we include variables such as the number of alternative treatments and patients’ health conditions. We further include patients’ communication characteristics to control patients’ online activity intent to socialize or seek information. A community-based treatment report provides patients with an assessment overview from patients in the community and helps them to compare their outcomes with those of others. No. of drugs provides a basic understanding of how many prescription drugs patients in a community are taking and suggests options from which a patient can choose and use to treat his or her symptoms. Another community-based treatment characteristic is no. of posts, which captures patients’ treatment-related interactions in the public discussion forum. Posts and threads attract patients with various conditions, and the contributed information can be diverse. The more often a treatment is discussed, the higher the chance that the treatment will be known by more patients and attract more attention. Patients who are currently undergoing a certain treatment can learn from these conversations and adjust their expectations about the treatment. In addition to the number of treatment reviews, this covariate further controls for the popularity of the treatment.

At the social network level are “key opinion leaders,” who are influential even without professional training. Many users visit their profiles, which enable these patients to receive timely information and to contribute to various conversations [42, 51]. No. of incoming ties (incoming connections to the patient) is thus used to account for this effect. Average network rating controls the influence of homophonous patients; the more similar they are to each other, the higher the chance they will adopt others’ advice [43].

Patients’ differing characteristics, such as age and gender, can have an impact on patients’ perceived treatment effectiveness at the time of treatment evaluation. Adults use the Internet to search for health-related information and actively engage in online social activities. Thus, gender, age, mood, and profile quality are used to control for individual heterogeneity. The website defines four levels of data quality on patients’ profiles, indicated by zero to three stars. At the time of registration, a patient will receive one star if he or she provides basic membership information and health data, or no star if no health data are provided. Three stars are granted when a patient has regularly updated his or her health profile and completed at least four mood maps. Health profile also includes health-related information, such as names of prescription medications, supplements, equipment used, and other interventions. We define three stars as high quality, and we code profile quality as 1, otherwise 0. In addition, it is also convenient for patients to report their mood by clicking one of the following categories: very bad, bad, neutral, good, and very good. We therefore transform and operationalize the mood condition as 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5, respectively.

We also control for a patient’s health condition characteristics, for example, how long since he or she has been diagnosed with a disease (diagnosis time), the severity of his or her disease, that is, the number of symptoms (no. of symptoms), the duration of treatment (time on drug), and length of membership in the health-care community (tenure).

## Model

Patients’ perceived treatment effectiveness, which is private information, is generally in keeping with their beliefs about the quality of a particular treatment. Patients in online health-care communities can undergo multiple treatments for the purpose of staying healthy (or at least not getting worse). As with other social media applications, a patient can write reviews and evaluate treatments that he or she is currently undergoing. The patient’s evaluation of the treatment can be adjusted as his or her opinion of the treatment experience is influenced by other patients’ opinions. In addition, the knowledge that he or she receives from other patients’ shared information can also affect his or her understanding of the disease and treatments, which facilitates experiential learning and might affect his or her future ratings.

Unlike in other social contexts, patients who suffer from similar diseases may significantly differ from one another. Our initial analysis thus starts with patients perceived beliefs about treatment outcomes and the influence of patients’ perceptions from the online health-care community. Specifically, following Rabe-Hesketh and Skrondal [38] and Heil [20], for patient i at time t, we model his or her latent perceived treatment effectiveness rating for treatment $k \left( \mathrm { R a t i n g } ^ { * } { } _ { i , t , k } \right)$ as depending on the controls for treatment (vector $\mathrm { D } _ { t , k } )$ , his or her health condition covariates $( \mathrm { P } _ { i , t , k } )$ 2 the social interactions covariates (vector $\mathrm { S } _ { i , t , k } ) .$ , and consensus covariates (vector $\mathrm { C } _ { i , t , k } )$ . The individual-level random intercepts $( \zeta _ { 0 , i } )$ thus allow us to incorporate individual heterogeneity, such as an individual patient’s ability to process the shared treatment reviews, which is not captured by our control or focal independent variables.

$$
\operatorname{Rating} _ {i, t, k} ^ {*} = \left(\beta_ {0} + \zeta_ {0, i}\right) + \beta_ {d} \mathrm{D} _ {t - 1, k} + \beta_ {p} \mathrm{P} _ {i, t, k} + \beta_ {s} \mathrm{S} _ {i, t - 1, k} + \beta_ {c} \mathrm{C} _ {i, t - 1, k} + \varepsilon_ {i, t, k}.\tag{1}
$$

As suggested in prior studies, some individuals are easily influenced, while others are more resistant to influence. We then extend our analysis to allow for heterogeneity in consensus coefficients. Specifically, we introduce variation effects in consensus covariates (vector $\mathrm { C } _ { i , t , k } )$ and model the degree of agreement as having a heterogeneous influence on patients through random coefficients $( \zeta _ { c , i } )$ for the consensus covariates.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Rating} _ {i, t, k} ^ {*} = (\beta_ {0} + \zeta_ {0, i}) + \beta_ {d} \mathrm{D} _ {t - 1, k} + \beta_ {p} \mathrm{P} _ {i, t, k} + \beta_ {s} \mathrm{S} _ {i, t - 1, k} + (\beta_ {c} + \zeta_ {c, i}) \mathrm{C} _ {i, t - 1, k} \\ \qquad + \varepsilon_ {i, t, k}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

We further assume that $\varepsilon _ { i , t , k }$ has a logistic distribution. Thus, the observed ordinal responses $R a t i n g _ { i , t , k }$ are generated by the threshold model (note that all three thresholds $\epsilon _ { s } , s = 1 , 2 , 3$ are identified):

$$
\operatorname{Rating} _ {i, t, k} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 0, & i f \quad \operatorname{Rating} _ {i, t, k} ^ {*} \leq \kappa_ {1}; \\ 1, & i f \quad \kappa_ {1} <   \operatorname{Rating} _ {i, t, k} ^ {*} \leq \kappa_ {2}; \\ 2, & i f \quad \kappa_ {2} <   \operatorname{Rating} _ {i, t, k} ^ {*} \leq \kappa_ {3}; \\ 3, & i f \quad \kappa_ {3} <   \operatorname{Rating} _ {i, t, k} ^ {*}. \end{array} \right.\tag{3}
$$

## Estimation Results

We deploy generalized linear latent and mixed models [38] to conduct the analysis for the multilevel data for two reasons. First, the reported treatment usage in our data set is distributed diversely, so it is inappropriate to use an ordered logic model. Second, the data are in a hierarchical structure such that the treatment effectiveness ratings are nested by patients, and patients are nested by treatments. The variations across different treatments could be inhomogeneous. For instance, each of the top 5 treatments receives more than 500 reviews over the 15-month time frame. However, the number of reviews quickly decreases to fewer than 100 for less popular treatments. In the process of estimating the proposed models, we first execute a basic ANOVA (analysis of variance) with nested random factors (an unconditional hierarchical linear model) to get the variances of effects at each level. The estimation result suggests that unobserved heterogeneity between patients is significant at the 10 percent level, while different treatments are similar to one another. Therefore, we report the estimation results for the two-level random intercept model (Model 1) and the two-level random coefficient model (Model 2), as seen in Table 3.

We find a positive main effect of other patients’ treatment experiences agreement on a patient’s treatment experience $( \beta [ \mathrm { c o n s e n s u s } ] = 0 . 5 9 9 9 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . This confirms that consensus is an effective cue when patients process shared information. As we expected, the higher the consensus among patients’ treatment opinions, the more diagnostic it becomes. That is, when patients combine various pieces of information to derive the quality of the treatment, the increased consensus becomes more important in their evaluation process. High consensus among patients’ treatment experiences provides a signal that the treatment demonstrates a stable effectiveness across a pool of patients, and thus reduces the skepticism. Consensus therefore becomes an influential cue for patients to evaluate the effectiveness of the treatment outcome, and H1 is supported.

Our estimate result also supports H2. Consistent with the findings of recent studies [18, 27, 49], volume has a negative impact on the later ratings $( \beta [ \mathrm { v o l u m e } ] = - 0 . 1 9 2 5 ,$ $p < 0 . 1 )$ . More importantly, the presence of the volume cue alters the diagnosticity of consensus. Specifically, the positive effect of treatment consensus is moderated by the volume of the available patients’ shared experiences. That is, the higher the volume of patients’ shared experiences, the weaker the influence of agreement among other patients’ perceived treatment effectiveness, as shown by the significant interaction effect between consensus and volume. As the variance is used to measure consensus in our analysis, the two-way interaction term (β consensus volume = ‒ 0.0675, p < 0.1) suggests a negative effect between consensus and volume. In other words, given the positive effect of consensus, its influence will be moderated by volume.

There is an alternative explanation for this moderating effect caused by high volume, which is the information overload caused by more patients’ sharing their opinions. Because treatment experiences can be easily shared in the online healthcare community in the form of online WOM, the treatment volume quickly grows beyond an individual’s capability to process the information. For instance, the most popular treatment in our data set received 641 reviews. It is unlikely for patients to read every treatment review and find what might be most useful for them. The argument for the problems caused by information overload is also supported by the negative significant effect for the number of treatments that patients undergo. When patients undergo more treatments, they need to process more information, and this difficulty negatively affects their perception of the treatment effectiveness.

The results of our analysis do not provide evidence that supports H3. Although we hypothesize that valence is positively associated with treatment effectiveness ratings and the diagnostic ability of consensus will be moderated by review valence, our results suggest a positive coefficient for valence and a negative coefficient for the interaction effect between consensus and valance, but neither estimate is statistically significant. Different from the consistent finding for review volume, prior studies show that the impacts of valence are inequitable [24, 54]. That our result does not provide supportive evidence for the moderating effect of valence may be due to the information processing needs of patients. It is possible that patients are not interested in the valence of others’ ratings; rather, they can adopt the experiences shared by other patients and use them in further discussions with physicians or consultations. This is consistent with the awareness argument seen in the literature [13, 18].

When considering the characteristics of patients, our results provide evidence for H4 and H5. We hypothesize that patients who provide multiple treatment reviews are committed to self-education and to processing other patients’ experiences in a selffavorable manner. Thus, they are less likely to be influenced by high consensus. This argument is supported by a negative significant estimation coefficient $( \beta [ \mathrm { c o n s e n s } \times \mathrm { e x p e r i e n c e } ] = - 0 . 0 8 4 9 , p < 0 . 1 )$ . ## Managerial Implications

Our work offers the following managerial implications. First, sharing health information and issues with other patients has grown in popularity [16], but concerns about information quality and credibility have been raised by researchers, particularly in regard to inaccurate or incomplete information [2, 11, 52]. In keeping with these concerns, discussions about the negative outcomes of treatments, such as side effects, have been posited to hinder the social value of online health-care communities because these negative thoughts can impose a significant emotional burden on patients. Our work, however, shows that learning about both positive and negative experiences can be beneficial for individuals when choosing treatments. Knowing others’ life events associated with a certain treatment can improve patients’ awareness of and preparation for potential risks. The more negative issues shared among patients, the more diversified the available information from which patients can make decisions. More importantly, our results suggest that the opportunity of learning various experiences matters more than the average opinion about a treatment.

Second, high volume and valence of reviews have been shown to be associated with persuasion [6, 13, 24, 29]. Our study, however, shows that these features are less influential in the health-care context. Our findings support the observation that patients still depend on professional resources for accurate information about prescription drugs and alternative treatments [16]. Based on conformity needs, however, patients turn to online health-care communities and access other patients’ experiences with certain conditions and treatments and can receive further confirmation of these experiences from doctors. Therefore, in contrast to the use of online reviews for books, movies, and other products, for which reviewers have competing needs, it is less likely for patients to diverge from the opinions and experiences of others, particularly when they feel connected to the social group. A divergence of opinions, that is, different treatment experiences and ratings, may occur when patients perceive a large discrepancy between their experiences and the experiences of others. As a result, even though online reviews have been widely studied in other contexts, there is still a need to understand the WOM effect in the health-care domain.

Third, in line with the above argument, our work is also of interest to health-care providers and drug manufacturers. Social media have been shown to be efficient marketing tools. Much effort is made toward brand building, with an emphasis on how often a topic is mentioned (volume) and the overall opinion in the community (valence). Our work shows the overlooked characteristics of consensus-effect of WOM, which could play an even more important role in better understanding and meeting patients’ needs. Consensus has important practical implications because it is easy to obtain in an online social media environment. The recognized discriminative mechanism of social influence in online health-care communities (conformity needs vs. needs to be unique) offers practitioners a new metric to understand the medium for conveying health information among patients and for supporting patients’ collaborative web-based education.

Finally, social integration is believed to be associated with health. As such, given the ease of communication in the online environment, especially on the Internet and through user-generated content, the online environment has a strong impact on health and well-being [1]. Therefore, it is of interest to understand who benefits the most and the least from social connectedness in the online environment. Although prior studies suggest that online social interactions provide social support to meet patients’ needs [34, 50], Wright et al. [48] find that individuals prefer to get support from someone other than one’s direct social contacts. Our results likewise suggest the positive effect of establishing a wide range of social ties to patients, which could be used by policymakers to develop effective social intervention strategies in the new realm of health-care delivery systems.

## Robustness Checks

We further construct two variables to process the review characteristics. The first alternative proxy captures the usefulness of a shared treatment experiences and is constructed by counting the number of “helpful” votes for the review. When a review is posted on the treatment venue, other patients can signal the quality of the information by adding a “helpful” flag. The more “helpful” votes that a review receives, the more influential it could be. In addition, we also adopt a text-mining technique to conduct sentiment analysis for text reviews and analyze the positive and negative emotions embedded in these messages. Our estimation results qualitatively hold after substituting these variables in our model. Tables A2 and A3 in the online appendix provide summaries of these estimation results.

## Conclusion

Health information is critical for one’s health and health care. In his address for the Healthtrac Award, Bandura [3] describes self-management as an important means to achieve huge health benefits. We have begun to see a gradual increase in patients engaging in health-information seeking as a result of the gaining popularity of Usenet, bulletin boards, and social support-oriented e-mail groups. The use of such media has become even more popular as the number of patients who “go online” grows exponentially [43]. According to the National Health Service, the increased acquisition of information from the Internet helps patients better understand their medical condition and self-care [44]. With the coexistence of the increasing use of health-care-related information seeking and criticism about such behavior, our work, which explores how social interactions affect patients’ opinions about their treatment outcomes, offers several insights into online social interactions and their impact on the emerging patient-centric health-care systems.

Because patients are not considered experts with regard to health issues, it remains unclear whether shared treatment experiences can be influential and, consequently, reshape other patients’ health perceptions. Although appropriate use of medication is the key to the self-management of most chronic illnesses, prior research has estimated that over 30 percent of prescribed medication is not taken as directed [9, 21]. In line with this concern, understanding the factors that affect patients’ perceptions of treatment effectiveness becomes important because, if patients do not believe that a recommended treatment is efficacious, they are likely to stop the treatment [36]. Our work presents evidence of social influence on such decisions. In particular, we find that the main effect of treatment consensus is positively associated with perceived treatment effectiveness. Based on the cue diagnosticity framework, we further consider the interaction effect between consensus and other cues. Our work shows that this positive effect can be moderated by other WOM characteristics and that patients who are experienced or more socially connected are less likely to be influenced by treatment consensus.

Our findings have immediate practical implications for the pharmaceutical industry. Contrary to previous attempts to prevent their being posted online from patients on healthrelated issues, practitioners today have considered patients’ experience as another element of the health and health-care system [22], yet how to make use of it remains a challenge. On the one hand, a large amount of health information is contributed by patients. One the other hand, physicians learn from both sample medications and repeated prescriptions to distribute treatment rather than learn from patients’ shared online reviews [54]. By presenting evidence of patients’ collaborative learning through treatment experience sharing in online health-care communities, we offer an actionable strategy for healthcare providers and the like to engage in communication.

Our results suggest that consensus among patients’ shared treatment experiences is a powerful predictive factor of social influence among patients. Our results also suggest how this effect can be moderated with other review-related characteristics and patient-specific characteristics. Given that health-information seeking is a multifaceted process and often consists of interactions between patients as well as environmental and social factors [35], policymakers and practitioners should develop effective interventions to help patients better engage in their health education and disease management.

In summary, our work extends the literature on user-generated content by identifying the important role of social influence, as seen in consensus among patients in regard to shared experiential or anecdotal information about treatments, on subsequent patients’ perceived treatment effectiveness. By controlling for individual heterogeneity and adding an inhomogeneous feature to consensus, we show the influential effect of consensus and its interaction with other WOM variables. We hope that as online health-care communities continue to increase exponentially, this study will contribute to improvement in the area of Internet health-information sharing as well as stimulate additional research in this area.

Additional research can focus on other health problems with different treatments and investigate whether review diagnosticity varies across these treatments. It is possible that some treatments are well-developed and therefore the insights contributed by patients are limited. On the other hand, some treatments are recently released to the market and there is a need to get more feedback from patients. Thus, reviews on newly developed treatments may be more diagnostic than those on well-adopted treatments. In addition, this study only focuses on reviews of treatment itself. It is possible that patients will also include their opinions on physicians or service providers in their treatment reviews. Hence, it is worth exploring whether these types of information will change diagnosticity of treatment reviews.
