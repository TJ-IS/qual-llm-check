# Social responsibility and stakeholder in<sup>fl</sup>uence: Does technology matter during stakeholder deliberation with high-impact decisions?

## a b s t r a c t

When organizations face potentially threatening situations, stakeholders with diverse viewpoints in<sup>fl</sup>uence decision-making processes and outcomes using different communication media. We apply theories from social in<sup>fl</sup>uence, group polarization, majority–minority in<sup>fl</sup>uence and deindividuation to investigate how computer-mediated communication (CMC) affects stakeholders’ decisions, satisfaction, and personal preferences. Our experiment <sup>fi</sup>nds that CMC is a viable alternative to face-to-face (FTF) deliberation involving judgment tasks. Stakeholders in CMC deliberation did not compromise their personal conviction, whereas stakeholders in FTF deliberation were more in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by opposing positions. Stakeholders in both media conditions made similar group choices, but reported higher satisfaction in FTF deliberation or holding majority position.

## 1. Introduction

As sensitivity to social responsibility and ethical issues intensi<sup>fi</sup>es, corporations face a dif<sup>fi</sup>cult challenge when confronted with con<sup>fl</sup>icts between corporate shareholder interest and socially responsible behavior. Socially irresponsible <sup>fi</sup>rm behavior, such as choosing not to recall a dangerous pharmaceutical drug, may have devastating effects to stakeholders, stockholders and the public at large [15]. Value-laden, high-impact decision making often requires a tradeoff between pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximization and the broader social welfare and is perhaps one of the most dif<sup>fi</sup>cult domains facing decision makers. Stakeholders represent diverse groups and perspectives, such as stockholders, corporate executives, government regulators, suppliers, customers, employees and community citizens [14,28]. Therefore, stakeholders harboring varying degrees of vested interest in the organization's survivability and/or pro<sup>fi</sup>tability affect how corporations handle socially responsible issues in con<sup>fl</sup>ict with pro<sup>fi</sup>t seeking goals [8]. As corporations operate in an increasingly globalized environment, stakeholders become more heterogeneous, not only with their values and opinions but also with geographical locations [76]. Consequently, it can become a necessity to use information technology (IT) to engage stakeholders to deliberate emerging critical issues facing organizations.

Stakeholders with diverse values and preferences in<sup>fl</sup>uence both decision processes and outcomes by representing personal, public and organizational interests [40]. To advance special interests in group deliberation, stakeholders will engage in various social in<sup>fl</sup>uences, including normative, informational and referent, resulting in group polarization and deindividuating effects. To elucidate these phenomena, research in the social psychology of group decision making draws from several theoretical backgrounds, including social presence theory [24], social comparison theory (SCT) [33], persuasive argument theory (PAT) [12,13], referent informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence theory (RIIT) [44,73] and self-categorization theory [72], underscoring the diverse sources of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence in group decision making. In addition, stakeholders who hold majority perspectives may use their in<sup>fl</sup>uence to change the values of minority stakeholders during the deliberation process [5,6].

Managers spend a tremendous amount of time engaging in meetings [51], but where face-to-face (FTF) group decision making may be costly and unproductive to organizations [10], computermediated communication (CMC) can be an effective alternative to facilitate group members conducting electronic brainstorming, analyzing issues, surfacing assumptions and prioritizing critical issues [18,20,25,56]. However, much of the CMC research has so far focused on two major kinds of tasks: idea generation [25,74] and intellective tasks [22,66]. Therefore, there is a need to expand CMC research to include judgment tasks, since managers are increasingly dealing with issues involving unstructured problems and con<sup>fl</sup>icting priorities. This study addresses whether CMC can be a viable alternative to facilitate and support value-laden decision making using a judgment task. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our study endeavors to address several important research questions: How does the communication medium and majority or minority role in<sup>fl</sup>uence the degree of compliance and conversion of member preferences in group decision making? Do differences exist between the communication medium regarding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence from members who hold majority or minority role? Do the effects of communication medium vary on group members’ personal preference to convert to group consensus and their satisfaction with group processes and outcomes?

Our study contributes to the existing literature in two speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways. First, it integrates interdisciplinary theoretical perspectives in group decision making to examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of different communication structures when deliberating on high-impact judgment tasks. Second, it distinguishes the effects of communication technology on personal and role-based decision preferences. Our study simultaneously examines the effects of media (FTF versus CMC) and role (majority versus minority stakeholder) conditions and their effects on various forms of social in<sup>fl</sup>uences (group polarization, deindividuation, compliance and conversion). By examining social in<sup>fl</sup>uences at the intersection of media and role characteristics, this study emulates the complexity of the group deliberation process.

The paper is organized as follows. First, we introduce theoretical perspectives on social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and group decision making, and their signi<sup>fi</sup>cance to the deliberation of judgment tasks using different communication media. Second, we present our research model and the operationalized hypotheses based on an integrated theoretical foundation, and describe the experimental design. Finally, we discuss the results and <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the study and their implications for practice and future research.

## 2. Theoretical foundations and hypothesis developments

Social psychological theories on group decision making and empirical computer-mediated communication (CMC) research inform our understanding of the group deliberation processes. Drawing from social psychology literature, social comparison, persuasive arguments, and social categorization theories are used to explain social in<sup>fl</sup>uences, such as normative, informational and referent informational, in the group decision-making process. Research in CMC lends insight into how IT shapes social in<sup>fl</sup>uences, group processes and the interaction of task type and participant characteristics. We examine how using CMC affects the group deliberation process and outcomes of stakeholders in high-impact decisions involving con<sup>fl</sup>icting positions and ambiguous decision requirements, in other words, judgment tasks. Finally, we explore the phenomenon of group dynamics, including group polarization, deindividuation, and majority/minority roles, using the aforementioned theoretical foundation in the context of CMC.

## 3. The nature of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence in group decision making

Groups exert profound in<sup>fl</sup>uence on individual members through three major forms of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence: normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence, informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence, and referent informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence [39,44]. Normative influence is the pressure a group exerts on individual members to conform to prevailing group norms [39]. Normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence manifests itself through social presence, or the degree that individuals are aware of other people and interpersonal relationships [24], such as negative/ positive feelings an individual senses from the facial expressions and body language of others [36]. According to social comparison theory (SCT), individuals attempt to reduce their ignorance about the group and its members by comparing their own position with the group's position [33]. The constant comparison to others coupled with the fear of ostracism and evaluation from group members forms the basis for normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence creates similar effects in CMC and FTF group decision making [20,25]; however, normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence is weakened in CMC groups by reducing social presence.

Informational influence is manifested through the recognition and acceptance of other viewpoints by communicating relevant information and arguments during group deliberation [39]. According to persuasive arguments theory (PAT), group preferences shift because group members are exposed to new arguments previously not considered during group discussion [12,13]. Group members experience informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence as new information and arguments are introduced and shared among group members, who evaluate the problem using the new information. The group preference and decision outcome of the group may shift by sharing relevant and factual information, resulting in group polarization toward well supported positions. Informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence is more prevalent when groups are deliberating using CMC, due to the lack of social cues that create normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence and the emphasis on persuasive arguments [30,62].

Referent informational influence (RII) represents a rejection of ‘classical’ normative and informational in<sup>fl</sup>uences [44]. According to RII, social actors do not compromise their personal opinion to group pressures from either (a) active normative pressures or (b) the persuasiveness of arguments. Instead, group members converge on perceived prevailing normative positions that individual group members perceive best represent the ideal group position [1,73]. Referent informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence theory (RIIT) emphasizes the strong role of group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation where group members do not compromise their positions for normative rationales (as with SCT) or from evaluating new information (as with PAT), but “converge on (perceived) prevailing ingroup norms or group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation” by avoiding contrary positions [1,72,73]. Based on the self-categorization theory of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence [67], group members spontaneously self-categorize themselves (i.e., compare their position with the prevailing group decision), and subsequently echo the group's position during deliberation. By reducing individuating factors that prevent individual group members from becoming “part of the group,” CMC increases the group consensus and polarization, and thus RII [44,60]. ## 4. Compliance and conversion in group deliberation

When an individual exhibits a change in position toward the group's position, individuals may exhibit compliance with the dominant group position or conversion of personal attitudes, values and beliefs [47,51]. Compliance is a temporary acceptance of the group viewpoint during group deliberation, and occurs when the individual succumbs to the pressure of strong normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence. While the public response may change, the individual's personal attitudes, beliefs, or values remains unchanged. Conversely, conversion represents a deep and enduring internalization of attitudes, perceptions, beliefs or values [47,48]. Conversion generalizes beyond the current situation into other contexts, and is typically a personal rather than a public response. Informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence is a common cause for an individual's conversion due to the voluntary nature of accepting the persuasive arguments presented by other group members.

Referent informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence can induce both compliance within groups and conversion within individuals. Individuals will actively seek prevailing group identities during group deliberation to avoid ostracism and feel more like “one of the group,” replacing their individual identity and position with the prevailing group identity, resulting in compliance with the group position. In essence, the more an individual must sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce individual preferences and viewpoints to be part of the group, the more the individual has complied with the group viewpoint, and individuals who have strong af<sup>fi</sup>nity with the group identity convert their original position after group discussion [49]. Interestingly, both compliance and conversion in group discussions are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the group membership of the information source, and not the information itself [49].

## 5. Computer-mediated communication

Computer-mediated communication (CMC) is the use of networked computers to exchange information [68]. Considerable research over the past two decades examines the effects of CMC on group decision making, including electronic brainstorming and decision outcomes of structured tasks [20,56]. CMC reduces many limitations of traditional FTF communications, such as geographical dispersion, sequential communication processing, and dominating political and social in<sup>fl</sup>uences [29]. CMC also facilitates effective decision-making participation [17] and promotes idea generation [25]. Emerging phenomena using CMC to support group communications and teamwork, such as global virtual teams [34] and social computing and social networking [58], illustrate that even after several decades research in CMC-supported organizational communication and group decision making continues to be relevant and essential.

CMC reduces social presence by <sup>fi</sup>ltering non-textual cues, reducing normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence and encouraging equal participation in the decision-making process [11,30]. Social presence is the “degree to which the medium facilities awareness of the other person and interpersonal relationships during the interaction” [24], in other words, the extent that individuals connect with one another on a personal level [65]. Social presence theory [65] suggests that media with low social presence (i.e., where individuals are not completely aware of others) may not be suitable when communication requires signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interaction and reciprocity, which is necessary for highimpact, value-laden decisions embodied in judgment tasks. Typically, electronic media (e.g. CMC) are considered to have low social presence, while FTF media have high social presence [71]. However, a reduction in social presence by using CMC has several bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, such as more informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence from greater scrutiny of the message content and less normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence from non-textual cues [66]. The importance of the speaker's identity is weakened while the signi<sup>fi</sup>- cance of what the speaker says is heightened, shifting the focus away from the identity and identi<sup>fi</sup>able characteristics of group members. This shift in focus from message source to message content can alter or even abate the ability of majority group members to impose their viewpoint on minority members.

Group members bring different and often con<sup>fl</sup>icting personal values and public interests to the group deliberation process. CMC reduces social presence and the impact of normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence, allowing group members holding minority viewpoints to express their arguments without duress [19,35,71], and facilitates the expression of extreme viewpoints, which may lead to group polarization [41]. Despite the importance of using CMC in the deliberation of judgment tasks, few studies have examined how CMC affects group processes and outcomes of high-impact decisions, and how CMC in<sup>fl</sup>uences the process of solving judgment tasks in general.

## 5.1. Social influence and decision task type

The relative strength of normative, informational and referent informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence in group deliberation depends on the type of decision task the group is performing. Intellective and judgment tasks are two prominent task types found in social in<sup>fl</sup>uence literature [39]. Intellective tasks have a “correct answer” that can be empirically veri<sup>fi</sup>ed by group members, independent of personal judgments or preferences. Since the decision has some analytical certainty, new information and arguments have a profound effect; and therefore, informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly stronger than normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Intellective tasks have a long history in CMC literature [17,22,66]. Judgment (or preference) tasks, however, have no clear answer, thus the “correct” answer is achieved through group in<sup>fl</sup>uence and consensus building. Judgment tasks reduce the primacy of informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence in group deliberation, yielding such effects to normative or referent informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence [39]. Social responsibility issues and ethical dilemmas often have no single correct answer and are, therefore, framed as judgment tasks.

In CMC conditions, individuals performing judgment tasks are generally more susceptible to normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence than individuals performing intellective tasks [15,22]; nevertheless, they are more resistant to normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence [76]. Since social responsibility issues tend to be judgment tasks, the group members holding dissenting, or minority, preferences should have greater in<sup>fl</sup>uence in CMC groups than in FTF groups since social presence is weaker, whereas the majority, should evoke greater in<sup>fl</sup>uence in FTF groups than CMC groups.

5.2. Communication media and task on social influence in group decision making

Overall, informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence is stronger than normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence [12]. However, the relative strength of informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence over normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence manifests itself in variations of task type and media. In intellective tasks, informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence is strong while normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence is minimal due to the objective nature of the task outcome [39]. However, in judgment tasks, persuasive arguments still have a role but the effects of normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence are more pronounced. The media also impacts the relative strength of informational and normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence. CMC reduces the effects of normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence in all tasks due to the reduction of social presence [22,25,76] and draws people's attention toward the persuasiveness of arguments, increasing informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence [30,62].

One or two group members may come to dominate group deliberation in FTF conditions by exerting normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence. However, CMC reduces the relative dominance of such individuals [25], promoting equality among group members [61]. Thus, CMC reduces the negative effects of normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence common in FTF groups [19,25,30], as the effects of persuasive arguments (informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence) are stronger in CMC conditions, while the effects of normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence are greatly reduced [22,30]. In addition to informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence, CMC also induces more RII in groups [44] through anonymity, deindividuation and other characteristics unique to CMC. RII occurs when individuating characteristics are less pronounced, and group members subsequently turn away from individuating characteristics toward group characteristics (in other words, a salient group identity) [72,73].

## 6. Group polarization

A common outcome of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence in group deliberation is the tendency for individual and group preferences to swing toward prominent or extreme group positions. Group member preferences may shift during group deliberation toward more extreme, and even more cautious preferences, drawn by the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of other group members [20,69]. These bi-directional social in<sup>fl</sup>uences that shift individual and group preferences are known as group polarization [54]. Group polarization occurs when individuals in groups make more extreme (or more cautious) public decisions than their individual personal preferences, or when the group preferences diverge from individual preferences [42,54].

Each of the prominent social in<sup>fl</sup>uence theories provides an explanation for group polarization effects in group decision making. According to SCT, group members will compare their position to other group members and associate themselves with the prevailing group preferences to avoid ostracism and evaluation, shifting their position toward the positions of other group members [33]. According to PAT, the group's preferences and choice will shift toward one or more prevailing viewpoints, depending on the strength of arguments presented in support of a particular viewpoint [38]. Overall, PAT offers a consistently superior explanation for group polarization, while SCT plays a moderating role in inducing group polarization [33]. However, the effects of social comparison on group polarization have been observed in studies demonstrating that mere knowledge of group norms can shift choice, absent any supportive arguments [9,27]. According to RIIT, group polarization stems from individuals self-categorizing themselves in order to form a group identity, and can emerge without prior information concerning individual or group identities [44,73]. Additionally, greater polarization was found in groups where ‘being a group’ was emphasized over ‘being an individual’ [44].

## 6.1. Communication media on group polarization

Research suggests that CMC may affect group polarization through changes in normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence [41,50]; however, studies on CMC and group polarization yield mixed results. Since CMC reduces the effects of normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence in group deliberation, and normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor in judgment tasks, CMC may reduce group polarization created by normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence. For example, group polarization is less pronounced in CMC than FTF groups when dealing with judgment tasks [22]. On the other hand, a reduction in social presence leads to stronger group polarization, and group polarization is even stronger in anonymous and dispersed CMC conditions, which are common for the communication media [66]. CMC groups make more extreme, unconventional, and risky decisions than FTF groups [41]. Despite the reduction in normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant group polarization effects are found in the CMC setting [66]. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1A. Group member decision preferences under computer-mediated communication will be more polarized than under face-to-face communication.

## 6.2. Majority and minority role on group polarization

The type of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence employed by individuals during the group deliberation changes not only with the communication media, but also with the relative power of majority and minority sub-groups. Typically, minorities will elicit conversion among group members [43], whereas majorities will elicit compliance [6]. Social in<sup>fl</sup>uence theories recognize a number of potential sources of in<sup>fl</sup>uence among majority and minority group members [51–53]. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence may be subtle, resulting from the observation of clear majority and minority positions, or overt, where minority positions may be the target of social pressures from opposing majority positions. Majority group members seek to create consensus using normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence to shift group member preference (thus inducing group polarization, particularly for the minority) [39]. In addition, the relative number of group members holding a speci<sup>fi</sup>c position strengthens the normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence exerted. Hence, the minority usually yields to majority in<sup>fl</sup>uence [6]. Nevertheless, minority members may be effective in converting others because of their deviant status within the group, as group members may seek to differentiate themselves from others [43], and even majorities may yield to minority in<sup>fl</sup>uence [55]. The use of normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence is more common with a strong majority, as majority members will rely on normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence more than informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence to shift group member preferences, creating compliance among the majority members and polarization of the minority members [39]. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1B. Group member decision preferences in the minority stakeholder role will be more polarized than in the majority stakeholder role.

## 7. Majority and minority in<sup>fl</sup>uence and communication media

CMC may alter the relative balance between majority and minority in<sup>fl</sup>uence in two ways [71]. First, the reduction of social presence may weaken the majority's ability to dominate the deliberation process, using normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence to pressure the minority members. Second, CMC allows members to enter information simultaneously, otherwise known as parallelism [20,56]. Parallelism allows minority members to express their viewpoints since majority members cannot dominate the discussion. Reduced social presence and parallelism allow the minority to effectively present the minority viewpoint [19].

The judgment task also plays a role in amplifying the effects of majority and minority in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Since judgment tasks rely heavily on the use of normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence to polarize the preference of other group members, and CMC reduces the relative strength of normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence, minority members should gain greater in<sup>fl</sup>uence using CMC. Alternatively, the majority should evoke greater in<sup>fl</sup>uence in FTF groups, not only from the media, but also from the nature of the task. Since CMC reduces normative pressures exerted by group members, majority members are less likely to exert normative in<sup>fl</sup>uences that force minority members to comply with the salient group preferences, while informational and referent in<sup>fl</sup>uence are more prominent in group discussion. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H2A. Group member holding the minority stakeholder role will have more in<sup>fl</sup>uence over the majority to comply in computer-mediated groups relative to face-to-face groups.

However, informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence is not an effective form of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence in judgment tasks, where normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence and referent in<sup>fl</sup>uence is more prominent in FTF deliberation. The ability for minority members to express their viewpoints is marginalized when deliberating judgment tasks, like the social responsibility issues used in this experiment. Minority members can therefore be easily swayed in the face of a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed majority, succumbing to strong normative pressures to conform in FTF environments. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H2B. Group members holding the majority stakeholder role will have more in<sup>fl</sup>uence over the minority to comply in face-to-face groups relative to computer-mediated groups.

## 8. Deindividuation

Deindividuation is a reduced state of self-awareness due to immersion and anonymity in a group environment. The attention of group members is drawn away from individual characteristics and directed toward group characteristics. As group members become more immersed in the group experience, they become less selfconscious and self-aware [21]. Early conceptualizations of deindividuation effects, known as classical and contemporary deindividuation theories, rely on social presence theory as the driving explanatory force. Classical deindividuation theories [21,78] suggest that group members become less self-aware in a group environment since more attention is paid toward group characteristics as opposed to individual differences, and are therefore less aware of their actions and behaviors, and experience fewer normative pressures to conform with group behaviors. CMC <sup>fi</sup>lters out social cues and inhibits social comparisons that induce deindividuating effects [22,66], which reduce selfawareness and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of social norms [60]. Contemporary theories [59] augment the classical theory by predicting an increase in anti-normative behavior due to the reduced normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence and self-regulation of in-group behaviors. The increase in anti-normative behavior may explain, in part, the increase in resistance of minority members to the prevailing majority preference.

More recent theoretical explanations of deindividuation effects use the social identity model for deindividuation effects (SIDE) [63]. The SIDE model predicts that deindividuation occurs not because people lose a sense of identity, but loses a sense of individual identity, replaced with a group identity. Through social categorization theory, individuals identify themselves as group members, adopting the behaviors and opinions embodied in prevailing group norms; and, members who identify strongly with the group identity will exhibit behaviors consistent with prevailing group norms [46,57]. Therefore, deindividuation under this model can be viewed as a shift from personal (or individual) identi<sup>fi</sup>cation to social (or group) identi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

Deindividuating effects are manifested by the conversion of personal preferences that extend beyond group deliberation through an individual's self-categorization with the group. Individuals deliberating in CMC groups are protected from the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence of other group members since the effects of the social presence and normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence from other group members is reduced [11,30,65], thus preserving their personal preferences or values after group deliberation. As such, a group member's personal preference will convert less in the CMC condition. Additionally, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence and credibility of information sources is reduced in media low in social presence (i.e. CMC) [62] further reducing the deindividuating effects in group deliberation using CMC. The lack of conversion is particularly evident in judgment tasks, as group members do not rely on persuasive arguments to generate informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence, instead rely on social pressures and normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence to build group consensus [22,30]. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H3. Group member's personal preferences will convert less in computer-mediated conditions than in face-to-face conditions.

## 9. Effects of media and role on satisfaction of decision process and outcomes

Discussants that are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the group and group deliberation have consistently shown several bene<sup>fi</sup>ts related to the decision making process, including reduced decision complexity, ef<sup>fi</sup>cient use of information and decision strategies, and increased cognitive effort and thoroughness [23,31,32], and discussants who are dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed exhibited similar, but opposing behaviors [45]. Use of CMC technologies during the deliberation process may alter the expressed satisfaction of group members. Satisfaction with group decision process, outcomes and members is often used as a determinant of decision acceptance [7] and subsequent adoption and use of CMC technologies [64]. In general, CMC groups experienced an increase decision quality, but a decrease in satisfaction [19,35,70]. However, due to the controversial nature of the judgment task, group members may have more emotion invested in the social responsibility issue to the extent that one's satisfaction may be dramatically different than expected, since our predictions are inferred from studies using relatively non-controversial tasks (e.g., idea generation or intellective task). Therefore, the increase in decision quality in CMC from prior studies may be overshadowed by the controversial, value-laden nature of the social responsibility task. We therefore hypothesize:

H4A. Group members in face-to-face conditions will be more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed than group members in computer-mediated conditions.

Minority members holding dissenting opinions experience high levels of stress and dissatisfaction from the social pressure to conform to majority views or adopt group norms [2,27]. Dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed group members may exhibit poor behavior during group deliberation resulting from dissatisfaction with the group processes, or sour subsequent relations due to dissatisfaction with the outcome. This dissatisfaction may impede group deliberation and consensus formation. Conversely, group members holding majority position are likely to experience less normative pressures to conform than minority members, contributing to the satisfaction of the group process and outcome. We therefore hypothesize:

H4B. Group members in the majority stakeholder position will be more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed than group members in the minority stakeholder position.

## 10. Research model

A research model is presented to elucidate the complex design of our study (Fig. 1). The major independent variables are the medium used for group deliberation (FTF versus CMC) and the role (majority versus minority) played by the participants. These two variables (media and role effect) are the major factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the process and outcome of stakeholder deliberation when dealing with a judgment task. Four dependent variables are used to investigate the effect of media and majority–minority stakeholder role, including: (1) personal value change (conversion by deindividuation effects), (2) group member choice change (compliance by group polarization effects), (3) satisfaction with the deliberation process, and (4) deliberation outcomes.

## 11. Method

## 11.1. Design and participants

One-hundred-and-eighty-two (182) MBA students voluntarily signed-up to participate in this study; however, only 169 were present to participate in the study. The study used an experimental design to investigate the aforementioned hypotheses. The research design consisted of one media condition (CMC versus FTF) and one role condition (majority versus minority). Participants were randomly assigned to <sup>fi</sup>ve- or six-person groups; thirty groups participated. For the media condition, the thirty groups were randomly assigned to either FTF or CMC conditions, resulting in 15 groups for each media condition. For the role condition, each group consisted of a majority position of 4 or 5 group members and a minority position of 1 group member. All groups were homogeneous with respect to gender to eliminate the confounding factors from gender difference. The average prior work experience of these MBA students is 3.4 years, and the average age and work experience between FTF and CMC were not statistically different, suggesting no potentially confounding effects from age and work experience differences.

Groups were asked to achieve consensus on a choice-dilemma judgment task concerning an organizational social responsibility issue. ## 11.2. Role playing in organizational behavior research

Role-playing has a long tradition in the study of group deliberation and is an effective methodological strategy in organizational research [26], providing insight into the (a) underlying cognitive processes (e.g. the effects of CMC on group decision making) or (b) attitudes and behaviors of organizational actors in a business scenario (e.g. how IT managers behave during a security breach) (Table 2). Our study investigates the underlying cognitive processes of group decisionmakers and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of CMC and majority on these cognitive processes, including group polarization and deindividuation. Therefore, we are not concerned with how managers and stakeholders may actually respond/behave in similar organizational scenarios, but rather how their cognitive processes are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by group dynamics embodied in the media and role conditions.

The level of involvement in this study is a limited simulation exercise, where participants assume a speci<sup>fi</sup>c role for a short period of time, such as a short discussion exercise. The limited simulation exercise is considered to have moderate or moderate-high involvement, more than suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for both studies of cognitive processes and even attitudes and behaviors in an organizational setting. The response specificity, or the structural constraints to the participants' responses, was high in our study, well suited for studying cognitive processes. The role being played was that of another and the scenario was largely unfamiliar to the participants. While MBA students may not be familiar with the organizational role and scenario, familiarity is not necessary to investigate cognitive processes. Nevertheless, the participants were provided instructions pertaining to their role and the viewpoints and preferences consistent with the role being played. This is consistent with effective use of role-playing to study cognitive processes, since individuals need not understand the subtle intricacies of a situation to elicit generalized responses.

## 11.3. Experimental case

The experimental case is adapted from a true corporate situation, and is designed to assess options along a continuum from more to less socially responsible [3,4]. In the experimental case, the board of directors of a pharmaceutical <sup>fi</sup>rm must meet to discuss how to deal with product liability exposure related to one of its most pro<sup>fi</sup>table drugs. Although the danger of the drug has not been proven, the evidence suggests that this drug will lead to death of a small percentage of people for whom it is prescribed. A competing <sup>fi</sup>rm markets a similar drug that is equally ef<sup>fi</sup>cacious but with no lethal side effects. Faced with this issue, a stakeholders’ meeting is called to discuss the consequences of <sup>fi</sup>ve possible corporate actions, one of which must be selected. The options are: (1) recall the drug and immediately destroy it; (2) stop manufacturing the drug but allow what has been made to be sold; (3) stop all advertising and promotion of the drug, but continue to provide it to those patients whose doctors request it; (4) continue to effectively market the drug until sale is actually banned; and (5) continue to effectively market the drug and take legal, political, and other action to prevent authorities (e.g., the FDA) from banning the drug. Options 1 and 2 are considered to be “more socially responsible,” options 4 and 5 are considered to be “less socially responsible,” and option 3 is considered a compromise.

## 11.4. Experimental procedure

The experimental procedure (Fig. 2) is as follows. Before participants were assigned to their roles, they were provided with an information booklet and briefed about the study and were asked to rate their personal option preferences based on the <sup>fi</sup>ve options as if they were making the decision by themselves (T1). Each option was rated on a scale from “1” (least preferred) to “10” (most preferred). After being assigned to and briefed about the board member roles but before the group discussion (i.e. the board meeting), subjects were asked to rate their role-based preference as they thought their stakeholder role would choose (T2). During the group deliberation, two moderators were assigned to oversee the experiment. Groups were given an average of seven minutes to discuss each option, which was established as a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient time for thorough discussion during pilot study procedures. Subjects were asked again to rate all options after group discussion and to select the option they believed their stakeholder role would prefer (T4). Subjects were then asked to answer the same questions, but from their own personal perspective (T5). Finally, subjects responded to questions that measured their satisfaction with group process, group members, and decision outcomes (T6). Compliance (T4 – T2) represents the compromise of role-based majority and minority stakeholder preferences, whereas conversion (T5 – T1) represents the change of personal majority or minority stakeholder preferences. For groups performing the task using the CMC, a collaborative technology Vision Quest™ by Ventana Corporation was used that possesses features allowing groups to deliberate anonymously in a predetermined procedural structure.

## 12. Results

## 12.1. Manipulation checks

In the post-experimental questionnaire, participants were asked, “To what extent did you ‘get into’ your stakeholder role in the case exercise?” The mean response on a seven-point Likert scale anchored by “1” for “not at all” and “7” for “very much ” was 5.37 (SD=1.22). In addition, prior to the group discussion, the difference between subject's ratings for majority members before (T1) and after (T2) assuming the stakeholder role (Table 5) demonstrates that the role manipulation was successful. In the initial rating (T1), both majority and minority participants favored the more socially responsible options. However, after subjects were assigned to a majority stakeholder role (T2), their in-role option preferences shifted dramatically toward the less socially responsible options. The inrole option preferences (T2) for minority roles were similar to their personal option preferences (T1), but shifted even further toward more socially responsible options. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest the role manipulation was successful.

12.2. The effects of media condition and majority–minority role on group polarization (H1)

Group polarization was measured by the group member's preference shift due to compliance between role-based preferences before and after the group discussion. Hypothesis 1A postulates that group member preferences under CMC will be more polarized than corresponding preferences in the FTF condition. Based on the rolebased, pre-group preferences, majority members in the CMC groups are predicted to become less socially responsible, while minority members should become more socially responsible during and after the group deliberation. The result of a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), repeated measures test suggests a media condition effect (F(4,169)=2.306, p=0.06), which is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at a 0.10 level. Therefore, Hypothesis 1A (media condition) is supported. Hypotheses 1B tests whether group member role assignments (majority or minority role) affect the level of group polarization, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the preferences of minority members are expected to be more polarized than majority preferences. We <sup>fi</sup>nd support for the role effect (F (4,169) = 2.289, p = 0.06), which is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at a 0.10 level. Therefore, Hypothesis 1B (role condition) is supported.

## 12.4. The effect of deindividuation on conversion (H3)

Hypothesis 3 predicts that group members' personal preferences will convert less in CMC conditions than in FTF conditions, independent of the roles assigned. In other words, there will be fewer deindividuating effects in CMC than in FTF conditions (Table 7). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings strongly support this assertion. The MANOVA results showed that the effect of CMC versus FTF condition on post-discussion personal value preferences is highly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (F(4,166)=5.21, pb.001). Therefore, Hypothesis 3 is supported. In FTF conditions, personal preferences of both majority and minority positions shifted from strongly socially responsible to moderately less socially responsible. However, in CMC conditions, following the deliberation, personal preferences were virtually identical for options 1 and 5 and fell slightly for option 3 relative to their initial preferences for majority members, while shifting toward even greater social responsibility for minority members.

## 12.5. The effect of computer-mediation on satisfaction (H4A)

Hypothesis 4A predicts that group members in FTF conditions will have more positive satisfaction measures than in CMC conditions. Overall, individuals in the CMC condition were less satis<sup>fi</sup>ed than FTF participants with the process and outcomes (see Table 8). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, ANOVA results showed that group members in the CMC condition were less satis<sup>fi</sup>ed (pb.05) and less con<sup>fi</sup>dent (pb.001) with the group's decision. Similarly, members of CMC condition reported fewer relevant arguments during the discussion (pb.001). In addition, the group members in CMC conditions did not feel that their <sup>fi</sup>nal decision represented the group's consensus in comparison to FTF groups (pb.01). Individuals in the CMC condition were less satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with their in<sup>fl</sup>uence over others (pb.01), felt they did not get along as well with each other (pb.001), liked group members less (pb.001), and were less willing to work again with each other (pb.01) than in FTF groups. On a positive note, few individuals in the CMC condition felt that one person dominated the discussion $\scriptstyle ( p < . 0 0 1 )$ . Thus, Hypothesis 4A is supported.

## 12.6. The effect of minority–majority role on satisfaction (H4B)

Hypothesis 4B predicts that the majority–minority role assigned to group members will in<sup>fl</sup>uence satisfaction outcomes such as satisfaction and con<sup>fi</sup>dence. The results support Hypothesis 4B (see Table 9). Group members in the minority position responded less positively toward the group decision and experience than those in the majority position. In all conditions, the minority members responded that their satisfaction with their group's decision was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower than majority members $\left( p < . 0 0 1 \right)$ . Similarly, minority members’ responded that they had signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly less in<sup>fl</sup>uence over others (pb.001), and did not get along as well as majority members (pb.001). These <sup>fi</sup>ndings were consistent across both FTF and CMC conditions. Minority members in CMC conditions were less con<sup>fi</sup>dent the group's solution was optimal (pb.001) and were less willing to work with the other group members again $\left( p = . 0 6 8 \right)$ . In contrast, under FTF conditions, no difference concerning the willingness to work together again was found between minority and majority members.

## 13. Discussion and theoretical implications

This study demonstrates that judgment tasks involving preferences and consensus building are more polarized under CMC than under FTF communication, as supported by Hypothesis 1A, which is consistent with previous research [41,66]. There are several theoretical implications that can be drawn from this <sup>fi</sup>nding. First, greater group polarization in CMC conditions suggests that CMC provides a platform allowing individuals to resist normative pressures from other group members. An increase in group polarization suggests that individual group members were less in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by prevailing viewpoints, maintaining and expressing more extreme positions than what would otherwise been expressed in FTF environments. Beyond the direct effects of the media, greater group polarization in CMC may also be enhanced by the deindividuating effects of the communication medium, downplaying individual characteristics and enhancing group characteristics and salient group identities. Finally, our study demonstrates increased group polarization in CMC conditions in judgment tasks that favor normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence, as opposed to intellective tasks that favor persuasive argumentation, which is again consistent with previous research on judgment tasks [22,43].

Hypothesis 1B suggests a difference in compliance between majority or minority stakeholder roles induced by group polarization. Majority group members exhibited signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly stronger compliance effects than minority members due to group polarization. Coupled with the strong manipulation effects of playing the majority role, it would appear that majority members exhibited strong af<sup>fi</sup>liations with other majority members (and the majority sub-group) in stark comparison with the lone minority member. One explanation of this <sup>fi</sup>nding may be attributed to the nature of the judgment task as highimpact decision involving signi<sup>fi</sup>cant consequences to the reputation and pro<sup>fi</sup>t of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Since majority members largely agree and prefer the less socially responsible options, this initial agreement may have induced group polarization, drawing the preferences of majority members toward each other. On the other hand, the high consequences for the general public coupled with the contrasting initial role preference of the minority member may explain the lack of group polarization as the minority member maintains their viewpoint despite the larger majority. According to Lee [44], greater group polarization was found in group members where “being in a group” was emphasized more than “being an individual.” This may have implications for the relative effects of majority and minority group members on group polarization.

We also found strong support for the reduced deindividuating effects in CMC when dealing with judgment tasks (H3), where the conversion of an individual's personal preference is reduced, while FTF facilitated conversion of minority members. The strong support for the reduced deindividuation effects in CMC suggests that group member viewpoints exhibited less conversion through group deliberation. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with previous literature [44,63] as the presence of stronger social presence and normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the FTF condition leads to more deindividuation of majority members. However, this is not consistent with social categorization theory [72] and the SIDE model [59,60,63], which suggests that deindividuating effects are stronger in communication media with less social presence and other individuating characteristics. Therefore, our study supports the existence of conversion effects caused by normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence in FTF conditions, however, it does not support the prevalence of RII in CMC conditions regarding the conversion of group member viewpoints.

Our study <sup>fi</sup>nds signi<sup>fi</sup>cant media and majority–minority role effects on process, outcome and group member satisfaction (H4). Members deliberating in either the FTF condition or majority role have the most positive experiences and satisfaction toward the process, outcome and their team members. Majority members in the FTF condition also demonstrated more satisfaction toward their choice and more willingness to work together in the future. However, no group decision outcome aligned with the minority's socially responsible preference, and minority members frequently confronted con<sup>fl</sup>icting viewpoints, contributing to the dissatisfaction of minority members with the group process and outcome. The reduced satisfaction for group members in the CMC condition is consistent with previous research [19,64,70].

This study found insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant results for the effects of media condition on the relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of majority and minority members. Minority members did not have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence in CMC conditions than in FTF conditions, implying that CMC alone is unable to enhance minority in<sup>fl</sup>uence on majority members. The lack of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence of minority members is understandable, as only one group member represented the minority viewpoint. Similarly, majority members did not exert stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence in FTF conditions than in CMC conditions, which is inconsistent with <sup>fi</sup>ndings of similar studies [77]. Again, one explanation may be the relative numbers of majority to minority members drowns out the effect of the media condition. Smaller group sizes and greater ratio of minority to majority members may strengthen the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the media condition on majority–minority in<sup>fl</sup>uence. The implications of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings are both positive and negative. On one hand, the majority members are unable to exert more in<sup>fl</sup>uence over minority members in FTF conditions. However, minority members are unable to use CMC to maintain or strengthen their position against the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of majority members.

## 13.1. Implications for practice

The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between group decision outcomes and group members’ personal preferences is striking. Group members suppressed personal values favoring the socially responsible options to support corporate economic interest and less socially responsible options. CMC groups were more resistant to pressure from opposing viewpoints, holding more consistently with their respective personal preferences. Since people assume multiple roles in daily life, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting both their obligations to organization and society, the demands of these multiple roles may create internal con<sup>fl</sup>icts of interest. In another word, stakeholders may hold personal viewpoints consistent with an opposing position, but succumb to popular positions due to normative pressures. Promisingly, our study suggested that the shielding effect of CMC protects group members' preferences from normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence.

However, CMC had detrimental effects on satisfaction measures compared with FTF groups. As suggested by our results, participants preferred FTF deliberation to CMC. Nevertheless, CMC has some positive impacts in mitigating normative pressures from other group members. The deindividuating and geographically dispersed characteristics of CMC may suggest that a hybrid approach combining the two methods is ideal. For example, a decision-making group could use CMC for a straw vote involving potential con<sup>fl</sup>icts of interest, and may continue to use CMC to discuss various arguments. Finally, the group could meet FTF to discuss unresolved issues further. Ultimately, the decision to use FTF or CMC need not be a mutually exclusive decision; a combination of both processes may be the best approach.

The effectiveness of the role-playing exercise evident in the strong manipulation check has interesting implications for practice and policy. Participants, when acting out the majority stakeholder role, left their personal preferences “at the door,” and enacted the perceived role values and expectations. Management should be sensitive about the nature of the role assigned to employees, and the expectations and values that are conveyed with the role. Even more surprising from our study <sup>fi</sup>nding was the lasting impact evident in the conversion effects on the participant's personal values. Participants assigned the majority role appear to have clung to the role preferences consistent with the majority stakeholder role even after group deliberation. To extrapolate this <sup>fi</sup>nding further, if decision makers in corporations perceive their roles as solely to maximize pro<sup>fi</sup>t for their stakeholders, they will <sup>fi</sup>ercely carry out these roles, sacri<sup>fi</sup>cing social responsibility for short-term pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximization. It is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to rely solely on the business interests of corporate stakeholders to make critical decisions with both the long-term pro<sup>fi</sup>tability and social responsibility in mind. Therefore, external pressures through government regulation may be necessary to promote long-term, socially responsible decision making in organizations.

Another recommendation of our study is that in group deliberation of judgment tasks, using either CMC or FTF media is acceptable, since the group decision outcomes are not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different, which has also been demonstrated in other studies [75]. This is a promising <sup>fi</sup>nding, as this suggests that the communication barrier can be overcome by adopting CMC. There were no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>ndings that group discussion media affects the decision outcome or the relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of majority or minority members. However, even in the short-term, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant conversion effects in stakeholders’ personal preferences were found. One caveat of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings is that stakeholders in FTF deliberation and/or majority roles were generally more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the decision process and outcome.

## 13.2. Limitations and future research

One limitation of this study is that the experiment did not carry real consequences for the participants. However, the manipulation check results show that group member positions were dramatically changed after the stakeholder role was assigned, demonstrating that participants were immersed in their assigned roles. In addition, applying the guidelines for role-playing studies [26], the experimental design seems more than appropriate to deliver the study objectives. Another limitation of our study is the use of MBA students enacting roles of members of a board of directors, who may not fully understand the many complex factors involved, including legal and ethical considerations. Nevertheless, MBA students are suitable in studying cognitive processes, including the effects of communication media and majority in<sup>fl</sup>uence in group decision making, and students are often sampled in similar studies [16,75].

Our study found little effect of majority and minority in<sup>fl</sup>uence on group decision making, which may be due to the use of judgment tasks instead of intellective tasks that are often used in group decision-making studies. Judgment tasks are important for managers and executives who must deal with decisions with incomplete information and no clear or de<sup>fi</sup>nite answers. However, judgment tasks strongly emphasize normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence over informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence [37,43]. Majority members rely on normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence to ostracize and alienate minority members, limiting the effectiveness of informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence and persuasive arguments. Since minority members rely on informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence, the emphasis on normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence in judgment tasks may detract from the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the minority. In other words, the nature of the judgment task combined with the CMC condition may have cancelled the positive effects on normative and informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence, respectively. Future research may reveal differences in majority–minority in<sup>fl</sup>uence during judgment and intellective tasks using CMC.

The strong effects of FTF and majority role on group members’ satisfaction with group processes and outcome may have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant long-term implications for recurring group deliberation. However, the difference in satisfaction levels of communication media and majority–minority role did not have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impacts on group outcomes. Management should be aware of this initial hurdle and provide training or hands-on practice before using CMC as a normal communication tool or assigning minority members to fend for themselves alone on CMC. Future research using a longitudinal study through repeated interactions may help shed light on how communication media and majority–minority role in<sup>fl</sup>uence group decision making over time.

## 14. Conclusion

Stakeholder group deliberation of value-laden social responsibility issues is often complicated and chaotic. The objective of our study is to examine the impact of different communication media on group decision making involving high-impact judgment tasks. Our study <sup>fi</sup>nds that the communication media has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive effects on group decision making. Group members in the CMC condition were shielded from social comparison and normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence and afforded more freedom to reject or ignore the prevailing group viewpoint and, therefore, were able to maintain their personal viewpoints and preferences. We <sup>fi</sup>nd similar effects for the assigned majority–minority role, where the minority members were more polarized than majority members, suggesting that the minority resisted the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of majority members, thus effectively maintaining the deviant status in the group.

However, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings do not support the prediction that minority members in the CMC condition will exert more informational in<sup>fl</sup>uence over the majority due to the reduction of social cues, while the majority members in the FTF condition will exert more normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence over minority members due to the rich interactions of FTF. Both predictions were not realized in our study, which is inconsistent with <sup>fi</sup>ndings based on social presence theory [24] and majority/minority in<sup>fl</sup>uence [5,53,71]. The nature of the value-laden judgment task may be one explanation, since normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence is more effective in judgment tasks (favoring the majority) and the high-impact decision creates additional emotional investment of the stakeholders (favoring the minority).

In addition, our study <sup>fi</sup>nds strong deindividuation effects in FTF conditions, where the group members quickly shed their personal preference, shifting from socially responsible options to corporateinterested options, but group members were able to maintain their personal preferences in the CMC condition. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings support classical and contemporary deindividuation theories [21,63,78], but not self-categorization and social identity theories [1,72], since these theories postulate that reduced individuating characteristics in CMC conditions would increase conversion. Our study also <sup>fi</sup>nds that the communication medium and stakeholder role had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bearing on the satisfaction of group members. Group members in CMC conditions and minority stakeholder roles were less satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the group process and outcome. Therefore, effective decision making in group environments requires an optimal balance between protecting minority viewpoints, and also maintaining the satisfaction of group members.
