# Outcomes of effective explanations: Empowering citizens through online advice

Manning Li ⁎, Shirley Gregor

School of Accounting and Business Information Systems, College of Business and Economics, Australian National University ACT 0200, Australi

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 27 September 2009 Received in revised form 30 May 2011 Accepted 5 June 2011 Available online 12 June 201

Keywords: Online advisory service Online advisory systems E-government Empowerment Transparency Government Service Provider Image

## a b s t r a c t

Online advisory services are increasingly being adopted by modern service organisations as an effective way of interacting with their customers. However, despite their importance, our understanding of the value of online advisory services is limited, particularly in the e-government context. Systematic studies addressing design guidelines and best practices for online advisory tools are sparse. This study experimentally investigates the key role that effective online advisory tools play in generating a sense of empowerment among consumers in a government–citizen context. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that tools that have ‘enhanced’ explanatory facilities lead to both improved decision-process satisfaction and decision-advice transparency, in line with prior theory. Further, the study extends the theory on explanations and service design by showing that the higher levels of satisfaction and transparency <sup>fl</sup>ow on to empowerment effects, namely a higher sense of control and a lower perceived power distance. A further positive outcome is the improved perceptions of the service provider, in this case the government agency.

## 1. Introduction

Numerous online advisory tools have now been made available to the public by service organisations. These tools offer advice, decision support and a better understanding of problem situations by allowing people to potentially explore different scenarios or by giving them an ‘expert’ answer to a problem. From online loan terms to health care bene<sup>fi</sup>t enquiries, online advisory services can address a wide range of client needs and are expected to create bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for both the organisation and its customers [38]. However, such services have received scant attention in the extant research literature.

The motivation for this paper arose from reports on sophisticated online advisory systems currently in general use in the public sector. The public sector presents a fertile ground for studying online advisory services, as governments have realised that these advisory systems can provide more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient, more consistent and more useful services to their constituents, many of whom have complex and pressing needs for assistance (e.g. minority groups and welfare recipients). The problem context is well illustrated by a company called Haley,<sup>1</sup> which has many years of experience and success in building online knowledge-based systems for government. ELMNet is an example of an online service, offered by the Australian Department of Veterans' Affairs (DVA), that assists veterans in determining their pension eligibility.<sup>2</sup> This online advisory system was built by the DVA in response to criticism from the Australian National Audit Of<sup>fi</sup>ce, which found DVA's advice in the administration of veterans' compensation programmes to be inconsistent, unreliable and sometimes ungrounded. Dayal and Johnson from Haley observed that members of the general public perceived themselves as powerless and intimidated in the face of the government's determinative processes for entitlements, obligations and rights. Further, some citizens experienced a ‘sinking loss of control’, confusion, uncertainty and vulnerability in these situations [18] (p. 3). These authors claimed that online advisory systems, such as those developed by their company, could provide bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to the citizens, including increased transparency of the decision-making process, a greater sense of control and more positive perceptions of their power situation. This claim was evidenced in the success of ELMNet, which improved DVA's productivity (by 80%), the quality and consistency of its primary decisions and its customer satisfaction [18].

Further motivation for this study was derived from interviews with staff in government agencies (Centrelink, the Australian Taxation Of<sup>fi</sup>ce (ATO) and the Australian Department of Health and Ageing), who con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that online advisory services provide the public with a sense of empowerment.

Despite their association with consumer empowerment and their potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, as claimed by practitioners, online advisory services have not attracted much academic discussion [18,51,64]. The value provided by these systems on government portals for public use and their potential impact on the government's image as a service provider needs to be systematically examined.

This study aims to investigate the design features of online advisory systems that contribute to consumer empowerment, such as explanatory capabilities. A key feature of ELMNet's success is the quality of its user interface, which includes a sophisticated explanatory facility that assists users by explaining terms during data entry. It also provides a case history with justi<sup>fi</sup>cations for the determination of eligibility. This explanatory capability is an exemplar of the theory of explanations discussed in the context of knowledge-based systems [31]. The Gregor and Benbasat theory [31] suggests that an ‘enhanced’ explanation facility is a key factor in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the degree of transparency and user acceptability of an advisory system. An enhanced explanation facility typically includes features such as the automated offering of useful, case-speci<sup>fi</sup>c explanations and justi<sup>fi</sup>cation-type explanations. However, this theoretical framework does not extend to outcomes such as consumer empowerment or image enhancement of the service provider, indicating a gap in the literature. The theoretical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the current study is rooted in its attempts to address this gap.

The aim of this paper is to examine the phenomenon of consumer empowerment through online advisory service delivery in the context of government service portals, with particular emphasis on the explanations offered by an advisory system. The questions addressed are as follows:

(i) Whether the provision of enhanced advisory services, with enhanced explanations of the advice given in a decisionmaking process, leads to greater transparency and greater satisfaction with the advisory process;

(ii) Whether increased satisfaction with, and transparency of, the advisory service has consumer empowerment effects, including better perceived power relationship and a greater sense of control;

(iii) Whether the empowerment effect of online advisory services enhances the perception of the image of the service provider.

The study addressed these questions by conducting an experiment that compared three versions of an online migration advisory service. The results indicated that providing more sophisticated versions of an explanation facility led to improved perceptions of transparency and greater satisfaction with the service, congruent with prior theory. Further, the results showed that these improved perceptions <sup>fl</sup>ow through to consumer empowerment, leading to a higher sense of control and a lower perceived power relationship. The perception of the service-providing organisation is also enhanced.

The rest of this paper is arranged as follows. The Conceptual background section introduces the empowerment theory and the theory relating to knowledge-based systems and explanation. It also reviews existing online advisory services. This is followed by a discussion of the research model and hypotheses. Next, we describe the experiment for testing the proposed research model, followed by an analysis of the experimental results. The last section presents the main <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the study and highlights the implications for theory, policy and practice.

## 2. Conceptual background

The theoretical background of the study is based on the empowerment theory and the theory of knowledge-based and decision-support systems [39]. Consequently, several streams of literature are reviewed, including (a) empowerment theory and the impact of empowerment on the image of the government service provider; (b) theory relating to the key features of online advisory services that can potentially enhance the empowerment effect; and (c) literature on existing online advisory services and empowerment.

## 2.1. Empowerment theory and research framework

Empowerment refers to the process through which people, organisations and communities ‘gain mastery over issues of concern to them’ [65] (p. 581). It is a concept that applies at individual, organisational and community levels, which interact and in<sup>fl</sup>uence each other. The empowerment concept has been discussed in psychology (e.g. psychological empowerment) [58,65], management (e.g. employee empowerment) [6,7,10,15] and medical science (e.g. patient empowerment) [29]. However, an understanding of this concept in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of information systems is still in its infancy.

Zimmerman [65] proposed that the psychological experiences of empowerment should be analysed by differentiating the empowering process from empowerment outcomes. The empowering process refers to an experience ‘where people create or are given opportunities to control their own destiny and in<sup>fl</sup>uence the decisions that affect their lives’ [65] (p. 583). Empowerment outcomes refer to the consequences of the interventions or mechanisms designed for an empowering process. Fig. 1 shows the framework for empowerment vis-à-vis online advisory services, which is the subject of this study. This framework includes both an empowering process as well as empowerment outcomes. Further, it shows the impact of empowerment on the public's satisfaction with a provider organisation. The components of this framework are discussed in greater detail below.

In the organisational science literature, Thomas and Velthouse [58] proposed that empowerment should be examined in terms of four dimensions: sense of impact, competence, meaningfulness and self-determination. Of these four dimensions, impact and selfdetermination are most relevant to the research context, as indicated by earlier reports of systems in use. Impact refers to the ‘degree to which an individual can influence strategic, administrative or operating outcomes at work’ and self-determination refers to the ‘individual's sense of having choice in initiating and regulating actions' and re<sup>fl</sup>ects their autonomy in the process [55] (p. 1443). These dimensions are related to concepts such as perceived power relationship (that is, the power to influence decision making) and sense of control over one's destiny (that is, a sense of having a choice and autonomy), which are discussed in reported real-life system use [18].

Empowerment theory also plays a role in improving our understanding of how to minimise the gap between different power groups and balance their power situation. The empowerment concept has been widely used by researchers to examine the powerlessness of minority groups such as women, racial groups and the handicapped [16]. Conger and Kaungo [16] suggest that ‘organisational actors who have power are more likely to achieve their desired outcomes and actors who lack power are more likely to have their desired outcomes thwarted or redirected by those with power’ (p. 472). In the publicsector decision-making context, members of the general public are likely to perceive themselves as being in a poor position to in<sup>fl</sup>uence decision outcomes compared with the people behind the bureaucratic wall. Moreover, since more powerful parties may be more concerned with their self-interest in a decision-making process, the decision outcomes are frequently perceived as not favouring the people at the weak-end of the power situation (that is, the general public and minority groups in particular).

## 2.2. Sense of Control

Sense of Control (SoC) over one's destiny is a key outcome of the empowerment process [65]. The concept of SoC is derived from the concept of ‘Locus of Control’ (LoC) in social psychology [50]. LoC refers to a general personality trait and is a more long-term orientation than SoC. SoC refers to the degree to which an individual believes that his/ her voluntary activity can change an outcome's likelihood [46]; it can vary with situations and actions [37]. A SoC over one's own destiny is critical for the general public that uses online government services, especially during self-assessment. Anxiety can be reduced when questions are explained fully, and the consequences of the information being entered into the system become more apparent. Hence, effective online advisory tools should deliver full, timely and accurate feedback to a decision maker, thus increasing his or her SoC over the assessment outcome.

Prior literature suggests that satisfactory and transparent website features, such as recommendation agents, enable online visitors to enjoy high levels of control and convenience [37]. Similarly, online advisory systems with effective decision-support capabilities (e.g. as re<sup>fl</sup>ected by user satisfaction and perceived transparency), which increase the predictability of the assessment outcomes of applications, are expected to be positively related to SoC. Overall, value-added online advisory services on government portals are expected to induce a higher level of SoC for system users.

## 2.3. Perceived power relationship

In our research context, perceived power relationship (PPR) in decision making refers to the individual's perception of his/her power situation relative to the government body that has the authority to make a decision related to his or her welfare. The construct of PPR depends on a comparison of the power of the government agency and a member of the general public. In this context, the basis of power refers to (1) having access to key information of concern (e.g. policies); (2) having the capacity to assess how different policy alternatives impact one's own interests; and (3) being able to use information resources to in<sup>fl</sup>uence policy, political agendas and decision making. Transparent advisory processes in online advisory tools can equip the general public with the capacity to interpret expert domain knowledge (e.g. migration rules and veteran pension polices), explore different decision scenarios and, consequently, seek for recourse or voice differences of opinion when necessary.

Moreover, an effective online advisory tool that satis<sup>fi</sup>es citizens' self-assessment needs on a government website can be seen as a re<sup>fl</sup>ection of the government's ‘sincerity’ in delivering real ‘citizencentric’ services, thus promoting a more democratic image of the government. Further, since a well-designed online advisory system facilitates more satisfactory and transparent decision making through disclosure of decision criteria and processes, it becomes more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult politically to ignore or override legitimate decisions and to deny mistakes made in assessments. This outcome impacts the public's perception of the power relationship between them and the government.

These discussions lead to the hypotheses that effective (i.e. transparent and satisfactory) decision support with online advisory tools can lead to better perceptions of the power relationship with the government agency among citizens.

2.4. Improved government service provider image as a result of empowerment

Online advisory tools can potentially enhance the image of various organisations. This study focuses on the image of government service providers.

Government service provider's image (GSPI) refers to the public's subjective perception of the image of the government as a service provider, formed through using its online portals. This concept is related to perceptions of service quality. There have been many attempts in the literature to measure service quality, including SERVQUAL [44], the Kano model [35] and SERVPERF [17]. Although these constructs shed light on GSPI, they are not suitable for describing public perception of the government agency as a provider of services via the web, where there is no face-to-face contact. GSPI focuses on the symbolic aspects of the GSPI rather than perceptions based on details (e.g. one SERVQUAL item measures how uniform the staff dress code is). The spillover of the perceptions of online advisory systems to GSPI can be accounted for by the ‘halo effect’, a psychological phenomenon in which a person tends to make similar evaluations across many dimensions based on a general overall impression [36]. Such phenomena are frequently observed in our life. For example, it is found that an organisation's website design in<sup>fl</sup>uences the perceptions of job applicants who might not have much knowledge of the organisation itself [14]. It has also been discovered that including URLs in an advertisement has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant halo effects on the perceived corporate image [56]. Similarly, the public's experiences of decision-advice transparency and satisfaction with the decision process of advisory systems on government portals is expected to lead to the perception that the government endeavours to deliver quality services to the general public, which enhances the image of the government service provider.

Further support for this argument comes from prior research that demonstrates that SoC is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor of client loyalty and trust in an organisation [37,48]. It is reasonable to assume that an increase in SoC can have a similar positive impact on the public's perception of a government agency as a service provider.

## 2.5. Theory linking explanations and online advisory tools

How is it possible to produce empowerment outcomes such as a greater SoC and an improved power relationship? This section explains how the discourse level and explanations in an advisory system are expected to lead to improved perceptions of decision-support effectiveness (as re<sup>fl</sup>ected by user satisfaction and perceived transparency of this process) and subsequently to better SoC and improved PPR.

## 2.6. Explanatory features of online advisory tools

Explanations are a pivotal design feature that can increase the value of online advisory systems because users rely heavily on explanatory features to use the systems properly and to comprehend the decision processes and outcomes. When appropriately provided, explanations can improve user performance and learning outcomes and result in more positive perceptions of a system [28].

There are three primary ways of classifying explanations: by content type, by presentation format and by provision mechanism [28]. With regard to content, explanations that conform to Toulmin's model of argumentation[59] that is, justi<sup>fi</sup>cation-type explanations, provide more value to users than other types of explanations, such as trace and strategic explanations [28]. As such, an online advisory system with enhanced explanatory features should be based on Toulmin's model of argumentation—using appropriate, justi<sup>fi</sup>cation-type explanations to enhance the user's decision-making experience. Terminological-type explanations have also been found to be useful [27]. These should be used to annotate dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, yet unavoidable, terms and complement justi<sup>fi</sup>cation-type explanations. Explanations should preferably be casespeci<sup>fi</sup>c (tailored to the case of the user) rather than generic [23].

With regard to the provision mechanism, ‘explanations that require less cognitive effort to access and assimilate will be used more and will be more effective with respect to performance, learning or user perceptions’ [28] (p. 508). Hence, to optimise the explanatory features of an online advisory system, explanations should be provided in a highly structured format that (1) minimises user effort to locate them; (2) can be easily triggered by the user in real time; and (3) hides complexities through hierarchical decomposition upon user request [54]. Explanations should be available near text <sup>fi</sup>elds that request user input and where the user needs instant ‘advice’ to make an informed choice between several options. Moreover, enhanced explanations should allow users to control the <sup>fl</sup>ow of self-assessment tasks. For example, users should be able to get relevant help immediately; they should not be directed to navigate through several web pages, which can cause them to lose sight of their objective. Users should also be able to remain on the main assessment window while browsing explanations related to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c assessment item. This feature is important because many users of online government services state that they tend to get lost in the course of being directed to web pages and then are unable to return to the web page that addressed their original problem [1]. Further, it is important to ensure that userinvoked explanations are used wherever necessary to complement automatic or intelligent ones. User-invoked explanations present the user from viewing several automatic explanations on the same assessment window at the same time, which can be overwhelming. Such design helps to reduce users' cognitive load, especially on the short-term memory. Provision of explanations can be further enhanced by the use of multimedia, but this aspect is beyond the scope of the study.

Overall, to design an effective online advisory system, it is important to optimise the content as well as the provision mechanism of the system's explanatory features. It is expected that advisory systems with enhanced explanations will give rise to more favourable perceptions of the system [31], which will, in turn, lead to improved power-related outcomes. Important perceptions that are implicated in this process are discussed in the next section.

2.7. Empowering process enablers: Decision-process satisfaction and decision-advice transparency

Access to resources and a ‘critical understanding of one's sociopolitical context' are key aspects of the empowering process [65]. Thus, the overall effectiveness of advisory tools on government portals becomes the empowering enabler of the empowerment effect. In other words, it is by providing the public with more effective (i.e. satisfactory and transparent) online advisory services that the government can achieve its goal of making the citizens feel empowered. The literature suggests that user satisfaction is a powerful predictor of individual impact [33], and user satisfaction has long been an important indicator of information system success [19,33,34]. Members of the public who experience a satisfactory decision-making process via online advisory tools are more likely to feel that they can control their destiny and participate in the decision making that is critical to their lives. Further, users' improved understanding and maturity in the knowledge related to their self-assessment as a result of using online advisory systems are of paramount importance for successful empowerment, since ‘fostering understanding is really the central goal of informed decision making’ [9] (p. 2314); that is, ‘knowledge is power’. In sum, only when users perceive an online advisory system as satisfactory and the reasoning and decision outcomes recommended by the system as understandable (transparent) is it reasonable for the empowerment effect to be expected. Hence, this research examines decision-process satisfaction (affective aspect) and decision-advice transparency (cognitive aspect) as the key empowering enablers.

## 2.8. Decision-process satisfaction

Decision-process satisfaction (DPS) is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the user's subjective state of satisfaction gained from the decision-making process after using decision-support services on a government website [45]. DPS focuses on the overall subjective feeling about the full experience of using an online advisory tool.

Dayal and Johnson [18] claimed that self-services, such as online advisory tools, can improve the con<sup>fi</sup>dence of citizens using egovernment services. Other research has demonstrated an increase in effectiveness resulting from the use of advisory systems [60]. Con<sup>fi</sup>dence and effectiveness were regarded as key dimensions in assessing DPS [4]. Further, appropriate use of explanations in an advisory system is shown to be positively correlated with improved user perceptions of the advisory system [28]. Users are more likely to evaluate the self-assessment process positively if they are given appropriate explanations. Hence, it is hypothesised that enhancement of an explanatory facility will heighten the DPS.

## 2.9. Decision-advice transparency

Decision-advice transparency (DAT) refers to the extent to which an involved user perceives the underlying rationale of a decision-making process as comprehensible [8]. DAT refers to the advisory system clearly presenting the premises (normative and factual) to the decision maker and explaining the reasoning, leading from the premises to a certain conclusion, so that the user is given suf<sup>fi</sup>cient information to understand the decisionmaking process.

Unlike non-intelligent tools, sophisticated advisory systems can simplify the complexity of rules and regulations [18] and therefore improve transparency. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, these tools educate the user about how problems are solved and support the user by allowing him/her to assess the soundness of the reasoning behind a solution. A number of studies have shown that the provision of enhanced explanations in advisory systems contributes to learning, understanding and informed decision making [21,28,41]. Since ‘understanding and explanations are inextricably tied together’ [48] (p. 447), advisory systems with enhanced explanatory facilities are expected to improve users' understanding of the decision-making process and enhance DAT compared with those that have limited explanatory facilities.

## 2.10. Existing studies on advisory systems and empowerment

Following discussions on the empowerment outcomes and the empowering processes, we now situate this study in the extant literature to highlight its unique contribution. Prior literature has focused more on advisory systems for organisational users, typically individuals in managerial positions. Few studies have examined the value of online advisory services meant for public use. Table 1 summarises the studies that have examined advisory services intended for public use. However, as highlighted in bold in Table 1, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts discussed in the literature are mostly speci<sup>fi</sup>c to a certain research context (for instance, online <sup>fi</sup>nancial planning systems that assist users to better manage their investment portfolios). There has been very limited attention to the empowerment effect of online advisory systems in the e-government context, the exception being [39]. This recent study had similar aims to the current study but had a more limited theoretical treatment and included a construct for decision quality in the research model. The results of this study showed the inclusion of the decision quality construct was not supported, and, on re<sup>fl</sup>ection, was not well based in theory. The current study omits the decision quality construct and allows for a better contrast between the effects of DPS and DAT.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Fig. 2 shows the research model developed from the preceding discussions with accompanying hypotheses. The level of the advisory service was conceptualised in this study in terms of the degree to which advice was tailored to the client and the level of the explanatory facility. Two levels were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed: (1) advisory system with enhanced explanations and (2) advisory system with limited explanations. Enhanced explanations that can boost the value of online intelligent support systems (ISSs) are the result of different types of strategically tailored explanations fed into the right part of the ISS system. For instance, an online ISS with enhanced explanation features not only provides terminological explanations during the decision-making process but also offers well-organised justi<sup>fi</sup>cations at the end of the consultation process. For further comparison, a system that provided a very basic, non-intelligent service was included as a third level. This baseline tool provided only text as guidance and users had to perform the required calculations manually.

The hypotheses advanced are as follows.

First, it is expected that enhancing the advisory service with a higher level of explanations will lead to more satisfaction with the advisory service.

H1a. An advisory service with enhanced explanations will lead to increased levels of user DPS compared with a service with relatively limited explanations.

H1b. An advisory service with limited explanations will lead to increased levels of user DPS compared with a non-intelligent tool.

Second, it is expected that enhancing the advisory service with a higher level of explanations will lead to advice that is perceived as more transparent.

H2a. An advisory service with enhanced explanations will lead to increased levels of user DAT compared with a service with relatively limited explanations.

H2b. An advisory service with limited explanations will lead to increased levels of user DAT compared with a non-intelligent tool.

Further, it is hypothesised that increasing levels of DPS and DAT will lead to an increased SoC and a more positive PPR.

H3a. Increased levels of user DPS will lead to an increased SoC.

H3b. Increased levels of user DAT will lead to an increased SoC.

Further, respondents reported an increase in satisfaction and a positive feeling about their power relationship with the government as a result of using these advisory services.

## 4. Research method

This study consisted of an exploratory stage that involved interviews with government sector personnel responsible for delivery of online services and citizens using such services, in addition to a study of relevant practitioner reports and academic papers (for details, see [39]). This exploratory work provided the grounding for the study and served as an applicability check for the research model and constructs, as recommended by Rosemann and Vessey [49] and in line with Van de Ven's model of ‘engaged scholarship’ [62].

Following the development of the research model, the research hypotheses were tested in an experiment that involved purpose-built migration advisory websites. The experimental design included six primary variables: Service Type (manipulated in the experiment), DPS, DAT, SoC, PPR and GSPI. The experiment entailed the use of online advisory tools by people seeking migration-related advice.

## 4.1. Participants

The sampling strategy ensured that the sample was representative of general Internet users. With the rapid increase in Internet usage in households, of<sup>fi</sup>ces and schools, the Internet has become more accessible and cheaper than ever before [43]. For example, in Australia, there has been a dramatic increase (from around 16% in 1998 to 75% in 2008) in the proportion of households that have Internet access at home [3]. The sample in the study was restricted to people who have access to Internet services and are Internet literate [30], which is a pre-requisite for their being able to use e-government services. The sample for this experiment consisted of 128 students and staff members from the Australian National University (ANU) with varying education levels and from a range of academic disciplines (for example, accounting, <sup>fi</sup>nance, information systems, engineering, arts and music). Participants were recruited through open invitations. Each participant was given a AU\$10 lunch voucher as an incentive to take part. After removing eight incomplete cases, the remaining 120 cases consisted of 75 Asian (63.3%) and 45 Australian (36.7%) participants (based on participants' reporting of their culture). There were 71 (60%) males and 49 (40%) females. Forty participants were under 20 years of age (33.3%), 66 were in their 20s (55.6%) and 14 were in their 30s (9.2%) or 40s (2.5%). These demographic data represent general Internet users reasonably well, since they shared many common characteristics with Internet users across different countries. For instance, there is no apparent bias in gender among Internet users; most adult Internet users have relatively high education levels; and most users mostly fall within the age range of 18–24 years [2,13,61].

## 4.2. Experiment materials

The experiment websites consisted of advisory tools offered by a <sup>fi</sup>ctitious Department of Immigration and Citizenship of Australia (DIAC). The advisory tools simulated the migration self-assessment tools provided by the actual DIAC (immi.gov.au). DIAC in Australia was chosen as a model department because of their well-developed online advisory tools as well as the public's relatively less frequent personal contact with DIAC compared with other government agencies such as Centrelink and the ATO. To further minimise the effect of participants' prior familiarity or perceptions of the government agency, participants were briefed by the experiment supervisors at the beginning to ‘assume this is a government agency that you know nothing about and all your evaluations should be based on this online self-assessment experience’. The same instructions were repeated on the <sup>fi</sup>rst page of the instruction sheet (Fig. 3).

The different online advisory services included (1) an advisory system with enhanced explanations; (2) an advisory system with limited explanations; and (3) a non-intelligent tool (textual descriptions only and requiring manual calculation). The <sup>fi</sup>rst tool, an online advisory system with enhanced explanations, was designed largely based on the principles of Gregor and Benbasat [28]. The background of this advisory tool automatically refreshed to show explanations that corresponded to the self-assessment component selected by the participant. The explanations were presented in a well-structured way, and the complexities were hidden until the user requested them through hierarchical decomposition [26,54]. Discussions with pilot participants con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that the explanations were easily understandable and helpful. The second website, an online advisory system with limited explanations, differed from the <sup>fi</sup>rst in that justi<sup>fi</sup>cation-type explanations were removed [28]. The resultant lower level of transparency made it more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to understand the reasons for the <sup>fi</sup>nal assessment outcome. The non-intelligent tool was a 10-page electronic document that contained all the relevant rules and regulations for self-assessment. With this tool, it took ‘lots of patience and energy to <sup>fi</sup>nd out your eligibility’, as one pilot-test respondent commented. All three tools had identical rule bases in terms of the information contained.

The questionnaire used to collect participant feedback consisted of <sup>fi</sup>ve parameters: DPS, DAT, SoC, PPR and GSPI. Except for GSPI, the measures for the constructs utilised in the questionnaires were all derived from existing scales that exhibited good psychometric properties. Table 2 outlines the scales in the questionnaire and their sources. For new scales like GSPI, we rigorously followed a three-stage process for construct development, testing and re<sup>fi</sup>nement. The three-stage process involved exploring the domain and dimension of a construct, purifying candidate items and, <sup>fi</sup>nally, statistically validating and con<sup>fi</sup>rming the measurement model (refer to Table 2). The validity and reliability of the adapted scales have demonstrated good measurement properties in the partial least squares model (refer to Section 5 for detail).

## 4.3. Design and procedure

Pilot tests were carried out before conducting the actual experiment to ensure that the process was smooth and that the level of dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of the case scenarios was appropriate. Nine people participated in the pilot test. The time to complete the self-assessment task was about 20 min. Different case scenarios with varying levels of assessment dif<sup>fi</sup>culty were incorporated into the pilot to examine the impact of ‘task complexity’, which was a controlled variable. Feedback provided by the pilot subjects provided was valuable for the actual experiment.

The experiment was conducted in computer laboratories. A between-subject design was used. The treatment variable was the service type available on the e-government website. Experiment participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups and directed to one of the three versions of the simulated government website to conduct the self-assessment: the <sup>fi</sup>rst group used the advisory system with enhanced explanations; the second group used the advisory system with limited explanations; and the third group used the non-intelligent tool. Experiment supervisors <sup>fi</sup>rst instructed each participant to read a hypothetical case scenario (Appendix I), which contained details such as the age, marital status, education background, language capacity and country of origin that the participant would use during the self-assessment. Then, participants were asked to utilise the assigned online advisory services on the simulated government website to complete the self-assessment tasks, which involved answering questions such as ‘Which of these basic requirements (for migration) do you meet?’, ‘How many points did you get for each of the sections?’, ‘How many points did you get in total?’ and ‘Are you eligible for migration to Australia?’. Finally, participants completed a questionnaire (see Section 4.2) to report their experiences.

## 4.4. Controlled variable

The experiment was controlled in several ways. First, the variable – task complexity – was held constant in the study to eliminate its impact on the dependent variables (DVs). This control was needed because higher task complexity tends to result in longer processing times and reduced likelihood of a satisfactory outcome [63]. Second, the time for conducting the self-assessment was also held constant across different experimental groups to prevent time from in<sup>fl</sup>uencing decision-support effectiveness, especially the perceived transparency of the process. Prior literature indicated that time has an effect on the quality of the decision made [31,53]. Therefore, the time for the selfassessment was a controlled factor during the experiments (20 min) [53]. Finally, we expected the participants to be competent in using the computer and the Internet, so that the DVs in the study were mostly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the type of advisory support provided, rather than users' expertise at using the computers and the Internet.

## 5. Data analysis and results

Initial data screening and checking for incompleteness or failure to pass consistency checking led to eight questionnaires being removed from the 128 cases collected. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests on demographic data of different treatment groups showed no signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant bias in the demographics between the three groups [52].

After data screening, hypotheses H1 and H2 were tested with ANOVA to examine the impact of different types of support on DPS and DAT. There are primarily two assumptions for ANOVA [40]. First, checking for skewness and Shapiro–Wilks value showed no problems with the normal distribution assumption for all treatment groups. Second, there were no major concerns with homogeneity of variance based on Levene's test (pN0.01). ANOVA analysis showed that there were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences among the groups in terms of DPS $( \mathrm { ~ F ~ } ( 2 , 1 1 7 ) = 3 1 . 0 6 8 , \mathrm { ~ p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ and DAT $( \mathrm { F } ~ ( 2 , 1 1 7 ) = 1 4 . 7 9 9 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$

Post-hoc tests using least signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference (LSD) showed that for DPS, the group using the online advisory tool with enhanced explanations (M=5.43, std.=0.56) had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher mean than the group using the advisory tool with limited tailored explanations (M= 4.75, std. = 1.01), which, in turn, had a signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly higher mean than the group using the non-intelligent tool (M=3.74, std.=1.20), supporting H1a and H1b. Similarly, with DAT, the group using the online advisory tool with enhanced explanations (M=4.97, std.=0.61) had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher mean than the group using the advisory tool with limited explanations (M=4.32, std.= 1.20), which, in turn, had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher mean than the group using the non-intelligent tool (M=3.81, std.=1.15), supporting H2a and H2b.

Next, hypotheses H3 to H6, which involve complex relationships between multiple DVs, namely DPS, DAT, SoC, PPR and GSPI, were analysed with the partial least squares (PLS) method, which is suitable for a wide variety of analyses, from complex models to low case-to-parameters ratios [32].Two aspects of the model were assessed: (1) the measurement model, which re<sup>fl</sup>ects whether the instrument has acceptable measurement properties and (2) the structural model, which examines the relationships between different variables [25] (p. 37).

The measurement model (Table 3) was examined for internal consistency, convergent validity and discriminant validity [5]. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's α, which exceeded 0.7 for every construct, demonstrating high internal consistency. Convergent validity of the constructs was evaluated through AVE values, all of which exceeded 0.5, indicating at least 50% of the construct variance was due to its indicators [12]. Lastly, comparing item loadings on the original construct (highlighted diagonal values in Table 3), which were all greater than inter-construct correlations (off-diagonal values), indicated good discriminant validity. Overall, all statistical results indicated good psychometric properties for this measurement model.

The structural model was assessed with PLS bootstrapping methods (with 600 samples). The relationships between latent variables were interpreted by examining the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients and T-value (Table 4 and Fig. 4). Starting with H3 hypothesis set, DPS had a positive impact on participants' SoC over the decision outcomes (pb0.01) (H3a). DAT positively affected the participants' SoC over the decision outcomes (pb0.01) (H3b). Of these, DAT had the stronger effect (β=0.47) on SoC. For the H4 series hypotheses, H4a was not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (pN0.05), indicating that DPS had no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on PPR. DAT was shown to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact the public's PPR (pb0.01), supporting H4b. Overall, DAT had a stronger predictive power on PPR (β=−0.38; pb0.01). In the H5 series, DPS (H5a) was shown to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly positive effect on GSPI (pb0.05). H5b was also supported (pb0.01), with DAT having a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly positive effect on GSPI. For GSPI, both DAT (β=0.33, pb0.01) and DPS (β=0.24, pb0.01) had similar substantive impacts. H6a was not supported, indicating that SoC had no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on GSPI. H6b was supported (pb0.05), indicating that PPR had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on GSPI.

## 6. Discussion and conclusions

## 6.1. Overview

Online service delivery has the capacity to dramatically transform the service industry, just as mass production revolutionised manufacturing [57]. Online advisory services are expected to serve as the primary channel of interaction between modern organisations and their customers and are therefore an important topic for research [57]. Inspired by insights from theory and practice, this study adopts an ‘empowerment’ perspective for examining the value of online advisory services meant for public use in the e-government context.

The study provides useful practical insights. For example, a welldesigned online advisory tool provides a more transparent decisionmaking process. An online advisory tool with enhanced explanations achieves this goal better than those with limited explanations, which, in turn, are shown to be better than basic, non-intelligent tools. Among the effectiveness indicators of support tools, transparency is shown to be the most critical for improving citizen's perceptions of the government, especially in terms of the public's SoC over their own destiny in application and PPRs. Of interest here is the comment by Dayal and Johnson [18]: ‘some agencies are uncomfortable with this power shift. Inevitably it requires a greater focus on audit and checking that people have been providing the correct information. So the new relationship is a more accurate re<sup>fl</sup>ection of the way things should be’ (p. 12).

Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on the procedure of the study, there are three key points of interest. First, the iterative method used for model development proved a useful starting position. This method combined practical exploration with a study of industry reports and applicability checks through initial interviews [49]. Second, by using a variety of exploratory techniques, we were able to develop a practical understanding of the phenomenon despite the dearth of published work in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Third, the process produced a theoretical model that can be used as a basis for future testing and empirical analysis.

## 6.2. Limitations

The limitations of this study should be taken into account while interpreting the research <sup>fi</sup>ndings. A key limitation is related to the use of explanations by the participants assigned to the ‘online advisory system with enhanced explanations’ during their selfassessment. It is important to note that explanation availability is not equivalent to explanation use and ‘accessing an explanation is not necessarily the same as reading and understanding of explanations’ [27]. The dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in measuring actual ‘explanation use’ has been acknowledged by various researchers [20,22,27,42]. Alternatives such as a protocol analysis technique with concurrent verbalisation [22] sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce experiment realism and run the risks of sensitising participants [27]. However, it must be mentioned that in this study, the participants in the group using the online advisory tool with enhanced explanations were asked to indicate ‘how frequently they had accessed the explanations’ at the end of the questionnaire. The responses showed high frequency of usage, suggesting that this limitation had minimal impact on the results of the study.

## 6.3. Theoretical contributions

The study has implications for the theory of empowerment, especially in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of information systems. The ability of information systems to empower the general public is a topic that has rarely been studied. The empowerment theory proposes that through empowering processes, people, organisations and communities can ‘gain mastery over issues of concern to them’ [47,65] (p. 581). Effective online advisory systems empower people with the capacity to conduct self-assessment, explore different decision scenarios, interpret administrative decision-making processes and predict their application outcomes. Thus, the public can gain mastery over application-related issues of concern to them. The psychological empowerment theory states that both qualitative and quantitative measures ‘may be used to study the effects of interventions designed to empower participants’ [65]. The study serves as a pioneering attempt to quantitatively re<sup>fl</sup>ect on empowerment outcomes.

Further, the study contributes to the theory of explanations discussed in the context of knowledge-based systems. Gregor and Benbasat [31] reviewed research and theory to conclude that the design features of explanatory facilities in<sup>fl</sup>uenced learning, performance and user perceptions of the system. A key <sup>fi</sup>nding of their work was that enhanced explanations improved many outcomes of a system's use, strengthening persuasiveness and users' trust, agreement, satisfaction and acceptance of a system. However, at the time, the notion that explanations in online advisory systems could in<sup>fl</sup>uence empowerment outcomes was not conceived. As the current study shows, well-designed explanatory facilities can have <sup>fl</sup>ow-on effects to other desirable outcomes, including higher levels of SoC, decreased power distance and an improvement in the service provider's image. The Gregor and Benbasat framework can, therefore, be extended to include these additional outcomes, with a theoretical grounding in the empowerment theory. This extension is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, as a key motivation for innovations in advisory systems (and information systems in general) is providing better service to stakeholders. Only when we understand how innovative technology features in<sup>fl</sup>uence key stakeholders' experiences of service and associated relationship dynamics can we fully appreciate the value of such systems.

Finally, the research also has implications for the impact of information systems use on social and community change. Zimmerman [65] stated that a conceptualisation of psychological empowerment ‘is rooted <sup>fi</sup>rmly in a social action framework that includes community change, capacity building, and collectivity’ and ‘social change, however, may take many forms and may not necessarily result in a power struggle’ (p. 582). From this perspective, the transparent nature and the empowerment effect of online advisory tools can be regarded as a starting point for a long-term process of social change. West [24] argued that ‘the virtue of studying short term change is that it provides hints about longer term shifts and gives policy makers benchmarks for evaluating how close they are to achieving particular goals and outcomes’ (p. 15). This study serves as a reference point for future work in empirically linking online information system usage to outcomes that alter a citizen's mindset with respect to traditional bureaucracy.

## 6.4. Implications for practice and policy

The research has implications for policy and practice in the following aspects. First, in order to improve public perceptions of their service image, where feasible, government agencies should strive to improve transparency in the self-assessment and administrative decision-making processes. This improvement could be greatly facilitated through the provision of effective and interactive selfassessment tools online. Merely moving the traditional lengthy documents that incorporate rules or regulations online will not earn the government agency the credit of being citizen-centric in its approach to service delivery—neither will providing a seemingly intelligent tool that tells citizens little about how and why a decision is made. Given that many government portals in Europe, Great Britain and the United States are still ‘predominately non-interactive and non-deliberative’ [11], the results of the study serve as a good starting point to improve the current status of e-service delivery and responsiveness to citizens, which will help in generating greater public satisfaction and con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the government.

One of the key considerations for practitioners wanting to enhance the empowerment outcomes of other people through an empowering process is ‘creating opportunities for community members to develop skills so that they do not have to be dependent on professionals’ [65] (p. 584). Effective online advisory services are empowering enablers that can help service providers to ‘kill two birds with one stone’. These online advisory tools facilitate the public's understanding of the process and set professionals (including government of<sup>fi</sup>cials) free from mundane aspects of public administrative work. For example, these tools can reduce the need to repeatedly answer similar phone calls or reply to e-mails. Valuable staff resources could be more wisely invested in other key areas. At the same time, while staff resources devoted to addressing the public's enquiries can be reduced, the online advisory services ensure that the clients still feel empowered and develop a sense of autonomy, which leads to their having better perceptions of the service provider.

Another important implication of this study is for the practitioners who are responsible for designing government websites. The design principles for enhanced explanatory features of online government advisory systems discussed in the paper should serve as a useful guide for these practitioners (see [31]).

## 6.5. Directions for future research

Some opportunities for extending the study are discussed in this section. First, this research limits its sample to citizens familiar with the Internet. However, in order to have an overall picture of whether, and how, e-government initiatives serve the purposes of extending democratic outreach, especially among people who cannot access the Internet, future research could consider linking online self-assessment services to the easily available of<sup>fl</sup>ine services that embrace a broader range of citizens. Second, the study focuses on explanatory features of online advisory services. With rapid advancement of various arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence technologies, future experiments could consider incorporating more advanced functions into advisory services, such as query by voice. Finally, with mobile devices emerging as the preferred medium for Internet browsing, the optimisation of explanatory facilities constrained by mobile interaction limitations serves as a further avenue for research.
