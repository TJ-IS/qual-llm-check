# REPEATED INTERACTIONS VERSUS SOCIAL TIES: QUANTIFYING THE ECONOMIC VALUE OF TRUST, FORGIVENESS, AND REPUTATION USING A FIELD EXPERIMENT<sup>1</sup>

Ravi Bapna Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN 55455 U.S.A. {rbapna@umn.edu}

Liangfei Qiu Warrington College of Business, University of Florida, Gainesville, FL 32611 U.S.A. {liangfeiqiu@ufl.edu}

Sarah Rice Mays School of Business, Texas A&M University, College Station, TX 77843 U.S.A. {srice@mays.tamu.edu}

The growing importance of online social networks provides fertile ground for researchers seeking to gain a deeper understanding of fundamental constructs of human behavior, such as trust and forgiveness, and their linkage to social ties. Through a field experiment that uses data from the Facebook API to measure social ties that connect our subjects, we separate forward-looking instrumental trust from static intrinsic trust and show that the level of instrumental trust and forgiveness, and the effect of forgiveness on deterring future defections, crucially depend on the strength of social ties. We find that the level of trust under social repeated play is greater than the level of trust under anonymous repeated play, which in turn is greater than the level of trust under anonymous one shot games. We also uncover forgiveness as a key mechanism that facilitates the cooperative equilibrium being more stable in the presence of social ties: If the trading partners are socially connected, the equilibrium is more likely to return to the original cooperative one after small disturbances.

Keywords: Trust, forgiveness, social ties, repeated games

The best way to find out if you can trust somebody is to trust them. \~ Ernest Hemingway

## Introduction

What determines trust levels between individuals? Granovetter (1985) offers two explanations:

individuals with whom one has a continuing relation have an economic motivation to be trustworthy, so as not to discourage future transactions and … departing from pure economic motives, continuing economic relations often become overlaid with social content that carries strong expectations of trust and abstention from opportunism (p. 490).

Following these two explanations, prior literature has confirmed that trust can be sustained by repeated interactions between the same trading partners (Ba and Pavlou 2002; Cochard et al. 2004) or by the strength of social ties (Aral and Walker 2014; Bapna et al. 2017; Bapna and Umyarov 2015; Rand et al. 2011; Sundararajan et al. 2013). On the other hand, researchers have begun to realize distinctions between the static (emotional) and dynamic (rational) dimensions of trust (Gefen et al. 2003; Lewis and Weigert 2012). As summarized by Sobel (2005) and Cabral et al. (2014), trust can be categorized into two types: intrinsic trust and instrumental trust.

The key difference between intrinsic trust versus instrumental trust is whether the level of trust is backed by the option of rewarding and punishing a trustee in the future. Specifically, with respect to the explanations for trust provided above from Granovetter, the first explanation most closely aligns with the definition of instrumental trust and the second explanation most aligns with the definition of intrinsic trust. From a game theory point of view, intrinsic trust exists even in a static game without considering the option of rewarding and punishing in the future. A trustor who exhibits intrinsic trust will be willing to sacrifice her own utility to increase the utility of a trustee because the trustee’s utility directly enters into the trustor’s utility function. Conversely, instrumental trust is a strategy in repeated games. Even if a trustor is perfectly self-interested (the trustee’s utility does not directly enter into her utility function), she is willing to sacrifice her short-term payoff to increase her future gains in the presence of instrumental trust.

Intrinsic trust is often discussed in the psychology literature and refers to the static internal kindness or reciprocal altruism in human nature (Bohnet and Baytelman 2007), such as a generalized expectancy, where exhibited trust is an interaction between one’s internal code and his or her external environment (Rotter 1967), an enduring attitude or personality trait (Giffin 1967).<sup>2</sup> Therefore, intrinsic trust is generally motivated by the psychological benefits that an individual derives from being kind to others (Couch and Jones 1997; Rempel et al. 1985). Standard survey questions (e.g., “Do you believe that people seldom tell you the whole story?”) (Evans and Revelle 2008) are often assumed to tap the basic dispositions of individuals who reveal their fundamental psychological identity and reflect intrinsic trust.

In contrast to intrinsic trust, instrumental trust is a dynamic forward-looking behavior that takes into account the possibility of profitable repeated interactions in the future (Brown et al. 2004; Cochard et al 2004; Dal Bó and Fréchette 2011).<sup>3</sup> The concept of instrumental trust has been widely discussed in the contexts of the folk theorem in economics (Cabral et al. 2014) and relational trust in sociology (Cook 2005). Axelrod (1984) called the prospect of future interaction the “shadow of the future” (p. 126), as it gives the trustee an incentive to obtain a profitable reputation for being trustworthy. After taking account of this effect, the trustor is willing to exhibit a higher level of instrumental trust. In other words, instrumental trust can be observed because people are forward looking in the sense they realize the impact of their current actions, as well as those of their opponent, on the outcomes of future interactions.

The research framework of our study is illustrated in Figure 1. Previous related experimental work conducted in the laboratory or in the field has mainly focused on (1) the effect of social capital/ties on intrinsic trust (Bapna et al. 2017; Glaeser et al. 2000), and (2) the effect of repeated interactions on instrumental trust (Cabral et al. 2014; Cochard et al. 2004). In the first instance, researchers typically treat trust as the act of taking a risk in a single play of the game. For example, Glaeser et al. (2000) showed that trusting behavior and trustworthiness arise with social connections in a one-shot game. The key point is that in a single play of the game, the observed level of trust is mainly intrinsic trust which has nothing to do with future repeated interactions. However, as argued in Cook (2005), instrumental trust cannot be captured readily in one-shot games because by definition it is a function of repeated interactions. In the second instance, researchers have begun to examine repeated games in which trust is potentially instrumental. For example, Cochard et al. (2004) found that the level of trust is higher in a finite repeated anonymous game than in a one-shot anonymous game. In their experiment, the one-shot investment game captures preexisting trust (we interpret it as intrinsic trust in our context), and the repeated investment game allows for reinforcement of trusting behavior.

While we have theory and evidence on the effects of both intrinsic and instrumental trust, the impact of social ties on the level of instrumental trust remains unclear. In order to bridge this research gap, we begin by asking our first research question: (1) How does the level of instrumental trust vary with the strength of social ties? If, as the prior studies on social capital claim, social connections can facilitate cooperative social interaction in the future and play an important role in solving free rider problems and reducing opportunism (Greif 1993; Knack and Keefer 1997), the effect of social capital/ties will be underestimated in a static one-shot game.

Studying the impact of social ties on instrumental trust is not only theoretically important; it also has practical implications. For instance, many rapidly emerging practices, such as how e-commerce on social networks (e.g., WeChat) conducted in China, rely on technology mediated mechanisms of instrumental trust to facilitate economic activity embedded in the social graph. However, little is known about the underlying mechanisms at work through which social ties may increase instrumental trust. In particular, to the extent that such settings involve repeated interactions that can benefit from patient or forgiving behavior, wherein cooperation is given a second chance (Dal Bó and Fréchette 2011; Fudenberg et al. 2012), we are interested in exploring how forgiveness is influenced by the presence of social ties. As examined in prior experimental literature, subjects showed a considerable level of forgiveness, that is, they are willing to give cooperation a second chance when they know repeated play will continue (Dal Bó and Fréchette 2011; Fudenberg et al. 2012). Dal Bó and Pujals (2013) theoretically showed that as players become patient, unforgiving strategies tend to lose in payoffs to forgiving strategies. Accordingly, our research agenda also involves testing the notion of forgiveness as the underlying mechanism through which social ties increase instrumental trust.

Benefits of forgiveness in the psychology literature have been well documented (Wallace et al. 2008), but past research has not directly addressed our next two research questions: (2) Are people more willing to forgive “bad” behaviors (defections) when they are network friends? (3) Does expressing forgiveness discourage future malfeasance (offenses) in the presence of social ties? In other words, question (2) asks which strategy (a fierce strategy or a less fierce strategy) a player will adopt if her friend defects, and question (3) asks which strategy a player will adopt if her friend expresses forgiveness.

With respect to the first of the two forgiveness related questions, strong social ties may serve to repair and enhance relationships, and people may thus be more willing to forgive their friends’ malfeasance. However, one could also argue that perhaps people are less likely to forgive in this setting because they have positive expectations based on prior experiences with that individual. When those expectations are not met and/or noncooperative outcomes are realized, it is possible lower levels of forgiveness would ensue. Both cases are theoretically plausible, which presents a viable opportunity for our empirical tests. With respect to the second of the two forgiveness related questions, expressions of forgiveness often convey a willingness to maintain or build positive relationships with offenders. In contrast, offenders who have not received forgiveness may view their relationship as irreparably damaged and may, therefore, have little incentive to treat the victim better in the future. However, in repeated games, forgiveness may also encourage future offenses, and expressing forgiveness could cause problems for victims if offenders get the false impression they will bear no consequences for their transgressions. Accordingly, the second half of our study takes a closer look at forgiveness by examining how current rounds impact future play, as a function of each dyad’s social context.

In recent years, the debate on the role of forgiveness has been steadily growing (Wallace et al. 2008). In contrast with the repeated game theory, the political philosopher Hannah Arendt (1958) pointed out that without forgiving defections, human acts would be

confined to one single deed from which we could never recover; we would remain the victims of its consequences forever, not unlike the sorcerer’s apprentice who lacked the magic formula to break the spell (p. 237).

Our experimental results try to provide a complete picture of the role of forgiveness by reconciling these two different views in a unified framework: We find that, when individuals are anonymous, forgiveness tends to encourage future malfeasance; however, forgiveness can deter future offenses in the presence of social ties.

An empirical challenge in answering our questions on trust and forgiveness is the econometric identification task of accounting for endogenous social ties (Aral and Walker 2011; Manski 1993). The reality, and possible confound, is that repeated interactions may create a context in which social ties can emerge as the outcome of repeated relationships that take on a social element. As such, measuring the extent to which these repeated interactions can impact social distance and economic decision making remains an important challenge, particularly when testing these associations empirically. For example, in many real-world markets, contracts specify traders’ obligations imprecisely and trading relations are riddled with informal agreements and unwritten codes of conduct. Subsequently, this type of relational contract is based upon a relationship of trust between parties, and has been studied analytically in the economics literature (Levin 2003). However, empirical evidence has been limited in part because it is almost impossible to generate ideal field data that permits researchers to separate whether successful long-term relations between trading parties are driven either by reputation built through repeated play or by social ties that emerge endogenously from long-term interactions.

To address the challenge of endogenous social ties, we design a field experiment that uses data from the Facebook API to measure social ties between subjects, and subsequently deploy a randomized experiment based on an interactive voice response system (to avoid collusion between socially connected subjects in a repeated game setting) that implements our various treatments. Specifically, we manipulate the nature of connectivity between individuals such that some pairings are between socially connected players, while others play anonymously and are only connected through repeated economic transactions. We then test how the manipulation of connectivity impacts observed trust and forgiveness outcomes. Ideally, social tie strength should be exogenously assigned to pairs in an experiment. However, it is difficult to imagine that closeness and intimacy in friendships can be exogenously assigned in the field (Parks and Floyd 1996). Even if it is possible in some situations, in general such assignments may seem artificial and unreliable. Therefore, in our field experiment, we are not exogenously assigning social tie strength. Instead, we use a within-subject design that is detailed later to address the possible correlation between unobserved individual heterogeneity and social tie strength.

Answering our research questions also requires composing quantifiable metrics of trust and social tie strength. Accordingly, our study is one where we conduct a field experiment on Facebook using the investment game, a wellvalidated two person game that provides an economic measure of trust (Berg et al. 1995). The investment game is our main building block for our various treatments. By conducting this experiment within the Facebook social network we gain access to quantifiable social measures, which allows us to categorize different levels of social ties between players, and we use these as covariates in our analyses. Further, by recruiting individuals along with their socially networked peers, rather than running the game within artificially contrived networks, we increase the external validity of our results.

Our approach of using field experiments on Facebook can apply to other research contexts with appropriate modifications and additional treatment conditions. For example, Coval and Moskowitz (2001) found that social networks help fund managers earn above-normal returns in nearby investments, and investors are more willing to invest in local firms. They conjecture this might occur because local investors can visit the firm’s operations, talk to employees, managers, and suppliers of the firm, and assess the local market conditions in which the firm operates. Also, local investors may have close personal ties and repeatedly interact with local executives (e.g., run in the same circles, belong to the same country club). Pool et al. (2015) constructed a distance measure using residential addresses to proxy for social interaction among fund managers. In all of these studies, however, the authors do not have data to directly measure fund managers’ social networks and repeated interactions. Our advantage is that we have micro-level social interaction data from the Facebook social network and an infinitely repeated game, which allows us to predict more precisely how the levels of investment are influenced by social networks and repeated interactions.

## Literature Review

Our study is related to a large stream of information systems (IS) literature on trust in online markets. McKnight et al. (2002) and Gefen et al. (2003) developed trust measures and examined the role of consumer trust in online commerce. Piccoli and Ives (2003) and Jarvenpaa et al. (2004) looked at how trust affects task performance in IT-enabled relationships. Komiak and Benbasat (2006) explored the impact of perceived personalization and familiarity on the level of intrinsic trust (cognitive trust and emotional trust). Gefen and Pavlou (2012) focused on the boundaries of the effects of trust in online marketplaces. Fang et al. (2014) studied the effects of e-commerce institutional mechanisms on trust. Most of these prior studies have mainly focused on the static intrinsic trust without considering the roles of social ties and repeated interactions in the formation of trust. Some recent IS studies have begun to realize the distinction between intrinsic trust and instrumental trust and investigate the impact of social ties and repeated interactions. For instance, Rice (2012) examined the effect of reputation building through repeated interaction on the level of instrumental trust. Ou et al. (2014) showed that computer-mediated communication tools can help build trust through social interactions between buyers and sellers. Bapna et al. (2017) demonstrated the impact of social tie strength on intrinsic trust.

We selectively review top journals in the IS literature (Information Systems Research and MIS Quarterly) on empirical studies of trust in Table 1. We can see that our paper differs from these recent studies in two aspects. First, using an experimental setup with both social and repeated treatments, we are able to separate the dynamic instrumental trust from the static intrinsic trust and quantify the causal impact of social ties on the level of instrumental trust, a gap in the extant literature as emphasized by Gefen et al. (2008, p. 277): “Trust, of course, is not only about one-time interactions.” Our study mainly focuses on the dynamic instrumental trust instead of the static intrinsic trust, which is widely discussed in the literature. Second, very few prior studies have looked at the role of forgiveness in the dynamic formation of trust. In our study, we examine the effect of social ties on the level of forgiveness and the moderating role of forgiveness on future trust. Our results suggest that while repeat interactions alone are able to solve the lack of information problem between transacting partners, forgiveness is not as easy to obtain unless the repeated partners are endowed with social relations.

Our research is also related to the literature on online reputation mechanisms. Ba and Pavlou (2002) examined the impact of the numerical ratings in online reputational feedback mechanism (such as e-Bay’s feedback forum). Pavlou and Dimoka (2006) further considered the feedback text comments in online reputation mechanisms. Ye et al. (2014) quantified the causal impact of online reputation mechanisms on sellers strategic behavior using a natural experiment. From a different angle, we investigate the role of social ties in building reputation and instrumental trust. Our study is also related to the literature on social networks. Researchers in the existing empirical IS literature have widely discussed the role of social networks in product adoption. The key question is to identify the causal social influence from friends’ homophily effects (Aral and Walker 2011; Bapna and Umyarov 2015; Sundararajan et al. 2013). Our work extends prior research by exploring the impact of social ties on the level of instrumental trust and forgiveness. In particular, we examine whether people are more likely to forgive their friends’ defections, which is a question that is currently unaddressed in the literature.

A large body of literature in economics and sociology has examined how trust develops or how it plays a role in maintaining cooperation. However, very few studies have looked at how violated trust can be repaired through forgiveness. In the psychology literature, forgiveness is defined as a suite of prosocial motivational changes that occurs after a person has incurred a transgression (McCullough 2001). When people forgive, they become less motivated to harm their transgressor and simultaneously become more motivated to act in ways that will benefit the transgressor. Earlier studies on forgiveness have mainly relied on self-report measures of people’s tendencies to forgive and have shed light on the personality that correlates of forgiveness (Berry et al. 2001; McCullough et al. 2001). For example, most researchers elicited ratings of the extent to which people would forgive in a series of hypothetical transgressions.

Recently, the approach of laboratory experiments is more widely adopted in the forgiveness studies. Several laboratory experiments in game-theoretical frameworks examined the role of forgiveness in supporting cooperative equilibrium (Fudenberg et al. 2012) and in deterring future defections (Wallace et al. 2008). Other consequences of forgiveness have also been studied in the literature (Karremans and Van Lange 2008; Zheng et al. 2015). For instance, forgiveness can improve victims’ well-being and facilitates reconciliation between victims and offenders. In another laboratory experiment, Leunissen et al. (2012) investigated the role of forgiveness and apology in the process of bargaining and found that transgressors apologize significantly more when the likelihood that the victim will forgive is high. McCullough et al. (2014) also found that conciliatory gestures (e.g., apologies, offers of compensation) can accelerate forgiveness and reduce reactive anger.

Our study differs from the previous literature on forgiveness in two aspects. First, although the psychology literature has touched on the three basic questions regarding forgiveness (which types of people (personality) are more likely to forgive; when people are more likely to forgive, and the consequences of forgiveness), the research on forgiveness is still in its infancy (Zheng et al. 2015). In particular, most of the prior studies have not looked at the moderating role of social ties on the formation and consequences of forgiveness. In our study, we find that (1) people are more likely to forgive their friends’ defections, and (2) the role of forgiveness in deterring future defections depends on the strength of social ties. Second, the prior studies on forgiveness used either selfreported questionnaires or anonymous laboratory experiments, so their research design did not focus on measuring the strength of social ties using micro-level social interaction data. In our study, we extract data from Facebook and construct quantifiable measures of social ties.

## Experimental Design

Corresponding to our research questions, we implement four experimental treatments (see Table 2) which involve manipulating the social “connectedness” of players (i.e., players are anonymous or identities are known), as well as manipulating the extent of player interaction over time (i.e., single shot versus repeated game play). Specifically, we first run a series of one period investment games between socially connected individuals known as “Facebook friends” (hereafter referred to as the SocialONE treatment) as well as multi-period games between individuals who are otherwise anonymous (hereafter referred to as the AnonRP treatment). Further, besides the AnonRP treatment, we run multi-period games between Facebook friends (hereafter referred to as the SocialRP treatment). These repeated play treatments allow us to study player responses over time and explore variations in forgiveness levels as a function of the dyad’s tie strength. To determine the baseline forgiveness, which will be defined later, one period games between anonymous pairings (hereafter referred to as the AnonONE treatment) are also conducted.

We employ a within-subject design where each sender and each receiver is assigned to all four treatments, and each is paired with different partners randomly across treatments: “Many people think within-subject analysis is the only proper analysis in choice experiments, because expected utility requires consistency of individual preferences” (Camerer 1995, p. 633). The primary advantage of a within-subject design is the reduction in error variance associated with individual heterogeneity. In a between-subject design, even if the treatment is randomly assigned, the control and treatment groups may differ with regard to important individual heterogeneity. A potential weakness associated with a within-subject design is the order effects (the order of presenting the treatments may affect the results) which can confound results if learning occurs in a systematic fashion. Additionally, it is also possible that participating in all treatments sequentially may induce people to anchor on the first game they see, decreasing the differences in behavior between the various treatments. To address this concern, the order of presenting the treatments is randomized for each set of pairs.<sup>4</sup> We then conduct pilot tests and show that the anchoring effect is not significant in our experiment.

It is important to note that in our treatments SocialONE and SocialRP, each participant knows the identity of the other participant in their dyad. The literature on experimental economics demonstrates that decreases in anonymity frequently increase cooperative behavior due to social incentives (Gächter and Fehr 1999; Masclet et al. 2003). Specifically, Rege and Telle (2004) showed that revealing each person’s identity can increase voluntary contributions in a public goods experiment. Using a combination of experiments and surveys, Glaeser et al. (2000) examined how different levels of social connection influence trusting behavior in an investment game. Following Glaeser et al., we view the absence of anonymity as a reflection of reality and examine how cooperation can be enhanced by the strength of social ties. A unique advantage of our experiment is that the detailed micro-level social interaction data from the Facebook social network allows us to measure social ties more precisely than the conventional survey method.

Our experiment utilizes the Facebook social network to recruit subjects to play the investment game (Berg et al. 1995). To mitigate the risk of collusion between subjects, which is of particular concern in the repeated play treatments, we conduct the experiment via a conference call and only inform subjects about the identity of their partner (in the nonanonymous treatments) at the start of the call. The game begins when subject pairs call in to our system at a certain time, after which they receive verbal prompts directing them through the game. All decisions made by subjects are input over their keypad and communication occurs via an automated voice system to avoid any social cues that could occur if subjects were to converse directly. We also impose a time constraint on each round of play to further lessen the possibility that pairs might collude. Because participants were familiar with the games and played several rounds in preceding pilot tests, the effect of time pressure should be minimized in the experiment. The investment game (stage game) begins with two players randomly chosen to be either a sender or receiver. For ease of exposition, we refer to the sender as “he” and the receiver as “she.” If the player is a sender, he will receive 10 tokens $( 1 0 \mathrm { t o k e n s } = \mathbb { S } 1 ) ^ { 5 }$ from the experimenter and will then be asked to decide how many tokens he wants to send to the other player (the receiver). If she is a receiver she will get a message telling her how much the sender has sent her. The sender may send any amount between 0 and 10. The amount sent to the receiver is then tripled by the experimenter; so for example if the sender sends 5 tokens, the receiver gets 15. After the receiver gets the tripled amount, she may send a portion back to the sender. We play this game with real US Dollars and a one to one correspondence with what is earned in the experiment. The individual payoffs per round are as follows:

To simulate an infinite game (for the repeated game settings), as is common practice, subjects do not know for certain when the final round of play will occur. Following Cabral et al. (2014), participants play six rounds with certainty<sup>6</sup> and then we impose an uncertain endpoint, such that the probability the game ends at the end of each stage is $q = 0 . 2 5$ . By obfuscating the endpoint of the game we not only approximate an infinite play setting, but also mitigate end-game effects that could lead to potential unraveling via backward induction.

When subjects sign up for our experiment and agree to participate they grant us access to their Facebook wall. This means that in the treatments where subjects are socially connected on Facebook we are able to use individuals’ data to characterize the social ties between sender and receiver using well validated proxies (Gilbert and Karahalios 2009). Participants were recruited via an initial e-mail asking that any interested parties sign up for our Facebook application. This initial list of e-mail addresses was obtained from undergraduate and graduate students, as well as from staff and coworkers, in a large university in the United States in 2014. Our subject pool consists of 400 people who have Facebook accounts. From this pool of potential players, subjects were randomly paired.<sup>8</sup> In our experiment, 100 senders and 100 receivers are paired and participate in the game. On average, each sender has 21.5 friends within the set of receivers. Each participant played several rounds of our games in the pilot tests, so they were familiar with the experiment procedures and potential learning effects were minimized. In the experiment, the roles of senders and receivers do not rotate. The summary statistics of the tie strength measures are shown in Table 3. In Appendix G, we describe the distribution of the strength of social ties in Figure G1.

Prior to the start of our game, participants read written instructions, had the option to receive further instruction via a YouTube video, and had to get all answers correct on a quiz that tested comprehension of the game rules. (Screenshots are presented in Figures A1 and A2 can be found in Appendix A.) Display panels of the game protocol are available upon request from the authors. We conducted several pilot tests to ensure the stability of our application and to test differences between the four treatments. Our goal was to make sure there were main effects that differed between these four treatments, at which point we could be assured of the validity of our Facebook-based experimental protocol and proceed to the larger scale field experiment.

## Empirical Framework

## The Effect of Social Ties on Instrumental Trust

As per our first research question, one of the primary goals of this paper is to examine the effect of social ties on the level of instrumental trust. In a theoretical model, Abdulkadiroglu (2013) studied how trusting and reciprocal behaviors may emerge from cooperation among self-interested players in repeated interactions with private information. Rigorous empirical evidence on the role of repeated interactions and social ties on instrumental trust remains limited, largely due to the difficulty in addressing the endogeneity of that relationship (Manski 1993). For example, social ties can be formed or strengthened through repeated interactions. Economic theory suggests that repeated interactions among individuals can help build and maintain social capital (Feigenberg et al. 2010), and failure to account for this might lead to a biased estimation of the effect of social ties.

In our empirical framework, we denote $a _ { i t } ^ { m , n }$ as the amount sent by sender i in period t when the game is one of the following treatments: $m = S$ (social treatment) or A (anonymous treatment), and $n = O$ (one-shot game) or R (repeated game). Similarly, $b _ { j t } ^ { m , n }$ represents the amount returned by receiver j in period t under different treatments. Note that if $n = O ,$ , we have a one-shot game: the first period of the game is also the last period. Intuitively, we can measure instrumental trust by comparing the amount sent in a one-shot game with that in the first period game of a repeated play. Recall that the essence of instrumental trust, which is different from intrinsic trust, is a forward-looking behavior intended to sustain a profitable long term relationship or to obtain a profitable reputation. Therefore, in one-shot games, the observed level of trust is mainly intrinsic trust that has nothing to do with future repeated interactions. In contrast, in the first period of repeated games, the observed level of trust includes both intrinsic trust and instrumental trust. By comparing the observed level of trust in repeated games with that in one-shot games, intrinsic trust will be canceled out and we can measure instrumental trust more precisely. We then ask whether this type of forward-looking instrumental trust can be affected by social ties. Note that the anonymous treatments (AnonOne and AnonRP) have no social components. Conversely, in the social treatments (SocialOne and SocialRP) we pair individuals who have known Facebook ties, where cooperation incentives are largely based on social accountability. In the presence of stronger Facebook ties, it is possible that a high level of instrumental trust could be sustained by social capital when frequent interactions are unlikely.

Next, we formally present how we measure instrumental trust and estimate the effect of social ties on the level of instrumental trust. Note that each sender was assigned to our four treatments, and they match randomly with different receivers in different treatments.<sup>9</sup> We model the amount sent by sender i in period 1 under different treatments as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} a _ {i 1} ^ {m, n} = c _ {i} + g _ {i} \cdot S o c i a l _ {m} + \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} S o c i a l _ {m} + \\ \beta_ {2} R e p e a t _ {n} + \beta_ {3} \big (S o c i a l _ {m} \cdot R e p e a t _ {n} \big) + \varepsilon_ {i 1} ^ {m, n} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

where Social and $R e p e a t _ { n }$ are dummy variables that take the value 1 if $m = S$ (social treatment) and $n = R$ (repeated game), respectively, $g _ { i } \cdot S o c i a l _ { m }$ represents the unobserved “out-ofgame reciprocity” in social treamnents, and $c _ { i }$ is the fixed effect for each sender i. In our context, “out-of-game reciprocity” means that in the treatments of SocialONE and SocialRP, a sender may interact with her opponent again outside of the context of our experiment.<sup>10</sup> In anonymous treatments, $S o c i a l _ { m } = 0 \mathrm { a n d } g _ { i } \cdot S o c i a l _ { m } = 0$ , which implies no outof-game reciprocity in anonymous treatments. The specification in equation (3) has a flavor of difference-in-differences (DID) and is similar to the DID method with panel fixed effects proposed by Tucker and Zhang (2011).<sup>11</sup> We are interested in the coefficient on the interaction term and from equation (3), we can obtain:

$$
\hat {\beta} _ {3} = \underbrace {\left(\overline {{a}} _ {1} ^ {S , R} - \overline {{a}} _ {1} ^ {S , O}\right)} _ {\text {II}} - \underbrace {\left(\overline {{a}} _ {1} ^ {A , R} - \overline {{a}} _ {1} ^ {A , O}\right)} _ {\text {I}}\tag{4}
$$

where $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , R }$ is the mean amount sent by senders in period 1 when the treatment is SocialRP, $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , O }$ is the mean amount sent by senders in period 1 when the treatment is SocialONE, $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { A , R }$ is the mean amount sent by senders in period 1 when the treatment is AnonRP, and $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { A , O }$ is the mean amount sent by senders in period 1 when the treatment is AnonONE. Note that $\hat { \beta } _ { 3 }$ has two terms. Term I is the difference in mean amount sent under AnonRP and AnonONE, and we use it as a measure of the instrumental trust when people are anonymous. Recall that in a one-shot game, such as AnonONE, the first period is also the last period (no future gains), so the observed trust, $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { A , O }$ , should be purely intrinsic trust. In contrast, in the first period of a repeated anonymous game, a sender is more likely to exhibit a higher level of trust because they anticipate benefits in future periods. Therefore, the observed trust, $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { A , R }$ , includes both intrinsic trust and instrumental trust. By calculating the difference in term I, we are able to separate instrumental trust from intrinsic trust, and measure instrumental trust when people are anonymous using $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { A , R } - \overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { A , O }$ . Similarly, term II, $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , R } - \overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , O }$ , represents the instrumental trust in the presence of social ties. Therefore, if $\hat { \beta } _ { 3 } > 0$ , the presence of social ties leads to a higher level of instrumental trust, which implies that social capital may substitute for instrumental trust build through repeated anonymous interactions. In other words, a positive $\hat { \beta } _ { 3 }$ suggests that strong social ties may sustain the same level of instrumental trust with less frequent repeated interactions.

A positive impact of social capital on instrumental trust (a positive $\hat { \beta } _ { 3 } )$ is consistent with the social collateral theory proposed by Karlan et al (2009). The social collateral theory is a model of trust based on informal contract enforcement in social networks. In their model, social ties create trust when people use their connections as social collateral to secure informal transactions. The possibility of losing valuable social ties secures informal transactions in the same way that the possibility of losing physical collateral can secure formal lending. They also showed that the value of social collateral increases with multiple rounds of exchange: the effect of social collateral is the sum of its value in a static game plus the indirect value, which derives from the ability to conduct more secure transactions through the connection in the future.

A potential concern of our empirical framework is the out-ofgame reciprocity when participants are Facebook friends. In the treatments of SocialONE and SocialRP, a sender may interact with her opponent again outside of the context of our experiment, and therefore, even in SocialONE, the observed trust, $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , O }$ may reflect some instrumental trust instead of , purely intrinsic trust. However, our DID type specification (3) can take care of this concern because the out-of-game reciprocity should affect both $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , O }$ (trust in SocialONE) and $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , R }$ (trust in SocialRP). By calculating term II, $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , R } - \overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , O }$ , in equation (4), the potential out-of-game reciprocity in social treatments will be canceled out.

As a summary, we illustrate our identification of the effect of social ties on instrumental trust in Figure 2. The instrumental trust between friends is identified by the term II, $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , R } - \overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , O }$ , because $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , R }$ measures the sum of intrinsic trust, out-of-game reciprocity between friends, and instrumental trust, while $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , O }$ measures the sum of intrinsic trust and out-of-game reciprocity. Similarly, the instrumental trust between anonymous people is identified by the term I, $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { A , R } - \overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { A , O }$ , because $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { A , R }$ measures the sum of intrinsic trust and instrumental trust, while $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { A , O }$ measures only intrinsic trust. Therefore, we are able to identify the effect of social ties on the level of instrumental trust by comparing $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , R } - \overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { S , O }$ with $\overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { A , R } - \overline { { a } } _ { 1 } ^ { A , O }$

It is worth noting that the effect of intrinsic trust is canceled out in the average level instead of in the individual level. For an individual, it is likely that we may underestimate or overestimate instrumental trust. However, thanks to the process of random matching, on average, the probability of underestimating and overestimating should be equal. To be clear, our goal is not to estimate the instrumental trust for each sender. Instead, what we want to estimate is the average instrumental trust in the presence of social ties.

To further examine how instrumental trust varies with the strength of social ties, we estimate the following equation:

$$
\begin{array}{c} a _ {i 1} ^ {m, n} = c _ {i} + \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text {Social} _ {m} + \beta_ {2} \text {Repeat} _ {n} \\ + \beta_ {3} \left(\text {Social} _ {m} \cdot \text {Repeat} _ {n}\right) + \beta_ {4} \left(\text {Social} _ {m} \cdot \text {Enbeddedness} _ {i j}\right) \\ + \beta_ {5} \left(\text {Social} _ {m} \cdot \text {PhotosTagged} _ {i j}\right) + \beta_ {6} \left(\text {Social} _ {m} \cdot \text {SharedWallposts} _ {i j}\right) \\ + \beta_ {7} \left(\text {Social} _ {m} \cdot \text {Repeat} _ {n} \cdot \text {Embeddedness} _ {i j}\right) \\ + \beta_ {8} \left(\text {Social} _ {m} \cdot \text {Repeat} _ {n} \cdot \text {PhotosTagged} _ {i j}\right) \\ + \beta_ {9} \left(\text {Social} _ {m} \cdot \text {Repeat} _ {n} \cdot \text {SharedWallposts} _ {i j}\right) + \varepsilon_ {i 1} ^ {m, n} \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

where Embeddedness , PhotosTagged , and SharedWallposts are tie strength measures in social treatments defined in the previous section. We are interested in the coefficients $\beta _ { 7 } , \beta _ { 8 } ,$ and $\beta _ { 9 } ,$ and they have similar DID interpretations.

A potential concern in estimating equation (5) arises from our matching process in the experimental design. When we match players, we require non-anonymous pairings to be known friends on Facebook under SocialONE and SocialRP. In other words, the matching process is not completely random. One may wonder whether the homophily effect could bias our estimation results: there are inherent similarities in unobserved personal characteristics between a sender and a receiver if they are friends (Aral and Walker 2011; Bapna and Umyarov 2015). In our econometric specification equation (5), the homophily effect implies that the unobserved individual heterogeneity of sender $i , c _ { i } ,$ is likely to be correlated with the tie strength measures, such as Embeddednes $\mathrm { \Delta } \ S _ { i j } ,$ PhotosTagged<sub>ij</sub>, and SharedWallposts<sub>ij</sub>. If $c _ { i }$ is left in the error term, we face a typical endogeneity problem. However, in a within-subject design, we employ the DID method with panel fixed effects (Tucker and Zhang 2011) by differencing out $c _ { i }$ in equation (5).

## Forgiveness: AnonRP Versus SocialRP

Our next goal is to identify the effect of forgiveness: our hypothesized mechanism that drives the effect of social ties on instrumental trust. Addressing our second set of research questions requires comparing the different strategies employed by players in the AnonRP and SocialRP treatments in order to study forgiveness in the presence of repeated interactions with and without social ties. The repeated game strategies in prior literature provide us with a validated measure of forgiveness (Fudenberg et al. 2012) and we expect to observe heterogeneity, such that people with different strengths of social ties are more/less likely to forgive offenses. We also expect that for people with different strengths of social ties, forgiveness may deter/encourage repeat offenses.

Our comparison of AnonRP and SocialRP tests whether senders are more/less likely to forgive as a function of their social ties. From a theoretical point of view, we are actually asking how the strategies used by players vary with the strength of social ties. Because repeated-game strategies map game histories into actions, we explore the relationship between game histories and the level of trust to measure forgiveness. We can also examine whether expressing forgiveness deters repeat offenses when receivers defect in repeated interactions. If the amount returned is positively correlated with the amount sent following a noncooperative prior round, we can infer the expression of forgiveness leads to fewer future offenses in the presence of social ties. That is, expressing forgiveness can act as a deterrent to future noncooperative outcomes and can serve as a mechanism by which social ties ameliorate market frictions. Alternatively, if the correlation is negative then it shows forgiveness could encourage future offenses by failing to punish prior poor behavior.

In our experiment, a larger amount returned means that a receiver is more willing to forgive a sender’s bad behavior or defection. Our definition of a bad behavior or defection is that the amount sent in a stage game (period t) under AnonRP or SocialRP is less than the average amount sent under AnonONE: $a _ { i t } ^ { A , R } < \overline { { a } } _ { i t } ^ { A , O }$ or $a _ { i t } ^ { S , R } < \overline { { a } } _ { i t } ^ { A , O }$

We collect a subsample that includes all defections (the amount sent), $a _ { i t } ^ { m , R } , m = A$ or S, and the corresponding amount returned, $b _ { j t } ^ { m , R }$ , from th e observations under AnonRP and SocialRP. where the binary variable SocialRP takes the value 1 if the treatment is SocialRP, and 0 if the treatment is AnonRP. Note that if a game is played by anonymous strangers, the value of the tie strength measures, Embeddedness , Photos $T a g g e d _ { i j } ,$ and $S h a r e d W a l l p o s t s _ { i j } ,$ are zero. We empirically define forgiveness as the difference between the amount returned after a defection in a repeated game and the amount returned in an anonymous one-shot game. If $b _ { j t } ^ { m , R } - b _ { j 1 } ^ { A , O }$ is larger, we would say that receiver j has a higher level of forgiveness. Intuitively, forgiveness means that the receiver returns the expected normal amount as if the sender has sent the expected normal amount even though the sender actually sends an unexpected smaller amount. It is worth noting that our estimation of the effect of social ties on forgiveness is conditional on defections we defined. A potential concern is that the probability of defections can be endogenously affected by the strength of social ties in the experiment. In order to address this concern, we conduct an additional exogenous manipulation in the experiment. In another subject pool consisting of 200 people, we conduct a similar experiment with four treatments described in the “Experimental Design” section. The only difference is that the amount sent by senders in the first period of games is exogenously changed to a small amount (one, two, or three tokens with equal probabilities) by the experimenter. For instance, a sender may send six tokens, but we change it to two tokens (which means a defection), and the receiver in that pair is informed that the amount sent is two tokens. This manipulation ensures that the defections are exogenous: the defections occur in the first period no matter if people are anonymous. After observing the exogenous defections, receivers may have different responses: a larger amount returned by a receiver implies a higher level of forgiveness. Conditional on these exogenous defections, we can reestimate equation (8) and examine how the level of forgiveness (the amount returned by a receiver) varies with the strength of social ties. Another two confounding factors in measuring forgiveness is that (1) a friend is likely to return more than a stranger, even if the friend and the stranger forgive the defection to the same extent, and (2) players in an anonymous repeated game can learn from the history of the game and revise their strategies (Crawford and Haller 1990). We address these concerns in Appendix F.

## Robustness Check: Pair as a Unit of Analysis

In the previous analysis, the unit of analysis is an individual. Because our randomization is conducted at the pair level, we change the unit of analysis to pair level as a robustness check and examine the effect of repeated interactions and social ties on the level of cooperation in Appendix C. The dependent variables in the two regression equations in the pairwise analysis is (1) the sum of the average amount sent in a stage game and the average amount returned in a stage game, and (2) the sum of the average period payoff of a sender and a receiver in each pair. Dependent variable (1) measures the level of cooperation, and dependent variable (2) measures social welfare. We find that the cooperation level is significantly higher when the game is played repeatedly or played between Facebook friends. Tie strength measures, such as the number of photos tagged together and the number of shared wall posts, have a significant impact on the cooperation level. We also find that repeated play and social ties can achieve a higher level of social welfare.

## Social Ties and Instrumental Trust

We examine our research question 1 more carefully in estimating the regression equation (3), and the results are shown in Table 5. Results of the ordinary least squares (OLS) regression and default standard t-statistics are reported in column 1 of Table 5. As stated in the empirical framework, we are interested in the coefficient on the interaction term Social∗Repeat. We find that this coefficient is significantly greater than 0, which implies that the presence of social ties leads to a higher level of instrumental trust. The implication of our result is that social capital may sustain a high level of instrumental trust (even with less frequent repeated interactions). For instance, Fehr and Zehnder (2009) examined how the involvement in repeated interactions affects lenders level of trust and borrowers’ default rate in credit markets and found that the intensity of repeated interactions (IRI) between a lender and a borrower plays a critical role. The prior literature has shown that our investment game based on Berg et al. (1995) can be applied to many economically relevant settings such as investment with imperfect contracts (Glaeser et al. 2000). In our experimental design, a sender/receiver can be interpreted as a lender/borrower: A sender sends (lends) a portion of his money to a receiver who has a project with triple returns. Our findings complement the results in Fehr and Zehnder and suggest that when IRI is low, social capital may facilitate the cooperative equilibrium and sustain the same level of instrumental trust as the one with high IRI.

Based on the theory of social image, we further explain why social ties have a stronger effect in repeated games. Strong social ties can create additional social image incentives to the focal repeated transactions under consideration and, more importantly, the additional social image incentives created by strong social ties are greater as interaction intensity increases (Blau 1964). Social exchange theory posits that people engage in contributing behavior based on social rewards such as approval, status, and respect (Blau 1964). Social image is one of the most important motivations for social rewards (Ariely et al. 2009), and it refers to an individual’s tendency to be motivated by others’ perceptions. Image motivation or self-presentation, therefore captures the idea that people desire to be perceived as “good” and present themselves in a positive light (Barasch and Berger 2014).

In our context, a potential way an individual can benefit from giving more to her partner is the perception that the behavior enhances her social image. If individuals are looking to gain social approval of their behavior, they should give more to their partners to yield a positive social image. A large body of literature on public goods provision showed that the opportunity to express social approval generates a strong increase in voluntary contribution levels (Gächter and Fehr 1999). In the context of fundraising campaigns, social approval can significantly incentivize individual contributions (Frey and Meier 2004).

A crucial property of social image is its dependency on social ties and repeated interactions. In contrast to a one-shot game, the repeated interaction makes people care more about their close friends’ perceptions because the interaction intensity is positively correlated with the importance of social image incentives (Blau 1964). If the social image utility obtained from giving more to a partner is positive, strong social ties will increase the image utility of conducting prosocial behavior and, as a consequence, lead to a higher level of instrumental trust in repeated interactions. Therefore, strong social ties can enhance the social image utility of conducting prosocial behavior and leads to a higher level of instrumental trust in repeated interactions. In SocialRP, participants can also be incentivized by their revealed social identity. In our research, we do not empirically identify and distinguish between social effects and identity revealing effects. In the absence of third-party enforceability of contracts and institution-based trust (Pavlou and Gefen 2004), people care about the identity of their trading partners (Brown et al. 2004; Burtch et al. 2014; Forman et al. 2008; Taylor et al. 2014).

To further examine how the level of instrumental trust varies with the strength of social ties, we estimate the regression equation (5). In Table $^ { 7 , }$ we are interested in the coefficients on the three triple interaction terms (Social∗Repeat∗ Embeddedness, Social∗Repeat∗PhotosTagged, Social∗Repeat ∗SharedWallposts) and find significant positive effects of three tie strength measures on the level of instrumental trust (the difference between the amount sent in the first period of a repeated play and that in a one-shot game). These results suggest that the level of instrumental trust increases with the strength of social ties.

In Table 7, We also find that the role of the tie strength measure, embeddedness (shared common friends), in building instrumental trust is much weaker than the role of the other two measures, tagged photos and shared wall posts $( \beta _ { 7 }$ is much smaller than $\beta _ { 8 }$ and $\beta _ { 9 } )$ . Gee et al. (2017) differentiated between two categories of tie strength based on theoretical modeling differences: “contact-based” and “structure-based.” Contact-based tie strength records the number of interactions between an individual and a friend. Our tagged photos and shared wall posts are contact-based tie strength measures. In contrast, structure-based measures use the structure of the network to model tie strength and capture the idea of bridging across different groups. Our embeddedness (shared common friends) is a structure-based tie strength measure. Our finding $( \beta _ { 7 }$ is much smaller than $\beta _ { 8 }$ and $\beta _ { 9 } )$ implies that in the process of building instrumental trust, contact-based tie strength is more important than structure-based tie strength. The strand of research on social ties originates from the “strength of weak ties” hypothesis proposed by Granovetter (1973). Granovetter’s original work has mainly focused on the structure-based tie strength instead of the contact-based tie strength. The gist of the “strength of weak ties” is that we always get truly new information from acquaintances, rather than from our close friends. The groups with which we have strong ties, although they are filled with people eager to help, are also filled with people who know roughly the same things we do. Thus, strong ties usually result in informational redundancy. Weak ties, meanwhile, are much more valuable in terms of contributing genuinely new information. In other words, weak ties are bridges (where a bridge occurs if a user and a friend are connected by only one path between them). Granovetter showed that structure-based tie strength measures are important in the context of information diffusion (job finding). However, in a different context of building instrumental trust, we find that the actual social contract (tagged photos and shared wall posts) is more important than the network structure (embeddedness).

In other words, weak ties are important collectively because weak ties usually comprise a majority of a person’s network (Gee et al. 2017). Granovetter’s original work emphasizes the importance of weak ties, but more recent studies find in favor of strong ties. For instance, Bakshy et al. (2012) found that strong ties are more influential than weak ties using a field experiment on Face book. In another field experiment, Aral and Walker (2014) showed that individuals are more influential on peers with whom they are more embedded: They experience a 0.6% increase in influence for each friend they share in common. Shi et al. (2014) demonstrated that strong social ties are more likely to retweet a message on Twitter than weak social ties. Our finding is in line with Gee et al. (2017) and reconciles the previous disparate results in the context of building instrumental trust: Strong ties are important individually because of their quality, but weak ties are important collectively because of their quantity (numerous weak ties). ## Social Ties and Forgiveness

Our second set of research questions focuses on forgiveness. In Figure 4, we compare the amount returned from a receiver if a sender defects under AnonRP and under SocialRP. We find that the amount returned under SocialRP is higher than that under AnonRP. The implication is that in contrast with anonymous participants, people are more willing to forgive their friends’ defections. In other words, the level of forgiveness is higher in the presence of social ties.

We further investigate how the level of forgiveness varies with the strength of social ties by estimating the regression model (8). Table 8 shows the estimation results. In column 1 of Table 8, we find that the level of forgiveness is positively associated with the tie strength measures. The results show that people are more likely to forgive their closer friends. Specifically, friends are likely to give more than strangers, which means the amount sent in social repeated games is likely to be greater than that in anonymous repeated games, $a _ { i t } ^ { S , R } > a _ { i t } ^ { A , R }$ . In our regression model (8), we explicitly control for the amount sent in social repeated games or anonymous repeated games, $a _ { i t } ^ { m , R }$ , and we still find that the strength of social ties can increase the level of forgiveness. Similarly, our results are robust when we compute the statistics using bootstrapping and clustered standard errors in columns 2 and 3 of Table 8. It is worth noting that the sample size in Table 8 has been significantly decreased because we focus only on the subsample that contains senders’ defections. As a robustness check, we also change the dependent variable (DV) in equation (6) to be the difference between return ratio: $b _ { j t } ^ { m , R } \big / a _ { j t } ^ { m , R }$ , where $b _ { j t } ^ { m , R } \big / a _ { j t } ^ { m , R }$ is the amount returned divided by the amount available to return under AnonRP and SocialRP. A higher level of $b _ { j t } ^ { m , R } \big / a _ { j t } ^ { m , R }$ indicates a higher level of forgiveness. The regression result is consistent and shown in Column 4 of Table 8. In column 5, we present the estimation results when the defections are exogenously manipulated, and the results are similar.

Interestingly, the coefficient on SocialRP is quite large relative to other measures. Similar to our interpretation of the impact on instrumental trust in regression model (5), this finding implies that although an individual strong social tie is more important in generating forgiveness than an individual weak tie, an additional increase in tie strength after establishing the initial link is not as critical as establishing the initial link of being in each other’s network (a weak tie). Additionally, the coefficients on tagged photos and shared wall posts are larger than that on embeddedness. It implies that in the context of generating forgiveness, contact-based tie strength is more important than structure-based tie strength. The coefficients on PhotosTagged and SharedWallposts are not only statistically significant, but are also practically significant. The results on practical significance are presented in Appendix E.

Next, we examine whether expressing forgiveness deters future malfeasance in repeated interactions. Li and Chen (2012) summarized the measurement of forgiveness in the sociology and psychology literature. Most of these studies measure forgiveness as a disposition using self-reported questionnaires and might suffer from common method biases (Berry et al. 2001, Podsakoff et al. 2003). In this study, we propose a behavioral measure of forgiveness based on the repeated economic games (AnonRP and SocialRP), and empirically investigate the effect of forgiving perpetrators on deterring future malfeasance. Recall that the definition of a defection is that the amount sent in a stage game (period t) under AnonRP or SocialRP is less than the average amount sent under AnonONE. We define forgiving behavior as follows: the amount returned by the receiver after observing a defection is larger than the average amount returned under AnonONE. This means that the receiver forgives the sender’s bad behavior. Our results are robust when we use the amount returned of each receiver under one-shot games as the threshold. According to the definition of forgiving behavior, we divide the subsample including all defections into two groups: one group contains forgiving behavior, and the other does not contain forgiving behavior. Therefore, we can examine the effect of forgiveness in the prior round on the amount sent in the current round. If we observe that the average amount sent in the group that includes forgiving behavior is larger than that in the group that does not include forgiving behavior, we would know expressing forgiveness can deter future malfeasance, otherwise, expressing forgiveness could encourage future malfeasance.

In Figure 5, we show that the answer to our research question 3 crucially depends on the presence of social ties. First, Figure 5 shows that the amount sent under AnonRP & No Forgiveness is larger than that under AnonRP & Forgiveness (t statistics, p value < 0.05). In anonymous repeated games, expressing forgiveness could encourage future malfeasance because the receiver’s punishment strategy is not sufficiently harsh to deter the sender’s deviation from the cooperative equilibrium. However, surprisingly, we also find that the amount sent under SocialRP & No Forgiveness is smaller than that under SocialRP & Forgiveness (t statistics, p value < 0.05). This implies that forgiving friends’ defection can deter future malfeasance. Our experimental results show that the effect of forgiveness in the presence of social ties is different from the theoretical conclusions in the economics literature on repeated games (Fudenberg and Tirole 1991).<sup>13</sup> The reason is that in the standard game-theoretical setup, the role of social ties has been abstracted away. However, the effect of forgiveness on deterring future defections is critically dependent on the presence of social ties. In other words, forgiving friends’ defection often conveys a strong willingness to maintain friendship with them.

## Implications and Conclusions

In this study, we contribute to related literature by separating instrumental trust from intrinsic trust and examining how social ties influence the level of instrumental trust. We make an additional contribution by uncovering that the expression of forgiveness is a key mechanism linking social interactions to a higher level of trust. Although using the investment game to measure trust and forgiveness might be overly simplistic, it is a useful metaphor and enables us to clearly identify the impact of social ties on the levels of instrumental trust and forgiveness.<sup>14</sup> Our experimental design also allows us to test forgiveness as a self-correcting mechanism in the presence of social ties. Specifically, when person A accidentally deviates from the cooperative behavior, we find that her closer friend is more likely to forgive her relative to an anonymous trader, and this forgiving behavior deters future offenses from person A. In other words, the cooperative equilibrium is more stable in the presence of social ties: If the trading partners are socially connected, the equilibrium is more likely to return to the original cooperative one after small disturbances. A future research direction of our study is to further investigate the impact of the cause of deviation on the level of forgiveness.

It is worth noting that intrinsic trust belongs to intrinsic motivations (e.g., intrinsic kindness) that are discussed in the literature. In contrast, instrumental trust belongs to extrinsic motivations, such as future economic rewards, reputation, or respect (Cabral et al. 2014; Fehr and Falk 2002; Sobel 2005). A large body of experimental and field evidence indicates that when the extrinsic motivation is explicit monetary reward, there is a “crowding out effect,” where extrinsic motivations and intrinsic motivations may undermine each other (Ariely et al. 2009; Fehr and Falk 2002; Gneezy et al. 2011; Osterloh and Frey 2000). For instance, Titmuss (1970) claimed that private monetary incentives for blood donations would decrease blood supply; and the introduction of monetary motives may shift an individual’s decision frame from a social frame to a monetary frame (e.g., Heyman and Ariely 2004). On the other hand, the crowding out effect is much less for symbolic extrinsic incentives (such as reputation and respect) than monetary extrinsic incentives (Roberts et al. 2006). The economics literature on reciprocity and trust assume that intrinsic reciprocity/trust and instrumental reciprocity/trust are independent and additive (Cabral et al. 2014; Sobel 2005). In our specific context, instrumental trust is built through repeated interactions, and it includes both the explicit monetary incentives and symbolic motivations.

(1) Monetary incentives: In our repeated game, the sender may want to exhibit a higher level of instrumental trust because of future economic gains in the repeated game. If monetary incentive dominates, we may see the crowding out effect, and our estimation provides a lower bound of the effect of instrumental trust in the presence of social ties. In reality, the magnitude of the effect of instrumental trust may be stronger.

(2) Symbolic extrinsic incentives can be generated through repeated interactions. Possible successful cooperation and reputational concerns in repeated interactions may strengthen instrumental trust. If symbolic extrinsic incentives dominate, we may see that intrinsic trust is independent of instrumental trust.

There are a number of real-world settings to which our study applies. Our results have important managerial implications on how to use social technologies to facilitate trust and support electronic transactions over distance. The most recent trend is the shift from a website based service to an app-based service where small businesses are shifting their entire operations to an app and focusing less on their websites. Small businesses are now setting up their e-commence stores inside social messaging apps,<sup>15</sup> such as WhatsApp<sup>16</sup> and WeChat.<sup>17</sup> A Wall Street Journal article reported that Huawei Technologies released its new smartphone in China in July 2014, and the company chose WeChat as one of its main sales channels and socially interacted with their customers on WeChat (Osawa, 2014). WeChat offers much more than just messaging, including the ability and social features to interact with customers.<sup>18</sup>

The prior literature has shown that in e-commerce, customer trust is a primary reason for why customers return to an evendor (Gefen et al. 2003; Pavlou and Gefen 2004). An evendor can make use of social ties to increase trust between a customer and itself. Given the social nature of WhatsApp and WeChat, small businesses can tap into WhatsApp/ WeChat groups to build a community (social ties) and interact with customers. For instance, e-vendors may engage customers by creating a WhatsApp or WeChat group where they can share discussions, pictures, and videos on products and services. E-vendors could leverage existing social platforms such as WhatsApp, WeChat, or Facebook to build reliable digital trust infrastructure (Sundararajan 2016) and further enhance the level of trust. In China, guanxi refers to a trusted relationship that can be used to obtain services either directly or indirectly from a person’s social network (Karlan et al. 2009). Guanxi on the WeChat network serves as a substitute for legal contracts, and helps overcome institutional weaknesses of the Chinese legal system. More importantly, our results also inform marketing practitioners as to which type of social tie (weak tie versus strong tie) is most useful in building instrumental trust. Given the limitation of this information resource, our finding suggests that small business should develop many weak ties to facilitate instrumental trust and support electronic transactions over distance instead of directing at a small number of strong ties. Establishing a strong tie usually involves time-consuming, costly communication, which can be used to establish multiple weak ties. Therefore, e-vendors should consider the trade-off between developing a strong social tie versus multiple weak social ties. Under some circumstances, it is the interest of e-vendors to extend close-knit communities to a broader, loosely knit digital community of semi-anonymous peers.

Our results on forgiveness suggest that an e-vendor can make the cooperative equilibrium more stable by introducing social interactions in the design of online reputation mechanism. Unexpected shocks (weather uncertainty, supply chain disruptions, etc.) may cause defections from the e-vendor side. The forgiveness needed to make economic exchange possible comes primarily from digital social ties in the context of electronic commerce. E-vendors have two strategies to enhance their ability to build social capital digitally and to make use of forgiveness in the presence of social ties: First, e-vendors can encourage social interactions in their online forums. It is important for e-vendors to engage with less satisfied customers, respond to their online comments, and work at creating a positive influence on their future experience and satisfaction. Second, e-vendors can spend time and resources embracing new online engagement features supported by social media platforms such as Facebook, WhatsApp, and WeChat. For example, they may use Facebook social ties as the basis for trusted exchange (Sundararajan 2016). These social ties in social media platforms are digitized representation of our physical-world social capital.

As social technologies advance rapidly, we expect that social capital/ties will play an important role in facilitating the cooperative equilibrium and increasing the level of trust. For instance, the online feedback technology has made it so that companies can seamlessly engage their customers through social media outlets such as Facebook and Twitter (Ba and Pavlou 2002). Furthermore, computer-mediated communication technologies can mimic traditional face-to-face social relations and help build trust by enhancing buyers’ perceptions of interactivity and social presence (Ou et al. 2014). The obvious question, and one we aspire to shed light on, is how do these social ties affect the online reputation mechanism (trust and forgiveness in an online transaction)? While ours is some of the first work that could apply to these settings, we also provide impetus for other researchers to follow suit. We have merely scratched the surface of what can be learned by leveraging the powerful tools of experimental economics in the online social graph to exploit the rich data that can be gathered from such platforms.

## Pair as a Unit of Analysis

In our main analysis, the unit of analysis is an individual. Because our randomization is conducted at the pair level, we change the unit of analysis to pair level as a robustness check and examine the effect of repeated interactions and social ties on the level of cooperation. Our regression model in pair level analysis is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r} \overline {{a}} _ {k} ^ {m, n} + \overline {{b}} _ {k} ^ {m, n} = \varphi_ {0} + \varphi_ {1} R e p e a t _ {n} + \varphi_ {2} S o c i a l _ {m} + \varphi_ {3} E m b e d d e d n e s s _ {k} \\ + \varphi_ {4} P h o t o s T a g g e d _ {k} + \varphi_ {5} S h a r e d W a l l p o s t s _ {k} + \varepsilon_ {k} ^ {m, n} \end{array}\tag{C1}
$$

where $\overline { { a } } _ { k } ^ { m , n }$ is the average amount sent in each period of pair k when the game is one of the following treatments: $m = S$ (social treatment) or A (anonymous treatment), and n = O (one-shot game) or R (repeated game), and $\overline { { b } } _ { k }$ is the average amount returned in each period of pair k. For instance, in a 10-period repeated game, if the total amount sent by a sender and the total amount returned by a receiver is 60 and 80 tokens, respectively, then $\overline { { a } } _ { k } ^ { m , R } + \overline { { b } } _ { k } ^ { m , R } = 6 + 8 = 1 4$ ; in a one-shot game, if the total amount sent by a sender and the total amount returned by a receiver is 4 and 5 tokens, respectively, then $\overline { { { a } } } _ { k } ^ { m , O } + \overline { { { b } } } _ { k } ^ { m , O } = 4 + 5 = 9$ . In general, $\overline { { a } } _ { k } ^ { m , n } + \overline { { b } } _ { k } ^ { m , n }$ measures the total level of cooperation of pair k. In equation (9), Social and Repeat are dummy variables that take the value 1 if $m = S$ (social treatment) and n = R (repeated game), respectively. The variables Embeddedness , PhotosTagged , and SharedWallposts are our three measures of social tie strength of pair k mentioned earlier. If a game is played by anonymous strangers, the values of these three variables are 0. If both $\varphi _ { 1 }$ and $\varphi _ { 2 }$ are greater than zero, it implies that repeated play and social ties can sustain a higher level of cooperation. If $\varphi _ { 3 } , \varphi _ { 4 } ,$ and  are greater than zero, the level of cooperation of pair k is an increasing function of the strength of social ties.

If $\varphi _ { 3 } , \varphi _ { 4 } ,$ and  are greater than zero, the level of social welfare of pair k is an increasing function of the strength of social ties.

## Robustness Checks on Separating Confounding Factors in Forgiveness

A potential concern in measuring forgiveness is that a friend is likely to return more than a stranger, even if the friend and the stranger forgive the defection to the same extent. We address this concern by conducting two robustness checks. First, we modify our measure of forgiveness and defections as follows: if $a _ { i t } ^ { A , R } < a _ { i 1 } ^ { A , O }$ in anonymous games or $a _ { i t } ^ { S , R } < a _ { i 1 } ^ { S , O }$ in social games, then we call it a defection. Then, we measure the level of forgiveness in SocialRP:

$$
b _ {j t} ^ {S, R} - b _ {j 1} ^ {S, O}
$$

where $b _ { j t } ^ { S , R }$ is the amount returned in a stage ga me in SocialRP treatment after a defection, and $b _ { j 1 } ^ { S , O }$ is the amount retuned in SocialONE treatment. Using the difference, $b _ { j t } ^ { S , R } - b _ { j t } ^ { S , O }$ , we try to rule out the conf ounding factor (the effect that a friend is likely to return more will be cancelled out) and uncover the extra amount that is attributed to forgiveness. Similarly, we measure the level of forgiveness in AnonRP as follows:

$$
b _ {j t} ^ {A, R} - b _ {j 1} ^ {A, O}
$$

where $b _ { j t } ^ { A , R }$ is the amount returned in a stage game in AnonRP treatment after a defection, and $b _ { j t } ^ { A , O }$ is the amount retuned in AnonONE treatment.
