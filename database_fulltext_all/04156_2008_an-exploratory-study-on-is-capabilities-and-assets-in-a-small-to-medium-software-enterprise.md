## Abstract

There is a dearth of research on the capabilities of innovative small-to-medium software enterprises (SMSEs). Understanding how SMSEs build and apply business and information systems (IS) capabilities is important, as such firms account for over 90% of software enterprises operating in Europe and the US. This paper elaborates and applies dynamic capability theory to explore and help understand the web of conditions and factors that shaped and influenced business and IS capability development and application in one European SMSE. Drawing on the overarching theory of dynamic capabilities, a theoretical model is presented that posits relationships among (1) a firm’s past activities; (2) its integration, learning and reconfiguration, and transformation capabilities; (3) its financial, complementary, locational, and technological asset positions; and (4) the products and services that result, and which are of value to an SMSE’s customers. The paper refines and elaborates the model by describing and enumerating the business and IS capabilities, assets, and products and services of the SMSE under study. To properly assess whether this firm’s products and services were of value to its customers, research was also conducted at two customer sites in Ireland and the US, in addition to the investigation at the primary research site in Dublin. The study therefore informs both practitioners’ and researchers’ understandings of this complex and underresearched phenomenon: for practitioners, it highlights the characteristics required to build innovative software solutions; for researchers, it illustrates the patterns and regularities associated with the development and application of business and IS capabilities.

As small-to-medium-sized software enterprises are argued to be wellsprings of innovation (cf. Baskerville and Pries-Heje, 1999), there is an imperative to explore and understand the locus and origin of the business and information systems (IS) capabilities and resources that influence the performance of such firms. There is also a need to investigate the influence of social, institutional, and firm-specific factors on the software development process. This paper reports on an exploratory, post-hoc longitudinal study (Yin, 1989) of the development and application of business and IS capabilities and assets in a small-to-medium-sized Irish software firm, Interactive Multimedia Systems (IMS), in the years 1991–2000. The theory of dynamic capabilities is used in order to identify how business and IS capabilities are developed and applied in an SMSE; in so doing, the study contributes to the articulation of an applied theory of dynamic capabilities for software enterprises.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: the next section presents this study’s perspective on the theory of dynamic capabilities; the third section describes the research approach; the fourth section presents this study’s findings; the last section then offers a short analysis and several concluding insights.

## Understanding firm-level capabilities: the resource-based view and dynamic capabilities theory

Theory from the resource-based view (RBV) considers the firm as a bundle of idiosyncratic resources and related capabilities, the interplay of which delivers competitive advantage (Rumelt, 1984). Henderson and Venkatraman (1993) argue that business and IS capabilities are embodied in the firm-specific knowledge of organizational actors – which is itself an intangible asset or resource (Grant, 1996). Thus, the notion that knowledge is the only firm-specific (valuable, unique/rare, and imperfectly mobile – i.e. inimitable and non-substitutable) asset or resource was readily accepted in the IS field (see e.g. Wade and Hulland’s (2004) comprehensive overview). It is significant that several complementary theoretical perspectives grew out of the RBV, the most notable of which is, according to Nelson (1994), Teece et al.’s (1990) theory of dynamic capabilities (see also Newbert, 2007). The following section therefore presents this theory.

## Theoretical grounding

In assessing the contribution of the RBV, Williamson (1998: 28) poses the following question:

[W]hat – in addition to an inventory of its physical assets, an accounting for its financial assets, and a census of its workforce – is needed to describe the capabilities of a firm[?]

He (ibid.: 28) argues that this will require the articulation of an ‘intentionality view y that [incorporates] microanalytic attributes that define culture, communication codes, and routines,’ he also emphasizes that this ‘is an ambitious exercise.’ This section begins the task of describing just such a set of ‘microanalytic attributes.’ It is clear from arguments made by Richard Nelson (1994) that Teece and Pisano’s (1998) theory of dynamic capabilities, which builds on that proposed by Teece et al. (1990), is the most appropriate candidate to answer Williamson’s call, as it incorporates extant theory from the RBV, while correctly focusing on the dynamic capabilities of firms (see Wheeler, 2002; Newbert, 2007).

Following Teece and Pisano (1998), Eisenhardt and Martin (2000), and Wheeler (2002), this paper defines dynamic capabilities as ‘those firm-specific processes or routines that integrate its activities, promote learning, and help firms build, reconfigure and transform its asset/ resource positions (tangible and intangible), processes, and structures in order to deliver products and services that are of value to all stakeholders, both internal and external.

Dynamic capabilities theory has, generally, been well received in the strategic management and related literatures (Newbert, 2007). Recent research by Teece and Pisano (1998) developed it into a conceptual framework that help capture and describe the nature of a firm’s capabilities. In presenting their framework, Teece and Pisano focus on the development and renewal of internal and external firmspecific capabilities as being of strategic importance to business enterprises. Dynamic capabilities theory leads to two valuable observations: first, the shifting character of the economic environment renders it dynamic – for example, decreasing time to market for products, shifting barriers to entry through technological change, globalization of national economies, and environmental uncertainty caused by political strife; second, firm-specific capabilities lie at the source of ongoing commercial prosperity of organizations. In elaborating their perspective, Teece and Pisano (1998: 195) state that, to be of commercial value, core capabilities must be ‘honed to a user need,’ must be ‘unique,’ and ‘difficult to replicate.’ Enabling capabilities, on the other hand are those deemed necessary for firms to enter the game, while supplemental capabilities are non-proprietary and imitable (Leonard-Barton, 1995). Teece and Pisano present a theoretical framework that incorporates a set of descriptive dimensions, constructs, and their relationships which help researchers and practitioners evaluate and understand the source, development, and application of such capabilities – these are now briefly delineated.

## Organizational and managerial processes

These describe the patterns of current practice and learning in a firm, tangible evidence of which is to be found in its routines (Nelson and Winter, 1982). For example, integration routines or processes are concerned with the efficient and effective internal coordination of organizational activities and production. In knowledge-intensive firms, integration is also concerned with routines and mechanisms for knowledge sharing. Learning routines or processes involve repetition and experimentation to enable tasks to be performed better and more rapidly – this occurs at individual, group, organizational, and interorganizational levels. Finally, reconfiguration and transformation routines or processes relate to the capabilities required to evolve a firm’s asset structure and also to develop new capabilities.

## Asset positions

These include a firm’s endowment of technology and intellectual property (as indicated by its difficult-to-trade knowledge assets) as well as its relational assets with partners, customers, and supplier. Technological assets, such as IT infrastructures, may generally be considered commodities, and confer no strategic advantage (Carr, 2003); however, if they are highly firm- and task-specific, or if generic technologies can be configured in such a way as to make them unique, then they may be of strategic value (Wade and Hulland, 2004). Also, if the knowledge that created such assets is also proprietary and firm-specific, this adds a further ring of protection (Nordhaug, 1994;

Grant, 1996): furthermore, while an IT architecture is a tangible asset or resource, the knowledge used to build, operate, and maintain this resource may be conceptualized as an intangible complementary asset (Butler and Murphy, 1999). Complementary assets involve the use of related assets to develop new products and services or the mechanisms by which they are to be delivered. Perhaps the most influential complementary assets are intangible knowledge assets (Leonard-Barton, 1995; Grant, 1996; Butler and Murphy, 1999). Financial assets include the state of the balance sheet, a firm’s cash position, and degree of financial leverage. Experiential knowledge and skills in financial management may be of strategic value here. Finally, a firm’s locational assets may influence its ability to produce and distribute products and services at low cost. Teece and Pisano (1998) observe that some locational assets are non-tradable and therefore the source of difficult-toreplicate advantages.

## Paths

The strategic alternatives available to a firm are a function of its past activities and positions. A consideration of path dependencies helps us understand exactly how the firm’s present market position is a function of its past performance and future possibilities. It is clear, however, that a firm’s past investments and present repertoire of productive routines may act to constrain its future behavior and choice of action (Leonard-Barton, 1995). The technological opportunities presented to a firm are often down to internal and external organizational and institutional structures, collaborations, and knowledge links. Quite often it is the idiosyncratic experiential knowledge of firms that guides them in choosing the most appropriate and feasible of opportunities, and leads them to develop the business and IS capabilities that enable them to realize such opportunities.

The following section builds on the dynamic capabilities theoretical framework to articulate a theoretical model or ‘lens’ that helps understand the phenomenon of IS capability development and application in organizations.

## Theory and method

According to Metcalfe (2004), the role of theory in the IS field and cognate disciplines remains ill-defined at best. He argues that to be considered as such, a theory should operate from a perspective, seek to explain, present an argument, provide evidence, and be ‘generalizable.’ An example of a theory that meets these criteria is Wheeler’s (2002) net-enabled business innovation cycle theory, which is an applied variant of dynamic capabilities theory. Of import here is that Wheeler (ibid.: 129) successfully argues for dynamic capabilities as a molar theory; that is, one that operates at a high level of abstraction by presenting a set of ‘causal laws stated in terms of large and often complex objects.’ This paper follows Wheeler in using dynamic capabilities theory to guide its efforts in exploring the creation, development, and application of business and IS capabilities in SMSEs in order to better understand their relationship with firm performance; in so doing, it contributes to the elaboration of an applied theory of dynamic capabilities. Having introduced the conceptual components of this study’s model of business and IS capabilities and assets in the previous section, a model is presented in Figure 1 and discussed in the following section.

Toward an applied theory of dynamic capabilities and assets in SMSEs

Several theoretical observations may be drawn from this study’s theoretical model as applied to SMSEs, viz.

\- A software firm’s capabilities and resources/assets are the product of its history.

\- Business and IT-based assets (tangible and intangible resources/assets) may be core (that is valuable, rare, and inimitable), enabling, or supplemental.

\- Business and IS capabilities are simply knowledge in action – they may be core, enabling, or supplemental and are often embedded in a firm’s processes, routines, and operational procedures. Complementary assets, such as intangible knowledge assets (experiential and technical), underpin all capabilities. Capabilities are ‘sticky’ and difficult to imitate or replicate, even across ‘communities-of-practice.’

\- Valuable business and IS capabilities are dynamic in nature and are indicated by a firm’s integrative capacities, its ability to learn, and its abilities to transform and reconfigure its operations in response to environmental conditions.

\- Capabilities operate on resources/assets to produce services that are value to customers. Services may be tangible and intangible and include products.

\- It is the services produced by a firm’s capabilities and resources/assets that are of strategic and operational value, and that enhance firm-level performance, not its resources or capabilities, as such.

These observations describe at a high level of analysis the central tenets of this paper’s elaboration of dynamic capabilities theory. They are the product of logical deductions informed by a critical analysis of the different strands of theorizing that surround the RBV and nascent dynamic capabilities literature. In so doing they address many of the theoretical limitations of the RBV (cf. Nanda, 1996; Priem and Butler, 2001), while also extending and elaborating the theory of dynamic capabilities. Thus, they prepare the way for processbased research of a qualitative nature, while also helping to inform the conduct of variance-based research strategies.

## Research objective and method

This study’s objective is to deepen the IS field’s understanding of how SMSEs create, develop, and apply business and IS capabilities to build firm-specific IT assets and deliver unique products and services to customers. The research is exploratory and it therefore applies the above theoretical model in its case study of IMS, an SMSE that operated in Ireland from 1990 to 1999 before being absorbed by its parent company in 2000. The following section briefly outlines the research approach adopted to help attain the research objective.

## Research approach

The IMS case study conforms to what Stake (1994) describes as an instrumental case study design. An instrumental case study is undertaken to obtain particular insight into an issue or to refine a theory. The case occupies a supportive role and is of secondary interest. However, the case is examined in depth, the contexts scrutinized, and its ordinary activities detailed. Also Stake argues that the choice of case allows the increased comprehension of other issues. Research into the case was conducted through the use of individual interview, participant observation, and documentary sources over a 2-year period (cf. Stake, 1994; Yin, 2003), with the field research component occurring over the 6 months of May through to October 1998. Social actors from several different ‘communitiesof-practice’ within IMS participated in the study: these were the CEO, the company’s technical consultant, and four analysts/programmers. In order to provide independent and practical confirmation of the development and application of IS capabilities and assets at IMS (i.e. to assess external stakeholder/customer value), one of the SMSE’s customers, Analog Devices Inc. of Boston, MA, was incorporated in the study. Participants at Analog Devices Inc. included the Manager of Central Applications, the IT Manager for Sales and Marketing, the company’s Webmaster, and two application engineers, all of whom were Boston-based; a senior design engineer based in Analog’s plant in Ireland also contributed.

Purposeful sampling was used throughout, with key informants being sought for interview (Patton, 1990; Yin, 2003). Research was conducted at three sites, two in Ireland and one in the USA. A wealth of documentary evidence was also gathered at the two primary sites, and a significant amount of data accrued from informal conversations and observations while on-site at the research locations. The documentary activity of data gathering continued until 2000. Standard qualitative data analysis techniques were used in the study. For example, content analysis was undertaken using a combination of open, axial, and selective coding (Strauss and Corbin, 1990). The theoretical model and framework were used as a lens to analyze the data and to provide seed categories. The perspectives of interviewees on various dimensions of the phenomenon of research and development of innovative software services were noted and indexed and contributed to the identifica tion of additional categories (i.e. capabilities and assets) generated during this stage. Triangulation techniques were also extensively used to provide insights into events, relationships, etc. between primary data sets (Patton, 1990).

Issues of construct, internal, external validity, and reliability were addressed as per Yin (2003).

## Understanding the development of innovative software at IMS: a case study

IMS was established in 1991 as a non-trading, wholly owned subsidiary of Irish Medical Systems Ltd. In 1996, IMS began to trade as a member of the Irish Medical Systems Group along with International Medical Solutions, Irish Medical Systems’ UK-based subsidiary. However, when Irish Medical Systems Group merged with Voyager 2000 in 1999, IMS ceased to exist as a separate entity and was incorporated into a new company, IMS MAXIMS Plc. This was significant in that the transfer of capabilities and resources into the newly merged company strengthened its core capabilities in developing IT-based solutions for the healthcare industry. The former CEO of IMS remained active in R&D in the new company. ## Exploring IMS’ historical paths

In the late 1980s, having established a growing customer base with its healthcare solutions, Irish Medial Systems’ CEO decided to expanded the focus of the company’s ITbased research and development activities to include decision support systems and knowledge management solutions based on emerging multimedia technologies. By this time, the company had acquired significant capabilities in acquiring R&D funding from agencies such as the European Community (now the European Union or EU). A chance inquiry from an academic researcher, on the issue of European Community funding for IT projects, had the CEO retain his services to conduct primary research in the area of knowledge capture that would extend beyond typical expert systems. The researcher and technical consultant recalled his initial involvement thus:

[The CEO] had a good way of identifying EU-funded projects. One project was based around the development of an authoring system that would enable an expert in a domain to convey his knowledge into a knowledgebase and not be too much of an expert in the construction of such a system – a user-friendly system for capturing knowledge. This project ran from 1988 into 1989 and was an experimental project – they recruited me on foot of that.

Although this initiative did not bear fruit, it did provide IT professionals at IMS with experiential and technical knowledge of the capabilities of authoring systems, and of the difficulties in capturing the domain knowledge of experts. ## Institutional influences on business and IS capability development at IMS

The success of IMS’ research and development activities resulted from the energetic manner in which its CEO secured funding from the European Community to conduct R&D, while at the same time creating knowledge links with other organizations in order to allow the company to develop IS capabilities in several unique areas. The CEO commented on the institutional obstacles that had to be surmounted in order to secure funding from the European Community and of the need to adhere to the strict criteria laid down by the European Commission: ‘They did not part with the funding just like that. We had to illustrate the commercial applicability of CBR and our other projects, and realise our goals, while managing the bureaucrats in the Commission at the same time.’ It was clear from IT professionals at IMS that the ability to develop and maintain formal and informal working relationships across Europe with fellow practitioners and academics in Germany and France, and with civil servants in Brussels and Strasburg, contributed to its success in obtaining the required financial and technical resources for research and development activities at IMS.

IMS experienced varying degrees of success in developing its portfolio of technologies; however, its interest in developing an all-purpose decision support system for the medical profession stimulated its involvement in the INRECA I program (1994), the outcome of which set the stage for the development of significant IS capabilities and future success in developing commercial applications. INRECA stands for INduction and REasoning from CAses and was the brainchild of research and practice in IT. For example, the INRECA consortium was created in 1992 by three European-owned business enterprises and a German university, viz.

\- AcknoSoft, a French company that specialized in data mining and the prime contractor in the consortium;

\- tecHinno GmbH, a German company that specialized in case-based reasoning (CBR) technologies;

\- IMS, whose role was that of software integration;

\- the University of Kaiserslautern in Germany, whose international research center joined the consortium because of its expertise with CBR.

The research and development arm of Daimler–Benz also became involved in the project and was one of the first users of the CBR applications produced. The consortium received funding under the Esprit program (INRECA P6322) for the first and subsequent phases of this project.

The overall objective of the first phase of INRECA I was to develop innovative technologies to help organizations make business decisions by using cases based on their previous experience, and to integrate these technologies into a single software platform that would allow widespread use of CBR technologies. Two key technologies emerged from the first phase: the first was the INRECA Tool or Integrated Case-based Reasoning Tool, which contained the inference engine and component systems; and the second was CASUEL, the interface language between all the INRECA component systems. Important elements of a case-based technology platform were put in place in INRECA I; however, for the technology to be of commercial use it would require extensive modification. Consequently, it was the objective of the INRECA II (1996) program to produce a core methodology for developing and maintaining CBR applications – this program was titled Information and Knowledge-Re-engineering for Reasoning from Cases.

There were several successful basic applications that emerged from the INRECA I and II programs (1994–1998). IMS were responsible for two: the Wind Risk Assessment application at Coillte Teo (the Irish Forestry Service), which used KATE-Tools from Acknosoft, and the Parametric Search Tool, which used CBR-Works from tecHinno.

Developing and applying IMS’ business and IS capabilities at Analog Devices Inc.

The genesis of the parametric search application is described by the head of R&D at IMS.

When we had the CBR application out of INRECA it seemed like a good idea to go to the market and find an application for it. We did, initially, with Coillte [in 1996/ 1997] but that didn’t work out. [However,] during the search process I spoke to an engineer friend of mine on an informal basis [in 1996], who worked for Analog Devices. Following that discussion, we came up with an initial concept which was related to the analysis of product failure in the field: these [analyses] were on record and would lend themselves to CBDS.

Identifying and addressing the causes of product failure is a critical activity for design engineers at Analog Devices Inc. of Norwood, MA, in the US. IMS’ proposal was therefore of interest to product design, marketing, and application support engineers at Analog. IMS’ CEO traveled to Boston to meet with manager of Analog’s Central Applications function during 1996 in order to discuss the possibility of developing an application to identify the causes of product failure in the field. Subsequent to that meeting, IMS decided ‘that the structure [of the problem domain] was very complex and [it] couldn’t make any impact on it – it was too complex for the system to capturey[But] in a random lateral leap in Analog itself the concept of profile matching in the product catalogue lookup emerged as being a needyThis took us in another direction altogether.

Analog Devices’ application support engineers were, at that time, grappling with the not insignificant task of supporting thousands of products, the most numerous and widely used of which were integrated circuit-based operational amplifiers. Case-based decision support seemed to offer a promising solution for Analog Devices to the problem of rapid search and selection of specific products. From IMS’ viewpoint, its parametric search technology was not an idiosyncratic solution to a domain-specific problem; thus it did have the potential to lend itself to widespread use. However, the experiential and technical knowledge gained in the development of a case-based decision support system, and the commercial kudos that would accrue from its successful development, made it an appealing project for IMS. There was also the challenge of taking what was essentially a client/server technology, the CBR-based KATE-Tools platform, and using a subset of it as a standalone runtime application. IMS’ CEO commented on the project and its outcomes:

The Irish market for such a product did not exist, and the same could be said today. The technology was not considered as a solution to organizational problems. However, the likes of Gateway 2000 and Dell use an Inference product for help desk support. The ADI product was successful; however, the major emphasis is now on WebSell. It was only in the last month that serious work has gone into the development of WebSell applications. These are based on the same technology as used for the web-based version of the ADI product – CBR-Works.

It can be deduced from this statement that IMS’ overall goal was to ultimately develop the parametric search application for Internet use, and leverage this to widen the scope of application of its CBDS platform.

Following the success of the INRECA II initiative, participants formed a new consortium under the institutional umbrella of the European Economic Interest Group (EEIG). This forum allowed small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) to collaborate on bringing products to market, typically to other SMEs, under the aegis of this pan-European consortium. Participating companies shared a common goal in continuing to develop and enhance the commercial potential of CBDS, and each of the companies involved were shareholders in the consortium based on their estimated contribution in terms of development effort, marketing activities, and so on. The income from their joint activities was distributed by the EEIG to the shareholders.

IMS was particularly active in the WebSell initiative under EEIG. The WebSell application was based around CBR-Works, and hence the involvement of tecInno and the University of Kaiserslautern; however, the Web-based nature of the project saw the addition of three new partners – Adwired of Switzerland, EuroWeb, and the AI Research group from Trinity College Dublin. Together, these six institutions brought a highly innovative product to market that contributed significantly to IMS’ revenues. It can be deduced from this that political capabilities, on the basis of the experiential knowledge of senior managers in acquiring EU funding and establishing and managing inter-organizational relationships, contributed to the successful development of IS capabilities and the development of a distinctive competence at IMS.

Thus, the ability to develop and maintain formal and informal working relationships across Europe with fellow practitioners and academics in Germany and France, and with civil servants in Brussels and Strasbourg, was the source of IMS’ success in obtaining the financial and technical resources for its R&D activities. More important, however, was the learning-enabled knowledge transfers that occurred between the participants in the various initiatives. Coupled with the dynamic process of internal learning and knowledge integration, the external knowledge linkages helped IMS develop its most valuable, rare, and imperfectly imitable resource – the experiential (tacit) and technical (explicit) knowledge of its human asset, the IT professional.

## IMS’ asset positions

## Technological and complementary assets at IMS

IT professionals in this organization were continually exposed to new IT tools and techniques and therefore were engaged in an ongoing learning experience. This is evident from the above delineation of the manner in which the organization forged knowledge links with others across Europe. As indicated, these knowledge links also compromised IMS’ intangible assets. However, it will be shown that the successful development of the company’s portfolio of tangible assets was a direct consequence of the way in which such intangible assets were leveraged (cf. Nordhaug, 1994).

IMS grouped its products under three general headings: knowledge transfer, knowledge capture, and knowledge delivery. The CEO stated that the company’s development activities centered on these core applications, viz. IMS ‘provide[d] software development services for multimedia and decision supportyknowledge management tools and consulting for a variety of domainsy [was] an experienced supplier of case-based solutions for the Internet, Intranets and CD-ROM media.’ IMS’ tangible assets constantly evolved as it built and leveraged its intangible knowledge assets.

As an SMSE, IMS depended heavily on the complementarities of several of its knowledge domains and on their embodiment in key individuals within the organization. For example, the experiential knowledge of project management techniques and the technical knowledge of software development were complementary intangible knowledge assets. Also vital was the ability of IT professionals to apply both experiential and technical knowledge of scripting and story-boarding techniques to represent the knowledge/ problem domains of clients. The tangible resources that resulted from the application of IMS’ intangible knowledge assets were evident in the systems and services supplied to customers. ## Locational and financial assets

Dublin is the major location for Irish software firms; hence, IMS’ presence there in the 1990s, during the formative years of the Celtic Tiger, was an obvious locational asset. So too was the location of IMS’ development center, which was situated across the street from its parent company. The close proximity of both development centers strengthened the professional and social contexts and facilitated knowledge transfers between the two groups of IT professionals that constituted the ‘communities-of-practice’ in these companies. In addition, IMS’ physical location also reaped benefits for its role as the marketing arm of the European consortium that it helped establish to develop commercial applications using INRECA technologies. These locational assets afforded opportunities for developers from both operations to build close professional relationships, transfer their expertise, and help build development-related knowledge and skills. This was particularly important for IMS during the growth years of the early 1990s. Dublin also proved a useful location from which to market CBR technology in the UK and US. Chief among IMS’ firmspecific assets were its complementary assets, the major component of which was the intangible knowledge resource of its IT human asset.

Evidence of the significance of IMS’ intangible knowledge assets outlined above is found in the group balance sheet for the financial year ending 2000, which had an entry of d14,312,000 sterling against intangible assets, while tangible assets accounted for a mere d209,000 sterling. (This figure for intangible assets was based on estimates of the firm’s human capital, associated capabilities, product, service brand image, etc. The low figure for tangible assets reflects the industry practice of leasing office space and computer equipment.) It is clear from the financial statements that the company’s intangible assets consisted mainly of (1) the experiential and technical knowledge of systems developers, (2) the experiential and technical knowledge that was embedded in the company’s products, (3) the firm-specific organizational routines that underpin the IS development process in the organization, (4) the documentation and multimedia applications that describe how these systems were developed, (5) the relationships that were established with development partners across Europe and with customers on both sides of the Atlantic, and, finally, (6) the company’s reputation in the marketplace.

## Understanding IMS’ organizational and managerial processes

Integration, learning, reconfiguration, and transformation Previous studies of IS capabilities in organizations report on the importance of managerial and technical capabilities (Mata et al., 1995; Bharadwaj, 2000). The following statement by a senior IT professional at IMS indicates that such capabilities were a vital ingredient in building dynamic capabilities at IMS:

The key people that really know this, the ins and outs of [our case-based reasoning technologies] are Roy and Sean [ – the technical director and CEO]: Roy knows it at a technical level very, very well; Sean is more alert to its commercial potential in a lot of different areas, so there is a marriage of different skills here – Roy is good at technical things, Sean is good here too, but he’s got a better overall view.

IMS’ CEO imprinted his personality and management style on the ‘character’ of the organization. As CEO, he was the main spokesperson and marketing strategist for the company, its various activities, its relationships with development partners, and its products. To realize his vision, he built a team of highly committed, creative IT professionals, while he forged links with external institutions and software vendors – IMS’ partners and collaborators provided the fundamental software building blocks for its applications. In describing the outcomes of his endeavors to build a highly competent ‘community-ofpractice’ at IMS, the CEO put it thus:

The things we are good at and we have accomplished are part of our routine: Training, Pathways, and CBR – this is becoming one of those. The collaborative workspace product is emergingy. The focal point between these teams is Roy and myself; Roy is responsible for keeping the big picture, keeping track of papers and conferences, and so on, ensuring that what we are producing is what is required in the marketplace. The outside world does not see this vision; it’s part of our task to convey our product capabilities to them.

Thus the organization’s two senior managers acted to control and coordinate (i.e. integrate in Teece and Pisano’s (1998) schema), manage, and mentor individual and team activities across all development projects.

In many ways the company’s senior executives acted as ‘knowledge nodes,’ in that they interpreted the inner workings of new technologies like those that emanated from the INRECA initiative, with which they had become intimate; however, they also facilitated individual and group learning by fostering a culture of experimentation. This approach found concrete expression in the practice of delegating challenging tasks to young IT professionals at

IMS and instilling a commitment to build and apply knowledge and capabilities. This strategy ensured the growth and expansion of IS capabilities in the ‘community-of-practice’ at IMS and facilitated employee retention. Take, for example, comments from two IT professionals, one a senior analyst, the other a junior analyst/programmer:

I am paid a fixed salary, and paid wellyBut when you think of the experience we have as well, I don’t think I would have got it in any other company.

Because this is such a small company they ask a lot more of you, but you learn a whole lot of different skills.

It was clear from the CEO, and the software engineers interviewed, that employee retention was not a concern at the time of the study. Nevertheless, when the junior analyst/ programmer quoted above announced (in the months before the study) that he was thinking of a career change, the CEO, who was concerned about the loss of a talented programmer and the valuable knowledge he possessed, provided a convincing argument for him to stay.

The dynamic nature of the learning environment at IMS was manifested in the organizational routine of moving developers on to new areas of practice, to enhance their existing competence base, or to develop new capabilities. IT professionals were challenged not only by the development problems they had to solve, but also by the very tools that they used to develop solutions to those problems. Social cohesion, informal communication, and close personal and professional ties among members of the development ‘community’ at IMS were strengthened by this approach, as developers came to rely on the experiential knowledge of others to help them negotiate the learning curves involved in coming to grips with new software concepts and tools.

Learning and knowledge transfers between IMS’ European partners and its IT professionals occurred in two ways – person-to-person, and using IT to collaborate across time and space. One young IT professional described the role he played when working with software engineers at TecInno during the 6 months he was assigned to the task:

I was over in Germany learning how to build Java applets that would make everything much more jazzy [in terms of the application’s interface] and to determine how to establish communication between the Java applets and the case-based reasoning server. I also had to learn how to put to the case-based reasoning servers on the Web and how to get the Web clients to communicate with themy[Also, in order to] to build my own applications, I had to be able to understand the internal structure of their case-based reasoning engine, which had been written in Smalltalk. It wasn’t so much actually learning Smalltalk, but it was becoming familiar with the environment more than anything.

The payback from this form of interorganizational learning was substantial, as much of the Web-based development of the parametric search application was performed in collaboration with IMS’ German partners at TecInno. As a result of this collaboration, the IMS developer stated that ‘[i]t is much easier for us to develop applications quicklyywe are more self-reliant now, [and] we don’t need to keep referring to [TecInno] all the time. However, like all fruitful associations, his learning experience was a two-way process in that TecInno benefited from the enhancements this developer made to the CBR-Works user interface. He describes the process of collaboration thus:

I created the graphics [for the Parametric Search application] and inserted the text for them. But I also helped make changes to the user interface for CBR-Works; there were a lot of aspects that we found difficult to use, so I was in a position to offer some constructive feedback as to how the bumps could be smoothed out. They were also developing an application for Daimler– Benz, so I brought in ideas as to how to make the interface much easier to use, and work more quick flowing, like having menus and having the correct layout, and so on.

This statement is evidence of significant capabilities in interface design at IMS.

That period in the company’s history saw IT professionals learn and transfer knowledge within IMS and with the company’s development partners across Europe. The organizational routines or, to be more precise, informal patterns of institutionalized behavior that developed over time helped integrate the activities of IT professionals and facilitated further learning.

On the asset position dimension of this study’s theoretical model, technological, financial, and locational assets are, with some exceptions, non-firm specific – nevertheless, the CBR technology was very much firm-specific for IMS, with competitors who possessed such technologies being few and far between in a European context. However, the intangible complementary assets of experiential and technical knowledge, especially those which inform capability development, are perhaps the most valuable, rare, and inimitable resources an SMSE such as IMS has. These also include knowledge of how to build and maintain technological, financial, and locational assets – hence, employee loss or transfer can be a significant blow to capability/asset configurations in an organization. Accordingly, previous research by Nowak and Grantham (2000) argues that attracting and retaining the right talent are among the core capabilities of successful SMSEs. One of the key observations of this study in regard to employee retention is that if the work of software engineers in an SMSE is sufficiently ‘leading edge,’ innovative, and intellectually stimulating, then, ceteris paribus, employee retention problems may not be problematic for such firms. Given this, it is then logical to assume that such problems may emerge as a particular software product matures and creative programmers become restless for new challenges and move on, with the concomitant loss of critical knowledge assets and associated capabilities.

While such observations on employee knowledge are in keeping with recent findings in the literature on knowledge management (Sambamnurthy and Subramani, 2005), there is a paucity of studies on how such knowledge informs the development and application of capabilities (i.e. integration and reconfiguration and transformation capabilities) or how related capabilities (i.e. learning capabilities)

can shape and influence the development of intangible knowledge assets. This paper extends the extant understanding of such issues by illustrating the interplay of integration, learning, and reconfiguration and transformation capabilities in the organization studied, and how such capabilities were, in essence, knowledge in action. Tables 2 and 3 therefore help elaborate the theoretical model previously presented (see Figure 1) by describing, in an IS context, the integration, learning, and transformation/ configuration business and IS capabilities of an SMSE and how they relate to, operate on, and are informed by a firm’s IS-related knowledge and other assets. This paper’s cataloging and description of IS capabilities and assets is presented in the context of the products and services that IMS provided to its customers. This is important given the argument that it is the superior value proposition delivered by a company’s products and services that is of competitive importance, not business and IS capabilities and assets. Nevertheless, since the former is highly dependent on the latter, knowing which combinations of IS capabilities and assets deliver valuable products and services is important for researchers and practitioners alike.

One of the limitations of the present research concerns Newbert’s (2007) argument that empirical work on the dynamic capabilities is still in its ‘infancy’; consequently, the theory’s empirical fidelity may be drawn into question. This study is also limited because of its exploratory nature, and because it reports on the experience of one organization; as such, the capabilities and resources here identified may neither be generalizable nor applicable to other SMSEs (although the findings of extant research cited in Table 2 suggest otherwise). Nevertheless, the paper does, as a theoretically based exploratory case study, help build and refine extant theory for empirical testing using a positivist approach (cf. Wheeler, 2002): the paper therefore answers Bharadwaj’s (2000) call for a refined theoretical model of IS capabilities.

In conclusion, this study contributes to the articulation of an applied theory of dynamic capabilities for SMSEs by identifying and describing in context business and IS capabilities and assets; however, it is outside the scope of this paper to develop further, and fully articulate, this theory, as such an exercise is an extensive undertaking in itself (see Wheeler, 2002). Nevertheless, we believe that the paper makes a valuable contribution by (1) presenting a theoretical model of organizational capabilities and assets, (2) describing in an applied IS context the various component constructs – business and IS capabilities and assets, etc. (i.e. the independent variables in variance research) and their interactions, and, finally, (3) illustrating the product or outcomes of such processes (i.e. the dependent variables). Future research on the dynamic capabilities of SMSEs could therefore use the theoretical model proposed herein to test and elaborate on the patterns and regularities associated with the development and application of business and IS capabilities and assets in SMSEs. This should help build a cumulative body of evidence that helps evolve and articulate a robust empirically valid dynamic capabilities theory.
