# The rise and decline of the ETHICS methodology of systems implementation: lessons for IS research

Amany Elbanna $^{1}$ , Mike Newman $^{2}$

$^{1}$ School of Management, Royal Holloway, University of London, Egham, Surrey, UK; $^{2}$ Manchester Business School, University of Manchester, Manchester, UK

Correspondence:

A Elbanna, Senior Lecturer, School of Management, Royal Holloway University of London, Egham, Surrey TW20 0EX, UK. Tel: +44 (0) 1784 276697;

E-mail: amany.elbanna@rhul.ac.uk

## Abstract

Professor Mumford has contributed significantly to the information system (IS) field. Among her achievements and pioneering thinking is the development of an integrated methodology for systems implementation named Effective Technical and Human Implementation of Computer Systems (ETHICS) that incorporates job design as part of the systems planning and implementation effort. This study questions why ETHICS initially rose in popularity and then declined over the years. To answer this question, we apply Latour's (1999) five-loop framework to describe the formation of science. The findings reveal that Mumford held and aligned many heterogeneous actors and resources that together contributed to the shaping of ETHICS. As the content of ETHICS was shaped by the intertwining of many elements, when some of these elements later changed and undermined their previous alignment, the content of ETHICS was not reshaped, and hence it lost its status and declined. The paper ends by drawing more general lessons for IS research. Journal of Information Technology (2013) 28, 124–136. doi:10.1057/jit.2013.7;

published online 26 March 2013

Keywords: Information systems implementation; ETHICS; participation; Actor Network Theory; Social Process Model; formation of science

## Introduction

M umford is one of the most highly respected figures in the information system (IS) field. Her work contributed considerably to the development of the academic discipline of ISs (Stahl, 2007). She was born in Britain in 1924 and died in 2006, and was the first woman to hold a full professorship at a UK Business School (Rickards, 2006). Her name is associated with the socio-technical movement and the work of the Tavistock Institute that originated in Britain in the 1950s and 1960s. The socio-technical movement was characterised by putting the improvements of the quality of working life at the forefront of organisational design.She is one of the pioneers who paid attention to the socio-technical aspects of ISs. In 1968, she co-authored a 'liveware' oriented book, Computers: Planning for people, in which she presented a new socio-technical view of computer systems in contrast to the software and hardware orientations that dominated the academic arena at that time (Mumford and Ward, 1968: 88). In this book, Mumford and Ward attempted to set out an integrated approach to planning for computers and made the point that ‘computer technologists are not merely designers and implementers of electronic data processing (EDP) systems, they are also “agents” for change’ (ibid, p. 10). In 1979, Mumford integrated her interest in computers and their impact on organisations, individuals, and job design (Mumford and Banks, 1967; Mumford and Ward, 1968; Mumford, 1969) with her views on the quality of working life (Cooper and Mumford, 1979a). With Weir, she introduced a method of work design and change related to the introduction of computer systems that they labelled Effective Technical and Human Implementation of Computer Systems (ETHICS) (Mumford and Weir, 1979). Working with John Hawgood and Frank Land (Hawgood et al., 1978; Mumford et al., 1978; Mumford et al., 1979), she added a number of tools to enhance the ETHICS method.

This paper examines the rise and decline of ETHICS and provides a brief interpretation of the history of this method of systems implementation. The paper adopts an analytical perspective from Science and Technology Studies (STS). STS is an interdisciplinary body of work that seeks to understand the practices of science and social science in terms of the social context in which they are situated, rather than a consequence of the operation of universal beliefs such as rationality, truth, and progress (Barnes, 2001). To examine further why a method may survive or not, we will also briefly review the Social Process Model (SPM) using the same analytical perspective that was applied to examine ETHICS. Although a full comparison between the two methods is beyond the scope of this paper, contrasting it with an enduring method such as SPM gives further insights on why ETHICS has declined.

The paper focuses on the ETHICS method and not the amount or value of Professor Mumford contribution to the IS field. In addition to developing ETHICS, Professor Mumford has contributed invaluable to the advancement of many other areas of research including action research, risk and change management, and socio-technical design. The value of her contribution is unquestionable as the value of pioneers is not in the popularity of their contribution but in the quality of it and their dedication and determination to pursue new and different ideas.

The paper is divided into eight sections. After the introduction, the second section 'STS and the circulatory system of scientific facts: the five-loops' framework' presents and describes the STS framework adapted from Latour's (1999), which consists of five loops incorporating people, institutions, ideas, and objects that have to be taken into account simultaneously for 'any realistic rendering of science' (Latour, 1999: 100). The third section 'Research methodology' presents the research methodology and data collection methods. The fourth section 'The ETHICS methodology: a brief history' provides a brief historical account of the emergence of ETHICS. The fifth section 'The circulation of ETHICS: the five loops' applies Latour's framework to understand the relation between social elements and the rise of ETHICS. Latour's framework is also applied in the sixth section 'The decline of ETHICS' to understand the later decline of ETHICS and in the seventh section 'Further insights on change and durability' to shed further light on change and durability. The final section 'Conclusion' concludes the paper and draws lessons for future IS research.

## STS and the circulatory system of scientific facts: the five-loops' framework

STS asserts that scientific and technological facts are shaped by the contingent local circumstances in which scientists carry out their practices. These contingent factors are claimed to shape the very truth claims of the scientists themselves (Barnes, 2004). STS consists of different approaches and ontological strands to examine the construction of scientific knowledge and technological innovation. Regardless of the diversity of approaches and disagreements in some cases, STS as a branch of sociology seeks to understand both how contingent local circumstances make truth as it is, and why the latter perseveres at certain times and places and not others. This focus provides a strong theoretical entrance to examine the following research questions: why did ETHICS flourish in the 1970s and 1980s to hold the ‘truth’ about effective socio-technical implementation of computer systems and became favoured at that time and why did it decline later?

One of the prominent STS strands that establishes a close link between social context and scientific practice is Latour's framework describing the circulation of scientific and technological 'facts'. Latour challenges the traditional view of the existence of a hard scientific core that is independent of its context. He refutes the notion of the existence of a scientific core surrounded by context or conditions of its existence. The traditional view of science – as presented in Figure 1 – portrays science as a core surrounded by social context where the social environment can encourage or hinder the development of science, but that ‘... never forms or constitutes the very content of the sciences’ (Latour, 1999: 91). Instead, he argues that the scientific content is not separate from its context of production. He explains that ‘the notion of science isolated from the rest of society [is] as meaningless as the idea of a system of arteries disconnected from the system of veins ... [it is] the rich vascularisation that makes the scientific disciplines alive’ (Latour, 1999: 80). He maintains that this claim of having separate realms for the content of science and its context dissolves once we begin to examine the rich vascularisation that makes the scientific disciplines live and flourish. Latour uses the term ‘science’ to include both social and natural science such as psychology, philosophy, history, biology, and astronomy (Latour, 1999: 1–3 and 111–112).

In his book, Pandora's Hope (1999), Latour – conceptually and through examples and case studies from social and natural science – shows that the connection between science and society ‘depends on what the actors have done or not done to establish it’ (Latour, 1999: 86). As shown in Figure 2, the vascular system of science consists of five interrelated loops: Mobilisation of the World or instruments, Autonomisation (colleagues), Alliances (allies), Public Representation, and finally the Links and Knots or the conceptual elements of the science that tie all the previous four loops together. Each of these five loops is as important as the others, and each feeds back into itself and into the others. The conceptual element or content of science is situated in the middle ‘more like a central knot tying the four other loops than like a stone surrounded by a context’ (ibid, p.92). These four loops give the conceptual element or content of science meaning, and hence the weakness of any loop would affect the rest of the circulatory system.

The first loop, Mobilisation of the World, is a general expression that includes all the means by which non-humans are progressively imbricated into a discourse. This could include the instruments and equipment of a laboratory, the science practices of expeditions, surveys and questionnaires, or any other means by which ideas are progressively loaded into a discourse depending on the nature of the scientific discipline. It could also include the sites in which all the objects of the world thus mobilised are assembled and contained, such as data repositories and sources. Mobilisation involves engaging all possible resources and keeping them engaged and available to support the articulation of propositions and arguments that scientists have. The Mobilisation of the World concludes with transforming the world into immutable and combinable mobiles through which an entity is transformed and becomes materialised into a document, a sign, an archive, or other forms that are transportable and intact, while allowing new translations and articulations (Latour 1999: 102. 306–307). Through this mobilisation, a scientist can construct an argument and speak ‘more authoritatively and with more assurance’ (Latour 1999: 101).

The second loop is Autonomisation. It deals with how the researcher finds colleagues who can criticise and use the results of the research. The credibility of the previous Mobilisation of the World relies on finding and convincing peers to get engaged with the findings and to form a discipline or profession with its own criteria of evaluation and relevance. The increase in the credibility of science presuppose the existence of colleagues who are capable of criticising and using the products of Mobilisation of the World whether it is a survey, expedition, experiments, or any other scientific procedures and practices that end up in articulating propositions. Autonomisation also includes the existence of scientific institutions, organisations, resources, and regulations to keep the newly formed group of academic colleagues together. For a scientist, the circulation does not stop at this second loop but has to continue to the other loops.

The third loop is Alliances. It includes other people and groups beyond academia who could take the work of the scientist, multiply it and scale it up. According to Latour (1999), this labour of making people interested is crucial for the circulation of science. He explains: ‘without colleagues and without the world, the researcher won’t cost much but won’t be worth much either’ (1999: 104). Making people interested and making allies ‘do not pervert the pure flow of scientific information but are what makes this blood flow much faster and with a much higher pulse rate’ (ibid, p.104). Latour notes that, ‘the skills required for getting others interested are again different from those necessary for setting up instruments and for producing colleagues’ (ibid, p. 104).

The fourth loop is Public Representation. It deals with the scientists' efforts to widen the horizon of their alliances beyond colleagues and support groups. It presents the impact on people's every day practice and their systems of beliefs and opinions. It also 'brings people with other qualities and competences into the fray' (ibid, p.105). This loop is part of the fabric of science. It 'makes up a lot of the presuppositions of scientists themselves about their object of study' (ibid, p.106).

Finally, the Links and Knots present the scientific conceptual element, content, or theory. A concept becomes scientific when it is more intensely connected to and holds a large repertoire of heterogeneous resources together. Latour recognises the differences between the natural and social sciences or what he calls hard and soft disciplines, and finds that 'hard disciplines need bigger and harder concepts than soft ones, not because they are more remote from the rest of the world of data, colleagues, allies, and spectators – the four other loops – but because the world that they churn, steer, move and connect is vastly bigger' (ibid, p.108).

Latour (1999) has discussed the conceptual premises of the five-loop framework using the term ‘science’ to include social and natural sciences and gave examples from social and natural sciences. The framework has been applied in several studies in different social science fields such as Human Geography (Barnes, 2001; Barnes, 2003; Johnston, 2006), Librarianship (Jablonski, 2001), Science Education (Van Eijck, 2010), and Public Health (Young, Borland and Coghill, 2010).

An alternative framework to understand disciplinary change could be Kuhn's model of scientific revolutions (Kuhn, 1962). Kuhn focuses on understanding the transition in science from one paradigm to another. His work is influential in bringing forward the potential fruitfulness of historical and sociological research in understanding scientific revolutions and paradigms shifts. Kuhn's model was developed for the physical sciences and although his model is widely cited by social scientists, 'he has not returned the compliment with attention. He sticks to natural science for examples' (D.H. [as requested to cite by the Journal], 1978). Social scientists who tried to apply it to understand disciplinary change in a social science discipline 'reject ... [it] ... as valid for appreciating changes' and also found it lacking 'any attention to the politics' of disciplinary change (Johnston, 2006). When compared with Kuhn's model of scientific revolutions (Kuhn, 1962), Latour's five-loops framework was found to provide ‘a valuable framework within which a political element can be firmly inserted’, whereas Kuhn’s model lacks a political dimension (Johnston, 2006). For the purpose of this study, the five-loop framework was chosen as an analytical lens as it could explain both the construction and change of science and hence could provide an answer to the research question regarding both the rise and fall of ETHICS. It also holds a balanced view of the content and context of science that is valuable when examining change in a social science discipline. Latour’s framework also considers a wide range of contextual elements and advocates the existence of an intertwining relationship between science and its context that could prove insightful in explaining disciplinary change in a social science field.

It should be noted that Latour's early versions of Actor Network Theory is well known and applied in the ISs field (Walsham, 1997; Elbanna, 2011) despite the criticism they received from other sociologists (see, e.g., Collins and Yearley, 1992; Bloor, 1999). The notion of translation and network are important to Actor Network Theory, 'but they are only the beginning points of what now has become a complex, variegated, and self-critical body of literature' (Barnes, 2001). Latour's later work that included extensive presentation and significant elaboration and extension of the theory have been largely overlooked in ISs research (Elbanna, 2010, 2012).

## Research methodology

This research belongs to the qualitative research tradition in ISs (Kaplan and Maxwell, 1994; Myers, 2009). It applies a case study approach in order to explore the development, progress, and decline of ETHICS. Data collection was based on literature and document reviews supported by a limited number of formal interviews. It does not amount to an empirical investigation as the number of interviews was limited and served mainly as pointers and triggers for documentary and literature reviews. The six key formal interviews provided a starting point for the research. They gave pointers and threads that were later followed, verified, complemented, and examined by documentary reviews. They also offered interpretations of some of the historic events and relationships. Both a literature and documentary review played a significant part in this research. Books, conference proceedings, research papers, archived advertisements, and other publications were visited. Most of Mumford's books and research papers on ETHICS were read thoroughly and analysed to follow the development of the ideas, the connections of different research strands, and the evolution of her ideas over time. Other books and papers were also consulted to gather more data on different opinions regarding ETHICS. These books and articles were chosen according to their subject and were directly related to the method studied. Some articles that report interviews with Mumford (e.g., Porra and Hirschheim, 2007), a summary of her life and work, or a tribute to her (Avison et al., 2006) were also analysed according to the framework adopted.

The six formal interviews consisted of five formal unstructured interviews with senior scholars who were involved with ETHICS and worked with Mumford. These were conducted in July–September 2006 followed by one interview in November 2006 with a mid-career scholar who studied Mumford's work in depth as part of her interest in systems development methodology and action research. $^{1}$ However, these interviews were very informative because they alerted the researchers to the possible decline of ETHICS as a methodology of systems implementation. This suspicion led to the use of further informal interviews and discussions with junior researchers. These researchers were either in their final year of their Ph.D. studies or held their Ph.D. for 1–2 years. These informal discussions took place in academic conferences including AMCIS 2008 and 2009 and ICIS 2009 and 2011. Two more interviews were conducted with senior IT managers in international IT companies. Details of the interviews are presented in Table 1. Notes were taken during and directly after each interview and any mentioned documents or literature were reviewed.

The formal interviews portrayed ETHICS as a distant history. Interviewees struggled to remember ETHICS details and contents and preferred to speak about Mumford as a strong character who managed to convince others to try the method, her industry and academic engagement, and trade union support, in addition to stories about her efforts and contribution. The informal discussion with the junior researchers showed that the majority were not introduced to Mumford's work in their studies and hence were not familiar with ETHICS. Many referred to the origin of the socio-technical thinking in ISs to be the work of contemporary IS scholars. Twelve of the informal interviewees were aware of Mumford's work; however, only four were familiar with ETHICS. The information of these four interviews informed the data analysis. All the interviews gave support to the claim of ETHICS decline that was somewhat of a surprise to the researchers. These encounters in addition to Mumford's own confession (Mumford, 2000, 2006) and other researchers' academically published and reviewed studies (Howcroft and Wilson, 2003b; Stahl, 2007) confirmed the previous suspicion that the use of the method of ETHICS has declined.

The research team consisted of two researchers. Following Eisenhardt's (1989) suggestion, the tactics for the involvement of its members was for the first author to conduct the research and the second to act as the ‘resident devil’s advocate’ (Eisenhardt, 1989). Therefore, the first author conducted the data collection, analysis, and writing of drafts while the second author, being detached from the previous stages, read final drafts to verify the account and in addition, contributed to section 7.

## The ETHICS methodology: a brief history

Enid Mumford was a social science graduate of Liverpool University. She started her career working in the industry as a personnel manager and later as a production manager. She then joined the Faculty of Social Science at Liverpool University as a Lecturer and later a Senior Lecturer in Industrial Sociology. She carried out research on industrial relations at both the Liverpool Docks and the North West coal industry. Concerning the latter, she was involved with the Tavistock Institute in a 3-year project to study industrial relations in coal mining, a traditional and highly unionised industry (Scott, 1963). This experience contributed to the shaping of her views regarding the connection between industrial relations and work design. She concluded that unions' complaints and disputes, workers' discontent, high absence rate and high turnover rate could be all reduced through better design of the work processes and conditions. Following this project, Liverpool University was responsible for the British contribution of an international study funded and organised by the European Productivity Association on the consequences of computers. The research was on the impact of EDP on organisations and the people who work for them. Mumford was interested in understanding how firms are shaped by the new techniques of EDP, and whether the kind of change that results from EDP differs radically from the consequences of other sorts of technical innovation (Mumford, 1964, 1965, 1996b; Mumford and Ward, 1966). This project presents her first research into the impact of computers on people (Mumford and Ward, 1966). The final report of this study was published in a book entitled 'The Computer and the Clerk' with Banks (Mumford and Banks, 1967).

Mumford then joined Manchester Business School as a Senior Lecturer in Industrial Sociology. She became interested in developing an integrated approach to planning for computers. The first attempt to conceptualise this line of thinking was in a book with Ward (Mumford and Ward, 1968). In this book, she made what was then a revolutionary point that ‘computer technologists are not merely designers and implementers of EDP systems, they are also “agents” for change’.

Mumford continued with her interest in studying job satisfaction and designed a method to analyse the level of worker satisfaction (Mumford, 1972a). She then tried to establish the redesign of work as a mean to achieve job satisfaction (Mumford, 1976a,b). She adopted the Tavi-stock Institute theorisation of the socio-technical systems drawn from the coal mining industry, with its blue collar workers and use of new technical tools for the automation of production lines to study another different setting: offices, white collar workers, and computers.

An important feature of her work, reflected in her writings, was her practical approach to testing out her ideas in a series of action research studies. She persuaded managers in industry to let her try out her approach on live projects and disseminated the outcomes in several books. She developed ETHICS as a method to deliver effective and efficient change that followed the management's agenda and at the same time contributed to the workers' job satisfaction. One of many examples of the importance she attached to making her work known is the book she wrote with Henshall, the manager in charge of the project, reporting the successful application of ETHICS on the introduction of a computer system in Rolls-Royce Limited (Mumford and Henshall, 1979/1983).

In 1983, Mumford re-launched ETHICS in a 'big bang' through the publication of two books and two video films that support the method and present successful stories in addition to two sets of working sheets that assist readers to implement and assess the method (Mumford and Henshall, 1983; Mumford, 1983b). This is in addition to her re-publishing the 1979 book with Henshall (Mumford and Henshall, 1979/1983). From then onwards, Mumford 'stood' for ETHICS trying to convince her academic colleagues and different industries in Britain, Europe, and the United States of the viability and effectiveness of the method (Mumford and MacDonald, 1989; Mumford, 1989, 1994, 1995, 1996a,b, 1997, 2003). Many of her projects in different countries had successful outcomes, but in others the outcomes were more equivocal (Mumford, 1997).

## The circulation of ETHICS: the five loops

This section argues that the success of ETHICS was not only because of its scientific contents, but also because of the rich vasculatory system it created and became part of. This vasculatory system consisted of the active program of reporting and participating in different international forums and conferences that Mumford pursued, the engagement of colleagues and convincing them of the value of the method, and the involvement of the industry and convincing the business that the method could improve the industrial tension that results from the introduction of new technology while simultaneously achieving the managerial goal of business efficiency. This vasculatory system also involved the wider society in debate about human rights and the humanisation of workplaces.

## Mobilisation of the World: the instruments

The first loop of Latour's framework is Mobilisation of the World by which he means the ways and location of information gathering scientists employ to master the phenomenon of concern. It also allows them to speak more authoritatively and with more assurance to advance the argument to their colleagues.

Before developing ETHICS, Mumford was interested in job design through her involvement in research with the Tavistock Institute. However, as an industrial relations researcher, she was also interested in studying job satisfaction. She developed tools to measure employees' job satisfaction (Mumford, 1972a). Around 1965, she became involved in a study of computer impact on work (Mumford, 1971). She then realised that computers (at that time, mainframe computers) brought in change to work structures, as well as introducing some tedious tasks such as data entry that contradicted the growing movement towards job enrichment and job enlargement that was taking place at that time. This change resulted in increase of job dissatisfaction and absenteeism (Mumford, 1972b). She argued that the job satisfaction problems associated with computer systems could be improved by seeking better fit between the technical requirements and the social requirements of each job. She then suggested a different design method of jobs that takes the two strands of requirements into consideration. In doing so, she mobilised the socio-technical conceptualisation of job design that the Tavistock Institute established, and combined it with her research interests in job satisfaction and improving work conditions. The first two themes of ETHICS were then developed. The first deals with effective and efficient job design and the second with job satisfaction. She applied these themes in action research endeavours and treated each case study as an experiment and personal challenge and hence tried her best to achieve successful results that satisfied both management and workers.

## Autonomisation: colleagues

Once she had ‘mobilised the world’ and found evidence to support the possibility of including job satisfaction and job re-design ideas in the introduction of new computer systems, Mumford carried out a number of action research studies to support this pioneering conceptualisation. The achievements of these experiments were widely reported in books, videos, and conference publications to show the success of her method. She video-recorded interviews with project members of her cases and exchanged them with colleagues, students, and other business managers trying to convince them of the validity of ETHICS and the achievements that could be reached from its adoption (Interviews 1, 2). She also published the different questionnaires and working sheets that she used in the application of ETHICS to encourage others to use the method. In doing so, she created what Latour (1990) calls immutable mobiles, which are objects that travel around carrying the message of the success of ETHICS as a participative method for systems implementation.

She actively participated in building a community of researchers who believe in the ideals of ETHICS. She published with several authors and edited books to include the work of others (e.g., Mumford and Pettigrew, 1975; Legge and Mumford, 1978; Mumford, 1985b). She was a founding member of IFIP TC9 conference on Human Choice and Computers in 1974 (Mumford and Sackman, 1975), IFIP Working Group 8.2 on Organisational and Social Implication of ISs in 1978, and the Socio-technical Special Interest Group in the British Computer Society's in the 1980s. She also became a council member of the Tavistock Institute and a member of the US Socio-technical Round Table. In doing so she created the discipline and research community essential to her work.

## Alliances: allies

Mumford was actively engaged in different streams of business and trade union activities and conferences through which she was reaching out to involve different types of allies (Avison et al., 2006). She also published outside the realm of academia (e.g. Mumford, 1985a). She introduced ETHICS as a solution to trade unions and employees who were struggling to gain industrial rights in contrast to the employers' focus on efficiency and profit. At the same time, she translated management interest in efficiency and profits by promising that ETHICS would be a method for achieving successful results of change programmes that could leverage both the human and technology capability and create a positive environment for change. She problematised ETHICS as a solution for management to solve their industrial problems, increase productivity, and reduce conflicts, and at the same time provide a vehicle for workers to be seriously involved in organising their own work to their satisfaction. ETHICS promised management better success and gains, and workers better involvement and democracy in the workplace. In doing so, she gained both management and trade unions support, a remarkable achievement (Mumford, 2003).

Mumford observed the critical problems in industrial relations in Britain and was supporting the different movements in backing employees' rights for a better working conditions and participation in decision making in Europe (e.g., Cooper and Mumford, 1979a). Among these movements was the industrial democracy movement in Scandinavia that called for the involvement of employees at all levels of decision making of their organisation, regardless of their position in the organisation hierarchy (Cooper and Mumford, 1979b). She joined the movement towards employees' participation and suggested that the technical-social fit could be achieved through employees' participation. Through the existence and influence of these movements and her involvement in them, the third pillar of ETHICS was advanced (Mumford, 2006).

Her wide involvement in academic and business activities gained her and the method a wider reputation that in return ensured the involvement of more allies and the enrichment of the method with more case studies. An example of the creation of a business alliance is the story of Digital Corporation's involvement. In March 1981, Gerhard Fiedrich, an American Senior Manager at Digital heard her name in a conference organised by the British Psychological Society. 'He phoned her and they met before he returned to the United States'. During this meeting, Mumford gave Gerhard many video tapes about ETHICS and its success. This encounter led Gerhard to return to Britain in June 1981 to gather more information about the method and arrange for Mumford to go to Digital in September 1981 and work with the team in the XSEL project (Mumford and MacDonald, 1989).

## Public representation

This loop represents the scientists' efforts to widen the reach of alliances beyond colleagues and support groups. Mumford tried to engage in societal debate regarding the humanisation of work, efficient and effective management, and workers rights. She joined the Quality of Working Life Council that was at that time an international movement drawn from groups of many different European countries, as well as India, North America, and Australia. Its mission was to spread the quality of work life message throughout the world through meetings, training sessions, books, and articles. This group ‘was very influential’, had ‘a common purpose and a strong network of relationships’, and its ‘members acted as information conduits in their respective countries and through attendance at international conferences’ (Mumford, 2006: 331). In sum, Mumford became part of a new social movement that was spreading internationally in many countries in Europe, the United States of America, and India (Mumford, 1993, 2006), which gained ETHICS even greater recognition and reputation.

## The Links and Knots

This fifth loop, according to Latour, is where it all comes together or falls apart. This loop, as Latour explains, is not the hard core of the science, but rather it is the formed heart. It is through the connection and close relationships with the other four loops that the conceptual content of the method becomes stronger and sturdier (Barnes, 2004). This was the case with ETHICS. It became a method of participative systems implementation that was supported by successful action research cases, endorsed by the academic community, supported by trade unions and employees, and approved by businesses.

Mumford was enthusiastically involved in action research studies liaising, inspiring, encouraging, solving problems and conflicts, and educating members of the design group of how to go about their job design task. For example, in Designing Secretaries, she took minutes of group meetings and distributed them between members. She also suggested solutions and ideas that a secretary 'directly' adopted and followed (Mumford, 1983b). In Designing Participatively, she assisted the design group to cope with a mass of diagnostic information to assemble this into a logical form (Mumford and Henshall, 1979/1983). She also helped group members to take decisions by suggesting alternative ways of approaching problems while still leaving all decision-making to them. Initially she was responding to the group pressure to provide answers rather than ideas (ibid, pp. 54–55). She was involved in the meetings and the details of the project, and only by the end of the project had the staff gained the knowledge to take matters into their own hands. She expressed her happiness that by the end of each of her projects, the design group involved could take design decisions without her support and considered this as a sign of the transfer of her knowledge over the period of the project.

She also carried out the task of distributing and collecting the job satisfaction questionnaires and transferring them to Manchester University for analysis. She analysed the questionnaire on job satisfaction based on her theory of job satisfaction and then fed the results back to all staff of the design group (Mumford and Henshall, 1983: 33). She assured everyone who completed a questionnaire of the anonymity of the exercise and that no one, other than her, would see the questionnaires. In doing so, she gained the employees' trust based on her own personality and qualities as an external consultant, and not based on the institution or the project the employees were involved in.

ETHICS was evolving with each case study in order to suit an organisation's needs and limitations and to gain management trust. For example, at Digital, Mumford moved from the consensus participation employed at Rolls

Royce, and consultative participation as in British Manufacturing, to a representative participation to suit the complexity and size of the project.

## The decline of ETHICS

Mumford states in her publications (1993, 1999, 2000, 2006) that the decline of ETHICS began after the 1990s when the industry moved to focus on efficiency and effectiveness, and workers' movements were institutionalised and their influence faded (Mumford, 1993). This section argues that the loss of allies (trade unions and business partners) (Mumford, 2006) and the changes in the software industry and market competition, in addition to the business' redefinition of efficiency, have affected the continuing adoption of ETHICS negatively. However, it was the loss of mobilisation of the content of ETHICS to meet the changes to the elements that once shaped it and the passivity of doubting colleagues that significantly contributed to the decline of ETHICS.

## Mobilisation

The Mobilisation of the World in each experiment was dependent on Mumford's hard work and her personal enthusiasm to achieve results despite the inherent conflicts found in organisational life. Mumford's experiments were seen as taking place in 'green house' settings where projects were shielded from the harsher realities of organisational life. She was constantly involved to resolve the inherent contradictions between users and management, ensuring that management opinion should be considered in the final design (Land, 2000). Her role became viewed as being a double-faced 'Janus' $^{2}$ as she combined, using her tactfulness, the two opposite positions of employees and trade unions on one side and management on the other (Howcroft and Wilson, 2003a). She was also resolving the tension and frustration between the design team and technical team. The charismatic nature of Mumford's personality played a significant role in encouraging the design team in Rolls Royce – for example – to meet the challenge that was lying in front of them. For example, when the morale of the design team collapsed during the project, she changed the team's daily 1-h meeting to be one morning meeting every week, on a day that she could attend herself to resolve the tension between the technical and business members of the team and infuse enthusiasm among the members.

Significantly, ETHICS studies (with the exception of Digital) were based on the development of single-function in-house systems. The developments in hardware and the move away from mainframe computing towards distributed computing changed the nature of the problem in terms of scale and definition. Distributed computing and databases invited more radical view of the design of work away from the small-scale design that Mumford was then involved in. The first experiment of the method in a complex system development context was in the XSEL project at Digital. The XSEL project objective was to develop an integrated system for a distributed development team. Both the notion of integration and distributed teams were rather new at that time. Organisations and employees had little or no experience in the development and implementation of such complex systems. Mumford was also challenged by the large scale and wide scope of this project. The ideal notion of ETHICS regarding consensus participation was not possible considering the large scale of the system and the hundreds of employees involved. She adapted the technique of participation to use representative participation to suit the distributed nature of the system development and not the consensus or consultative approach as before. She also agreed for team members to be selected by management and not by the trade unions or employees' nominations as before. The results of the experiment in the XSEL project at Digital were later doubted by colleagues and business managers, as explained in the following sections.

In the 1990s, the software industry was booming and off-the shelf software was gradually penetrating the market especially with the development of very large packaged software such as Enterprise Resource Planning systems (ERP). Business Process Re-engineering (BPR) was also in its ascendency with its promise of delivering significant cost reduction and speed of operations through integrating and streamlining business processes. The notions of integration, business processes standardisation across locations, streamlining of processes, and cost reduction that emerged as part of the BPR found resonance in businesses that were suffering from global competition and had a need to reduce cost and increase speed and effectiveness of operations. This emerging business wisdom was sought to be achieved through developing large integrated systems or implementing large software packages such as ERP.

Mumford observed the rise of BPR in the 1990s and felt the threat of the efficiency rhetoric that BPR brought. She tried to position ETHICS as a tool for efficiency and progress (Mumford and Beekman, 1994) and she warned of the negative impact of BPR (Mumford and Hendricks, 1996) and its risky ideas (Mumford, 1996a). She also published two books to compare ETHICS conceptually with BPR and position ETHICS as an effective way of leveraging technology and introducing business change (Mumford, 1995), and also as an ethical way of introducing an effective change (Mumford, 1996b). In the early 2000s, $^{3}$ she posted her 1983 book online on her personal home page (Mumford, 1983a).

Although ETHICS claimed to achieve efficiency and effectiveness, it did not engage with the emerging business re-definition of them. The emerging rise of global operations, business process integration and development or implementation of standard software across multiple locations did not trigger any serious revision of the content of ETHICS, even though the XSEL project at Digital had signalled the emergence of these changes. No new experiment of ETHICS was attempted after the dubious success of ETHICS at the XSEL project.

## Colleagues

Other researchers examined the case of the XSEL project at Digital after Mumford left the company. Digital was the second largest computer manufacturer in the United States and their effort to manage the 'literarily millions of design possibilities for working configuration' that could be offered to its customers caught the attention of other researchers. Professor John McDermott of Carnegie-

Mellon University, Professor Leonard-Barton from Harvard University, and the Ph.D. student at that time, Keil from Harvard University were among the researchers who studied this project. The latter two researchers examined the impact of the XSEL project on the organisation. They reported the failure of XSEL to address organisational needs and criticised the early days of the project including the set up of the users' design group and the requirement gathering that Mumford was involved in (but without a mention of her name) and their negative impact on the system's efficiency (Leonard-Barton, 1987; Keil, 1991). Keil's work showed that participation does not always lead to successful systems and that the XSEL project that Mumford saw as a success turned into a failure as soon as she pulled out from the field work (Keil, 1991). These studies opened the door for other researchers to doubt that the systems developed according to ETHICS have ever succeeded operationally beyond implementation. As a senior scholar put it: 'I am not sure that it [ETHICS] has ever been a success' (Interview 4).

It is surprising that no profound academic debate was initiated at that time to closely scrutinise and draw lessons from the case of the XSEL project at Digital. Such debate should have been encouraged not only to revise the content of the method, but also to detect the changes in the industry and the nature of IS projects that impacted on the relevance of the content of ETHICS. The XSEL project at Digital signalled the advent of changes in the industry where global operations, business process integration, standardisation of business processes and operations, and development or implementation of standard software across locations were emerging as new definitions of efficiency and effectiveness that ETHICS once stood for. However, by avoiding open debate with Mumford and inviting her and other scholars to revise and reshape the content of ETHICS, the IS academic community missed an opportunity to detect early signs of the change and adapt its methods to new contexts. Only later, in 2003, did Mumford admit her surprise at the ‘eventual rejection of the XSEL expert system by the Digital sales force’ and that ‘the large financial savings that had been forecasted were never achieved’ (Mumford, 2003: 172).

The relationship between participation and project success was doubted and research has reached inconclusive results regarding this relationship (Howcroft and Wilson, 2003b). Studies doubted the notion of participation itself (Cavaye, 1995) stating that participation might not be always desirable or could ‘always produce beneficial results in practice’ (Lucey, 1995: 116). The claim that participation could lead to effective and efficient project was also questioned as researchers examined this relationship and found modest (Franz and Robey, 1986) or only tentative support for this claim (Baroudi et al., 1986).

A counter claim appeared in the scientific community that the functionalist efficiency and effectiveness promises of ETHICS are rather problematic to achieve. Mumford turned to position ETHICS as a philosophy and ethical stance in the introduction of technology, which is evidenced in her 1990s publications and onwards (Mumford, 1993, 1994, 2003, 2006). Indeed, other researchers found ETHICS to be a suitable methodology to advance emancipation ideals (Hirschheim and Klein, 1994) and provide a humanistic approach to technological changes (Avison et al., 2006; Porra and Hirschheim, 2007).

In Business Schools, the method has been increasingly removed from the IS curriculum. However, it is still part of some of the industrial relations courses, particularly in Europe (Interviews 1, 2, and 3).

## Alliances

The relationship between the method and the success of systems development and implementation projects was inconclusive. Management found that ETHICS-based projects took a long time and did not guarantee financial success (Howcroft and Wilson, 2003b). However, as a senior scholar commented: No method used in a social situation can guarantee success [yet] this is something business managers do not want to hear (Interview 4). Furthermore, the increased market competition and globalisation changed the management priorities from an internal focus to be more externally focused. Hence, employees' satisfaction dropped down at the bottom of business managers' priorities when introducing a new system (Interview 3).

The method did not provide a stand-alone recipe for success. As reported, its success was dependent largely on systems analysts' tactfulness and willingness to listen rather than providing a mechanism for the involvement of systems analysts that could be replicated in other situations. For example, it was reported in Mumford's book with Henshall that '… fortunately the two systems analysts recognised the problem and were both tactful and willing to listen' (Mumford and Henshall, 1983: 36).

Employees' decisions in designing their own job are not always financially or organisationally viable, and hence the tension between employees and management over design would recur rather than get resolved. The employees would typically suspect the hand of management in the application of the method. This led to putting projects on hold for some time until the situation became clear. For example, in the Rolls Royce case, the project was brought to a standstill because of the trade unions' suspicions over management intention (Mumford and Henshall, 1979/1983). The management, on the other hand, tried to push their agenda forward in order to ensure a viable organisational solution. Employees' decisions in designing their own jobs were also problematic in the development of large complex systems such as the XSEL project at Digital. It was Mumford's personal qualities and tactfulness that resolved these tensions and other critical situations and pushed projects forward.

The technique of participation was also problematic. In the Rolls Royce case, she opted for consensus participation. However, members of the design group were actually selected by the management rather than elected by their department as she hoped for, but she had to conform to management's preference in selecting the members of the design group. When problems were raised in the members' original department as a result of their colleagues seeing them as the management 'yes men' (Mumford and Henshall, 1979 and 1983: 25), she resolved the issue herself by meeting with the members of the department to convince them of the benefits of the method and involving them in the project (ibid, pp. 27–29).

At Digital Corporation, the project manager – MacDonald – chose representative participation because XSEL development was taking place in two separate locations and the users were too scattered and too many in number to bring together as one large user design group. The representative design group were in this case drawn from people known to the XSEL team as interested in new tools and methods or recommended because they were bright and energetic (Mumford and MacDonald, 1989). The impacts of representative participation were viewed as problematic when researchers continued to study the XSEL implementation after Mumford's withdrawal (Leonard-Barton, 1987).

MacDonald reported in his book with Mumford that the XSEL project differed from the kinds of projects she had been involved in at the United Kingdom, as it was a much larger software development task than any of the 'English projects'. The user design group in XSEL had not only to tackle the organisational problem of fitting the technology in the work practices, but also to create 'an excellent technical tool that would do the job required of it'. However, 'in many of the English projects, the software was given' and 'the design task had been restricted to creating a work system and individual job roles ...'. In pointing this out, MacDonald highlighted an important weakness in the method regarding the relative separation of job design from software design and that however much they informed each other they did not coalesce. (Mumford and MacDonald, 1989: 88).

Despite these new and emerging issues that impacted the business view of the value of the method, no major revision to the content of ETHICS was undertaken. Instead, tweaks around the participation techniques and the choice of involved employees were conducted but not a significant rethinking of the premises of the method.

## Public representation

The influence of the international movement of Quality of Working Life declined. The International Quality of Working Life Council broke up at the beginning of the 1980s. The quest for democracy and participation were channelled through institutional routes to be expressed in formal channels. The institutionalisation of many of the industrial movements and their integration in the business environment (Mumford, 1993) reduced the support for ETHICS and other socio-technical approaches.

The rise of globalisation uprooted many activities and relationships from their local origins and cultures (Gray, 1999: 57). Many of the previously direct face-to-face or local interactions started to be conducted over long distances. In industry, it marked the formation of networks of production, management, and distribution where organisations required to have the capacity to work as a unit in real or asynchronous time on a large scale (Castells, 2001). In ISs, this marked the emergence of distributed development, outsourcing, virtual teams, and development and implementation of large complex integrated system; none of which the content of ETHICS was initially developed for.

The job market also changed. The scarcity of jobs and industry being urged to retain workers that characterised the 1960s and 1970s gradually changed and gave way to redundancies as part of organisations' desperate need to reduce cost and improve efficiency. This trend later shifted to accessing low-cost labour markets through off-shoring and hence a sinking demand for labour in higher-cost labour markets. New ways of remote and virtual work and new class of workers emerged. Employees who are skilled began running their own lives and protecting their knowledge, and hence became less dependent on employers (Mumford, 2006).

The arrival of complex classes of technology and new types of employment also altered the way technology was perceived, and invited a more sophisticated conceptualisation that recognise the co-evolvement of both these features and the non-traditional work environment (Interview 3).

Fierce global competition forced industry to cut its costs and reduce prices in order to maintain profits. Methods that do not deal directly with cost reduction became unfavourable as business increasingly sought direct economic value in activities (Interview 6). Instead of updating the content of ETHICS, inviting socio-technical researchers to rise to the challenge, and convincing the business to try the new revamped method, Mumford admitted that the socio-technical approaches, including ETHICS, were seen as having little to offer in such a changing global environment (Mumford, 1996a).

## Links and Knots

Following Latour's five-loop model, the content of ETHICS was sustained through the performance of the other four loops. Hence, any failure in the other loops would cause the content to collapse as this section shows.

The content of ETHICS was dependent on mobilising different actors in each experiment. The techniques of participation, who should be involved and how the choice of people involved could be made were evolving as Mumford engaged with different types of organisations and new case studies were conducted. The content was also dependent on colleagues subscribing to a similar evaluation of success and being convinced of the validity of the method and its link to success. As ETHICS was based on action-based studies, it was dependent on forming alliances with businesses that believed in the value of the method and its possibility to bring change and benefits. The development, and hence publication, of experiments were dependent on finding colleagues and alliances as much as creating a community of researchers. Finding alliances was dependent on publications, reporting success, and other autonomisation efforts. It was also dependent on the international business environment and global concerns and calls. Many of these actors undermined their mobilisation for the various reasons demonstrated in the previous sections.

The content of ETHICS was not sufficiently reshaped in light of the new challenges from integrated large systems, a quest for efficiency in business processes efficiency in speed, cost, and integration, the existence of new classes of technology, and the various impacts of globalisation. Mumford resorted to a theoretical analysis and defence of the socio-technical movement in general (Mumford, 2000; Mumford, 2006) while admitting its unfulfilled promise. No new experiments or revision and update of the method were conducted, and hence the development of the contents to suit the changing technology, social, business, and research environment ceased.

The academic community treated ETHICS as an unchangeable core surrounded by a context. Hence, when the context changed, little effort was spent to revise its content. Consequently, the content of ETHICS was pushed back into history. It became a past movement that was once shaped by what has become an historical context. Although ETHICS retains some of its ethical appeal, this too could fade in the future as the vasculatory system of ethical IT takes another turn to focus on other societal issues such as green IT and data protection and privacy that have more rapid circulations.

## Further insights on change and durability

In order to further examine the issue of changing the content of science that was once shaped by different elements as these elements change, we briefly reflect on another IS research model that has been applied in IS research since before 1992 and is still currently employed. For this purpose, we want to reflect on the creation and evolution of the SPM developed initially by Newman and Robey (1992) and that evolved into the Punctuated Socio-technical Information system Change (PSIC) model (Lyytinen and Newman, 2008) and beyond.

Newman was a visiting professor at Florida International University in the late 1980s. Being discontented with the prevailing IS factor research model, he sketched out a process oriented model. Sharing this idea with a colleague named Robey, he was able to mobilise Robey's knowledge and experience to further develop the model. After further refinement and reviews by the research community, a research paper was published in Management Information Systems Quarterly (MISQ) (Newman and Robey, 1992) and further illustrated with other empirical examples (Robey and Newman, 1996). Over the next few years, through collaboration with others around the globe, he and collaborating colleagues built a community that believed in the value of this model and could build evaluation criteria for this type of research. This community was kept alive through new research, different contexts of study, and continuous involvement of junior academics and researchers $^{4}$ (Heiskanen et al., 2008; Newman and Zhao, 2008; Pan et al., 2009). Through publishing in reputable journals, presentations at conferences, creating assessment and evaluation criteria for the model, and having students applying and adding to the model (Autonomisation), the research has created a value in and of itself.

The engagement of industry or government agencies (Alliances) in research was limited to the provision of data collection opportunities. As this model was based on case study research, and data collection was mainly based on interviews and not action research as in Mumford's case, the alliance building was less extensive. SPM/PSIC colleagues did not connect with a particular social movement or business bandwagon (Public Representation); however, they continued to build and enhance a model that could suit different organisational types in different countries (Lyytinen et al., 2009; Newman and Zhao, 2008). Regarding the content of the model (Links and Knots), it claims to have explanatory power but did not claim to directly solve organisational problems. However, the content did not cease to develop through new contexts of study, collaborating with other colleagues in different universities around the world (e.g., Pan et al., 2009), and interaction and feedback from the research community. This resulted in the evolution of the model from an SPM to a PSIC model (Lyytinen and Newman, 2008) and the inclusion of multi-level variations (Ahmad et al., 2011). The claim of the model also changed from having some explanatory power to become a pictorial data depiction method that uses multiple theories of explanation (Lyytinen and Newman, 2008).

Newman's example shows that Loop 3 and 4 do not make or break social science; however, they do increase its relevance and societal value. Therefore, although the development of the SPM and subsequent PSIC model has survived for over 20 years despite being less informed by Loops 3 and 4 (Alliances and Public Representation), Latour's framework suggests that it may need further work in these areas in order to increase its relevance and value.

Latour claimed that developing alliances is for the fast circulation and higher pulse rate of ideas. Mumford's case revealed, however, that although connecting with societal and organisational movements gives a high pulse rate that increases the circulation of science, it also brings higher risk. Once the movement itself changes or declines, the content of science also loses its momentum, slows down, and declines. Reflecting on the case of ETHICS, the direct link that Mumford created between ETHICS and business efficiency was brave but problematic in the long run as the content of the method could not be updated to suit the new narrative of efficiency in a global business environment. Had Mumford and colleagues revised the method to include the changes in the context and initiated a debate with the scientific community about possible ways to modify the method to cope with the change, it may have resulted in another version of ETHICS that could have flourished.

## Conclusion

The article discusses how ETHICS was once celebrated as an IS methodology and how it declined. It supports the argument that the content of ETHICS was largely dependent on and developed through the linking of four other loops that comprise colleagues, allies, and public representation. The vasculatory systems of ETHICS was weakened partly when the academic support, particularly in North America, falsified its success in XSEL and showed that the first release of the system after she left was a failure and had to be recalled. Colleagues increasingly became convinced that the success of the method owed much to Mumford's personal intervention, her ‘persuasive charm’ (Rickards, 2006), and her significant role as a consultant, group facilitator, and business-trade union moderator, and hence replicating ETHICS in different contexts and with different personnel would be problematic. The business and trade union support also declined as businesses could not find the financial justification of the lengthy method for improving job satisfaction and industrial tension particularly with the high turnover rate in the IT industry, as workers seek different experiences and better salaries and the emergence of new types of workers and employment. The movement of industrial democracy and humanisation of work also declined as it became institutionalised and incorporated in organisations (Mumford, 1993). ISs also became far more complex with the wide implementation of corporate-wide integrated systems such as ERP, Customer Relationship Management and SCM. In addition, the systems development environment became more complicated with distributed and virtual teams and on-shore/off-shore development. All these actors contributed to the weakening of the vasculatory system of ETHICS, and hence its decline.

It should be noted that Professor Mumford pioneered the integration of the Tavistock Institute's socio-technical knowledge and the action research approach developed to study the automation of industrial process (assembly line automation) with office systems, the introduction of new computer systems, and job re-design. The legacy of Professor Mumford goes ‘far beyond ETHICS and the human use of computers’ (Porra and Hirschheim, 2007) to include what she called ‘wicked problems’ of today’s global world such as crime (Xu et al., 2007). Through her legacy, she continues to this day to be ‘a recognised world leader in the application of socio-technical principles to ISs design’ (Xu et al., 2007) and an inspirational figure for women in academia.

## About the authors

Amany Elbanna is a Senior Lecturer in Information Systems (IS) at the School of Management, Royal Holloway, University of London, UK. She graduated with a Ph.D. in ISs from The London School of Economics and since then, has published in leading IS journals including European Journal of Information Systems (EJIS), Journal of Strategic Information Systems (JSIS), Journal of Information Technology (JIT), Information Technology & People (IT&P), and Communications of the Association of Information Systems (CAIS), in addition to leading IS conferences. Her current research focuses on IS development and project management including ERP projects, Agile, cloud computing, and the collective organising of action and innovation in ISs development and management.

Michael Newman is a Professor of ISs at the Manchester Business School, University of Manchester, United Kingdom. Since graduating with a Ph.D. in MIS from the University of British Columbia in 1981, he has authored many academic articles in leading MIS and management journals in Europe and the United States including Management Information Systems Quarterly (MISQ), Information Systems Research (ISR), JIT, Journal of Information Science (JIS), Journal of Management and Science (JMS), Information Systems Journal (ISJ), European Journal of Operational Research (EJOR), Information and Organization (I&O), EJIS, Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE), CAIS, Information Technology & People (IT&P), ACM Transactions on Information Systems (ACM-TOIS), and Omega. His research focuses on the process of ISs development and he has conducted many empirical studies in a variety of organisations. He has held visiting positions at the University of Connecticut, Florida International University, Georgia State University, CWRU, Cleveland, Erasmus University, Rotterdam, the University of Agder, Norway, NHH Bergen, Norway, and the Free University, Amsterdam, and has recently finished a 2-year term as Microsoft Visiting Professor at Copenhagen Business School and a similar period at University of Lund, Sweden. He has given courses to Ph.D. students in IS on qualitative research approaches and has developed a speciality in how to improve the effectiveness of interviewing for Ph.D. students and others conducting empirical studies.
