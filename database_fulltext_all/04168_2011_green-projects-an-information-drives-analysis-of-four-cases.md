# Green projects: An information drives analysis of four cases

Richard T. Watson <sup>a,</sup>⇑, Marie-Claude Boudreau <sup>a,1</sup>, Adela J. Chen <sup>a,2</sup>, Héctor Hito Sepúlveda <sup>b,3</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Management Information Systems Department, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, 312 Brooks Hall, Athens, GA 30602-6273, USA <sup>b</sup> Departamento de Geofisica, University of Concepción, Casilla 160-C Concepción, Chile

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Available online 1 November 2010

Keywords: Transport system Symbiotic relationships Ubiquity Uniqueness Unison Universality Innovation Global climate change Green IS

## a b s t r a c t

An analysis of four transportation systems (Vélib, Zipcar, ERP Singapore, and Transantiago) shows the strategic value of creating a symbiotic relationship between physical and informational systems in order to increase the convenience and patronage of these systems. The four information drives (ubiquity, uniqueness, unison, and universality) are extended to a physical setting and used to report the key characteristics of each system from both a physical and informational perspective. The article argues that the traditional customer orientation to determining systems requirements may fail to create the breakthrough systems designs that are required to reduce society’s climate harming emissions. Rather, we need an innovation orientation based on understanding consumers’ fundamental physical and informational needs.

## 1. Introduction

We all have a role in reducing global climate change, from personal behavior to professional action. As information systems (IS) professionals, we need frameworks for comprehending the potential role of IS in promoting sustainable societal practices. Societies are collections of individuals embedded in physical systems, such as highways, mountains, and houses. We can wrap information systems around these physical systems to make them more useful to promote desirable changes in human behavior. Consequently, we need some broad guideposts for helping systems designers to identify some essential characteristics of an information system that can improve utilization of a physical system.

Humans are addicted to information. Neuroscientists have indeed suggested that our brain contains a system of seeking that promotes the spontaneous capacity to forage, explore, or investigate information about our world (Panksepp, 2004). Similarly, writings drawing from evolutionary psychology refer to the human need for learning, which pushes us to constantly collect information about ourselves and our environment (Lawrence and Nohria, 2002). Practically, our addiction to information is clearly noticeable as we sit on planes cocooned in our private informational systems. Our cars have become informational hubs with connections to radio, mobile phone, and GPS networks. Indeed, the addiction is so bad that some legislatures have forbidden the obviously inane and dangerous act of texting while driving a car or train.

Because of this seemingly insatiable desire for information, we intrinsically seek information systems that provide ubiquity (e.g., mobile phones), uniqueness (e.g., navigation systems), unison (e.g., synchronized calendars), and universal devices (e.g., smart phones) (Junglas and Watson, 2006; Watson et al., 2002). These four factors today underpin convenience, service, security, and efficiency. While it was once difficult to support these inherent information drives, today we are witnessing the satiation of these drives. In this article, we use the four information drives (Junglas and Watson, 2006) to explain the tight coupling that is necessary between the physical and informational components of green projects to improve their usefulness. Four green transport projects (Vélib, Zipcar, ERP Singapore, and Transantiago) are examined and their success or failure explained in terms of the four information drives. These four systems each represent major strategic initiatives to change human behavior to reduce environmental impact. They also illustrate how the implementation of an IS aligned with the four information drives is a strategic necessity for success.

We focus on the strategic alignment between transport and information systems for two reasons. First, in developed economies, the transportation sector is a major source of greenhouse gases, and the carbon footprint for many countries is dominated by transportation and logistics. For instance, in the US, the transportation sector is the largest source of ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emissions (Energy Information Administration, 2007), a major contributor to global climate change. Second, information and communication technologies (ICTs) investments have a large economic impact (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000) and were responsible for the productivity surge in the late 1990s (Stiroh, 2002). Reducing green house gases and creating sustainable business practices will require, among other things, investment in information systems that increase the efficiency with which resources are deployed. Specifically, we need to promote energy efficiency because of the direct link between burning fossils fuels and ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emission. In the case of transportation, sensor networks and associated systems can provide the information necessary to allocate resources more prudently and allow consumers to modify their behavior with a minimum of inconvenience. In addition, systems designers need a framework for thinking strategically about the key characteristics of the new transportation systems they devise; as argued in this article, the four information drives can be used to define such characteristics.

In the following sections, we first present the information drives (ubiquity, uniqueness, unison, and universality), from both an informational and physical perspectives; as we will show, physical limitations can often be alleviated by the inclusion of an informational layer. We then introduce four transportation systems (Vélib, Zipcar, ERP Singapore, and Transantiago) and analyze each with the lens of the information drives. Following this analysis, we reflect on the success (or lack thereof) of the aforementioned systems, and discuss the strategic need to embrace an innovation orientation, which suggests a move from eco-efficiency to eco-effectiveness. Lastly, we propose directions for future research and share some concluding thoughts.

## 2. The information drives

Most people in advanced economies have become so habituated to the fulfillment of their fundamental information drives that, if we are to change their behaviors in the direction of greater ecological sustainability, we need to be highly conscious of feeding these drives. These information drives, which were derived (Junglas and Watson, 2006) from work conducted on u-commerce (Watson et al., 2002), include: ubiquity, uniqueness, unison and universality. Although the drives were originally defined from an informational perspective, they can be interpreted in a physical sense as well. Yet, as will be discussed, it is the informational perspective that helps overcoming the constraints embedded in the materiality of the physical perspective. Each drive is now further explained in the context of both perspectives

## 2.1. Ubiquity

Ubiquity, in an informational sense, is ‘‘access to information unconstrained by time and space” (Junglas and Watson, 2006) In a physical sense, we define ubiquity as the ready availability of the desired resource. While humans might now expect, following the introduction of smart phones, that information should always be accessible, wherever they might be, their expectations of physical resources are tempered by experience and reality. Nevertheless, there needs to a certain density of physical resources for them to be generally useful (e.g., the frequency and placement of public transit stops will affect patronage). Information systems can increase the sense of physical ubiquity by providing patrons with information about a physical system, such as the location of the nearest resource and its availability (e.g., the location of the nearest bus stop and the arrival time of next bus). Providing ubiquitous access to current information about a service enables potential customers to explore their options. Readily available information increases the value of a service.

## 2.2. Uniqueness

Uniqueness, in the information domain, means “knowing precisely the characteristics and location of a person or entity” (Junglas and Watson, 2006). Physically, we interpret uniqueness as the capability to tailor precisely the use of a physical resource to one’s unique needs. Thus, in a car we find adjustable seats, mirrors, and steering wheels to accommodate the different needs of a particular driver. To support physical uniqueness, we need to use information systems to enable consumers to find the best match of available physical resources to current needs (e.g., how to use a transit system to get from the airport to a hotel). If we want people to change their behavior, we need to provide information that enables them to fashion new choices that best meet their individual needs while replacing old environmentally damaging behaviors.

## 2.3. Unison

In an information sense, unison is ‘‘information consistency” (Junglas and Watson, 2006). This includes ideas such as a single view of the customer and synchronization of calendars across devices. From a physical standpoint, we define unison has procedural consistency, where the method for accessing or using a resource varies little. For example, a city transit system might have the same system for buying tickets for bus, train, and water transport so that consumers have to learn only one procedure. In a society, where attention is a scarce resource, procedural consistency is desirable. Information systems can provide simple interfaces to hide procedural complexity and integrate information across physical systems (e.g., in a foreign country, using an ATM-like kiosk with an English script and a US credit card to purchase a ticket that covers use of multiple transit systems).

There is also a need to pursue unison at the societal level so that there is information consistency across transport systems. For example, all public transit systems might be described using a common XML standard. This information consistency would enable consumers to learn one interface (procedural consistency) for handling whatever mass transit system they encounter. Consider the advantages of learning to use a single smart phone app rather than learning one for each system.

## 2.4. Universality

Universality describe the desire to ‘‘overcome the friction of information systems’ incompatibilities” (Junglas and Watson, 2006), and this describes the drive for standards (e.g., Open Document Format), currency unions (e.g., the euro), and multi-functional smart phones (e.g., phone, GPS, camera, PDA, media player). On the physical side, universality is sought to overcome the friction of physical differences. We buy, for example, universal adaptors because of regional differences in electrical outlet connections. Physical payment systems are a major form of friction (e.g., national currencies), and a smart card based information system can lubricate payment by storing multiple currencies. Consider the advantages of a smart phone app that can integrate information across transit systems (reducing IS friction) to compute the best path for the traveler. The XML standard mentioned previously would minimize friction between information systems. Information systems can help translate between physical systems and be designed to support different physical systems.

## 3. Four cases

The following four cases were chosen because each attempts to reduce the environmental footprint of human movement. This is considerable, given that about one-quarter of the carbon emissions we produce pour out of vehicles’ tailpipes (de Rothschild, 2007). The cases’ levels of success, however, are mixed. To understand their outcome, we analyze each case in terms of ubiquity, uniqueness, unison, and universality. The fundamental features of each system discussed are summarized in Table 1.

## 3.1. Vélib

Vélib (short for Vélo Liberté, i.e., Bicycle Freedom) is a public self-service bicycle rental program that was launched in July 2007, in Paris, France. Its goal is to provide an alternative to cars for short trips (typically less than 30 min) within Paris. Claimed to be the largest bicycle rental project in the world, Vélib started with 10,648 bicycles and 750 stations, and by the end of 2008 it had doubled in terms of bikes and stations. Vélib is considered a third generation bicycle-sharing program, with high-tech, electronic payment, tracking and locking systems.<sup>4</sup> Its premise is simple; subscribers go to a station, identify themselves, and borrow a bicycle. Each 30 min of riding is free, with an incremental cost thereafter. Subscriptions are avail able for 1 day (€1), for 1 week (€5), or for a year (€29), with a security deposit of €150.

The technology supporting Vélib is more sophisticated than with other bicycle-sharing programs. Each station has a computer terminal (‘‘borne”), from which a rider can obtain a subscription, recharge an account (for 1-year subscriptions), find out which other stations are nearby and the number of bicycles available (useful if the current location if full), or see the state of an account. Also, each bicycle stand is equipped with a ‘‘bornette”, which holds the bicycle, and a swiper, which reads the subscriber’s information. As to the bicycles, each has an RFID tag, such that when a subscriber borrows or returns a bicycle, the system knows.

From an informational perspective, ubiquity is high. Subscribers can determine the number of bicycles available at a given station from any device linked to the Internet or from another station. On the physical side, ubiquity is also high as bicycle stations are 300 m apart, which is more than four times the density of Métro stations. Because bicycles and customers are uniquely identified, the beginning and ending point of every ride can be tracked, and this information can be used to decide the placement and size of stations and when to move bicycles between stations. On the physical dimension, riders can tailor journeys to personal needs quite accurately (at most 300 m from the desired destination). Both the physical and informational aspects also rank high on unison. Indeed, on the physical side, there is single process for rental (i.e., the same type of payment card and method for all stations). On the informational side, there is a single integrated database that gives instant record of the state of the system (e.g., number of bicycles at each station). Informational universality is high, because payment and subscription systems work across multiple systems. Vélib terminals accept all traditional forms of payments (bank card, credit card, and cash), including the Moneo card, which is an electronic purse system for cashless small purchases. The Navigo smartcard, which works with the entire Parisian public transportation system, also gives access to Vélib for 1-year subscribers. In the physical domain, universality is high because of the uniformity of bicycles, which reduces human learning and gives economies of scale in manufacturing and maintenance.

## 3.2. Zipcar

Zipcar is one of several car-sharing services operating in some 60 US cities {Novak, 2006}. With more than 120,000 mem bers and 3500 cars throughout North America and partnerships with 30 universities, Zipcar provides access to a variety of cars on a pay-as-you-go basis. Members can reserve online or by phone the time period required and location of a car. Members open the car with their Zipcard, which will open only the reserved car.

In Greater Boston, there are about 500 cars in nearly 300 locations, a moderate level of ubiquity for something like a car. In 2009, Zipcar released an iPhone app to locate and reserve a car, view forthcoming reservations, find directions to a reserved car, unlock and lock a car, modify a reservation, and contact Zipcar for help. Members owning iPhones have high information ubiquity. Uniqueness is moderate on the physical dimension because Zipcar is an urban system and cars must be rented from and returned to specific locations within an urban area so a driver has somewhat limited ability to customize a trip. On the informational side, uniqueness is high as both the car and rider are identified, which is important for tracking car misuse or accidents. Electronic billing for services and a database for keeping track of cars and renters supports high informational unison. Physical unison is also high because the rental process is simple and executed via an electronic connection. A variety of cars are available, which likely causes some efficiency losses on the maintenance side and thus physical universality is low. Customers have an electronic card for opening a car for which rental has been approved, but because this card serves this purpose and is not a more general smart card, informational universality is also low.

## 3.3. ERP Singapore

Electronic road pricing (ERP), a cordon pricing system, became operational in Singapore in 1998. It automated the preexisting Singapore Area Licensing Scheme which was an anti-congestion policy introduced in 1975. Based on the pay-asyou-use principle, ERP is a fully automated system that charges motorists when they contribute to peak hour congestion. Restricted zones, or priced routes, are equipped with over overhead tolling gantries, which communicate with an in-vehicle unit and automatically deduct tolls from an installed in-vehicle CashCard unit, which contains a refillable debit card that can be topped up at ATMs or gas stations all over Singapore. Motorists decide at their own discretion whether to drive, when to drive, and where to drive based on their personal needs. Motorists can choose to drive at another time, take another route, or switch to public transportation

The density of tolling gantries, nearly 50, in downtown Singapore provides high ubiquitous access to the inner city as drivers are not funneled through a few payment points. ERP also has a moderate level of informational ubiquity, because its operating hours and rates are published on the web site of the Land Transport Authority. As travel patterns are selfdetermined, drivers have high physical uniqueness. Fach in-vehicle unit comes with a unique serial number, which links to the ownership information of the vehicle. Therefore a motorist is uniquely identified by the serial number of the in-vehicle unit installed in the cars. ERP charges are directly deducted from a CashCard with a short beep indicating a successful trans: action, and the remaining balance momentarily displaved, which provides motorists with informational uniqueness. When a violation happens, such as passing the gantry with no CashCard or a CashCard with insufficient balance, ERP will pick up the serial number of the in-vehicle unit and track down the vehicle owner. CashCards are transferable between vehicles, and can also be used widely within Singapore for many purchases. For example, students can use their CashCards to pay for photocopier use on campus. On the informational side, the CashCard has become a universal smart card. The same electronic equipment is installed at each gantry, which suggests high level of physical universality.

ERP is based on a central computer system that integrates various local-control technologies such as the antennae controllers, vehicle detectors, and gantry video cameras. The central computer system operates 24 h to ensure proper functioning of the gantries. On a quarterly basis and during the June and December school holidays, traffic conditions on the expressways and arterial roads are reviewed and ERP rates adjusted if necessary. With the growth of the central business district, the boundaries of cordon areas expanded. Thus, there is a high level of informational unison, because ERP operates through an integrated information system. Physical unison also ranks high, because ERP charges (if any) are deducted from CashCards plugged in to the in-vehicle units in the same way at each gantry.

## 3.4. Transantiago

Transantiago is a major and controversial reform of the transportation system of Santiago, Chile’s capital city of more than five million people. Prior to this reform, which took place in February 2007, Santiago had a complex and ageing web of yellow and white buses traversing the city. It was not uncommon to see the streets crowded with too many buses, most of them empty, and aggressively competing, because the salary of the drivers was based on the number of passengers. Citizens complained that bus transportation was unfriendly, unsafe, and polluting. In contrast, they thought well of the subway system, whose four lines total just over 100 km and support 104 stations.

Transantiago was designed to combine newer and fewer buses, new information systems, highway improvements, redesigned bus routes integrated with the subway. It was conjectured that Transantiago bus drivers would be more courteous and drive more safely because their salary was not dependent on the number of passengers. In addition, travel times were expected to be less because of the system redesign.

The plans for Transantiago included an advanced payment system and fleet management technology. Patrons use a contactless smart card that identifies them and allows a free transfer between the different components of Transantiago for about 2 h after the start of a trip. Planned fleet management software included a GPS for recording a bus's location and sensors to count how many people boarded or exited at each bus stop. Passenger counts are used to determine the revenue generated by each bus, which are privately owned and operated by 10 companies, compared to over three thousand previously.

Implementation of Transantiago has been heavily criticized and has had a major economic impact (Walter, 2007). Problems include poor route design (e.g., no bus stops at many hospitals) and a patent infringement suit (regarding the passenger counting technology). As a result, the President of Chile apologized for the failure to the citizens of Santiago (McCarty, 2007).

From a ubiquity perspective, Transantiago fails on the physical and informational levels. The new bus network is not as dense and has fewer bus stops. The plan was to build an ‘‘optimal” bus network, but the new system is not sufficiently close to optimality (and thus not close to ubiquity), and the number of needed buses was underestimated. Timetables were not readily available or were not reliable when the system opened, and the vast majority of consumers did not have informational ubiquity. Physical uniqueness decreased and some riders now have to make multiple trips instead of one, particularly when going across the city. The plan was that buses would be precisely coordinated, and bus riders would be able to make long journeys on multiple buses in less time. A community leader sued Transantiago because he could no longer get to work. The poor integration of GPS data with fleet management software means there is inadequate knowledge of, where buses are and when they will arrive at a particular stop. As a result, it is difficult to keep buses to the finely grained schedule that is necessary for high levels of coordination. One affected municipality employed people to report dynamically via a laptop computer the location of buses (humorously called ‘‘human GPS”) (Garay, 2007). Thus, informational uniqueness is low. Physical and informational unison are high because the procedures for using the transport system are consistent across buses and the subway. The original intention was to standardize on a new bus. but because of shortages, old buses were repainted and added to the system while more new buses were acquired, thus reducing in the short term physical universality. The same smart card can be used for the bus and subway but not for purchasing other goods or services, so informational universality is moderate. Many of the problems of Transantiago are due to implementing the physical system before the information system was operational.

## 4. Successful green systems

As the preceding cases demonstrate, the more successful systems (Vélib, Zipcar, and ERP Singapore) have created information systems that support their physical systems by minimizing these physical systems’ limitations and thus meeting citizens’ fundamental information needs. Santiago’s bus system failed, partly because it was launched before the complementing information system was operational. Consequently, bus patrons could not get fundamental information, such as timetables, and the power of precise location information (i.e., uniqueness) could not be deployed to coordinate the bus fleet.

Information is a key ingredient for increasing the efficiency of energy consuming systems. Given access to the right information at the right time, energy reducing behavioral changes can be facilitated and energy consuming resources can be more efficiently managed. On the color wheel of sustainability, adding information creates a shade of green.

## 5. The strategic move from eco-efficiency to eco-effectiveness

The traditional approach to systems development is to analyze the client’s requirements and deliver a system to meet their expressed needs. This customer orientation results in systems that meet today’s needs (Band, 1991). With such a customer orientation, a responsive market strategy is adopted, and system development is incremental; organizations follow their competitors and adjust to their customers’ wants (Berthon et al., 1999). An alternative approach, innovation orientation, aims at creating systems that are technologically superior and anticipate tomorrow’s needs (Smith, 1980; Utterback et al. 1976). With an innovation orientation, an assertive market strategy is adopted, and system development is discontinuous; organizations shape customer behavior by providing new products or services that either define new markets or influence new expectations and trends (Berthon et al., 1999). For example, the graphical user interface (GUI) did not arise from asking computer users what they expected from an interface (they probably wanted ‘‘easier to remember commands”). The GUI was a product of the innovative thinking of a few who wanted to create the radically new rather than incrementally improve the old.

The aforementioned dichotomy in strategic orientations (customer versus innovation) is similar to the dichotomy that exists when organizations develop systems to address environmental sustainability: they are either driven by eco-efficiency or by eco-effectiveness. If organizations pursue the linear thinking of customer orientation, they lock themselves into the eco-efficiency lane, which is defined as ‘‘the delivery of competitively-priced goods and services that satisfy human needs and bring quality of life, while progressively reducing ecological impacts and resource intensity throughout the life-cycle to a level at least in line with the earth’s carrying capacity” (DeSimone et al., 1997, p. 47). Eco-efficiency aspires to make the old, destructive system less so and therefore only slows down the deterioration of nature with moral proscriptions and punitive demands (Chen et al., 2008). Such a slowdown is usually implemented through incremental solutions, that is, when a customer orientation is adopted.

Environmentalists see a goal beyond eco-efficiency; they advocate eco-effectiveness. They distinguish between efficiency and effectiveness in the same way that Drucker (1954, 2006) did over his long career. It is the difference between doing things right (efficiency) and doing the right things (effectiveness). Pursuing eco-efficiency, a firm might reduce its consumption of coal-generated electricity. An eco-effective organization would instead switch to renewable resources.

The concept of eco-effectiveness was introduced (McDonough and Braungart, 1998) without a clear statement of its meaning, though the general concept was easily grasped. It was later explained as ‘‘working on the right things—on the right products and services and systems—instead of making the wrong things less bad” (McDonough and Braungart, 2002, p. 76). Seeking an ultimate solution for ecological problems, eco-effectiveness oftentimes requires a shift of mindset and transformation of business models (Chen et al., 2008). This shift in mindset is reminiscent of the discontinuous solutions associated with the innovation orientation.

Similarly, we argue that addressing global warming requires strategic breakouts rather than tactical improvements. Several centuries of incremental additions of CO cannot be turned around quickly with incremental actions. It is the innovative systems that will be put forward by the ‘‘shapers” that will redefine and influence (Berthon et al., 1999) how we behave in a more environmentally sustainable way. The Information Systems field needs the revolutionary perspective underlying thinking such as the wedges model (Pacala and Socolow, 2004). Strategic action requires a deep understanding of what consumers want and the technological imagination to translate this comprehension into systems. An interesting feature of two of the aforementioned cases (Vélib and Zipcar) is that they are not IS-enhanced versions of systems that were previously in place. Rather, they represent innovations that endeavor to move sustainability beyond the incremental approach of eco-efficiency (DeSimone et al., 1997, p. 47) to the strategic advancement sought by those who advocate eco-effectiveness (McDonough and Braungart, 1998).

The physical and informational drives describe fundamental desires: they were here used in the specific area of transport systems and the general area of information systems. Thus, we argue that if we are to build innovative systems to reduce carbon emissions, we need to look beyond asking consumers what they need, which is likely to advance eco-efficiency. Rather, we need to examine how we can fashion information systems that meet fundamental desires as represented by ubiquity, uniqueness, unison, and universality, which might help us to become eco-effective. Whereas a customer orientation is likely to serve today’s customers better, an innovation orientation has the potential to create an entire new class of customers for public transport systems.

## 6. Directions for research

Global warming is an urgent problem, and some fear the tipping point might have already been reached (Lemonick, 2008; Schellnhuber, 2008). Thus, we strongly advocate action and the development of solutions rather than the traditional social science model of reporting what others have done or think. The traditional IS research model is not designed for urgent action. IS scholars have an opportunity for undertaking socially relevant and useful research, and it is likely to be through action research. We need to get involved in addressing how the information drives, among other issues of course, should underline an innovation orientation strategy (Berthon et al., 1999) to advance sustainable behavior and eco-effectiveness. IS scholars need to find opportunities to work with teams designing and implementing systems to advance sustainability and investigate dynamically and iteratively how to use information to change behavior in the direction of sustainability.

There is a need to rapidly create domain knowledge on building information systems for sustainability, because domain knowledge is frequently the key to systems success (Davis and Olson, 1985). The traditional multi-year cycle of research and publish is inappropriate for the pressing problem of climate change. Moreover, the impact of IS research is often limited by the incremental conservatism induced by a risk avoiding reviewing system. Reviewers reject the unfamiliar and cautiously, after several rounds of reviewing, approve the familiar. Thus, not surprisingly, the many papers, and trees, devoted to TAM research have probably had no impact on the revolutionary interface of smart phones. If IS research is going to contribute to solving global warming, it also needs to adopt an innovation orientation.<sup>5</sup>

## 7. Conclusion

Innovation often comes when one can scrape off the surface layer of needs. As Henry Ford realized, his customer thought they wanted a faster horse (the surface layer of their needs). The deeper need, but one that most customers could not express, was faster transport. We believe that a deeper layer for information system needs is captured by the four information drives, which take us beyond the surface layer of ease of use and usefulness. Thus, we contend that the designers of ecoeffective systems will need to consider how they create synergy between the physical and informational components of their system. They will have to understand how to use information to change behavior in favor of new physical systems. These will be the strategic information systems of the coming years because curtailing carbon emissions is the strategic problem facing our society.

A great challenge for society is to build physical and informational systems that synergistically can change individual and organizational behaviors in the direction of sustainable consumption and lower emissions. Innovation, as has been demonstrated many times before, is crucial to solving this massive social challenge. We have shown in this article how four fundamental desires can explain the success and failure of four transport systems and their associated information systems. Organizations can use this understanding to guide systems design in the general area of transport planning, and, we argue, can also apply these same four foundational concepts to strategically design innovative systems in many other areas

Research cycle time reduction will be necessary if IS is to contribute to solving the dominant issue of our time. This should be a strategic issue for our field, though only recently has it gained much attention (Watson et al., 2010). Journal editors, particularly those of journals that deal with IS strategic and leadership issues, need to rethink their modus operandi visà-vis articles that deal with sustainability, Green IS, and related topics. First, what steps will they take to reduce cycle time to ensure timely release of relevant knowledge? Second, how will they redesign the reviewing process to ensure that the innovation triumphs over conservative incrementalism? We hope that Green IS is more than a special issue of this journal but rather a galvanizing event that initiates a strategic transformation to create a knowledge management system designed to solve today’s critical problem.
