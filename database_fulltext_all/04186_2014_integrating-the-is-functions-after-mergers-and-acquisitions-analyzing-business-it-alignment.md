## a b s t r a c t

This exploratory positivist case study uses multiple case design to examine the impacts of strategic alignment during the mergers and acquisitions (M&A) process and the implications for strategic formulation of successful M&A outcomes. According to alignment theory, for a firm to achieve M&A integration success, the business-IS strategies should be aligned during the M&A execution. However, our results indicate that successful, non-aligned M&A integrations can also occur, strongly supporting the emergent perspective of strategy formation in M&A integration as a valuable addition to the a priori formal planning view of strategy formation.

## 1. Introduction

Mergers and acquisitions (M&A) constitutes a risky, frequent, and high stakes business activity. M&A is a massive contributor to the global political economy. Ernst and Young recognized some 1000 M&A with a combined market value of more than \$110 billion USD (Dharmapalan, 2011) from the global technology and telecom sector alone. Despite the large and increasing volume of mergers and acquisitions, however, the value of both acquired and acquiring companies tends to fall as a result of M&A (Pautler, 2003). A number of studies (Chatterjee et al., 1992; Gao and Iyer, 2006) suggest that this decline is a result of structural precursors such as the cultural distance between integrating firms. Further, McKiernan and Meral (1995) estimate that between 33% and 60% of M&A activities ultimately result in divestiture; similarly, Violano (1990) finds that 80% of merger deals destroyed the value of the organization for the acquiring company.

One potential source of added cost and failure to achieve positive goals from M&A activity derives from the difficulty in operationalizing integration. Following Christensen et al. (2011) this may be due to a poor matching of firm level integration strategy with the purpose of obtaining the firm. Such poorly selected firm level integration strategy will result in poorly chosen tactics for the integration (e.g. piling up investments, when the purpose is to streamline; or failing to invest sufficiently when the purpose is to expand product range or quality). We would further argue that successful M&A integration requires effective recombination of functional areas (e.g. marketing, accounting) from existing and acquired sources. Difficulties integrating existing and acquired firm assets can be a major contributor to the failure to realize financial benefits from M&A.

IS can be viewed as one of the critical functional areas that benefits from effective integration for the overall success of the M&A effort. Origitano (2006) argues persuasively that the integration of MIS assets can be an important influence on M&A outcomes. The IS function should play a critical role in the success of organizational integration for three main reasons: (1) Since business processes are closely tied to supporting information systems, information systems need also be accounted for when building a unified firm; (2) Because management decision making is largely based on complete, accurate, and timely information, effective integration of IS can provide the data needed for such decision making; and (3) Since the assets of IS functions can be substantial, integration of both computing and human assets may achieve either cost savings or capability extension. McKiernan and Merali (1995) and Shearer (2004) suggest that a major reason why merger and acquisition integration fails is poor management of information systems in the integration process (Shrivastava, 1986). Issues that complicate integration include, ‘‘definition of the new corporate information systems (IS), infrastructure requirements, the high cost of integration and development of information technology (IT) systems, and a reluctance to define both IS and IT in the ex-ante stage’’ (McKiernan and Merali, 1995, p.55).

One approach toward improving the probability of successful IS integration focuses on alignment of firm and IS level integration strategies. Following Venkatraman et al. (1993), higher degrees of alignment between the IS and firm-level business strategies will result in more successful enterprises. Thus, we expect acquiring firms that more closely align IS with firm-level business strategy should be more effective in actualizing IS integration. Although Venkatraman et al. (1993) did not specifically address post M&A integration, Mehta and Hirschheim (2007) follow this line of argument in their study of IS integration of three energy industry mergers. While rich in detail, longitudinal in nature, and highly informative, their data is limited to three cases in a single industry and their findings show significant ambiguity in: (1) the relationship between alignment and outcomes; and (2) the degree to which alignment is a mandated strategic approach or a by-product of local and ad hoc decision making. Given the financial impact of M&A, the potential influence of integration of IS on overall M&A success, and the lack of clear understanding in this area, the continued investigation of this topic represents an opportunity to address an important gap in existing research.

This study aims to contribute to our understanding of the relationship between strategy and integration of the IS function following M&A in three ways. First, we aim to examine the applicability of alignment theory to the specific instance of post implementation integration of the IS function from acquired and acquiring firm. We observe the tendency in particular cases to align IS integration strategy to the firm-level strategy. We also investigate the relationship between successful outcomes in the integration and occurrences of alignment. Second, for each of the particular forms of alignment and any other observed non-aligned approaches, we identify characteristic issues critical for success in that particular strategic alignment. Third, we examine whether instances of alignment (or non-alignment) derive from a ‘‘classic’’ approach where selected strategy drives subsequent decisions and actions or from an ‘‘emergent’’ approach where the solving of various elemental problems can be viewed retrospectively as a particular strategy. In the former case, we would have to consider why organizations would purposely choose a ‘‘non-aligned strategy’’; in the latter case, we would have to consider that alignment is a logical by-product of replicated goals and approaches at firm and IS function level rather than an organized objective.

In the sections that follow, we discuss in more detail the foundations of alignment theory as well as several alternative perspectives on strategy. We follow that by presenting details regarding the positivist case study method that we employ in examining this topic. Next we present the results of our investigation followed by a discussion of the implications for research and practice. We conclude with a summary of our contribution and acknowledgments of limitations as well as potential directions for future research.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

## 2.1. Alignment between business strategy and IS strategy

Business-IS alignment within an entity is the basis of a robust stream of IS research (Chan et al., 1997; Chan and Reich, 2007; Henderson and Venkatraman, 1999; Tallon and Pinsonneault, 2011). Achieving and maintaining alignment is a major practical concern for CIOs and other organizational executives (Armstrong and Sambamurthy, 1999; Chan et al., 1997; Grover et al., 1993; Preston and Karahanna, 2008; Preston et al., 2008; Smaltz et al., 2006). Tallon and Pinsonneault (2011) present a comprehensive review of this literature. In summary, their examination of the literature suggests a frequent but not undisputed link between alignment and firm performance and, secondly, an important role of moderators, particularly the strategy employed by the firm. ‘‘For example, research has found important differences in alignment based on the type of strategy adopted by firms. Defenders under the Miles and Snow (1978) typology reap little or no financial gain from alignment (Chan et al., 2006; Sabherwal and Chan, 2001); operationally excellent firms under the Treacy and Wiersema (1995) typology are also unaffected (Palmer and Markus, 2000; Tallon, 2008) (Tallon and Pinsonneault, 2011, p.467).’

Alignment has been a recurrent theme of IS research on post-merger integration. Brown and Renwick (1996), in a nonempirical article, propose the logic of matching the purpose of the merger, in terms of the preexisting relationship between acquired and acquiring firm as (1) within the same industry. (2) concentric adiacent industries: (3) vertical with a customer or supplier; and (4) conglomerate in unrelated industries. These are matched with recommended or predicted IS governance structures in terms of preponderance of centralization, decentralization, or hybrid form of organization

Robertson and Powell (2001) drawing on three case studies in the insurance industry conclude that business integration strategy was both the main driver and a major constraint on IS integration. In drawing on two case studies in the Australian banking industry, Johnston and Yetton (1996) argue that fit is important between strategies at the organizational, departmental, and technical levels of analysis. Emergent from the study is the suggestion that three merger strategies should each have a different model of change. The independent/unrated should result in coexistence; rationalization/cost reduction in absorption; and value-added/synergy in best of breed. Mehta and Hirschheim (2007) more explicitly examine alignment as a key component of integration of IS functions post M&A. This multiple case study in the US oil and gas industry reports on the strategies and decision making involved in three mergers. Data is longitudinal in nature extending over 3 years. For the most part. the study found no effort on the part of organizations to develop alignment of IT department and business strategies, rather goals pertaining to IT were entirely aimed at cost reduction. Most firms had not emphasized strategic alignment prior to nor following merger. All of these were considered successful mergers, though specific measures were not disclosed for confidentiality reasons. Wijnhoven et al. (2006) build theory based on three cases from hospitals in Holland. The basis for the conceptualization is IT alignment theory based on Henderson and Venkatraman (1992). In the three cases, alignment between firm and IS strategies held up, but with more complexity observed as the initial intentions of the companies changed as events unfolded. In case 3 the larger entity planned to leave the smaller intact for a short term, but eventually to absorb IT within its own systems.

In spite of some commonalities to these studies, much remains unclear about the role of alignment between firm and IS strategies. For example, in all of the empirical studies a variety of critical success factors are proposed. Although these overlap to some extent, they are certainly not identical. Robertson and Powell (2001) observe three success factors: (1) avoiding organizational paralysis in terms of unclear roles and authorities, interpersonal conflict, and the like: (2) clashing cultures: and (3) trust of senior management. They also found technical issues pertaining to integration of products and data to target systems. Johnston and Yetton (1996) list technology platforms, project management structures, and IT staffing practices as key factors. Wijnhoven et al. (2006) points out that while similar hardware and software systems can suggest a relatively easy merger, differences in procedures or business processes can present difficulties in actuation of the integration intentions. These studies do not distinguish the value or difficulty of these issues distinguished by type of alignment nor by whether or not strategies are well aligned.

## 2.2. Strategic formation theory and attendant views on IS strategy

Scholars have taken interest in the approaches available for strategy formation. Mintzberg and Lampel (1999) describe 10 schools of thought on strategy formation; they include three prescriptive schools and seven descriptive schools. The design, planning, and position schools approach strategy formation as a process of conceptual design (Chandler, 1962; Selznick, 1957), of formal planning (Ansoff, 1965), and of analytical positioning (Porter, 1980, 1985). In the design school clear and unique strategies are formulated a priori and the internal situation of the organization is used to match external conditions (Chandler, 1962; Selznick, 1957). In the planning school strategy formation entails following a rigorous set of steps from situational analysis to strategy execution (Ansoff, 1965). In the position school strategy formation places the business within the context of its industry and looks at how the organization can improve its strategic positioning within that industry (Porter, 1980, 1985). These schools view strategy formation as a top-down, formal process, discount the behavioral theory of a firm, and seek to maximize profit outcomes.

In sharp contrast are the seven descriptive schools (entrepreneurial, cognitive, learning, environmental, configurational, power, and cultural) that support strategy formation as a process and place power-behavioral approaches at the core of strategy formation. These include Quinn’s (1980) ‘‘logical incrementalism’’ and Mintzberg and Waters’ (1985) emergent strategy. Emergent strategy from the learning school views strategy formation as a bottom-up organizational process where the strategy itself is a pattern of actions emerging from past decisions the firm has undertaken. Management strategy theory (Mintzberg, 1987) defines IS strategy as an organizational perspective on the investment in, deployment, use and management of IS (Chen et al., 2010, p.235).

Three conceptualizations of IS strategy closely reflect the schools of thought on overall business strategy outlined above: (1) IS strategy as the use of IS to support business strategy; (2) IS strategy as the master plan of the IS function; and (3) IS strategy as the shared view of the IS role within the organization (Chen et al., 2010). In the first case, IS strategy must be linked with an established (a priori) business strategy and use IS strategy as a business-centric support. This ‘‘position’’-based concept of IS strategy firmly links it to normative, classical strategy theory. In the second case, IS strategy seeks to execute IS functionality effectively and efficiently. This conception shifts IS strategy from being business-centric to being IS-centric, and not necessarily linked to the overall business strategy because the two strategies are developed concurrently. In this conceptualization, IS strategy remains firmly rooted in classical strategy theory in the ‘‘planning’’ school. Finally, in the third case, IS strategy takes a shared organizational view of the role IS plays within an organization, one that guides future IS-related business decisions and activities (Chen et al., 2010).

## 2.3. M&A business strategy at the organizational level

Companies employ three major outcome-based firm-level business merger strategies – absorption, symbiosis, and preservation (Wijnhoven et al., 2006). In absorption, the acquiring company subsumes the acquired company, leaving little to no organizational remnants of the acquired firm intact. In symbiosis, the acquiring company retains the core competencies or key organizational pieces of competitive advantage from the acquired company. Although the organization appears to an outsider as a single merged entity, significant portions of the acquired company remain unchanged. In preservation, the acquired company remains fully intact within the merged entity. This strategy is often employed when the acquiring company is a holding company or is entering a new line of business where the acquiring firm possesses none of the operational expertise or capacity necessary to be completely absorbed.

Companies may move quickly toward integration or postpone it, according to their stated integration goals. The difficulty in choosing the timing of integration activities has been described as the ‘‘M&A management paradox’’ (Paruchuri et al., 2006). Paruchuri et al. (2006) describe this as a central dilemma in managing acquisitions. In the short run, a firm experiences costs and risks in aggressively pursuing integration. However, in avoiding these costs and speeding the integration, opportunities to create synergies, which often are at least part of the rationale for merging, may be reduced or lost. In the long run, accumulated redundant business processes may be more difficult and costly to integrate. Finding the appropriate balance for timing of the integration process is an essential part of MIS M&A integration success.

## 2.4. A framework of firm and IS strategy alignment

In this paper we use Wijnhoven et al. (2006) framework to combine logically all pairs of firm and IS integration strategies. The three possible firm-level merger objectives are absorption where the target company ceases to exist; symbiosis, where the business models of each company are retained and combined; and preservation, seeking to retain the independent strengths of each merging entity. These align loosely with Christensen et al. (2011)’s discussion of merger purposes to achieve improvements in current operations (by improving a premier position or cutting costs) and reinventing the business model to redefine the firm and its market presence. In the first case, the firm can perform an absorption strategy largely abandoning the acquired firm but assimilating key resources. In their terminology this is referred to as ‘‘leverage my business model’’ (LBM), but aligns with what Wijnhoven and colleagues term absorption. In the latter case the firm cannot routinely plug parts of the acquired firm into its business model and must integrate the two entities with significant adjustment. In their terminology this is referred to as ‘‘reinvent my business model’’ (RBM), but aligns with symbiosis in Wijnhoven’s parlance. Note that Christensen explicitly does not deal with what they call ‘‘conglomeration’’ or the continued independent persistence of the acquired and acquiring entities. This is the same concept as preservation in Wijnhoven’s terminology. Christensen et al. (2011) associates this strategy with the ‘‘leveraged buyout,’’ though it could also be termed the action of a holding company.

IS integration strategies are employed throughout the overall M&A strategic execution and loosely mirror overall business strategies.¹ Following Wiinhoven et al. (2006) the four identified firm level integration strategies are: transformation, consolidation, combination, and co-existence. Transformation entails merging in order to define a more optimal future MIS approach and evolve both companies’ processes toward that new information system (e.g., moving both original companies’ ERP implementations to an enhanced, comprehensive ERP single instance.) Transformation is set apart as an IT integration strategy as the objective of the resultant IS is not to retain or combine the existing systems, but to deploy a new IS to execute on the overall M&A business strategy of the merged entity. Consolidation seeks to eliminate one firm’s systems and expand the remaining system to serve both original companies. Combination identifies superior elements from both the acquired and acquiring companies and uses those components to form the resultant enterprise system for the new entity. Finally, co-existence leaves the systems of both companies intact to maximize the value delivered for the combined company (Wiinhoven et al., 2006).²

By combining the firm and IS level integration strategies we derive a matrix of combinations. This is represented graphically in Fig. 1. Note that we indicate the cells in which the ‘‘leverage my business’’ and ‘‘reinvent my business’’ models should appear as aligned firm and IS level strategies. Aligned approaches mirror MIS integration strategies with the overal M&A integration strategy. The three examples are absorption–consolidation, symbiosis–combination, and preservation–coexistence. Absorption–consolidation exhibits a complementary approach in that the overall M&A business strategic goal is for the acquiring firm to completely subsume the acquired firm, and the IS integration strategy involves the acquiring firm’s IS completely assimilating the acquired firm’s IS. Symbiosis–combination is also categorized as a complementary approach as the overall business M&A strategy goal is to combine the two merging entities and retain the aspects most advantageous competitively from each respective firm across all functional areas, with combination the category that describes ‘‘best of breed’’ combination of the two IS areas in the merger. Finally, preservation–co-existence is also presented as a complimentary approach with the overall business M&A strategy being to largely preserve the structure and operations of the existing firm, mirroring this in the IS areas, with each IS area co-existing in the new merged entity.

A non-aligned approach, on the other hand, occurs when the MIS integration strategy does not mirror the overall M&A integration strategy. Logically, there are two forms that such non-aligned strategies could take. In the first set there is less IT integration than would be expected with respect to the overall business M&A strategy. These are located in the cells below the aligned combinations shown on the diagonal. These cells include combinations of absorption with either consolidation or co-existence or symbiosis with co-existence. In each of these three combinations the firm’s overall plans are to more tightly integrate the acquired with acquiring firm while leaving IT functions less integrated.

The second outcome is that there was more IT integration than would be expected with respect to the overall business strategy. These are shown as the cells above the aligned combinations shown on the diagonal. These cells include preservation with either consolidation or combination or symbiosis with consolidation. In each of these three combinations the firm’s overall plans are to more tightly integrate the IS functions while leaving the overall firm integration less tightly coupled. Additionally, all combinations of the transformation strategy would imply more tightly integrated IS than firm level integration in that the IS would become a single new entity and may retain relatively little of the original components of either acquiring or acquired firm.

Following the largely positive findings of a relationship between alignment and performance outcomes (Tallon and Pinsonneault, 2011), we would expect to find evidence of the ‘‘classic’’ strategic approach in the aligned combinations. It is logical that if firms develop a planned approach they would anticipate benefits to aligning firm and IS strategies then following with decisions and actions that support this. We would expect to find evidence of the ‘‘emergent’’ strategic approach in the non-aligned combinations, if any are observed, on the assumption that these are more likely observed as arising from a series of independent decisions and actions rather than being pre-planned. However, there is no compelling reason that independent decisions and actions on various elemental M&A integration problems could not lead to aligned strategic combinations nor that analysis and selection of strategy could not lead to non-aligned strategic combinations based on the judgment of those responsible for implementation.

## 3. Method

This investigation was conducted as an exploratory positivist case study (Pare, 2004), using multiple case design where each M&A studied, a total of 22, was treated as a separate case (Yin, 2003). This method allows for the gathering of rich data sets pertaining to particular cases, but also observation or relationships among cases. Given that much prior research in integration of IS following M&A is based on small numbers of cases, this approach permits a broadening of examination to more general issues. It also provides the opportunity to examine whether expected results of alignment theory are robust and can be observed.

In this method a set of independent case studies are conducted. We gathered data for each case by conducting a series of interviews. The two researchers independently conducted these interviews with key IS practitioners across all levels within several different companies who had experienced mergers and acquisitions. Each interviewee answered the protocol primarily with respect to one M&A they participated in, if they had participated in several. None of the interviewees reported on the same M&A activity; thus, each interview correlates to one M&A case. (See Appendix A for more information regarding interviewees' demographics.) Using an identical interview protocol in several different scenarios in this multiple case design strengthens the reliability of our findings. We followed the replication approach to multiple case studies presented in Yin (2003, p. 49), where each case study had an individual case report and comparing the cases to each other were able to draw cross case conclusions based on our framework. Sampling strategies for the interviews included maximum variation (following Bala and Venkatesh, 2007) and snowball sampling (Pare, 2004), with each of the interviews containing verbal references to that M&A being successful in its firm level outcomes.<sup>3</sup> We used theoretical replication logic, with confirming and disconfirming cases chosen based on the IS-business alignment framework presented.

Participants ranged from CIOs and specialists guiding M&A integration to project managers and SAP integration specialists. The relative position of individuals within the organizational hierarchy reveals different perspectives on the strategies that were intended and realized throughout the MIS integration process. The interview protocol investigated several aspects of post M&A IS integration and strategies, with specific open-ended questions pertaining to overall business and IS specific strategy. This allowed interviewees flexibility in their responses. (See Appendix B for the interview protocols.) The interviews were recorded and transcribed, with one researcher listening to the interviews a second time in full to verify and provide clarity in the transcripts. The interviews were coded by both researchers independently to identify the IS-business strategy of each M&A, with subsequent discussion to reach complete agreement. We used the ‘‘explanation-building’’ strategy of data analysis presented by Yin (2003), described by Dube and Pare (2003) to be ‘‘a form of pattern-matching in which the analysis of the case study is carried out by building a textual explanation of the case.’’ This implies that it is most useful in exploratory case studies (e.g., Cavaye and Christiansen, 1996; Newman and Sabherwal, 1996)’’ (p. 619).

Each of these cases was stated by the interviewee to have had a successful outcome. An example of a respondent answer indicating a successful outcome is, ‘‘In the last analysis, it’s gone pretty good.’’ Another respondent stated, ‘‘The reduction of IT costs. So if you add the two together, the acquired company, that of course was a significant result.’’ To triangulate the interviewees’ assessments, we corroborated his or her report with external financial reporting, although the financial findings are not reported here to ensure respondent confidentiality.

Based on the interviewee’s response to questions about merger motivation and the tactics of their IT integration, we sorted responses into overall business merger strategy and IS integration strategy pairs. The collection of IS-business strategy pairings are presented in the findings. For the outcomes of the M&A activity, we defined M&A integration success as the respondent’s positive response to questions about the M&A outcomes. We conducted cross-case analysis to compare the business-IS alignment for each case simultaneously and summarized the perspective of IS strategy, business strategy, and alignment of the two and the overall outcomes. An example of an aligned strategy has a respondent stating, ‘‘I think that the goal in this case would be closer to the symbiosis. The direction that has been given to us as the integration process progressed is to take the best of breed approach from each IT organization . . . And try to become efficient and learn from each other and then move forward together as one organization’’, when asked about firm-level strategy, and ‘‘Yeah, I think this one is definitely providing the best of both parts,’’ when asked about IS level strategy. An example of strategies that are not aligned has a respondent stating, ‘‘So in principle it started out as a kind of symbiosis but in practice it ended up being somewhat of an absorption,’’ with respect to firm-level strategy, and ‘‘It was combination. We sort of jammed both systems together so that they kept some of their pieces, we kept some of our pieces and that was the part that was completed by the first target date. . .’’ about the IS level strategy.

Our findings include excerpts of our textual explanations of each case and how, based on these textual explanations, the results of the cross-case search for patterns. Based on the dimensions presented with respect to strategy, we looked at the cases on several different dimensions (timing, communication strategies, etc.) and reported similarities and differences within the strategic pairing groups and among the strategic pairing groups.

## 3.1. Findings

We present our findings in two parts. The first addresses issues of alignment presenting the outcome of assignment of cases to various pairings of firm and IS strategies. Within this we discuss those cases that fall within the aligned strategies and present characteristics and issues that we found to be particularly salient for each pairing. We present similar discussion for the observed cases among non-aligned strategies and concentrate in particular on the one case of transformation that was observed. The second addresses issues of ‘‘classic’’ versus emergent strategy in practice.

## 3.2. Alignment results

Results of the categorization of each of the 22 cases into the business versus IS strategy matrix is shown in Table 1. As can be readily seen, the majority of cases (14) were observed in aligned strategies, half in the RBM cell and the other half in the LBM cell with none found in the conglomeration model. An additional number of cases (8) were found spread among various non-aligned strategy combinations. No cases were found in four additional non-aligned combinations. It is likely that few if any cases occur in these cells. When the overall M&A strategy is symbiosis, the company would not approach the IS integration strategy as a consolidation, as they might overlook or lose some important piece of strategic technology in the IT consolidation, contrary to the overall goal. Similarly, a company would not likely undertake an overall strategy of preservation of the two entities and implement an IS integration strategy of consolidation. Alternatively, if the company were embarking on an overall M&A strategy of absorption, to preserve the two IS systems in the long run makes no sense. On the other hand, it is logical that firm level preservation and IS strategy of co-existence would likely occur frequently, but expect that potential respondents would not necessarily see this as an example of M&A integration and find their way into participation in a study of this nature.

## 4. Aligned merger and IT integration strategies

## 4.1. Reinvent my business model (RBM) (symbiosis and combination)

The RBM model, observed 7 times in this study, is the more challenging combination in terms of integration. As characterized by Christensen et al. (2011), this strategy involves significant rethinking of the positioning of the business in the marketplace. Where an M&A is meant to allow a pathway into a new line of business or gain value through owning the acquired company’s business model, it is crucial to know which resources (data and business process) to keep as is and which can be incorporated into the acquiring company’s infrastructure and processes ahead of time. In practice this involves actively determining which assets to eliminate, which to absorb, and which to combine between acquiring and acquired firm. A common mistake made by companies in RBM is focusing MIS integration decision-making on cost savings rather than future business value. Transitioning in an M&A integration too quickly from separate entities to a merged entity risks destroying the very value that the M&A seeks to capture. Phased timing stimulates greater interactivity and broader systemic decision making.

Issues in affecting the IS integration particular to RBM included the following:

1. Full integration of data is likely to lead to better support of integrated business and decision making processes and, eventually, greater synergy for acquired and acquiring organizational strengths.

2. Long term personnel retention is critical. Usual HR procedures such as interviewing acquired personnel and offering retention bonuses are helpful. If the acquiring firm offers a broader array of training and development opportunities, these should be made available to acquired firm personnel to ensure that talented personnel remain with the merged entity.

3. There are significant challenges in blending the culture of acquiring and acquired personnel. Because personnel from the acquired firm are likely to work together in the integrated entity, issues pertaining to formality, decision processes, aggressiveness, and the like can lead to escalating conflict or reduced performance. One approach is to demand acquired personnel conform to the acquiring firm culture, but this reduces the probability of successful retention.

4. Consistent with Graebner’s (2004) observation regarding success in integration following M&A in the high tech industry, retaining acquired organization personnel in key leadership roles is critical. Such personnel provide an important communication mechanism with more junior acquired personnel, allow quicker evaluation of acquired technical assets, and help provide diversity and perspective to the acquired firm’s leadership team.

5. Without consistent communication and in the absence of transition leadership, IS integration steps can lead to resources being unwisely consolidated or jettisoned in the interest of short-term.

## 4.2. Leverage my business model (LBM) (absorption and consolidation)

The LBM model, observed 7 times in this study, is characterized by elimination of all or nearly all systems from the acquired firm. As described by Christensen et al. (2011) this strategy involves careful assessment of assets and discipline in implementation. Nevertheless, it is not viewed as likely to lead to dramatic net benefits. When the acquired company’s resources are going to be used to leverage a business model, characteristic of the absorption strategy, deciding which of its components to retain is based primarily on cost savings. One respondent’s comments illustrate this scenario as follows:

It’s one of the key indicators, how much you’re going to save if you change the IT infrastructure if you merge two systems together. Sometimes it’s hard to calculate in monetary form but we did do those exercises. It was part of the optimization process and we have this methodology which actually allows you to do that. When you design the process, when you map it the organizational structure, and when you compare it with the old process, you can see where you saved like the document flow, the approval process, the time that requires to go from the request to approval to realization so this is some apparent wins that we provided.

Issues in affecting the IS integration particular to LBM included the following:

1. Timing the implementation of change. Moving too quickly and without sufficient investigation of the acquired firm can lead to costly revisions later. Unique business processes may require unusual MIS support inherent to acquired firm systems; rapid elimination of these support systems can entail difficulty and undue expenses. Further expense can arise in cases where the acquiring firm does not deal quickly and effectively with data integration. Even where the intention is to minimize continuation of the acquired firm, existing product, customer, and supplier data may need to be retained for regulatory if not operational reasons. Immediate efforts to simply hold the data separately and integrate at report creation time may result in massive translation costs (Stylianou et al., 1996); however, full data integration at table and record level can be extraordinarily taxing in terms of reconciling differences in format, definitions, and anomalies. Moving quickly can take advantage of expectations of change among personnel.

2. Although little to no long term personnel retention is expected with this strategy, it is important that a firm retain enough personnel to move through a transition period and any key personnel that support unique services or represent positive assets in the new integrated organization. In all reported cases, providing bonuses, sometimes including support in securing further employment, through the transition period proved effective in retaining desired employees. Taking the time to review the portfolios of acquired firm personnel against open positions in the acquiring firm can also lead to positive transfer opportunities. The corporate cultures of the entities involved in the M&A have a direct impact on the communication processes throughout the transition.

3. Clear and frequent communications to employees about ongoing M&A activity, primarily to lessen the uncertainty around jobs, processes, or timelines were also noted as critical in this approach. The value of good communication is widely known to be a central component of successful organizations; in the cases examined, the use of both face-to-face meetings and technological forms of communication during the M&A was cited positively by interviewees:

‘‘There was a lot [of communication} . . . we set up an intranet site, we posted jobs, I mean we tried to take the approach that everything was public so that anything that didn’t look right was at least logical to people. So IT kind of adopted that philosophy and we had our own website and we posted things and we had the plan out there visible to everyone. People knew at what point systems were going to go away and therefore those jobs would be eliminated. I visited a lot, you know, and held employee meetings and ‘‘get to know you,’’ gave a lot of food – people love to eat – we did team building, we did leadership transition sessions using our OD people so I think that corporately, by luck even, because the first one we ever did we had never really understood it.’’

In contrast to the RBM scenarios because few or none of the MIS personnel from the acquired firm remain after integration is completed, only the transition period is of concern in terms of blending cultures. To the extent that personnel from the acquired firms are invited to join the acquiring firm, it is expected that they will adjust to the prevailing culture.

## 4.3. Non-complementary merger and IT integration strategies

In all we found 8 cases that did not follow the aligned strategies. Of these in five cases, the integration of the IS strategy was greater than that of the firm strategy. This occurred when the IS strategy involved combination but the firm strategy involved either absorption (3 cases) or preservation (2 cases). In two other cases, the integration of the firm strategy was greater than that of the IS strategy. This occurred when the firm strategy involved absorption and the IS strategy took the form of co-existence. Additionally, we found one case of transformation which will be discussed in the next section below.

Just as the challenges of implementing the integration varied between different types of alignment, we found that different challenges presented themselves when IS integration did or did not exceed firm strategy integration.

In the two cases where we observed a pairing of preservation and combination, there was more IS integration than would be consistent with the firm strategy of preservation. We would expect separate continuation of each individual IS function, but in fact found significant blending of IS assets. In both cases, each firm decided to combine some amount of IS infrastructure to cut costs.

In three cases where we observed a pairing of absorption–combination, there was more IS integration than would be consistent with the firm strategy of absorption. We would expect elimination of one IS function with modest assets going to the acquired firm, however, in this pairing we found significant blending of IS assets.

In the two cases where we observed a pairing of absorption–co-existence, there was less IS integration than would be consistent with the firm strategy of absorption. We would expect the elimination of one IS function with modest assets going to the acquired firm, however, in this pairing we found continued separate maintenance of the two IS functions, In essence the firms found value in the IS assets that were not expected. One respondent explained that: ‘‘. . . their [business managers] lack of understanding about the complexity of the technology led them to underestimate the value of the staff that they had pur chased, in effect, so as they chased staff off, things became . . . things naturally became more and more difficult. . .’’.

Key issues in these cases, both those with greater and lesser than expected IS integration, can be traced back to little to no participation by IS leaders in due diligence prior to execution of the mergers. As a result the true value of the IS assets had been underestimated. Where due diligence was absent or late regarding the IS function, the execution of IT infrastructure was accomplished much more slowly than initially anticipated. As one respondent described this:

‘‘On the higher level, yes there were inventories that were made of the assets, whether they were resources in terms of hardware, software, and human . . . employees that is. . . so there was some high level assessments that were done and then when we got really into doing the transition into, assimilating into the acquiring company’s platform. . ..largely to compensate for lack of indepth due diligence. Once the integration plans were ready, then integration continued apace until completed, with many decisions made ad-hoc along the way.’’

Integration timing was also heavily affected by unexpectedly different cultures among the personnel of each IS function. At the same time, slow integration made it difficult to involve personnel productively in the integration process. One respondent illustrated this saying:

‘‘. . . the ones [acquired firms] we want to treat say as the holding company in that example, eventually they [management] do want to integrate. In my opinion is there is a big lost opportunity there because most of the acquisitions we’ve done, the company you’re acquiring, the personnel in that company are anticipating and expecting change. And that’s the time to get that stuff done. . .If you do nothing and say try to come back a year later, they’re re-entrenched into what they’ve done in the past and it’s very difficult and it takes a lot more time to get integration done at that point.’

Even where the firm strategies deemphasize IS integration, issues of incorporating the ‘‘best’’ of the acquired company were prominent. Comments included: ‘‘A lot of times it’s our way or the highway but this time around, they actually took a lot from us too in terms of best practices,’’ as well as from another respondent, ‘‘I think the acquiring company realized that we had a strong, not just a strong system but a strong set of employees that were very good at what they did.’’ In one case, the undervaluation of the acquired firm’s IS capabilities was seen as a lost opportunity. This is illustrated by the following comment: ‘‘. . .As a company, we tend to have blinders on and assume that what we have is better than what somebody else has. I’ve seen lots of examples that are the other way but they never get brought into the ‘‘mothership’’, so to speak, from the acquiring.’

We found that respondents in the non-aligned strategy cases put even more emphasis on the quality of communication and conflict resolution. As illustrated by one respondent: ‘‘. . .The strength of leadership was directly reflected in the effectiveness of the communications throughout the IT integration. Where there was poor leadership, even with good communication practices, outcomes were poor.’’ In most cases good communication was viewed as a positive influence on successful outcomes, but one respondent pointed out clearly that such communication has to be reflective of organizational realities. This individual commented:

‘‘So their communications was good but their execution was poor. . .. The follow-through and the execution of whatever they said was not good. In terms of just talking, they were good at that. They had meetings. You could go into people’s offices and communicate with them. It just seemed like after a while you just couldn’t believe necessarily what they’d say back to you so it wasn’t. . .’’

Although similar comments could be found among those in the aligned strategy cases, those in the non-aligned category were more prominent and forceful regarding these topics. This suggested that perhaps issues of leadership, communication and conflict resolution rise to consciousness when missing and become somewhat invisible when present. We would tentatively consider that although non-aligned strategies can be used with successful M&A, there may be some advantageous consistency between firm and IS expectations when the strategies are aligned that correlate with fewer interpersona difficulties.

## 5. The road less traveled: transformation

In only one case did a firm use the IS transformation strategy to extract the best of breed systems from two existing entities and transformed them into a single superior system. Transformation focuses on increasing the revenue or value of the IT as a part of the merger, rather than (or in addition to) cost cutting. The transformation case we investigated made it rather clear that the goal of employing IT to drive additional revenue and not simply cut costs, was decided pre-merger and communicated consistently to both merging entities. As noted by our respondent: ‘‘. . .that’s a lot of the strategic vision is that they want to innovate so they want to use the best strategies, the best knowledge, the best techniques from both organizations to produce the product in the future.’

In the short term the merger was conducted identically to a symbiosis–combination strategy, however, as the integration process dragged on, the opportunity was seized to upgrade both entities. The transformation strategy was unique in that while it started out operationalized as a complementary symbiosis–combination strategy, the explicit goal pre-merger was to go beyond that to a superior revenue generating group of IS. The vision for the organization is stated as: ‘‘Abolishing the IT of both and . . . then replacing with a brand new IT. . ..the strategic vision of the future all old networks are going away; there will be a new one.’

Pre-merger discussions were squarely future focused on the service platform providing the most value to the product going forward. Within the transformation, the MIS integration approach first chose the combination approach with respect to centralization of data centers. ‘‘We have support centers in high cost, middle cost, low cost areas – . . .They need to be centralized into one location and they need to run 24 by 7 and they're going to do that by essentially moving all of these centers into one central location.’

The pre-merger organizations were viewed as culturally quite different with unequal emphasis on documentation and paper trails. The acquiring (more document-oriented) firm the more document-oriented, used that documentation orientation to aid in resolving conflicts and decrease the impact of difference on the integration process. Having formal written procedures to refer to in resolving conflict allowed for all of the participants to have a clear path to reconciling their differences.

In addition, bolstering this successful outcome was effective communication to the staffs about transformation process. This invested everyone with a stake in the M&A’s outcome. As described by our informant:

‘‘. . .There was always short-term, the mid-term and the long-term goals that were communicated to us. The short-term are the quick wins and then the mid-term was to continue on with the quick wins while looking into the future and then the long-term would be the three-year plan. That was finally disclosed. . .The three-year plan is to give the clients what they’ve been asking for. . .communications have been very key so for example when they got to Day 100, they sent out communications companywide to show what’s been accomplished in terms of integrating with quick wins or sharing information across the organization. . ..They talk about their accomplishments and they also have an Intranet site dedicated to what’s happening in the company but also messages from the CEO of the markets divisions. He’s done a lot of traveling over the last few months going to every single satellite division office across the globe and trying to get feedback from personnel, trying to explain what we’re doing. It’s been very interactive. Nothing hostile about it whatsoever, nothing hostile about it so there are some basic concerns because there’s a lot of unknowns but they try to be very upfront, at least they give the appearance that they’re being very upfront about it.’’

As the merger progressed, there were no emergent aspects to the strategies employed, and the IS strategy executed was indeed transformation.

## 5.1. Classic versus emergent strategy findings

Our findings tend to support the emergent view of strategy. In not a single case did a respondent indicate that a strategy for the firm and IS integrations was formally constructed and used to guide decisions and actions throughout the integration process. In general firms used a project management strategy setting up various timelines and tasks then solved problems as they arose. As expressed by one IS manager, ‘‘. . . [we] followed the direction from the senior management in following certain time lines and also in having checkpoints all along the way where they [system integrations] all came together during different integrals (sic) of time. . . once that was communicated the groups were split from the top level to each of the individual teams.’

Consistent with the observations of Mehta and Hirschheim (2007), we noted some movement of strategy from inception through implementation of the integration process. One M&A started with an overall aligned symbiosis–combination strategy, but over the course of the integration the IS strategy moved to complete consolidation of systems. In other cases, the firms initially adopted an overall aligned preservation–coexistence strategy, evolving over the course of the IS integration to an IS combination strategy. Finally, in one case, the initial approach aimed at an aligned absorption–consolidation strategy, but the IS strategy shifted to combination when the acquiring company discovered acquired company systems it wanted to preserve in the merged entity after IS integration had started.

However, our findings also suggested attempts to use the classic model, but with significant drift based on facts ‘‘on the ground.’’ One respondent illustrated this as follows:

‘‘You start with what the business structure is going to be and then you build the IT alignment within that business structure and then the choice is for that business structure and the maturity of the business, do you align IT by function?... Generally speaking, if you want the best commercial solution, you align it by process. If you want the best political solution, you align it by function If you want the best cost solution, you align by system. . .And then there’s a balancing trade-off act about which way you want to go.’’

It was apparent in the non-aligned cases that even if the overall business and IS strategies were aligned initially, the IS strategy evolved throughout the course of the M&A activity, and a different strategy emerged. This suggests that the successful M&As that enacted DSAs were adhering to the rival conceptualization of the emergent school of strategy formation, and IS strategy as the shared view of the IS role within the organization (Chen et al., 2010).

## 6. Discussion

This study addresses the relationship between strategy and integration of the IS function following M&A in three ways: First, our findings regarding M&As successful at the firm level show that these can be enacted with either aligned or nonaligned strategies. This suggests that alignment, while it may be helpful, is neither a necessary nor a sufficient ingredient for integration success. Given the qualitative methods used in this study, we cannot make firm conclusions regarding the frequency of occurrences of each strategy pairing, however from this small cross industry sample it would seem that alignment is more prevalent than non-alignment; that different aligned pairs are about evenly present; and that non-aligned cases fall into a variety of categories both with more and less IS integration than the firm strategy would suggest.

Second we observe that for each paired firm and IS level strategy, both aligned and non-aligned, different challenges specific to that pairing are observed. In the RBM case where firm level symbiosis and is matched with IS level combination, we see the criticality of planning for full integration of data, personnel, and culture. In contrast in the LBM case where firm level strategy is absorption and the IS level is consolidation, we see the criticality of managing the transition. Across the nonaligned strategies we see particular emphasis on quick recovery from limits of access to information in the pre-merger finalization stage, in issues of timing, and of particular need for good communication and leadership. This leads to the recognition that no single detailed set of guidelines for integration practice is likely to yield optimal results. Prescriptions that address issues in one strategic pairing can be expected to be offset by neutral or negative results in another pairing. Since our goal was to surface issues across the various strategic pairings, we cannot rule out the possibility that among the population of post M&A integrations the distribution among cases is much less balanced than in our sample. It is possible that future researchers would find a preponderance of cases in one of the pairings. Such a finding could render prescriptions for the less frequent pairings statistically non-influential, but potentially serious for those cases that do fall into the infrequent pairings. Thus we caution researchers and practitioners from drawing too definitive conclusions relative to post M&A integrations without differentiating the strategic pairing to which they might be applied.

Third, we examined whether instances of alignment (or non-alignment) derive from a ‘‘classic’’ approach where selected strategy drives subsequent decisions and actions or from an ‘‘emergent’’ approach where the solving of various elemental problems can be viewed retrospectively as a particular strategy. We did not find any evidence of firms specifically choosing a non-aligned strategy, but rather saw them drifting toward these pairings in light of the facts of the case as they came into focus during the integration process. Moreover, even with the aligned strategies it seemed more coincident that firm and IS objects lined up to reinvent the firm or leverage the combined assets than that the organization purposefully selected a strategy and used that as a basis for decisions and actions. As a result our findings on this question lean toward the view of strat egy as emergent. However, even in the non-aligned cases, the firms seem to start with an informal vision of acting in a particular aligned strategic pairing but shift relative to new discoveries. It seems, at least in this sample, accurate to characterize those successfully integrating firms as gravitating toward a logical strategic alignment but open to varying from it based on tactical needs as they became apparent. Given these findings, we would caution that the rigid adherence to a particular aligned strategic pair, given unexpected assets in the acquired IS or other discovered issues, might lead to less rather than more successful integration.

## 6.1. Implications for research

These findings challenge the assumption that successful M&A must adhere to aligned strategies. Namely, our results illustrate that differentiated strategy (even non-strategic) MIS integration can be successful. Many of our respondents (8 out of 22) found that alignment in the M&A case under discussion might not lead to optimal outcomes, as dogmatic adherence to an intended, aligned IS strategy can force middle managers<sup>4</sup> in the IS integration execution to make decisions that may not recognize economic value. These findings also support viewing strategy itself as a practice, where the quality of strategic practice is determined by actors (managers throughout the organization) and the situated actions these actors take, rather than prescribed action (Hendry, 2000; Jarzabkowski, 2005). This represents a departure from previous findings that IS-business strategies do have a functional integration in a stable firm and that companies perform best when the realized business and IS strategy are aligned in the merged state (Henderson and Venkatraman, 1992; Chan et al., 1997). This functional integration is necessarily broken in an M&A scenario, and the strategies might not be aligned at the immediate conclusion of the transition state M&A activity for the deal targets to be reached. Our findings indicate that a firm can plan for strategic emergence (Grant, 2003). In strategic emergence, although a structure of demanding financial performance targets and clear corporate guidelines is in place, formal or informal strategic planning systems are the mechanism for coordinating decentralized strategy formation, and decisions are made by those actors closest to the points of IS integration execution.

The respondent case interviews enable us to more deeply examine the strategic process that occurs in post-M&A MIS integrations. At the outset, if the respondent paid attention to IS strategy at all, there was an adherence to Mintzberg and Waters’ (1985) strategy process model, where IS and business alignment as a deliberate strategy (intended and realized) would be of the utmost importance to realize the full economic gains of the M&A activity. However, many respondents were able to categorize the actions of the business and IS integration strategies post-hoc, but indicated that their actions during the integration were driven by sequential decisions on a range of problems as they arose, rather than a particular a priori strategy. This observed evolution suggests that the relationship of strategy to execution is not always regimented and may respond to the environment, the managers (actors), and their actions rather than determine the set of integrative activities.

Therefore, while those leading the M&A might have adhered to Concept I of IS strategy (Chen et al., 2010) at the outset (the use of IS to support business strategy), for some M&A cases we studied, Concept II of IS strategy was followed. M&A integrators viewed IS integration as a mere cost cutting measure whose primary aim was to support business strategy, ignoring the capability of IS integration to enhance the merged entity’s revenues or expand profit making potential overall. Successful outcomes in cases where a DSA took place exhibited more emergence in the strategy formation and execution process of IS management than rigid adherence to Concept II of IS strategy of overall M&A leadership from the start.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

Our findings show that IS alignment is not a sufficient or necessary prerequisite for effective integration in all circumstances. The findings also suggest that much of the strategy is emergent rather than pre-determined by the organization. This has a number of implications. First, though not a determiner of success, alignment does not preclude it either. An aligned strategy may be helpful in coordinating IS with firm decisions and actions whether this is done as an explicit stated plan or simply by repeatedly applying the same logic of reinventing the firm or leveraging assets as decisions and actions present themselves. Second, firm and IS leaders apparently do remain open to drifting from the logic of alignment when circumstances suggest that they do so. It appears that in the detailed investigation of the newly acquired assets of the firm there is sometimes the potential for taking advantage of unexpected value. It makes little sense to throw this away for the purpose of insuring strategic alignment. In at least one case, where a firm started with a combination strategy but eventually moved to a consolidation strategy, the assets of the acquired firm were less valuable or more costly to integrate resulting in the eventual release of most of the acquired IS assets. Even though the result was less IS than firm level integration, the source of non-alignment came from observing the actual rather than projected nature of the acquired firm’s IS assets. Leaders would be well advised to include IS in the due diligence process at the first opportunity given legal and insider constraints.

Most important for practice, however, is the observation that specific integration tactics should vary in their utility with the particular strategic pairing. Some prescriptions, like having strong leadership, attending to cultural differences, and carefully reviewing personnel opportunities are probably helpful admonitions in most managerial circumstances. However, the challenges of each aligned strategic pairing and the non-aligned pairings vary. Firms likely to engage in many M&A activities will likely benefit from tailoring their strategic pairing to the particulars of each occasion. Where such M&A activities are less frequent, leaders may want to steep themselves in understanding the challenges of each pairing, select the aligned strategy that looks most reasonable for their circumstance, then stay open to shifting strategies when and if new knowledge about the nature of the acquired IS assets changes.

## 7. Conclusion

Our results show that various firm-IS strategy pairings including both aligned and non-aligned can be observed in successful firm M&A integration. Where the non-aligned strategies were observed, they were generally a result of lack of inclusion in the due diligence process and the resulting poor estimation of the acquired IS assets. In successful M&A integrations, firms evolved their strategic pairings to take advantage of such surprises. Tactical actions supportive integration varied by strategic pairing suggesting that managers can benefit by being aware of the strategic pairing they are undertaking and planning more specifically to that end. The study also surfaced some evidence for the emergent strategy formation in M&A integration as a valuable alternative to the a priori formal planning view. This study illustrates that M&A success can result from multiple avenues and degrees of alignment and, thus, draws attention to promising but little-studied transformation practices and approaches.

This study has a number of limitations. Data were gathered from a diverse set of respondents spanning various industries, organizational levels and responsibilities, and acquired and acquiring firm origin. Although this approach has provided a rich set of insights, it limits the consistency of viewpoints across cases. Respondents were not randomly selected in large measure due to the proprietary and sensitive nature of the questions asked and information discussed. As a result we have little mechanism to make inferences about the entire population of integrations following M&As but rather focus on observations of the range and possibilities presented by the data. As noted above, the set of respondents was quite diverse and, to our knowledge, were not systematically representing any particular perspective, Most of respondents worked for companies in the US which might restrict the range of their views to those consistent with norms of management in the US. All of the cases were based on successful firm M&A experience. It is possible that the study of unsuccessful M&A might further distinguish the relative value of various strategic pairings. We did not observe any cases in a number of the non-aligned pairings. We do expect that many examples would populate the preservation–coexistence pair, for example. Future study may benefit from examining cases that populate these cells, assuming that they can be found. As the study is based on participants’ recollections obtained through interviews pertaining to their M&A experiences, the data might reflect errors of memory. Because the interviews were conducted at a point in time, they represent the conclusions of the respondents which may have evolved as events unfolded. Actions taken tentatively may appear more certain (or vice versa) in retrospect. Although the study was not longitudinal, respondents frequently summarized events that occurred over months or years providing some longer range perspective

The research findings raise a number of unanswered questions. It is evident that the IS managers in our sample only loosely selected integration strategies and primarily responded to emergent conditions. Although in many cases we observed an alignment between firm and IS strategies, in not a single case did our respondents report a conscious, purposeful and sustained employment of alignment theory. As a result, we are unable to determine the specific consequences of consistent adherence to top-down strategic alignment policy. Future research that was able to identify and examine such purposefully aligned (or non-aligned) strategies might illuminate the value of varied tactics given strategic pairing. Moreover, it might show the degree to which such adherence to strategic positioning in and of itself adds or takes away value from the integration. The study of results under such conditions would likely further illuminate both the decision making process and implementation dynamics under varied strategic approaches. Our study focused on alignment in the case of M&A, but the results ought to be tested in a variety of related situations – the creation of new alliances and joint ventures, reorganization of department structures, and on-going operations of organizations as they change business strategy. Such testing would indicate whether M&A is unique or subject to larger and consistent forces with other major organizational change phenomena.
