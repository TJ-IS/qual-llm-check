# Agency or resale: Efects of a platform-performance investment for frenemy platforms

A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Platform competition Agency model Resale model Platform-performance investment Content service Hardware service

## A B S T R A C T

Competing platforms with hardware and content diferentiation may adopt a one-way compatibility strategy, in which one platform owner allows its hardware users to purchase the competitor's content service, whereas the competitor does not. Moreover, the platform owners can choose a resale model or agency model. When a platform owner adopts the agency model, a platform-performance investment may force content providers to incur certain costs to ensure enhanced performance. In this paper. we develop a stylized model to investigate the efects of a platform-performance investment on a business-model decision by examining a platform ecosystem that consists of a unit mass of users, a content provider, and two downstream competing platform owners. We find that, when the unit misfit cost of hardware is much less than that of the content, if the royalty rate is too high (low), the agency (resale) model will be adopted by both platform owners. If the royalty rate is neither too high nor too low, the owners will adopt a diferent business model. When the unit misfit cost of hardware is weakly less than that of the content, the platform owners' business-model decision is jointly determined by the relative platform-performance investment eficiency and the royalty rate. Moreover, when both platform owners adopt the agency model, the price is not always higher than that when they use the resale model due to the negative efect of the performance investment. Finally, we present the condition under which the content provider would be better of if the provider is granted the right to change the business model by one platform owner.

## 1. Introduction

In 2018, International Data Corporation (IDC) predicted that over 50% of large enterprises and over 80% of enterprises with advanced digital transformation strategies would create and/or partner with industry platforms.<sup>1</sup> Platform owners, such as Apple, Amazon, and Google, are increasingly organizing ecosystems to foster innovation by diverse outsiders [1]. Most importantly, these platform owners might finance themselves through the members in their platform ecosystems. A classic example of such financing is seen when platform owners sell their own hardware and software services simultaneously. This leads to the question that this study seeks to answer: “In this environment, how should competing platform owners choose their business models?”

There are three typical characteristics of platform ecosystems. The first, which is a unique feature, is a one-way compatibility strategy adopted by competing platform owners. Under this type of platform collaboration, one platform owner allows its hardware users to access the competitor's content services but not the other way around. We refer to such a relationship between competitors as one of frenemies (friends and enemies). In the e-book industry, for example, Apple makes Amazon's Kindle Reader available on Apple devices (e.g., iPad), whereas Amazon has not allowed Apple's iBooks to be available on the Kindle. Another example is the music streaming industry. Amazon Music is available on iOS devices on which the iTunes store is available for users, whereas the iTunes store is not available on the Kindle.

The second feature is that platform owners can derive profit from the resale model or the agency model. A key diference between the resale and agency models lies in who sets the content price faced by end users. In the resale model, the platform owner buys content from the content provider at the wholesale price and then sets the resale price for the content sold to end users. In the agency model, however, the content provider sets the agency price and sells the content to end users via the platform, whose owner demands a fraction of the revenue as a commission [2,3].

The third is frequent platform development or upgrading, driven by advanced technological development. For example, since Amazon introduced its first-generation Kindle in November 2007, the company has announced ten generations for the device.<sup>2</sup> For iPad, Apple has announced five generations for the iPad mini and three generations for the iPad Air and 12.9-inch iPad Pro.<sup>3</sup> Thus, the level of a platformperformance investment has become an important aspect of decisions related to platform design. More importantly, the investment may have distinct efects on the content provider associated with the platform, depending on the business model that is adopted. More specifically, when the agency model is adopted, the content provider will incur costs for improved platform performance. For example, in video game platforms, if the console's performance is too advanced for the video game content, the game developers will incur great efort and cost to match their content with the performance requirement. When the resale model is adopted, however, the content provider is not afected by the improved platform performance due to his upstream role.<sup>4</sup> Therefore, when a platform owner invests in platform performance, the owner should consider not only how the enhanced performance afects con sumers but also its impact on its associated content provider.

Most importantly, for a platform owner, the business-model decision is a long-term strategic one. Thus, we need to consider how the platform owners choose their business models in the presence of plat form- performance investment. Thus, the goal of this research is to develop a theoretical framework to establish guidelines with respect to business-model decisions from the perspective of the platform ecosystem for competing platform owners who face these issues.

In this research, we capture the above three typical features in the platform ecosystem and use a game-theoretical model to study the impact of a platform-performance investment. The platform owners first make their respective choice of business model (agency vs. resale). If a platform owner chooses the resale model, the content provider would then announce the wholesale price, followed by the resale price setting by the platform owner. If a platform owner chooses the agency model, the content provider announces the agency price of the content. Based on these conditions, we aim to answer the following research questions: What are the optimal pricing strategies of the content provider and/or competing platform owners in the presence of a platformperformance investment? How does the efect of double marginalization influence the relationship between the content provider's agency price and platform owners' resale prices? What are the conditions under which the content provider and competing platform owners pursue their respective best business model? What are the implications for customer surplus?

We find that the performance investments of competing platforms have diferent efects on equilibrium prices. More specifically, the performance of a platform whose owner allows the competing platform's users to access the platform's content has a negative efect, whereas the performance of its competitor has a positive efect. When the content provider has the right to choose the business model with one of the two platform owners, he should consider the efect of the competing platforms' performance investments, as the result of adopting the same or diferent business models depends critically on the performance investment. For competing platform owners, in the content-largely-dominant-hardware case (i.e., unit misfit cost of hardware is much less than that of content), the business-model decision depends only on the royalty rate. In the content-weakly-dominant-hardware case (i.e., unit misfit cost of hardware is weakly less than that of con tent), however, the negative efect of the royalty rate, intensifying competition in content sales, and the positive efect of the relative investment eficiency jointly results in a more complicated decision for the platform owners' business model choices. The diferences have important implications for the platform owners. When the royalty rate is fixed, if platform owners try to adopt their original business models, they should seek digital technology strategies to enlarge rather than shrink the unit misfit costs for their content. Finally, when both platform owners adopt the agency model, the customer surplus is always more than the scenario in which a platform owner who allows the competing platform's users to access its content uses the agency model, and its competitor uses the resale model.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we discuss the re lated research. In Section 3, we present our model. In Section 4, we derive the market equilibrium outcomes when diferent business model profiles are adopted. In Section 5, we investigate the platform owners business-model decisions. In Section $^ { 6 , }$ we provide the content provider's incentive to change business model when he has the right to choose the business model. In Section 7, we present implications and discussion and then conclude the paper.

## 2. Literature review

For platform owners, business-model decisions are long-term strategic ones. Hence, the first related stream of literature considers business-model decisions [4–8]. Abhishek et al. [2] show that the online retailers' agency selling choice can be afected by the level of crosschannel spillovers in which online sales play a role in the sales of a traditional channel. Tan and Carrillo [3] find that the consumer acceptance level of digital goods (compared with that of traditional goods) afects dramatically the retailer's selling formats for its digital goods. Hao and Fan [9] study the diferent roles that complementary consumption of e-reader and e-books play under the wholesale versus agency models. Stoel and Muhanna [10] address the efect of the technology-enabled consumer information sharing on a name-yourown-price intermediary who may adopt two alternative business models: merchant and agency. We extend their study by considering (a) competing platform owners (e.g., Amazon and Apple), and (b) horizontal diferentiation in regard to hardware (e.g., Kindle and iPad) and content (e.g., e-books on the Kindle and iPad) simultaneously.

More broadly, our paper is related to the literature on platform ecosystems [18,19], especially on the efect of performance investment. Several papers in the information systems literature examine a platform-performance investment from the perspective of a platform ecosystem. Anderson et al. [20] explore the optimal level(s) of platform performance for a monopolistic platform (duopolistic platforms) that face a trade-of between enhancing platform preference to increase consumers' utilities and reducing investment to facilitate the development of third-party content. Guo and Chen [21] examine the efects of strategic consumers' diferent purchase options on a firm's strategies for the timing and pricing of multi-generation product difusion. In addition, several papers in the supply chain management literature also focus on the platform-performance investment. Levi et al. [22] show that idiosyncratic investment in information systems is one of the fundamental factors for the success of B2B exchanges. Distinctive from these studies, we focus on platform owners' business-model decisions under a platform-performance investment.

The platform ecosystem in our research is a classic two-sided market. Hence, the second related stream involves the literature on two-sided markets [11–14]. Based on the unique characteristics of diferent industries, researchers prefer to study the specific properties of two-sided or multi-sided market platforms. Hao et al. [12], for example, model the mobile platform's in-app industry; Clemons et al. [15] mode the music industry; Li et al. [16] model the competition between e-book retailers with asymmetric cross-selling capabilities; and Seamans and Zhu [17] model Craigslist's entry responses to newspapers. Most prior analyses of business strategy choice do not consider the one-way compatibility strategy between competing platform owners, which we capture in this research. In addition, in contrast to the previous literature, we allow the platform owners to adopt diferent business models rather than the same business model.

We also draw inspiration from the literature on decisions about product compatibility strategy in two-sided markets [23]. Doganoglu and Wright [24] show that multi-homing is not a perfect substitute for compatibility. The studies about the one-way compatibility strategy for competing platform owners, however, are limited. Some literature concludes that the platform that chose to be compatible with the rival platform has an incentive to steal market share from its competitor, whereas the rival does not. Viecens [25] shows that the level of firm dominance plays a significant role in the compatibility decision. In contrast, our focus is competing platform owners' business-model decisions.

Finally, in our research, the content provider simultaneously sells his content in competing online platforms. In essence, the content provider owns two competing online channels that are operated by the platform owners. Therefore, our study is also loosely related to the growing literature on multichannel competition for a retailer [26]. Basak et al. [27] investigate the implications of showrooming and find that the online retailer might incur detrimental efects from high levels of showrooming. Kim and Chun [28] address the impact of the cannibalization and competition efects on a manufacturer's retail channel strategy. In contrast to these studies, we extend the stream of the lit erature by addressing the efect of the competing platform owners diferent business-model decisions on the pricing strategy of a content provider who sells his content through the devices of the platform owners.

Our model and analysis contributes to the literature in the following ways. First, prior research on business-model decisions of the two-sided market considers platform owners as pure competitors or cooperators but neglects the existence of frenemies. In addition, prior studies also neglect the significant role that platform-performance investments play in a business strategy choice. We model the joint efects of performance investments and one-way compatibility. Second, we contribute to the platform collaboration literature in the field of information systems by investigating business-model decisions, a new frontier of platform economics. Our results serve as guidelines for platform owners who plan to sell hardware and content services simultaneously. In this regard, Apple has released its Siri-powered speaker, HomePod, and will introduce its video subscription service through Apple TV in the future. Finally, the agency price under the agency model is lower than the resale prices under the resale model when the platform-performance investment is medium. This finding raises an important concern for policymakers when they assert that the agency model is inferior.

## 3. Model

We consider a platform ecosystem that consists of two competing platform owners, an upstream content provider, and a unit mass of users. In this case, the content provider sells his content to users through the competing hardware platforms. For ease of exposition, we assume the platforms are Apple's iPad and Amazon's Kindle, which provide interactions between the content provider and users. It is worth noting that our model not only suits the e-book industry but also can be generalized in any platform industry in which competing platforms, characterized by two dimension diferences, such as hardware and software (content) diferentiations, adopt a one-way compatibility strategy. For example, in the automotive industry, although Google is building self-driving cars, and GM has its own OnStar app, GM still makes its cars compatible with Google's Android Auto. Following previous literature [29], we adopt the Hotelling setup to model these as horizontally differentiated products. We use k to label Kindle and Amazon and i to label iPad and Apple and let them be found at Loca tions 0 and 1, respectively, on a line of Length 1. Amazon and Apple have two business strategies to adopt: agency or resale model. Therefore, there are four potential market configurations: (1) Configuration AA, in which both Amazon and Apple adopt an agency model; (2) Configuration AR, in which Amazon adopts an agency model, and Apple adopts a resale model; (3) Configuration RA, in which Amazon adopts a resale model, and Apple adopts an agency model; and (4)

Configuration RR, in which both Amazon and Apple adopt a resale model. We let $j \in \{ A A , A R , R A , R R \}$ represent the potential market configuration.

As the production cost for an iPad is larger than that for a Kindle, we normalize the marginal production costs for a Kindle and iPad to be 0 and $c ,$ respectively. For ease of exposition, platforms' standalone values before performance investment are constant and the same. Because the platforms' standalone values play no role in which option the users will choose, we normalize them to zero. We further assume that platformperformance investments are exogenous, and the fixed costs of developing platforms are sunk.

## 3.1. Users' decisions

A continuum of users is uniformly distributed along a line with Measure 1. For a user located at x, there are three options for the access of platforms: (1) joining iPad, (2) joining Kindle, and (3) being inactive. A user will select the option that provides maximal surplus for her. When a user joins Kindle, she either pays $s _ { k }$ to use only the Kindle or pays $p _ { k } ^ { \ j } \ ( w _ { k } ^ { j } )$ additionally to use the Kindle and Kindle Reader simultaneously when Amazon uses the agency model (resale model). When a user joins iPad, however, in addition to using only the iPad for s or using both the iPad and iBooks for additional p <sup>j</sup> (w <sup>j</sup>) when Apple uses the agency model (resale model), she also can pay $s _ { i } + p _ { k } ^ { \ j } ( s _ { i } + w _ { k } ^ { \ j } )$ to use the iPad and Kindle Reader simultaneously when Amazon uses the agency model (resale model) due to the one-way compatibility for the iPad. Moreover, the devices may difer in size, color, and texture, and content may difer in their interfaces and design. Given a specific product (device or content service), there exists a distance between the product and a user's ideal product. Thus, when purchasing a product that does not match perfectly a user's preference, she might incur a disutility: the longer the distance, the greater the degree of misfit. ## 4.2. Comparative statics

4.2.1. Content-largely-dominant-hardware case

In Configuration $\mathsf { A A } , p _ { 1 m } \mathsf { \Pi } ^ { A A * } \mathrm { ~ } ( m \in \{ k , i \} )$ increases with the iPad's performance investment and the Kindle's price, whereas it decreases with the Kindle's performance investment and the iPad's price. In Configuration AR, $\bar { p } _ { 1 i } ^ { \phantom { * } A R * }$ and $w _ { 1 i } ^ { ~ A R * }$ are independent of the iPad's and Kindle's performance investments and prices. The reasoning for this finding is twofold: (1) the demand for the iPad's e-book service is independent of the iPad's and Kindle's performance investments and prices; and (2) the wholesale and resale prices of the iPad's e-book service are independent of sales of the Kindle and its e-book service as well as the iPad. We also find $p _ { 1 i } ^ { ~ A R * } > p _ { 1 k } ^ { ~ A R * }$ . This finding is due to the competition efect of e-book services between the content provider and Apple and suggests that the content provider priced the Kindle's e-book service lower to increase the consumption of Kindle's e-book service.

In Configuration RA, we find that wholesale and resale prices of the Kindle's e-book service decrease with the Kindle's performance investment but increase with the iPad's performance investment. The reason is that the larger performance investment for the Kindle enables some users who would otherwise use e-books to switch to using only the Kindle. As a result, both the content provider and Amazon have strong incentives to drop their prices. In contrast, the substitution relationship between Amazon's and Apple's e-book services (the complementary relationship between the iPad's and Apple's e-book service) induces the content provider and Amazon to increase prices as the iPad's performance investment (price) increases.

In Configuration RR, due to the competition efect, the efect of the Kindle's price is competed away, resulting in the independence between $p _ { 1 m } ^ { \ R R * } \left( m \in \{ k , i \} \right)$ and $s _ { k } .$ . Moreover, $w _ { 1 k } ^ { ~ R R * }$ and w <sup>RR∗</sup> increase with the iPad's performance investment and decrease with the Kindle's performance investment. The reason is that enhancing the iPad's performance investment can make users derive more surplus from e-books on the iPad, which induces Amazon and Apple to have incentives to increase their e-book services' prices simultaneously. However, an increase in the Kindle's performance investment might induce some users who would otherwise use e-books to switch to using only the e-reader. Thus, both Amazon and Apple have the incentive to drop their prices for ebooks. Interestingly, $w _ { 1 k } { } ^ { R R * }$ also decreases with the iPad's price. This is due to the one-way compatibility strategy between the Kindle's and iPad's e-book services, and the reduction of $w _ { 1 k } ^ { ~ R R * }$ can make Amazon capture more users for its e-book service. Finally, the competition efect also results in the independence between the resale price of the iPad's ebook service $w _ { 1 i } ^ { ~ R R * }$ and the Kindle's price.

Interestingly, under conditions of moderate royalty rate, we see a hybrid business profile. Choosing two diferent business strategies also helps to reduce the competition between platforms and enables them to generate more profits than they could have if both chose the same business model. Not only do the platform owners face a lower wholesale price, but the content provider also can charge a higher agency price. Specifically, as stated by Proposition 3(1), when the Kindle's performance investment is relatively low, the agency model can derive a higher equilibrium price than can Amazon's resale price for e-books, whereas it also can lead to a lower equilibrium price than Apple's resale price for ebooks. Proposition 3(3), however, shows that, when the Kindle's performance investment is suficiently large, the agency model can derive a lower equilibrium price than can Amazon's resale price for e-books, whereas it also can lead to a higher equilibrium price than Apple's re sale price for e-books. On the one hand, the agency strategy can make a positive impact on users, which makes the agency model derive a lower price than the resale price in the resale model. It is intuitive that the resale model sufers from double marginalization, whereas the agency model does not. On the other hand, the agency strategy also may yield a negative impact on users, which makes the agency model derive a higher price than the resale price in the resale model. The intuition is as follows. As explained in Section 4, with respect to the negative efects of the Kindle's performance investment on equilibrium prices, when the Kindle's performance investment is relatively low, the negative efect on the agency price is less than that on the resale price for e-books on the iPad, which, in turn, results in a higher former price than the latter price. Moreover, when the Kindle's performance investment is too large, the negative efect on the agency price is less than that on the resale price for e-books on the Kindle, which, in turn, leads to a lower resale price.

Interestingly, Proposition 3(2) reveals that, when the Kindle's performance investment is medium, the agency model can result in lower equilibrium prices for e-books than can the traditional resale model. The reason is that medium performance investment of the Kindle alleviates its negative efect on equilibrium prices so that the double marginalization efect dominates. The lower price in the agency model also has been found in several studies [2,3,34], which implies that the agency model has a positive impact on users.

## 6. Incentive for content provider's symmetric or asymmetric business model

In practice, the content provider may adopt the same business model (i.e., agency model or resale model) for all of his clients. Using diferent business strategies, however, also may be possible. Most importantly, in industrial practices, Amazon.com has ofered third-party sellers the option to choose between the resale and the agency business models [40]. In this section, we report on our study of the content provider's incentive for the symmetric $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , same) or asymmetric $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ diferent) business model.

From the perspective of economic theory, diferential pricing schemes may dominate the same pricing scheme. Thus, adopting differential pricing schemes may enable the content provider to obtain more payofs. From the perspective of organizational behavior, the up stream content provider may have diferent contract constraints with downstream platform owners, such as keeping the same pricing scheme during the period of cooperation. When some external factors change, such as a platform-performance investment, the content provider has a strong incentive to adjust his strategy choice with the platform owner, which gives him fewer or no contract constraints. Proposition 5 provides the guidelines for the content provider about when to adopt the same business model and when to adopt diferent models. ## 7. Implications and discussion

There are some important managerial implications for the com peting platform owners, the upstream content provider, and the pol icymakers. Each of these implications is discussed below.

## 7.1. Implications for the competing platform owners

Based on Propositions 1 and $^ { 2 , }$ the platform owners should take the royalty rate, the magnitude of unit misfit cost for content, and the relative investment eficiency between platforms into consideration when they make a business-model decision. When the magnitude of unit misfit cost for content fall into the content-weakly-dominant-hardware case, the highest royalty rate does not indicate that the agency model is optimal for competing platforms. If they prefer to make business-model decision based only on the royalty rate, they should take certain necessary actions to increase unit misfit cost with respect to content until the cost is suficiently high. For example, they can change and diferentiate their respective content application interface for users, improve content quality from diferent attributes, or provide exclusive content or service from their own efects or those of the third party.

## 7.2. Implications for the upstream content provider

Based on Proposition 5, when performance investments vary, if the content provider has the right to change the original business model with one of the two competing platform owners, switching to adopt the alternative business model may result in a win-win situation. This finding is strategic and constructive for the upstream content provider. He should negotiate with the platform owners for more market power when the performance of the platform ecosystem is changed by the platform owner.

## 7.3. Implications for the policymakers

Based on Propositions 3 and $^ { 4 , }$ policymakers should be cautious when they try to determine the superior business model, whether the agency or resale model, as the levels of performance investment for platforms play a significant role in the determination above. When the performance investment is medium, the agency price under the case in which both platforms use the agency model is lower than resale prices under the case in which both platforms adopt the resale model. In addition, compared with Configuration AR, the customer surplus is always higher under Configuration AA, regardless of the content-largely dominant-hardware case and the content-weakly-dominant-hardware case.

## 8. Conclusion and suggestions for future research

The goal of this research is to examine the competing platform owners' business-model decisions between the resale and agency models in the presence of a platform-performance investment. The competing platforms with hardware and content diferentiations may adopt one-way compatibility strategy in which one platform owner allows hardware users to purchase the rival's content service, whereas the rival does not. Our results suggest that business-model decisions depend only on the royalty rate when unit misfit cost of hardware is much less than that of content. When the unit misfit cost of hardware is weakly less than that of content, however, business-model decisions are jointly determined by the relative platform-performance investment eficiency and the royalty rate.

This study has certain limitations that can be addressed in future research. The most interesting extension is to adopt an empirical study to examine the validation of the hypotheses that are alluded to by the implications discussed in the previous section. Our theoretical study is limited to exploring insights into the efects of a platform-performance investment on competing platform owners' business-model decisions. Thus, empirical validation for our insights is valuable. On the theoretical side, future research could consider the upstream content providers' competition and investigate the competition efects in the platform ecosystem. Business-model decisions, when all prices are endogenous, also could be investigated.
