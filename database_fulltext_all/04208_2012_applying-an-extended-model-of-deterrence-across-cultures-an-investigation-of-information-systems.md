# Applying an extended model of deterrence across cultures: An investigation of information systems misuse in the U.S. and South Korea

Keywords: Deterrence theory Management of technology Cross-cultural research Security countermeasures End user security Information security management Questionnaire surveys

## A B S T R A C T

Intentional employee misuse of IS is a global problem. Research suggests that security countermeasures can deter misuse by increasing the perceived certainty and severity of punishment for such behavior. However, the value of generalizing this work beyond Western cultures is open to question. In our study, we examined whether national culture influenced the deterrent capabilities of security policies, security education, training, and awareness programs and computer monitoring. Using U.S. and Korean samples, we found evidence that the deterrent effect of certain security countermeasures varied between the two countries, as did the influence of age and gender. The results have implications for information security management practices in global businesses.

## 1. Introduction

Today’s global businesses have offices in several countries and employ people from different cultures. As such, there is a growing body of research on the applicability of traditionally Westernbased management theories to non-Western cultures. Studies have shown that national culture has a powerful influence on individuals’ attitudes and behavior. In the context of IS, research results suggest that national culture influences the development, adoption, use, and management of organizational IS [1].

The prevalence of offshore outsourcing and other managed data services has introduced significant information security and data privacy problems in global businesses. In our study, we focused on a particular IS security threat that plagues organizations worldwide – intentional employee misuse of IS – the unauthorized and deliberate misuse of organizational IS assets by individuals.

Information security specialists recommend a combination of procedural and technical countermeasures for combating IS misuse, including security policy statements, security education, training, and awareness (SETA) programs, and computer monitoring. Based on deterrence theory, these countermeasures can serve as deterrence mechanisms since users perceive an increase in the threat of getting caught and punished. D’Arcy et al. [2] developed an extended deterrence model that predicted that security countermeasures influence individuals’ IS misuse intention through perceived certainty and severity of organizational sanctions. Empirical results supported most of their hypothesized relationships. Other studies rooted in deterrence theory also provide evidence that security countermeasures can reduce IS misuse. Although this work contributed substantive theoretical knowledge to the IS security domain, its generalizability beyond Western cultures is open to debate because the samples usually consisted of respondents from the U.S. Hence, our understanding of the deterrent effectiveness of security countermeasures across cultures is limited.

In particular, when comparing U.S. and East Asian cultures, differences in terms of Hofstede’s national cultural dimensions combined with the influences of Confucianism and face-saving in East Asian society have implications for deterrence theory. For example, in more individualistic societies such as the U.S., users may have a greater concern for the perceived severity of organizational sanctions because this represents a tangible cost to the individual.

We decided to adopt a comparative approach by testing a modified version of the D’Arcy et al. extended deterrence model with data collected from two contrasting cultures: the U.S. and South Korea. As prior research suggested that behavioral models do not always hold across cultures, we hypothesized that national culture moderated the strength of the causal relationships in our model. Our objectives were to:

## 2. Conceptual background

## 2.1. U.S. and Korea: two distinct cultures

In our study, we based our comparison of U.S. and Korean culture on the first four of Hofstede’s dimensions (see Table 1). His masculinity/femininity dimension was intended to reflect the degree to which a culture emphasized competitiveness and material success (traditional masculine values) versus nurturing and quality of life (traditional feminine values). We decided that this dimension was not closely related to the IS security domain nor was there a strong theoretical rationale for its influence in our model.

As shown in Table 1, the U.S. and Korea differ significantly on the four dimensions we considered relevant to our study. It should be noted that Hofstede’s findings were derived from research conducted nearly three decades ago. However, more recent work using different international populations supports the stability of Hofstede’s scores. Research also indicates that the influence of Confucianism in terms of harmony, relational hierarchy, and traditional conservatism is still infused in Korean society.

## 2.2. Deterrence theory and IS deterrence literature

Deterrence theory predicts that sanctions dissuade potential offenders from illicit behavior and that as the certainty and severity of sanctions increase, the level of illicit behavior decreases [7]. This is consistent with the economic view of individuals as rational actors who try to maximize their rewards and minimize their costs. Within the context of IS security, deterrence theory provides a theoretical basis for the use of security countermeasures as a way to limit IS misuse by convincing potential offenders that there is too high a risk of being caught and punished.

Studies that have assessed the influence of various security countermeasures on IS misuse intentions and behaviors found some evidence of their deterrent effect [10,13]. D’Arcy et al. tested a model that expanded on deterrence theory by including security policies, SETA program, and computer monitoring as antecedents to perceived certainty and severity of sanctions, thereby exploring the underlying process through which security countermeasures impact IS misuse. Results indicated that user awareness of each of the countermeasures was positively associated with perceived severity of sanctions, which in turn was associated with reduced IS misuse intention. Perceived certainty of sanctions was not a significant predictor of IS misuse intention, except for a subset of individuals with high moral belief scores.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Our research model and hypotheses were derived from D’Arcy et al.’s deterrence model, Hofstede’s findings, and research on Confucianism and face-saving within East Asian society. We made adaptations to the D’Arcy et al. model based on advances in the deterrence literature [17] and due to the contextual nature of our cross-cultural investigation.

First, we categorized security policies and SETA program into a single construct called procedural countermeasures. This resulted in a parsimonious model with greater emphasis on the cultural effect.

Second, we included moral belief as an informal sanction variable. Contemporary deterrence theory accounts for the inhibi tory influence of informal sanctions such as social and selfdisapproval. Moral beliefs are closely linked to the idea that selfimposed punishment is an inhibiting factor. Third, we included a relationship between procedural countermeasures and moral beliefs. The basis for this derived from law legitimacy theory, which posits that the presence of law is an indicator of the morality of certain conduct and thus may influence individuals’ moral evaluations of such acts.

Finally, contrary to the bulk of deterrence studies that included age and gender as control variables, we included them as variables of theoretical significance.

## 3.1. IS misuse intention

We did not view intention as a direct proxy for actual behavior but as a measure of a motivational state just prior to committing an act. Thus, IS misuse intention is a predisposition to engage in IS misuse. In our study, we focused on four IS misuse intentions: sending an inappropriate e-mail, use of unlicensed (pirated) software, unauthorized access to data, and unauthorized modification of data.

These four types of IS misuse are by no means exhaustive. However, we believed that they represented typical IS misuse issues encountered by organizations. Moreover, numerous industry surveys have cited each of these as security concerns for organizations. Other more innocuous types of IS misuse, such as personal use of the Internet at work, although counterproductive, may not pose any security risk. Further, the IS misuse behaviors that we examined have been linked to more severe security breaches.

## 3.2. Perceived certainty and perceived severity of sanctions

Empirical support for deterrence theory comes primarily from the U.S. However, Grasmick and Kobayashi [4] suggested that the relationships posited by deterrence theory may function differently across national cultures: such differences might result from the effect of losing face in Korea [5], which seldom matters in the U.S. Face saving is an inherent behavior used for retaining social standing when a potentially damaging event or situation occurs.

The threat of embarrassment stemming from the discovery of IS misuse is likely to be particularly salient among Koreans. But there is no obvious relationship between the severity of punishment and embarrassment. In contrast, people in Western (individualistic) cultures are less influenced by group norms and opinions and place more emphasis on individual initiative. Thus, the concept of punishment in Western cultures is based more on the personal cost of committing a crime, while in East Asian cultures the sense of embarrassment is expected to ‘‘set people right’’ and discourage criminal behavior [9]. We therefore expected users from the U.S. to have a greater concern for the severity of punishment for committing IS misuse, as opposed to the threat of embarrassment or loss of face. Hence, we hypothesized:

Hypothesis 1. Perceived certainty of sanctions will have stronger negative association with IS misuse intention for Korean users than for U.S. users.

Hypothesis 2. Perceived severity of sanctions will have stronger negative association with IS misuse intention for U.S. users than for Korean users.

## 3.3. Moral beliefs

For our study, moral belief refers to the extent that one perceives a particular IS misuse behavior to be morally offensive. Contemporary deterrence models often incorporate moral belief and posit that those who view an act as morally offensive will be less likely to commit it. Moral beliefs can thus be regarded as an intangible cost that encompasses the threat of feelings of guilt or shame for performing a morally wrong act.

The Western utilitarian view of ethical behavior deals with moral worth as either an act that achieves value (e.g., achieving happiness for stakeholders) or reduces risk (e.g., reducing pain or suffering for stakeholders). In cultures influenced by Confucianism, morality is the de facto standard for proper behavior and people obey the law because they perceive it to be aligned with their moral views and group harmony rather than because of the potential cost associated with the crime [22]; moreover, failure to adhere to moral obligations suggests an absence of integrity and carries with it social stigma. We therefore posited:

Hypothesis 3. Moral beliefs will have a stronger negative association with IS misuse intention for Korean users than for U.S. users.

## 3.4. Age and gender (social status)

Research in the U.S. suggested that younger people and males were more likely to engage in IS misuse. Within Korean culture, however, the combined influences of PDI and LTO suggested that the impacts of age and gender on IS misuse intention would differ from that in the U.S.

In Korea, age and gender are equated with social status so that males and older people (seniors) maintain a higher position in the social hierarchy. For example, most promotions in Korean organizations are based on seniority. Furthermore, the concept of filial piety (i.e., obedience toward parents and ancestors) extends to relationships between supervisors and subordinates in Korean organizations. This is reflected in Korea’s high LTO score, indicating the inherent values of ordered social structures based on status, and its high PDI score, indicating the acceptance of social inequality between the young and old.

Preserving the face of elders is also important in high PDI cultures, as younger people revere their superiors based on their acceptance of power distance. Moreover, Confucianism teaches that punishment should not be extended to anyone in the rank of ta fu (senior officials) or higher, and that seniors should receive lighter punishment given their social status [16].

It can be expected that older Korean employees feel that they have greater privileges and are ‘‘above the law’’ in terms of their social obligations to abide by certain organizational policies and rules of etiquette that apply to lower level employees. We therefore posit that older Korean users will be more likely to engage in the less extreme IS misuse that is the focus of our study. On the other hand, we posit that younger U.S. employees will also be more likely to engage in these types of IS misuse. Hence:

Hypothesis 4a. Age will have a positive association with IS misuse intention for Korean users.

Hypothesis 4b. Age will have a negative association with IS misuse intention for U.S. users.

Korean women have been considered inferior to men per Confucian tradition. Moreover, the high PDI in Korean culture stresses that women are lower than men in the social hierarchy. Women rarely occupy top management professional positions in Korean organizations and are conservative in the workplace compared to their male counterparts [21]. In particular, any misdeed by a Korean woman results in loss of face not only for her, but also for her male colleagues. As such, it can be expected that Korean women will be reluctant to engage in risky or otherwise questionable workplace behavior.

This is in contrast to the U.S. culture, where gender differences are much less pronounced. While females have been found to be less likely than males to engage in IS misuse, we posit that the influence of gender on IS misuse intention will be more salient among Korean users. Specifically, we contend that Korean females will be less likely than U.S. females to engage in IS misuse. Hence, we stated:

Hypothesis 5. Gender will have a stronger negative association with IS misuse intention for Korean users than for U.S. users.

## 3.5. Procedural countermeasures

These include security policies (detailed guidelines for the proper and improper use of organizational IS resources) and SETA programs (e.g., security briefings and courses). From a deterrence perspective, procedural countermeasures rely on the same underlying mechanism as societal laws: providing knowledge of what constitutes unacceptable conduct and increasing the perceived threat of punishment for such behavior. Given that security and privacy perceptions differ across cultures [12], we contend that the impact of procedural countermeasures depends on culture.

Based on Korea’s PDI, IDV, and UAI, we would expect Korean users to be more receptive to security policies and SETA programs. However, predicting the deterrent influence of procedural countermeasures across cultures is more complex. We believed that the influences of face (for Korean users) and individualism/collectivism are paramount in determining how procedural countermeasures influence perceived certainty and severity of sanctions among U.S. and Korean users.

In collectivist East Asian cultures, losing face is a stronger deterrent to illicit behavior than tangible punishment. Losing face is related to the discovery of the act and not to the severity of punishment. We expected that procedural countermeasures would have a stronger impact on perceived certainty for Korean users due to the social stigma associated with getting caught. In other words, Korean users would be more receptive to a ‘‘you are more likely to get caught’’ message conveyed by security policies and SETA programs. Hence, we hypothesized:

Hypothesis 6. Procedural countermeasures will have a stronger positive association with perceived certainty of sanctions for Korean users than for U.S. users.

Actions in individualistic cultures are driven more by the welfare of the individual and less by face saving. Moreover, Western cultures consider punishment more in terms of the cost (moral or tangible) to the individual as opposed to a sense of shame and embarrassment. Given the individualistic nature of U.S. society and its greater concern for personal sanctions, we anticipate that messages conveyed regarding the level of punishment associated with IS misuse would resonate more strongly with U.S. users than Koreans. Hence:

Hypothesis 7. Procedural countermeasures will have a stronger positive association with perceived severity of sanctions for U.S. users than for Korean users.

The deterrence literature supports the direct relationship between procedural countermeasures and moral belief in our model [19]. This is described as ‘‘normative validation’’ where legal sanctions increase the perceptions that a criminal act is morally wrong. Similarly, legitimacy theory posits that the presence of a law enhances perceptions that engaging in a behavior that breaks the law is wrong [20]. Thus, to the extent that security policies and SETA programs represent organizational laws, it can be expected that the existence of these countermeasures influences individuals’ moral beliefs about IS misuse. Ethics researchers have also discussed the effectiveness of organizational training programs as a mechanism for raising the moral consciousness of employees. SETA programs as a form of education and training can be expected to influence moral beliefs toward IS misuse.

Confucian-based cultures believe that education reduces bad behavior by raising the level of morality of the individual and his/ her social network. However, unlike Western cultures, Confucianism does not support the notion of formal laws as a means to enhance individual morality [8]. In terms of our study, this suggests that Korean users’ moral beliefs are impacted more by informal factors, such as referent group norms and opinions, than by formal rules and procedures conveyed via security policies, etc. However, these countermeasures are expected to influence moral beliefs of U.S. users. We therefore hypothesized:

Hypothesis 8. Procedural countermeasures will have a stronger positive association with moral beliefs for U.S. users than for Korean users.

## 3.6. Technical countermeasures

These include tracking employees’ computing activities and performing security audits. Deterrence research showed that monitoring and surveillance activities increased perceived sanctions for illicit behavior; however, most of this research was conducted in the US

Monitoring is unique because it overlaps with the notions of privacy and one’s personal space. Research has shown that members of collectivist societies have less privacy concerns. Moreover, monitoring techniques that reward or punish individual users are less effective in such societies [11]. Furthermore, the impact of monitoring is dependent on who is doing the monitoring and its impact is based on saving face. Koreans perceive their ingroup affiliates as more influential than outsiders. Computer monitoring is abstract: users cannot see who is watching and/or tracking their activities. It is likely that Korean users will perceive monitoring as invisible or nonexistent and thus outside the boundaries of their in-group. Thus, these factors suggest that monitoring will have less impact on Korean users than on U.S. users. Hence:

Hypothesis 9. Technical countermeasures will have a stronger positive association with perceived certainty of sanctions for U.S. than for Korean users.

Hypothesis 10. Technical countermeasures will have a stronger positive association with perceived severity of sanctions for U.S. than for Korean users.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Survey instrument

Our study used English and Korean versions of the survey instrument developed and validated by D’Arcy et al. (see Appendix A). The instrument contained four IS misuse scenarios. Following each scenario, respondents were presented with a set of questions designed to measure their perceived certainty of sanctions (PC), perceived severity of sanctions (PS), moral belief (MB), and IS misuse intention (INT) for the behavior stated in the scenario. INT, PC, and PS were each measured with two-item scales; MB was measured with a single item scale. Although single item measures are not recommended, the MB item appeared immediately following each scenario because it was contextspecific. Similarly, we measured PC, PS, and INT following each scenario and therefore measurement error should not be of major concern.

We created composite measures (i.e., indices) of PC, PS, MB, and INT by summing the responses to these items across the four scenarios. The theoretical rationale for a composite approach is that the concept of deterrence is built around perceived sanctions that pertain to a set of behaviors rather than a specific act. In this vein, we were interested in gaining a broader understanding of IS misuse as opposed to exploring specific acts. Based on the principle of aggregation, summing the sets of items across the scenarios provided more stable and representative estimates of the measures and added an additional level of generality.

We also conducted separate analyses by applying the research model to each scenario for each country sample. For each scenario in the U.S. sample, the sign and significance of the path coefficients remained unchanged from those shown in Fig. 2 with two exceptions: the path from procedural countermeasures to PC became insignificant for the e-mail scenario and the path from PC to INT became significant (p < 0.05) for the unauthorized access scenario.

For the Korean sample, the sign and significance of the path coefficients remained unchanged with two exceptions: the path from PC to INT dipped to be insignificant (p < 0.10) for both the email and unauthorized modification scenarios. The e-mail scenario may not be seen as a serious offense in Korea and thus the threat of getting caught would not be a significant deterrent. Similarly, for the unauthorized modification scenario, the contextual nature of our scenario may have contributed to the differential results because it involved helping a friend. In collectivist cultures, helping a friend may not be viewed as a serious offense; the shame involved in refusing to help may outweigh the threat of embarrassment associated with getting caught when engaging in this type of IS misuse.

In sum, while individuals may not be equally inclined to engage in each of the scenario behaviors in our study (see Appendix C and Table 6), the results of our separate analyses suggested that the relationships in our research model were robust across the four forms of IS misuse.

Following the scenarios and their accompanying items, the survey included scales for procedural (PCM) and technical (TCM) countermeasures (Appendix B) that were adapted from D’Arcy et al. The items selected for our study were factor analyzed and loaded cleanly on two factors, consistent with our conceptualization of PCM and TCM. With the exception of demographic items, all scales used seven point Likert measures and were modeled as reflective.

We had the survey instrument translated to Korean by a bilingual expert in IS security. Subsequently, a different individual compared the Korean translation to the English version. ## 4.2. Sample

The population of interest for this study was all individuals who use a computer as part of their daily work activities. Our sampling consisted of both employees from some organizations and parttime MBA students. The latter were full-time workers and represented several organizations in their countries. However, they may differ from general organizational users in terms of age, education, and experience; hence, we included the industry respondents to improve the sample generalizability.

For the U.S. sample, responses were collected from evening MBA students at two mid-Atlantic U.S. universities and employees in eight organizations. The primary contact within each organization was asked to send a survey invitation to employees in various departments. A total of 805 employees received an email invitation to complete the online survey; 269 provided useable responses (38% response rate). For the MBA sample, instructors in several evening classes distributed the questionnaires and invited the students to take the survey during class time. Ninety-seven usable responses were obtained (89% response rate).

For the Korean sample, responses were collected from parttime MBA students from three large universities in Seoul, Korea and from employees in two medium-sized companies in Seoul. The Chief Security Officer within each organization was asked to send a survey invitation to employees. A total of 346 employees received the survey invitation and 145 provided useable responses (42% response rate). For the Korean MBA sample, instructors in several classes invited the students to take the survey during class time. A total of 215 usable responses were obtained (97% response rate).

Tests for nonresponse bias were not conducted for the MBA respondents due to the high response rates. We compared early and late respondents for both the U.S. and Korean industry samples – early responses were those received within one week. All t-test comparisons showed no significant differences with one exception: the U.S. sample indicated a slight bias toward younger employees as early respondents. We also tested the models with a dummy variable to indicate whether the respondent was MBA or industry and the path from the dummy variable to INT was not significant in either country. Finally, instrument validation tests were consistent across the MBA and industry respondents for both countries. Hence, we pooled the samples for the U.S. (n = 366) and Korea (n = 360). Demographic details are shown in Table 2.

As shown in Table 2, the two samples are fairly consistent in terms of age, position, industry, and company size. The Korean sample does have a larger percentage of male respondents. We ran additional models for each country sample with each of the demographic characteristics from Table 2 as dummy variables pointing to INT (excluding age and gender because they are already in our main model). The sign and significance of the path coefficients shown in Fig. 2 remained unchanged for both samples.

## 5.2. Common method variance

We attempted to avoid CMV by ensuring respondent anonymity, counterbalancing item order, and placing the PCM and TCM items in a separate section. In addition, we incorporated a common factor into our model that reflectively consisted of all principal construct indicators and calculated each indicator’s variances substantively explained by the principal construct and by the method (see [15]). As shown in Appendix D, the average substantively explained variance for the U.S. sample was 0.73, while the average method variance was 0.01 (about 72:1); for the Korean sample, these numbers were 0.68 and 0.02 (about 34:1). The small magnitude of the method variances provided evidence that CMV was not of serious concern.

## 5.3. Structural model

To test the hypotheses, we ran separate PLS structural models for the U.S. and Korean samples and statistically compared the path differences [18]. The results are shown in Fig. 2 and Table 5.

The combination of PC, PS, MB, and social status explained 38% of INT variance for the U.S. sample and 47% for Korea. The combination of PCM and TCM explained 35% of variability in PC, 21% in PS, and 16% in MB for the U.S.; for the Korean sample these figures were 16%, 10%, and 3%, respectively.

Consistent with H1 and H2, there were significant differences between the influences of PC and PS on INT across the two cultures, with PC being stronger for the Korean sample and PS being stronger for the U.S. As expected, the influence of MB on INT was weaker for the U.S. than for Korea. However, the difference was not statistically significant and therefore H3 was not supported. In terms of social status, age had a significant positive relationship with INT in the Korean sample and a significant negative relationship with INT for the U.S. sample. This suggested that older Korean users were more likely to engage in IS misuse, while for the U.S., younger users appeared more likely to misuse. The results also suggested that females were less likely to engage in IS misuse in Korea, while gender did not have a significant influence for U.S. users. Hence, H4a, H4b and H5 were supported.

For the countermeasures, the path coefficient from PCM to PC was larger for Korea than for the U.S. but the difference was not statistically significant and therefore H6 was not supported. Similarly, the path coefficient from PCM to PS was stronger for the U.S. sample but the difference was not statistically significant and thus H7 was not supported. In contrast, the path from PCM to MB in the Korean sample was significantly weaker than the corresponding path coefficient in the U.S. sample and therefore H8 was supported. Regarding TCM, the path coefficient from TCM to PC for Korea was significantly weaker than the corresponding path coefficient for the U.S., thus supporting H9. The path from TCM to PS was weaker for the Korean sample but the difference was not statistically significant and thus H10 was not supported. ## 6. Discussion

The purpose of our study was to examine whether deterrence theory was culturally dependent, while exploring the crosscultural efficacy of security countermeasures in deterring IS misuse. Our results showed that PC had a stronger influence on IS misuse intention for the Korean sample whereas the influence of PS was stronger for the U.S. Thus, deterrence theory is not culturally neutral. The comparison of means in Table 6 also indicates that, on average, Korean users perceived the IS misuse scenario behaviors as less morally acceptable than their U.S. counterparts.

The impact of PCM on PC and PS was significant for both samples. This finding is consistent with the recommendations of IS security advocates who contend that security countermeasures are effective as deterrent mechanisms. Contrary to expectations, we did not find a differential impact of PCM on either PC or PS across the two samples. However, the differential influences of PC and PS on INT do point to differences in the underlying process through which PCM impact IS misuse between U.S. and Korean users. Specifically, PCM appears to deter IS misuse through PS for U.S. users and through PC for Korean users.

As predicted, PCM had a stronger association with MB in the U.S. sample. However, in Korea, such laws have a lesser impact on individuals’ internal level of morality. Instead, Koreans’ sense of morality is shaped primarily by informal factors related to their social network. Evidence for this line of thinking comes from the R<sup>2</sup> values of the MB construct in our results; PCM explained only about 3% of variance in MB for the Korean sample compared to 16% for the U.S.

The influence of MB on INT was significant for both samples. However, contrary to expectations, MB did not have a stronger effect on INT for Korean users. Thus, while different factors appear to shape moral beliefs for U.S. and Korean users, moral beliefs exert a very strong influence on IS misuse behaviors across the two cultures. This is noteworthy because it extends prior work conducted with mostly U.S. samples that found moral belief to be the strongest predictor of illicit behavior.

In terms of TCM, it had a significant relationship with PC and not PS for the Korean sample, whereas in the U.S. sample TCM had a significant relationship with both PC and PS. This supported the concept that U.S. users are more receptive to the consequences of monitoring, while Korean users are less concerned with being observed. Our results still suggest that monitoring has a deterrent impact on IS misuse intention for Korean users and this is achieved via PC. For U.S. users, this effect is achieved via PS.

Regarding social status, the results suggest that the impacts of age and gender on IS misuse intention differ significantly between the two countries. For the U.S. sample, age had a significant negative relationship with INT. For the Korean sample, age was positively associated with INT. Gender is not a significant predictor of IS misuse by U.S. users whereas for Korean users, females are less likely to engage in IS misuse. Moreover, our results suggest that Korean females are less likely than U.S. females to misuse IS resources.

Finally, based on the lower R<sup>2</sup> values for MB, PC, and PS in the Korean sample, it appears that the overall impact of PCM and TCM on INT is lesser for Korean users than for those in the U.S.

## 7. Research contributions

By comparing the underlying process by which security countermeasures affect IS misuse intention across two distinct cultures, our research emphasizes the difference that PC versus PS play in reducing such behavior; more broadly, it shows that behavioral theories developed and tested in Western cultures may work differently in other cultures.

The study also furthered our understanding of factors that influence the deterrence process. Prior IS research incorporated individual and situational differences as moderators in deterrence models; for example, Li et al. [14] found that personal norms influenced the effectiveness of perceived sanctions in deterring security policy violations. We extended this by incorporating national culture into IS deterrence models.

The study also supports the East Asian perspective of compliance: INT in the Korean sample was influenced more by social status (i.e., age, gender) and moral beliefs than by tangible costs in the form of PS.

## 8. Implications for practice

Our results provided evidence that monitoring has less deterrent effect in Korea. In addition, countermeasures work differently in each country. Managers in multinational companies need to establish regional security countermeasures. For example, security policies and SETA programs deter Korean employees through PC, and thus should be designed to stress the probability of being caught. Similar countermeasures in the U.S. should emphasize the severity of punishment. Procedural controls in the U.S. may be a promising way for improving the moral standing of the user population, at least in terms of how they perceive IS misuse, but they will be less effective among Korean users. Finally, managers in Western cultures should design educational programs tailored toward younger users, while Korean senior managers should endorse social conventions that discourage older male employees from misusing computer resources.

## 9. Limitations

A limitation of this study is the single source for the dependent and independent variables, which was dictated by our scenario approach but nonetheless could have introduced common method bias. Although formal tests revealed that common method bias was not substantial, the research would be strengthened by a longitudinal design with a lag between collection of the dependent and independent variables or through measures of actual IS misuse obtained from independent sources.

A second limitation is that we relied on published country scores to support the cultural differences between the U.S. and Korea.

## 10. Conclusions

We examined whether the deterrent effectiveness of security countermeasures differ based on national culture. Our results suggested that the influences of perceived sanctions differed across cultures and that the predictive power of deterrence theory could be enhanced through the inclusion of morality and social status variables. The results also suggested that Korean users’ awareness of procedural countermeasures had some deterrent effect on IS misuse intention that is achieved primarily through perceived certainty of sanctions. U.S. users’ awareness of procedural countermeasures had a deterrent effect via the perceived severity of sanctions. Awareness of technical countermeasures has had a limited effect on Korean users, and a deterrent effect through perceived severity of sanctions for U.S. users. The results also highlighted the strong influence of moral beliefs on IS misuse intention across cultures. Finally, there is evidence that U.S. users moral beliefs about IS misuse can be enhanced through security policies and educational programs.
