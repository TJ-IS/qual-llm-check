merging from rapid advances in digitization and technological capabilities is a new form of information Esystems development project: cyber projects. Cyber projects are complex, massive, and ambitious, often involving hundreds of academic, government, and industry professionals, requiring years of development, and costing millions of dollars. In our study, we examine how control is exercised in cyber projects. Based on a longitudinal study over eight years, we develop a process theory of the control of cyber projects. Initially we observe that project control is driven by the field, i.e., all of the individual or collective entities that subscribe to the general purpose of the project. This form of control is later replaced by a more bureaucratic form from government-sponsored entities to ensure that traditional project objectives are met. Once construction begins and the field understands the implications and promise of the project, we observe that control is again exerted by the primary project users in the field, complemented by authority-based control exerted by the government-sponsored entisty in the field.

## Introduction

Project management involves carrying out projects as effectively as possible within given time, money, and resource constraints. Over time, advanced information and communication technologies that enable greater speed, complexity, and global integration capacity have considerably changed the nature of projects and project management (Schwalbe 2013). Today we are witnessing further changes in projects and project management as advanced information and communication technologies have enabled organizations to initiate projects with significantly expanded scale and scope (Hanseth and Lyytinen 2010), crossing organizational boundaries in geographically distributed environments that span time zones and cultures (Carmel 1999).

This paper reports on our study of an important new class of such projects, “cyber projects” (Kirsch and Slaughter 2013). These projects develop technologybased infrastructures known as “cyberinfrastructures” (Atkins et al. 2003), which enable radical scientific innovation across globally distributed research communities (Berman 2008, Edwards et al. 2009, Bietz et al. 2010).<sup>1</sup> These radical innovations are expected to explore problems that have long appeared unsolvable, such as reversing climate change or understanding the evolutionary relationships among living organisms, as well as to expand the capabilities of science and scientific inquiry in ways unimagined at a cyber project’s outset (Kirsch and Slaughter 2013). Cyber projects present a heretofore unknown challenge to project management not only due to their technical complexity and size but also because of the diversity and nature of their stakeholders as well as the ambiguity of the projects’ intended outcomes.

Cyberinfrastructures represent complex and comprehensive distributed knowledge environments that integrate a number of diverse technologies (e.g., hardware, software, storage, networking, etc.) together with less tangible assets such as design processes, best practices, and shared knowledge (Berman 2008, Atkins et al. 2003). To facilitate pursuit of transformational research objectives (Finholt and Birnholz 2006, de la Flor et al. 2010), these cyberinfrastructures promote collaboration among researchers, link research and data collection sites, and connect research community members with common data, software and analysis tools, and knowledge repositories. The cyber projects that develop these infrastructures are often completed over the course of many years, exceeding the scope, size, and budget of most traditional information systems (IS) projects (Kirsch and Slaughter 2013).

The diffusion of stakeholders in terms of location, objectives, and commitment level adds to the difficulty of managing a cyber project. Stakeholders may be anywhere on earth and may include a diverse set of experts from various private and public institutions, including scientists, researchers, industry professionals, government workers, and students (Lee et al. 2006). Consequently, stakeholders may not easily agree on project expectations and may have disparate needs. Furthermore, once developed, ownership of a cyberinfrastructure must transition from the funding agency to the relevant scholarly field and be embedded into the way research is conceived, conducted, and rewarded (Atkins et al. 2003, Finholt and Birnholz 2006). Therefore, these projects demand the involvement, cooperation, and commitment of the relevant scientific community starting at inception. Engendering the cooperation of a large group of dispersed scientists with varying expertise to work on massive and complex projects can be problematic (Hanseth and Lyytinen 2010), but for cyber projects is nevertheless necessary (Atkins et al. 2003). In addition, researchers participating in a cyber project likely work on other projects concurrently, implying an incomplete attentiveness and potentially loose commitment to the cyber project, thus making successful completion more challenging.

Management of these cyber projects is made even more unique by the ambiguous nature of the finished product, which makes a priori specification difficult and potentially counterproductive. The overarching goal that instigates cyber projects, that is, to revolutionize the way research is conducted in a given field, is ambiguous in that specific outcomes may be unknown at the start of the project and evolve over time, making the process of developing an infrastructure for such outcomes uncertain. Consider, for instance, the Internet, the development of which began with the idea of enabling new research in distributed systems, but has since done far more, not only transforming science through new means of communication and sharing and collecting of data but also transforming the means by which commerce takes place and becoming a primary conduit of interpersonal communication worldwide (Atkins et al. 2003). This sort of radical transformation requires a cyber project with an unknown end-state. Current IS project management literature generally assumes the presence of a known and specifiable technological end-state (e.g., Maruping et al. 2009). Enabling radical transformations of science, however, requires that these end-state capabilities remain ambiguous; discovering them becomes part of the project itself. As such, the expectations and best practices for managing a cyber project should be distinct from those found in more traditional, smaller, and less complex forms of IS development.

In addition to their focus on transformational goals, cyber projects must meet more typical project goals (e.g., budget and schedule). Managing the project to ensure that dispersed stakeholders with a diverse set of priorities work together to meet the transformational goals of the cyber project while also striving to meet schedule and budget goals presents an enormous challenge. An important tool at the project manager’s disposal is control, which encompasses all attempts to ensure that individuals act in a manner consistent with meeting goals and objectives (Ouchi 1979, 1980). Past work has examined control of different types of IS projects, including in-house, custom developed systems, packaged software, outsourced projects, and open source projects (Gallivan 2001, Mähring 2002, Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003, Markus 2007, Rustagi et al. 2008, Maruping et al. 2009). The extant literature suggests that, rather than a single dominant mode of control, portfolios, combinations of a variety of control mechanisms, are used to manage complex tasks such as IS projects (Boland 1979, Henderson and Lee 1992, Kirsch 1997, Gregory et al. 2013). Collectively, these studies have provided considerable insight into formal and informal controls, particularly for projects in a specific organization with committed project participants and defined hierarchical relationships.

However, because cyber projects are a new class of projects that do not belong to a particular organization, and because they involve a wide variety of stakeholders spanning many organizations and locations, the relevance of prior findings on control is unclear. Moreover, as they represent a new form of project work at greater scale than previously addressed, cyber projects may require new forms of control not addressed by existing literature on project management and control. Thus, cyber projects present distinct control challenges and require creativity to foster radically new innovations, discipline to manage their large scale and meet schedule and budget constraints, incentives to motivate a large number of stakeholders from various organizations with varying priorities and interests, and flexibility to accommodate emergent requirements and new technological opportunities. It is unclear which forms or modes of control, or the appropriate mix or collection of control mechanisms in a portfolio, are needed to manage such a project environment. This study seeks to address this gap.

We examine the control of two cyber projects funded by the National Science Foundation (NSF): the George E. Brown, Jr. Network for Earthquake Engineering Simulation (NEES) and the Global Environment for Network Innovation (GENI). By comparing and contrasting the control of NEES and GENI, the results of our study yield a process theory of the control of cyber projects. Furthermore, our investigation reveals a new form of informal control, to our knowledge not previously recognized in other studies. We designate this new control type as “field control” to reflect the projectspecific groups and subgroups that wield it and that, in a cyber project, act as fields and subfields as defined in the strategic action fields literature (Fligstein and McAdam 2011). We find that the re-emergence of this form of control during the construction phase enables at least one of the two projects to make substantial progress towards its goals.

## Literature Review

Control consists of mechanisms used to “ensure that production work is performed in compliance with established plans, standards, quality criteria, and in conformance with organizational goals and values” (Orlikowski 1991, p. 10). Formal controls are visible and objective mechanisms used to evaluate and reward performance in an effort to achieve a specific goal (Ouchi 1979, Jaworski 1988). Like formal controls, informal controls are used to evaluate and reward performance in achieving goals; however, informal controls are less objective, generally implicit, and not always management-driven (Cardinal et al. 2004, Orlikowski 1991). Specific modes of formal and informal control are elaborated below, drawing from typologies described in prior research (e.g., Ouchi 1979, Jaworski 1988, Orlikowski 1991, Cardinal 2001).

Formal control is composed of mechanisms that are officially sanctioned, usually codified, and institutionalized; examples include written rules, performancebased compensation systems, and managerial directives.

Input control is used to manage resource availability, and tends to focus on human, material, and/or financial resources being used in the project (Jaworski 1988, Cardinal et al. 2004). Sample mechanisms include the hiring and firing of personnel, formal application and selection processes for hiring, and the development and use of a vision statement that guides resource deployment. Output control (also referred to as outcome control) refers to the use of mechanisms to manage product and service outcomes through establishment and measurement of specific targets or metrics (Ouchi 1979, Cardinal et al. 2004). Key performance indicators and project milestones are examples of output control, as are pay for performance, project plans, and targets related to schedules, budgets, quality, and satisfaction (Jaworski 1988; Kirsch 1996, 1997; Rustagi et al. 2008). Process control (sometimes called behavioral control) refers to the use of mechanisms to manage task activities that transform inputs into outputs. Examples include ensuring that certain meetings transpire and verifying that an employee spends a prescribed amount of time working on a given assignment (Jaworski 1988, Henderson and Lee 1992). Other mechanisms of process control include day-to-day supervision, management by walking around, review meetings, development methodologies, vision statements that guide task behavior, and standard operating procedures (SOPs) (Kirsch 1997, 2004).

Informal controls include unwritten mechanisms, such as shared values, norms, and beliefs, which guide employee actions and behaviors (Jaworski 1988, Chua et al. 2012). Clan control refers to the use of social interactions to influence the actions of others in a group to attain common goals, given that the group consists of individuals whose work tasks are interdependent and who have developed a set of shared norms and values that guide how they work (Ouchi 1979, 1980). Clan control occurs where members of the clan take steps to help govern their activities and progress toward goal attainment. For example, a software development project team, whose work is interdependent (e.g., a programmer’s work depends on the work produced by a designer), may develop its own values, norms, and expectations; the team then uses socialization to encourage compliance among its members. Sample mechanisms of clan control include peer pressure, socialization, and sharing of relevant “stories” that shape norms and behaviors (Kohli and Kettinger 2004, Kirsch et al. 2010). Professional control refers to the use of mechanisms that affect the actions of individuals or groups of individuals who share membership in a particular external organization (Orlikowski 1991). Clan and professional controls are similar in that each has its own group culture based on shared norms and values, which results in a common understanding and agreement about proper work behavior. Where clan and professional controls differ is the locus of control: clans are typically internal to an organization, whereas professions are groups that cross organizational boundaries (Orlikowski 1991). In addition, whereas clan control emanates from shared norms and values that guide the behavior of members whose work is interdependent, professional control flows from the shared skill sets, values, and accepted ways of work that emanate from attachment to a discipline. A union bus driver, for instance, is not only influenced by his supervisor in the transit authority but also by the rules, regulations, and social norms established by the labor union. Sample mechanisms of professional control may include socialization into a discipline, a “code of conduct” developed for members of the profession, and relationship-strengthening mechanisms such as attending discipline-based conferences.

## Control and Cyber Projects

Prior research has found that large, complex IS projects are controlled through a wide variety of formal and informal mechanisms, including project plans, peer pressure, target budgets, and schedules (Orlikowski 1991, Henderson and Lee 1992, Kirsch 1997). Such projects are controlled using a portfolio of formal and informal mechanisms, rather than through a single mode of control (Boland 1979, Henderson and Lee 1992, Gregory et al. 2013). Controllers build portfolios from preexisting mechanisms with which they are comfortable and familiar, designing new control mechanisms when existing ones are inappropriate for the task (Kirsch 1997).

The application of prior research findings to projects designed to facilitate innovative applications such as cyber projects, however, is more problematic (Sherif et al. 2006). These projects often fail due to lack of shared vision, lack of coordination among key stakeholders, lack of required knowledge, and/or inappropriate controlling techniques (Henderson and Clark 1990, Chi and Hausmann 2003, Majchrzak et al. 2004). This is due in part to an implicit assumption that goals and task activities can be precisely articulated and therefore that effective controls can be designed. Indeed, the traditional control literature tends to emphasize the goal-oriented, purposive nature of control (e.g., Green and Welsh 1988, Jaworski 1988). This view focuses on monitoring and coordinating task activities and, thus, emphasizes formal controls while de-emphasizing the role of informal controls in fostering relationships, cooperation, and collaboration (Eisenhardt 1985, Kirsch 1997, Cardinal et al. 2004, Kirsch et al. 2010).

As with other projects, cyber projects must satisfy a number of goals, including accountability in the use of funds provided by granting agencies, responsible use of resources, coordination of tasks among many stakeholders, and information sharing among a diverse and distributed set of participants. Formal controls, with their emphasis on specifiability, measurability, and documentation (Eisenhardt 1985, Kirsch 2004), facilitate objectives such as these. However, when cyber projects are initiated, there is a high level of uncertainty as to how the project will be completed, what the ultimate outcomes will be, and how to arrive at those outcomes. The ambiguity and uncertainty of cyber projects suggests that formal controls would be less useful for ensuring the achievement of transformational outcomes for which the projects are intended. Moreover, formal control mechanisms typically include traditional incentives to ensure performance, such as pay and promotion; these may be less relevant for scientists or other professionals for whom individual status and stature are more likely to influence reputation (Stern 2004, Roberts et al. 2006). Thus, while formal controls may be necessary to help ensure that schedule and budget targets are met, they appear insufficient for fostering attainment of transformational goals.

Given this apparent deficiency, project managers might supplement formal controls with informal clan or professional controls to achieve the transformational goals of cyber projects. Informal controls are rooted in social relationships in which individuals are embedded and to which shared norms, values, and ways of working apply (Eisenhardt 1985, Kirsch et al. 2010). Common goals and visions are understood by members of a group and shape the behaviors of the individual members. However, unlike typical contexts in which informal controls are found, such as internal project teams or an open-source project community (Markus 2007, Kirsch 1997), complex projects such as cyber projects initially lack a defined group with shared values and common ways of working (Chua et al. 2012, Hanseth and Lyytinen 2010). While multiple groups in a project may form over time (Karasti et al. 2010, Ribes and Lee 2010), the scope and scale of cyber projects may inhibit or slow the rise of such groups as individuals become involved at different times and their engagement may wax and wane over the life of a project (Karasti et al. 2010, Ribes and Lee 2010). Because the vision, goals, and objectives of a cyber project are emergent rather than known a priori, and because there may not be a singular goal or singular group, the use of informal controls in the context of cyber projects appears challenging, particularly in the early phases of the projects. Furthermore, given the characteristics of the individuals and groups associated with cyber projects, as well as the nature of their involvement, it is unclear how informal controls can be adapted for use in this context.

In sum, cyber projects are designed to meet many goals, some of which are straightforward, some of which are highly uncertain, and some of which are emergent. These projects require the cooperation and collaboration of a dispersed group of scientists and other participants with specialized expertise and varying motivations, not only for the vision, design, and construction of the infrastructure, but also for its eventual adoption and ownership. Controlling a project under these conditions presents a substantial challenge. Yet without effective controls, successful outcomes are less likely (Kirsch 1997, 2004). As with other complex IS projects, we expect that portfolios of controls will be used, and that the mix of controls will change as the projects progress. Furthermore, existing modes of control might be used in unexpected ways, reflecting the novel context of cyber projects. In addition, the novelty of these projects may yield new forms of control not previously studied. To our knowledge, past research has not provided sufficient insight to understand the portfolio of controls used in cyber projects, thus prompting our central research question: How are cyber projects controlled?

## Research Design and Methodology

Because our research asks “how” control of cyber projects is exercised, a qualitative approach is appropriate (Yin 1994). With a focus on theory building, we generally followed the approach articulated by Eisenhardt (1989), which includes use of multiple investigators and multiple data collection methods and emphasizes the importance of maintaining rich field notes, conducting in-case analyses, and searching for cross-case patterns as a new theory emerges and unfolds. Eisenhardt’s (1989) approach to theory building is iterative: It involves an ongoing comparison of the case data with emergent theories. Through these iterations, hypotheses are shaped and constructs sharpened as emergent relationships and constructs are compared against the existing literature.

Consistent with the recommendations of Eisenhardt (1989) and others (e.g., Orlikowski 1993), we chose cases that were similar in elements deemed critical to the research, yet provided variation in other elements that might explain observed differences. GENI and NEES were chosen because they are cyber projects of similar scale and scope. In both cases, a mix of project management approaches and processes was used to meet a goal of producing infrastructures to facilitate innovative and novel outcomes and thereby spark transformational research. The projects involved stakeholders from a variety of institutions including universities, funding agencies, and industry and who have deep expertise in a particular area. Neither project’s stakeholders were in strong hierarchical relationships in a single institution. These characteristics are important for understanding the exercise of control of cyber projects. On the other hand, we chose projects that differed in terms of development methodology and overall project structure, thus potentially helping to explain observed differences in the control portfolios. The two sites are described next. Timelines for each project are available in Tables A.1 and A.2.

## Research Sites

NEES. NEES is an NSF program created to increase understanding of earthquakes and their causes and effects.<sup>2</sup> The main goal of NEES is to promote collaborative research by providing shared equipment and resources among the earthquake engineering community to facilitate discovery of ways to reduce seismic risk and mitigate the devastation caused by earthquakes and tsunamis. NEES was conceived as a geographically distributed network of shared engineering experimental facilities. A major component of NEES is a cyberinfrastructure called NEESgrid, a large scale network designed to connect advanced earthquake experimentation equipment sites. A waterfall approach was adopted to develop NEESgrid. It was hoped that, through NEESgrid, the NEES project could integrate information technologies and systems with civil engineering facilities and systems to provide a distributed collection of next-generation laboratory facilities with which scientists around the world could collectively conduct innovative and transformational model-based experimental earthquake simulations. In addition to the experimental facilities and NEESgrid, the NEES initiative envisioned development of a university consortium (the “NEES Consortium”) to operate and manage NEES after construction.

Establishing NEES required the input and participation of earthquake engineers, computer scientists, information technology (IT) and project management specialists from industry, and government sponsors. Project management specialists managed the NEESgrid development project, while computer scientists and IT specialists were integral to development of NEESgrid itself. Earthquake engineers provided specific objectives and detailed requirements for NEES and would become the future users of its digital infrastructure. Furthermore, the earthquake engineering scholars were, from the outset, expected to assume ownership of the NEES platform and infrastructure after its construction.

NEES also required substantial funding. Major Research Equipment and Facilities Construction (MREFC) accounts were created by the U.S. government to fund very large projects with high cost and long durations. NSF was successful in securing MREFC funding, and maintained general oversight of NEES.

In this research, we focus on three phases of the NEES cyber project (see Table A.1): pre-planning, planning, and construction. Pre-planning consists of events before

November 1998, by which time a vision of NEES had been formalized and funding approved. By early 1999, NEES moved into the planning phase and the NEES initiative was formally organized. Requests for proposal (RFPs) for NEES construction activities were issued and funded. By late 2001, construction was underway, including 15 universities constructing or upgrading earthquake engineering laboratory facilities. In addition, a systems integration project was undertaken to develop the cyberinfrastructure, including data repositories, telepresence tools, and networking. Finally, a project management group was organized to generate support from the engineering field for NEES and to provide governance for the NEES initiative. In 2001, an award was made to fund the NEES consortium. In 2004, NSF deemed the construction phase “complete” even though no deliverables had been produced; construction of the infrastructure continued for several years.

GENI. GENI was created to establish a virtual laboratory for exploring future Internets, Internet technologies, and protocols.<sup>3</sup> Its cyberinfrastructure supports large scale experimentation of Internet phenomena “at-scale”<sup>4</sup> on shared and heterogeneous GENI resources among multiple users, provides users increased interactions and experimentation throughout the network, and offers an environment for innovative networking and distributed systems research and education. At the time of this research, nearly 1,750 individuals, including computer scientists, engineers, researchers, educators, students, and industry professionals have participated in GENI activities. A private firm manages the GENI project using agile practices.

As with NEES, we focused on three phases of the GENI cyber project (see Table A.2): pre-planning, planning, and construction. Activities before 2004 constitute the pre-planning phase. By 2004, NSF agreed to support GENI, hired key individuals to help drive this major initiative, and began the planning phase. Between 2004 and 2005, there was an attempt to fund GENI as an MREFC project, however, this attempt failed and NSF announced internal funding of GENI. In 2007, management of GENI was awarded to a private sector company (referred to as the GENI Project Office or “GPO”). The failure to secure MREFC funding led to discussions between NSF and the GPO about the limitations of the waterfall development methodology for building the cyberinfrastructure; these discussions culminated in a decision to use a spiral approach (Boehm 1986). In October 2008, the first spiral began, marking the start of the construction phase, which, at the time of this research, includes spirals 1 to 4.

During each spiral, the GPO has organized multiple GENI Engineering Conferences (GECs) and required stakeholders from current projects to attend. Conferences feature speakers from GENI management and major project teams and include status updates and prototype demonstrations. There have been 14 conferences at the time of this research (GECs 1 to 14). Early in the construction phase, technical clusters were formed for different networking technologies (such as router and switch, wireless, and optical); development activities were undertaken in these clusters to build cyberinfrastructure technologies. In 2011, an effort was made to begin rolling out GENI projects on a larger scale, installing GENI technologies on college campuses and offering GENI technologies to experimenters. At the time of our study, construction is ongoing; it is anticipated that three additional spirals will be needed to finish building the GENI cyber project.

## Data Collection and Coding

As suggested by Eisenhardt (1989), we used multiple data collection approaches. Multiple investigators were involved in data collection, data coding, and analysis. We collected data through interviews, observation, and archival project data. We conducted 26 interviews with 21 NEES stakeholders and 31 interviews with 37 GENI stakeholders, together producing 282 single-spaced pages of transcribed text. Throughout the data collection effort, we maintained extensive field notes documenting our experiences, observations, and evolving understanding of the cyberinfrastructure development projects (Eisenhardt 1989). Details of our data collection procedures are provided in Appendix B. We summarize the sources of our interview data in Table 1 and archival data in Table 2.

## Data Analysis

We analyzed our data by drawing on approaches described in Eisenhardt (1989), Miles and Huberman (1994), and Yin (1994). All authors read the interview transcripts multiple times to become immersed in the data (Eisenhardt 1989). To understand how cyber projects are controlled, we first identified passages in the interview transcripts and archival data describing control-related events. Initially, one author identified passages in NEES, and one identified passages in GENI. These authors then switched roles, identifying passages in the other case to ensure that all relevant passages were identified. Finally, the other authors reviewed and validated the passage selections from both cases. Evidence of an event generally came in the form of comments on an action that took place to ensure desired behavior, such as a progress review conducted by the project sponsor to confirm or adjust ongoing activities or an approach taken by the cyber project participants.

## Results

As suggested by Eisenhardt (1989), we first present in-case analyses describing how NEES and GENI are controlled. As will be discussed, our analysis revealed a new form of informal control taking place in these projects. We call this new form “field control.”

How is NEES Controlled?

NEES Pre-Planning Phase. In the decades before the inception of NEES, a number of seismic events raised awareness of earthquakes and earthquake preparation throughout the United States. The Great Alaska Earthquake of 1964 registered 9.2 on the Richter scale and moved an entire Aleutian island 18 meters to the southeast.<sup>6</sup> The San Fernando Earthquake of 1971 caused over \$500 million in property damage and resulted in 65 deaths.<sup>7</sup> This was followed in 1977 by the emergence of the Palmdale Bulge, an 18-inch uplift in the earth’s crust that occurred along the San Andreas fault line in southern California.<sup>8</sup> These events raised awareness of shortcomings in U.S. earthquake research capabilities, which were not state-of-the-art and lagged those found in Japan. A group of leading academic earthquake researchers coalesced around an idea to significantly upgrade the nation’s earthquake research facilities

Emergence of a New Form of Control. The term “field control” comes from similarities between groups and events observed in cyberinfrastructure projects and those addressed by the literature on strategic action fields (hereafter “fields”). In that literature, a field refers to a “meso-level social order where actors (who can be individual or collective) interact with knowledge of one another under a set of common understandings about the purposes of the field, the relationships in the field (including who has power and why), and the field’s rules” (Fligstein and McAdam 2011, p. 3). This literature stream explored the interactions of dominant and subjugated groups in the context of social movements and organizations in the public and private sectors (de Bakker et al. 2013). According to this literature, a field is comprised of subfields, which are defined by a common element, such as expertise, view of the project, organization, geographic location, and/or prior experience.

In a given field, one subfield is typically dominant in that it controls the overall vision for the field and establishes its norms. Because it controls the vision adhered to by the rest of the field, this dominant subfield maintains influence over the field in general (Fligstein and McAdam 2011). While some subfields remain adherent to the overall goals and commonalities of the field, other subfields may develop divergent visions and opinions about the field’s status and objectives and may vie for field resources. These subfields may also be in conflict with each other as well as with the dominant subfield. This may result in a subfield challenging for primacy and attempting to become the dominant subfield (Fligstein and McAdam 2011).

In the context of our study, we define a field as all of the individual or collective entities that subscribe to the general purpose of the cyber project. At NEES, this would include earthquake engineers, NSF scientists, those involved with project management, IT professionals, and the eventual users of the project after it enters development. In this broad NEES-level field, various groups of actors represent subfields. The earthquake engineering scholars, for instance, may constitute a subfield based on their shared expertise and common backgrounds. The core group of original instigators of NEES are a subfield; in the early stages of the project, they are the controlling subfield who own and guide the vision that binds the field.

Given that a field of individuals possesses a shared sense of values, norms, and purpose, is bound by the pursuit of a common goal, and crosses functional, organizational, and professional boundaries, we label our new control type field control and define it as the use of social influence by a field or subfield of individuals to affect behavior inside and/or outside the field. Field control focuses on the attempts of a subfield within a field to exert its influence on others with the intent of altering the controllee’s attachment to the field. Its mechanisms may include actions such as proselytizing on behalf of a field, encouraging others in a field or subfield to adapt or conform to specific ideas about the field, and garnering support for a given vision in an attempt to supplant the overall vision and objectives of the project and, thus, the field.

We see evidence of this in the pre-planning stages of NEES. The original group of NEES instigators evangelized their vision of the project to earthquake scholars in general. This is an example of a subfield attempting to bring others into the broader field by creating an attachment to what would become a shared set of values and expectations. We also observe that the initial core group of earthquake engineers and NSF officers clearly envisioned how the cyberinfrastructure would enable the earthquake researchers to enhance their scientific studies. At the same time, we see that the subfield of earthquake scholars, while in agreement with the overall project idea, did not share the vision in its entirety, and remained skeptical about the need for the cyberinfrastructure component.

Undaunted, the core dominant subfield exerted its influence and, through formal and informal interactions with other scholars, began to persuade them as to the importance of the cyber component for the future of earthquake research. As a result, the subfield of earthquake researchers began to adopt the dominant group’s vision for NEES. In other words, as a result of field control exerted by the instigating core group, the rank and file earthquake researchers modified their own vision of the project to include the cyber component as championed by the dominant subfield that had secured NSF funding.

With GENI support growing among scholars and NSF, there were ongoing discussions about possibly funding GENI as an MREFC project, as was done with NEES. Again, MREFC funding would require the use of the waterfall methodology, which was seen as a source of some of the contentiousness that accompanied the NEES project. GENI Planning Phase. During the planning phase, we observe formal and informal mechanisms in the control portfolio (see Table 3). To avoid repeating the contentiousness and chaos of the NEES project, NSF decided not to solicit academic groups to manage the project. Instead, they funded an external private firm to establish the GPO and manage development of the cyberinfrastructure. Though the GPO was responsible for day-to-day management, NSF established a cooperative agreement between itself and the GPO; this form of input control ensured that the GPO worked closely with NSF on decisions and actions during the course of the project.

GPO employees had many years of project management experience, including large scale project development and network engineering. This breadth of expertise, along with knowledge of the NEES project experience, led to the decision to manage GENI construction using a series of spirals, rather than the waterfall approach. Each spiral was designed to last one year with funded project teams as the major working units. At the end of a spiral, each team’s performance would be reviewed and evaluated as a basis for funding decisions in the coming spiral. Successful teams might receive continued funding for further participation in the following spirals; unsuccessful projects might be dropped. Meanwhile, goals for the subsequent spiral would be determined and a solicitation announced. New component projects could be added to the cyberinfrastructure based on evaluation of the proposals in response to solicitations. The GPO intended to fund a number of projects in each spiral. Moreover, each project would belong to a technical cluster or platform. Although multiple clusters would be formed, each representing a different technical approach for the infrastructure, not all clusters would be represented in the final product.

The GPO clearly formalized and broadcast the legitimate behaviors and processes in the new field as they related to resource allocation and acquisition. Thus, the formal controls used during the planning phase (funding, project structure, and spiral methodology) set up the structures and funding approaches to encourage competition and reduce risk

Quarterly reports and detailed GEC reports were required and posted on project wikis.

These individuals became information gatekeepers who assisted many other projects, and thereby acquired power and legitimacy in the cluster and GENI in general.

## Discussion

Consistent with Eisenhardt (1989), we begin our discussion with a cross-case comparison of NEES and GENI, identifying patterns related to the exercise of control in cyber projects.

During the pre-planning phase, the portfolios of control observed in NEES and GENI are quite similar (see Table 5). In both projects, we observe fields emerging around a core group of initial academic actors. Seeing the need for large-scale scholarly advancement in the respective disciplines, these actors developed visionary goals that required cyberinfrastructure components to succeed. By creating the vision for their disciplines, these scholars also create a new field. They then exercise what we term field control by encouraging excitement both among those in their discipline as well as those at NSF and other government agencies that could fund the project. In the pre-planning phases of both projects, we also see NSF exercising input control through establishment of oversight groups.

This field control, which we first observed in the pre-planning phase of NEES, is a new form of informal control. As noted in Table 4, it differs from clan and professional control in three important ways related to the locus of control, the target of control, and the way in which the relevant groups are bonded. For the locus of control, clan control occurs in an organization working group (Ouchi 1979, 1980), while professional control occurs in a profession across organizations (Orlikowski 1991). Field control, by contrast, occurs inside and outside organizational and formal groups, spanning organizations, geographies, and hierarchies; it is marked by the emergence and evolution of fields and subfields, ad hoc groups that share an understanding of a unifying goal. The target of control for clan control is the members of the clan (Ouchi 1979, 1980), while professional control targets participants in a professional discipline (Orlikowski 1991). Field control differs here in that it targets actors inside and outside the field. Initial project instigators on NEES and GENI exerted field control, effectively folding NSF into their field; later, for NEES, a subfield of academics in the broader NEES field asserted themselves to gain adherents to their own project vision. Finally, members of a clan, where clan control is exercised, are bound by task interdependencies among clan members (Ouchi 1979, 1980), while members of a profession, where professional control is exercised, are bound by members’ attachment to the professional discipline (Orlikowski 1991). Field control contrasts these in that fields and subfield membership emerge based on shared understandings of a unified purpose and in that membership in fields and subfields is fluid and potentially ever-changing.

During the planning phase, both projects rely most on formal controls, supplemented by a few informal controls. As summarized in Tables 3 and 5, NEES and GENI include a number of mechanisms that focus on formally defined inputs (e.g., resource allocation, personnel selection, termination) and outputs (e.g., project deliverables, metrics). Establishment of oversight groups enabled formalization of individual expectations in both projects, while process mechanisms such as meetings and methodologies are also prevalent. While there are some informal controls present (clan, professional), the transition from pre-planning to the planning phase is, in both projects, accompanied by a shift from informal field control mechanisms to more formal controls. This reflects the shift of the locus of control from the initial, academic project instigators to NSF and NSF-sponsored governance groups. This phenomenon echoes what has been proposed in strategic action fields research, which argues that when a field becomes stable, the subfield that controls the project vision will establish governance groups to ensure that the field as a whole can be monitored and kept in adherence with the current vision of the field (Fligstein and McAdam 2011). Having obtained control of the field, the subfield then engages in a bureaucratic form of governance based on traditional/formal control methods to discourage change.

As the projects moved from the planning to the construction phase, control remains focused in the NSF-sponsored governance groups. Heavy reliance on formal mechanisms of control, seen in both projects (see Tables 3 and 5), was directed at achieving traditional project performance objectives such as timeliness and meeting of specifications. In fact, during this phase and in both projects, many additional formal control mechanisms were added due to performance issues (NEES) or to the spirals’ vast numbers of projects (GENI). During the construction phase, however, the two projects diverge, with field control reemerging at NEES, but not evident at GENI. Given the expectation that transformative research can only happen where the scholarly community takes owenership of the infrastructure, the emergence of field control in NEES appears to be a positive event in terms of meeting the project’s objective. By contrast, the lack of strong field control at GENI (at the time of this writing) may impede the long-term use and transformativeness of GENI, despite the impressive progress that has been made to build and use the infrastructure to date.

## A Process Theory of the Control of Cyber Projects

Figure 1 captures the essence of our proposed theory, which depicts how control evolves and is exercised over the life of cyber projects. The theory posits that cyber projects are initiated through the control exerted by the field in the pre-planning stage, as evidenced in each project. This enables a core group of individuals to spark interest among relevant parties and then secure the backing of those in key government agencies to fund the projects. Together, this core group establishes the overall project vision and secures funding for its development.

Once the project is formally initiated, planning phase control largely reflects the formal methods of project management and is thus characterized by a portfolio based on the authority of the centralized management group that controls project resources (in these cases, NSF and/or NSF-sponsored entities). During this phase, leaders of the respective academic communities become disengaged; the centralized project management groups fill the power vacuum created by the original core group, which has curtailed its involvement. The authority-based control mechanisms facilitate achievement of traditional project goals (e.g., budget and schedule) and direct the work of the newly created field, which, at this stage, is not primarily self-directed.

As the projects progress through planning, additional formal mechanisms are added. By the time they reach the construction phase, more process controls are added; the control used by the NSF-backed governance group is composed of many types of formal control mechanisms. Unlike traditional projects, however, cyber projects are designed to achieve transformational goals. Their success is not just a matter of satisfying traditional project budget, scope, and schedule goals. To succeed, the field must embrace the cyberinfrastructure to transform the way in which research is conducted and address the grand challenges of its discipline. During the construction phase, the field begins to understand the vision and promise of the cyberinfrastructure. This enlightenment can enable researchers to exercise field control to influence those associated with the cyber project to assist in the successful achievement of the project’s objectives. Thus, our theory posits that as the cyber project moves into construction, the project control exerted by the governance group is augmented with field-based control as researchers take ownership of the infrastructure and refocus the project based on research-specific (rather than project-specific) needs, thereby enabling the performance of transformational research.

As summarized in Table 5 and depicted in Figure 1, not only are cyber projects controlled with multiple control mechanisms, but there are “layers” of control with multiple controllers exercising control during each project phase. In pre-planning, the initial core group seems to be the dominant controller as the projects would not have existed without the vision and drive of these academic leaders. During this phase, however, we also see NSF exercising input control to influence the shape and oversight of the project. Similarly, during planning there are layers of control, which are dominated by NSF and NSF-sponsored organizations (e.g., NEES Inc., GPO) exercising formal controls. Complementing the layer of formal controls used by NSF and others is a layer of informal controls exerted by professions (NEES) and clans (NEES and GENI), although we see no evidence of field control during the planning phase. We again see a pattern of layers in the construction phase, when NSF and the SI Teams and GPO exercise formal controls, while field control (an informal layer) re-emerges.

Finally, Figure 1 illustrates the evolution of field involvement in cyber projects. In the earliest days of both projects, the projects’ core group of instigators kindled great enthusiasm and excitement and advocated for the projects. Thus, this initial core group of field leaders (including NSF academics) played a critical role in envisioning and initiating the projects. However, once project ideas were formalized into projects complete with structures, procedures, policies, and control, involvement among the original group of instigators diminished. NSF then assumed project primacy and selected oversight groups to move the project forward; at this point, there was no substantial complementary influence of academic members of the field. The resulting authority-based control of the project by NSFselected governance groups helps ensure that project resources are appropriately used. Although this allowed the project to achieve typical management objectives, it may also have dampened the interest of the stakeholders who must be engaged to ensure future project success. As others have argued, too many or inappropriate use of controls can damage relationships among stakeholders, diminish individual capacity for reflection, and result in negative outcomes such as low morale or lack of engagement (Markus 2000, Orlikowski 1991). In NEES, researchers respond to the chaos that authority-based control mechanisms could not forestall, which results in successful achievement of the project’s original goals. At the time of our investigation, GENI had yet to fully meet its original objective of transformational research enablement.

## Implications

One of the most significant findings from our study is the idea that a project itself can create fields and subfields among those involved. Building on this, and based on how field control was used in the two projects studied, it appears this development may also lead to an empowering of subfields that enables them to assume project leadership, as occurred in NEES. Given that this outcome is not common in more traditional IT development projects, we propose that it may be a function of the characteristics of cyberinfrastructure projects, in particular their scale, diverse and widely distributed participant set, and the lack of hierarchical legitimacy due to participants not necessarily being beholdent to the original project instigators or those overseeing project development.

Given this, our findings may also be applicable to other types of community-based IT development projects where these factors may exist. In particular, open source software development presents a similar lack of hierarchical legitimacy and a dispersed set of participants. These factors may explain why, in such projects, subsets of developers become disenchanted, vie to wrest primacy from existing project leaders, and/or opt to leave a project and head down a different ideological path. It could also apply to the manner in which a project gains supporters and developers. While previous research has shown that open source projects often involve establishment of a bureaucracy to enforce project management-focused tasks and affirm the power of the project owners (O’Mahony and Ferraro 2007), subfields arising in an open source community may help to explain the counterbalance to this bureaucracy. Additionally, the phenomenon of corporate-sponsored open source projects creates a dynamic that is, similar to that seen in the GENI project, where there exists an official, central management group (like the GPO) to guide the actions of individuals over which it does not have direct authority.

Our data also revealed an interesting evolution of the two projects’ control portfolios. Projects were largely initiated due to field control, which subsided during planning, then returned during construction. Past research has emphasized the importance of understanding the entirety of a portfolio of control mechanisms used in projects with diverse stakeholders (Kirsch 2004, Soh et al. 2011, Gregory et al. 2013). This “closing the loop” pattern demonstrated in cyber projects (particularly NEES), where the predominant control type reverts to a previous type, is novel. Note also evidence that evolution of control portfolios is not only a function of the practical necessity of a given project phase, but may also be the result of conflict and/or difficulties in the project, as with NEES veering strongly back into field control.

The positive effects of conflict and user involvement in the context of IS development has been studied for decades (e.g., Robey and Farrow 1982, Robey and Franz 1989, Barki and Hartwick 1994). The strategic fields literature, however, offers a new perspective on how constructive conflict may arise in large scale projects and influence the project’s direction. Through discord between the vision of those in power and those who stand to benefit from completion of a project, a rupture may arise that enables the challengers to overthrow the incumbents and ensure the final success of the initiative. Further research into the applicability of the fields perspective to IS development projects may yield new insights into the underlying forces that promote constructive conflict as well as explain resistance among project stakeholders.

Furthermore, previous applications of strategic action fields have occurred primarily in the context of statebased or company-based political and social movements (Fligstein and McAdam 2011, de Bakker et al. 2013). Note, however, that large scale projects also create political issues and social needs, in themselves as various actors seek leverage to steer a project toward their own aims. In addition, social strata may emerge in a large project as some involved may feel disempowered and seek to improve their station. Furthermore, project issues may accelerate the development of subfields and strengthen their cause, again, as occurred when academics asserted control during the latter stages of NEES construction.

Lastly, our study extends the work on cyberinfrastructure projects by exploring how they are and can be governed. Most research on these projects has focused on identifying outcomes (Bietz et al. 2010, Faniel and Jacobson 2010) and the impact of cyberinfrastructure technology on how work is done (de la Flor et al. 2010). This study furthers this research by showing the importance of groups of actors, which initially give life to the projects and have the ability to influence completion and effectively decide whether a cyberinfrastructure project will meet its goals of enabling transformational scientific research.

Thus, our theory suggests a complex web and layers of control, which are visually represented in Figure 1.

## Limitations and Areas for Future Study

We recognize that our study has some limitations. For instance, our data revealed relatively few cases of the use of clan, professional, and field controls, as compared to formal input, output, and process controls. As other researchers have observed, it is difficult to detect informal controls. For this reason, we may have underrepresented the degree of informal control actually used in these projects. However, in collecting our data we used multiple sources and coders to mitigate the risk of failure to code events. Additional research that endeavors to specifically address the degree to which clan, professional, and field control mechanisms are present in such projects may provide further insight not available in this study.

Next, our findings are based on an analysis of two cyber projects and, as a result, may not be representative of cyber projects in general. We believe, however, that the richness of our data overcomes some of the possible limitations caused by the small number of cases and helps increase the relevance of our findings for other projects that span organizational, geographic, and disciplinary boundaries. Future researchers may wish to replicate our approach on a broader set of projects. In addition, it may be useful to compare boundaryspanning projects in other contexts than those that, as in our case, are sponsored by NSF. Projects of similar scale and scope conducted wholly in the private sector may yield competing process theories or shed light on different portfolios of control.

Finally, this paper sought to understand how large scale, boundary-spanning projects such as these NSFsponsored cyber projects are controlled. In doing so, we have made inferences about the outcomes of these projects. With this in mind, however, further research could usefully analyze more specific data to better understand which control mechanisms should be used given the objective of enabling transformative research or other such outcomes that cannot reasonably be specified at the project’s inception.

## Conclusion

Our study makes several contributions. We observed the exercise of field control, which we have defined as the use of social influence by a field with a shared sense of values, norms, and purpose, and whose members are bound by their pursuit of a common goal to affect behavior in and/or outside the field. We find that control evolves over the life of a cyber project; the initial field-based control portfolio is critical in the earliest days of envisioning a project, galvanizing support, and securing funding. As a cyber project moves into a formal planning phase, field control diminishes and is replaced by an authority-based control portfolio designed to help ensure that the project meets traditional project objectives. Over time, the authority-based portfolio is supplemented with another field-based control portfolio given a successful confrontation by a subfield of challengers to the established dominant subfield (i.e., government-sponsored governance group in the field), which facilitates the research community engaging with the cyber project. We also observe layers of control as multiple controllers exercise control during the same project phases. We find that field ownership in the cyber projects evolves over time as well, in part as a response to the control portfolio in use. While other theoretical lenses may provide additional insights beyond those afforded by control, in this paper, we opted to focus on this lens given its generalizability to other projects and to provide a deep understanding of how control is used in this new form of work.

Cyber projects are meant to enable “big science,” transforming the way research is conducted and generating innovative outcomes that will have a profound effect on society at large. These projects involve stakeholders with diverse backgrounds, expertise, and interests and who are scattered around the globe. Cyber projects are challenging and risky and thus must be managed to yield the hoped-for research innovations. The results of our study suggest that field control is a key component in cyber project management. We call on other researchers to examine this phenomenon and contribute to our understanding of how to successfully manage cyber projects.

## Appendix B: Data Collection and Coding

## Details on Data Collection

To better understand the scale, scope, and early vision of the project, we began by conducting face-to-face interviews with key individuals who helped conceive the idea of the cyberinfrastructure project and were instrumental in “selling” the idea. We then interviewed stakeholders associated with the management and oversight of the cyber project, from its inception through the construction phase. We also interviewed individuals involved in the development and use of the evolving cyberinfrastructure. Most of the interviews lasted about an hour. Questions focused on the stakeholder’s role and nature of involvement in the cyber project, the control mechanisms used during that time, how problems were handled, the stakeholder’s view of what contributed to project success or failure, and project outcomes. The questions also focused on the cyber project’s history and trajectory. For the majority of the interviews, two of the authors were present, although sometimes an additional investigator was also present. When permitted, interviews were recorded and transcribed. If an interviewee declined our request for recording, we took copious notes during the interview and transcribed them as soon as possible. Notes taken during the interview became a means of validating the transcriptions.

Between 2006 and 2008, we conducted 26 interviews with 21 NEES stakeholders. As shown in Table 2, we interviewed project sponsors, project management, IT developers, and users. Throughout the NEES initiative, several governance committees were active, including the NEES Board of Directors, responsible for setting policies and strategy, making decisions, and governance; we interviewed these stakeholders as well. Most NEES interviews were conducted at the stakeholders’ institutions (university, place of business, or government office), though one interview was conducted by phone. The interviews yielded 160 single-spaced pages of transcribed text.

Starting in 2008 and lasting through 2012, we conducted 31 interviews with 37 GENI stakeholders, as summarized in Table 2. Our interviewees included project sponsors, project managers, principal investigators, experimenters, and campus Chief Information Officers (CIOs). The majority of the GENI interviews were face-to-face and conducted at the GECs, which were held quarterly at locations around the United States. The conferences attracted hundreds of GENI researchers (on average, 217 attended each GEC), experimenters, and other interested parties. Thus, the conferences provided a convenient environment to interview a range of stakeholders. In addition to the conference interviews, four more were conducted at individuals’ home institutions, and two interviews were conducted by phone. The GENI interviews yielded 122 singlespaced pages of transcribed text.

In addition to the interview data, we included archival data obtained from official board meeting minutes and committee meeting notes available from the NEES Governance website, the GEC conference data, and project data posted on the GENI wiki. We used these data to supplement and validate the interview data. We also reviewed project documents for both GENI and NEES, such as NSF RFPs and original design documents, which described the original goals of the projects. Table 3 outlines the archival data we used for this research. Finally, by attending 10 of the 14 GECs (GEC 5 through GEC 14), we observed interactions, technical demonstrations, and conference sessions, which significantly deepened our understanding of the GENI initiative.

## Details On Data Coding

To code the data, multiple coders worked independently to determine the control type for each event, with at least two individuals considering each event. After this, the authors met to discuss the resulting tables and resolve discrepancies. If an entered item did not actually reflect evidence of a control event (i.e., it did not show motivating behavior to propel the project forward), it was eliminated from the table. In the few instances where coding disagreements persisted, a third coder cast the deciding vote following group discussion. The coding rules are described next.

In the first step, we assigned each event in the table a control mode. Where an event fit the conceptual control mode labels noted in prior literature (input, process, outcome, clan, and professional), we assigned it accordingly. However, consistent with a theory-building approach (Eisenhardt 1989), we also noted any event involving a control mechanism that did not fit the previously studied classifications. Events coded as input control involved control exerted through the assignment or manipulation of tangible resources such as money, equipment or human capital. Such events included money awarded for a project, replacing one project leader with another, and establishment of policies to govern resource allocation. Events coded as process control involved control through monitoring and encouraging given behaviors. Examples included regularly held conferences, scheduled phone meetings, and use of a methodology. Output control events included those in which control was exercised through the establishment or evaluation of project goals, such as articulated due dates, project objectives, and formal evaluations of completed work.

We also coded for two different types of informal control, clan control and professional control. Both refer to control exerted through shared group norms and values, with clan control referring to groups that work together within some type of organizational boundaries (e.g., a team working together in a GENI technical cluster) and professional control referring to groups linked primarily by an external professional association (e.g., all civil engineers, all software developers). As noted earlier, professional and clan controls also vary in terms of the ties that bind members of the clan or profession together, be it attachment to a discipline (professional control) or task interdependency (clan control). In coding these two control types, we included not only evidence of events in which clan or professional control was exerted, but also evidence that shared norms and values informed a perspective, behavior, and/or level of effort (e.g., IT professionals preferring “exciting” research to the “mundane” and therefore resisting efforts to enforce more rigid schedules).

After classifying by control mode, the NEES-related events table included 127 events, while the GENI table included 119 events. Events from the tables were then transcribed into two new tables, one for each project, broken into columns based on the project phase. The events were categorized based on the type of control that each made evident. From this intermediate stage, the authors categorized events into the more general mechanisms. For instance, NEES included three separate events in which specifically named, key personnel were terminated; all three of these were categorized as “Personnel Termination.” Similarly, GENI included several references to individuals giving status reports; these were categorized as “Required Reporting.” An initial mechanisms table was produced and discussed by the authors; this enabled creation of another, even more reduced table.
