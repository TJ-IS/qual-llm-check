Abs trac t: Whether broadband service providers (BSPs) should be allowed to vertically integrate with content providers is a contentious issue. This is even more so when viewed through the lens of the net neutrality debate, since the vertically integrated firm can prioritize the delivery of its own content at the expense of that of its competitors if net neutrality is not enforced. Using a game-theoretic model, we analyze the issues of vertical integration of content and broadband services surrounding this debate from an economic perspective. Our analysis establishes the various equilibria in the game and shows that the vertically integrated BSP does not have any incentive to abide by the principles of net neutrality. If net neutrality is not enforced, social welfare might, in certain cases, decrease with vertical integration, and in such cases, the BSP’s objectives are at odds with that of the social planner. With other ranges of parameter values, social welfare increases with vertical integration at the expense of the competing pureplay content provider. Interestingly, we find that it is not always true that the BSP will always degrade the delivery of the competing content, and in fact will sometimes have the incentive to prioritize the latter over its own. The analysis thus provides crucial inputs to policymakers as they decide on whether to allow vertical integration between a BSP and a content provider in the absence of net neutrality.

Key words and ph ras es : broadband service providers, consumer surplus, content providers, economics of net neutrality, net neutrality, social welfare, vertical integration.

Th e is ue of net neutrality h as not rec eived much attention among the public at large and arguably even among researchers of information systems (IS). It is a topic hotly debated by researchers in the areas of computer science, public policy, and law, but so far has been largely ignored (with some prominent exceptions) within the IS research community. This state of affairs is somewhat surprising because the debate on net neutrality can potentially change the basic “rules of the game” of electronic commerce, insofar as changing the ways by which businesses and the general public access information through the Internet and determining the nature of competition among online content providers. As a result, the decisions that are taken on this issue can significantly alter the IS research agenda in future.

The issue of net neutrality received widespread media attention when some broadband service providers (BSPs) such as Verizon, Comcast, and AT &T proposed to charge popular online Web sites for priority delivery of the latter’s content to their residential and commercial customers [22, 43]. The proposal encountered stiff resistance from those who were supposed to be charged, and thus erstwhile competitors such as Google, Yahoo!, and Microsoft were soon lobbying before the U.S. Congress to pass legislation that would prevent the BSPs from carrying out their proposed plan [44] and thereby maintain what was termed the “neutrality” of the Internet (the term “net neutrality” is attributed to Columbia Law School Professor Tim Wu). This would involve the designing of “rules that prevent network operators and Internet service providers (ISPs) from using their power over the transmission technology to negatively affect competition in complementary markets for applications, content and portals” [41, p. 333].<sup>1</sup>

The supporters of net neutrality believe that a “maximally useful public information network aspires to treat all content, sites, and platforms equally,”<sup>2</sup> and although a formal definition of the operationalization of the principle does not exist, Hahn and Wallsten point out that it “usually means that broadband service providers charge consumers only once for Internet access, do not favor one content provider over another, and do not charge content providers for sending information over broadband lines to end users” [20, p. 1].

Lobbied intensely by both sides of the issue, Congress is currently considering proposals to introduce net neutrality legislation [10, 30, 46]. The U.S. House of Representatives and the Senate have held several hearings on the subject [3, 39]. The Federal Trade Commission has also chimed in and has recently published a report that has advised a wait-and-see approach on the matter [15].

As is to be expected in a debate that has implications in many different areas, academicians can also be found on both sides of the debate (for a recent example of such a debate, see [42]). The issues that are germane to the research in this paper are economic in nature. As Economides and Tag [13] point out, in sharp contrast to the large amount of literature that discusses the legal issues surrounding net neutrality, there is a surprising lack of rigorous economic analysis of the net neutrality debate. One economic argument considers the net neutrality issue as one of the BSPs acting as a monopolist producer that should be allowed to discriminate its services among its customers, which we believe completely misses the point—there has never been any doubt about the right or the ability of a BSP to differentiate its services at the consumers’ end. The correct perspective in this scenario is to consider the BSP as a twosided market platform that can, through its pricing and traffic prioritization strategies, effectively regulate the content that reaches the end consumers. For a comprehensive analysis, therefore, we need to consider the strategic interactions between the three players involved in this issue—the content providers, who jockey for a position in the consumers’ minds; the consumers, who gain utility by consuming the content of the content providers but endure the disutility of congestion by waiting for this content; and the BSP, who charges the consumers for access to the content and, depending on the legislation on this issue, can decide to charge the content providers for preferential delivery of their content—all within a two-sided market framework.

The issue of Internet pricing has been an important topic in the literature for some time now. This was especially relevant during the mid-1990s, when it was believed that the T1/T3 backbone of the Internet would soon reach unacceptable levels of congestion as a consequence of the “tragedy of the commons.” The work by Gupta et al. [18, 19] represents some of the seminal research in this area that rigorously analyzed the problem. The issue at hand in the net neutrality debate is, however, on the congestion over the last mile. BSPs often did not have the incentive to expand capacity, especially if they were a de facto monopolist whose business model depended on extracting the consumer surplus that arose from using a scarce resource—that is, the bandwidth at the edge of the network—and this is what gives rise to the net neutrality issue.

Cheng et al. [5] and Economides and Tag [13] are among the first to rigorously look at the net neutrality issue as an economic issue using a two-sided market framework. Economides and Tag [13] find that net neutrality regulation increases total social surplus in the presence of a monopoly BSP. The same finding also applies to the duopoly setting. For the purposes of analytical tractability, the authors assume that the content providers are independent monopolists with their own market shares and therefore do not compete with one another. In contrast, Cheng et al. [5] consider the effect of competition among content providers by limiting the number of content providers to two, who do not effectively differ in quality but are differentiated from each other in terms of consumers’ content preferences in a Hotelling sense. They find that net neutrality can sometimes result in lower social welfare in the short run, but when capacity expansion is an endogenous choice (i.e., in the long run) in the model, the BSPs have a higher incentive to expand under net neutrality in most scenarios, thus undermining one of the key arguments of the opponents of net neutrality—that BSPs will not have sufficient incentive to expand capacity under net neutrality.

The net neutrality debate has some similarities with the use of content delivery network (CDN) services by content providers, which can result in faster delivery of their content. However, as eminent observers such as Tim Berners-Lee [27] (the “father of the Internet”) and the noted academic and Creative Commons founder Lawrence Lessig [28] have observed, CDNs are actually not physical networks consisting of routers and so forth but are merely DNS (domain name system) servers placed at different locations around the world. When one queries an Akamai DNS server, “it calculates which of its thousands of caching servers is likely to provide the best performance for your particular request (based on your location, the load on various servers, congestion, and other factors) and returns its IP address” and “nothing a DNS server does can violate network neutrality, just as nothing a web server does can violate network neutrality, because both operate entirely at the application layer” (quotations are from Berners-Lee’s blog post).<sup>3</sup> Internet routers route Akamai’s packets exactly the same way as other packets. Thus, while CDNs do allow some content providers to make their content available faster to consumers, the issue is not germane to the net neutrality debate. We do believe, however, that it is an important issue that should be part of the broader broadband policy debate. In this context, we mention Hosanagar et al. [23], who model the decision of a content provider as it debates whether to outsource the delivery of its content to a CDN or develop the infrastructure in-house. The model represents a “one-sided” analysis—the consumers’ choice of content provider does not depend on these decisions, because the content providers are not competing for them. In our model (and more generally within the net neutrality debate), the analysis has to be “two sided” because the BSP can exploit a content provider’s incentive to reach a greater number of consumers (at the expense of its competitor) and thereby change its pricing for both the consumers and the content providers to reach its op timal profit level.

The literature on vertical integration focuses on a market structure with an upstream input market and a downstream market. Economides [11] considers a monopolist in the input market integrates with a downstream firm and finds that the integrated input provider always has the incentive to increase the rivals’ costs in the downstream market through nonprice discrimination. Foros [16] considers two BSPs that differ in their abilities to offer value-added services and studies the effect of an access price regulation. Inspired by the merger of AOL and Time Warner, Rubinfeld and Singer [37] analyze the integrated firm’s incentives to perform two different types of vertical foreclosure strategies—conduit discrimination and content discrimination. Other articles that look into the issue of vertical integration in the IS context include Bapna et al. [2], Clemons et al. [6], and Lucas and Baroudi [29]. In contrast to the extant literature, our research studies a different market structure where the monopolist BSP serves a two-sided market (both content and Internet access).

The literature on net neutrality thus far—especially those that look at the issue from an economic perspective—study the problem assuming that the BSP and the content providers are separate entities with conflicting objectives, with the two standing on opposing sides in this debate. However, recent developments indicate that the issue might not be that clearly delineated. BSPs such as Comcast and AT &T have struck deals with online content providers in which the latter provide exclusive co-branded content through the former’s “pipes.” For example, Comcast and Yahoo! recently signed a multiyear agreement so that the latter can display its advertisements to the end consumers who subscribe to the former’s broadband services [38]. Another example is that of AT &T and Yahoo! forming a strategic multiyear relationship in which the two companies will offer a co-branded version of Yahoo!’s popular messaging platform, Yahoo! Messenger, to AT &T’s broadband subscribers [4]. In other cases, BSPs such as Cox Communications have come up with their own indigenous content that competes with that from other “pure-play” content providers. As industry observers have noted, should net neutrality be abolished, it might motivate the BSPs to generate their own content (or, equivalently, have a strategic relationship with a content provider) and then prioritize delivery of such content to the end consumers. If net neutrality is not enforced, and the BSPs in these examples are allowed to prioritize content from their strategic partner, a section of the consumers might switch from their erstwhile content provider to the BSP’s strategic partner. A similar scenario can ensue with other classes of services such as news, Voice over Internet Protocol (VoIP) telephony, and streaming music.

Our research thus has some points of contact with some channel literature in supply chains. Several articles have investigated competition between manufacturers and their channel choices. The seminal article in this area is from McGuire and Staelin [31], who model two identical manufacturers that must decide whether to integrate into retailing or sell their products through an exclusive independent retailer. Several other articles used a similar modeling construct to extend research in this area, including Coughlan [7], McGuire and Staelin [32], and Moorthy [35]. Coughlan and Wernerfelt [8] counter results that tried to explain channel delegation decisions as a profit-maximizing strategy and show that, devoid of some crucial assumptions, channel delegation has no effect on manufacturer profits. Gal-Or [17] investigates the incentive of duopolistic manufacturers to integrate with a downstream agent for distribution purposes. In all such literature, the consumers pay the retailers, which in turn pay the manufacturers for the goods, and thus the markets (whether between the manufacturer and the retailer or between the retailer and the consumers) are one-sided in nature. In contrast, our research models the environment as a two-sided market where the BSP, as a gatekeeper of the content from the content providers, can charge both sides of the market, and the pricing on one side of the market affects the equilibrium strategy on the other side. Thus, the priority delivery charge that the BSP assesses on the content providers influences the decision as to whether the latter would pay the charge, which in turn influences whether a consumer goes to one content provider or another.

These developments prompt a new set of questions for the policymaker:

1. How does such vertical integration affect consumer surplus and social welfare? Specifically, under what conditions might the BSP want to vertically integrate with a content provider, even though the outcome might result in lower consumer surplus or social welfare?

2. Since the vertically integrated firm can prioritize its own content without paying the priority delivery fees, how does such vertical integration affect the independent pure-play content providers? Will such an arrangement amount to unfair competition?

3. What are the possible different equilibrium outcomes when the BSP is vertically integrated with a content provider?

4. Will the vertically integrated firm prefer no net neutrality over net neutrality?

In this paper, we endogenize the BSP’s vertical integration decision in the context of the net neutrality debate to answer these questions. In our model, we assume that there is a monopolist BSP that can form a strategic relationship with an online content provider to provide an online service that competes with that from a pure-play competitor that only provides online content. The latter has to depend on the BSP’s infrastructure for getting its content delivered to the end consumers. We assume an advertisement-supported revenue model that allows the content providers to provide their service to the end consumers for free (this revenue model is further expanded upon in the next section).

Our analysis shows that depending on the effectiveness of the vertically integrated BSP in generating revenue as compared to its competitor, consumer surplus and social welfare may increase or decrease as compared to the equivalent scenarios when the BSP is not vertically integrated. We identify conditions under which the BSP deviates from the social planner’s<sup>4</sup> preference by choosing to vertically integrate with a content provider in a way that decreases social welfare. Further, our analysis also indicates that given the choice, the vertically integrated BSP does not ever have the incentive to abide by the principles of net neutrality. Consumers sometimes gain from arrangements with lower access charges, while the rival pure-play content provider is never better off. We find that, interestingly, it is not always true that the BSP will always degrade the delivery of the competing content and, in fact, might sometimes prioritize the content of its competitor over its own.

The rest of the paper is arranged as follows. The second section develops the theoretical model on which our analysis is based. The third section analyzes the three players decisions under net neutrality, and the fourth section analyzes their decisions with no net neutrality. Depending on the choice of parameter values, the BSP makes its vertical integration decision, which is explained in the fifth section. The sixth section examines the welfare effect of net neutrality in the context of vertical integration of the BSP with a content provider. The seventh section compares the effect on social welfare when the BSP is vertically integrated to when it is not. The paper concludes with a discussion of our findings and their implications.

## The Model

In th is s ec tion, we s et up a game-th eoretic model to analyze the effect of vertical integration between content providers and BSP both in the presence and in the absence of net neutrality. The BSP has two vertical integration–related decisions to make: (1) whether it would like to vertically integrate with one of the two content providers and (2) if so, which one. As the results for the case of no vertical integration are readily available from Cheng et al. [5], we thus focus our analyses in this paper on the presupposition that the BSP has vertically integrated with one of the two content providers and then compare and contrast our findings with those of Cheng et al. to address the BSP’s vertical integration decision in the fifth section. Hence, there are three types of players in the game—the vertically integrated monopolist BSP, the two content providers (one that is vertically integrated with the BSP), and the end consumers. The BSP serves as an intermediary and transmits content from the content providers to end consumers. Because the BSP is vertically integrated, the BSP has its own content (from its strategic partner), which competes with the other independent pure-play content provider (we call the independent content provider “C”) for the attention of the end consumers.

## Content Providers

To model the competition between the content providers, we assume two content providers, H and L, that offer their contents for free to the end consumers. (One of the two content providers is vertically integrated with the BSP.) We assume that the content from these two providers is horizontally differentiated in a Hotelling sense [24] with the two of them located on the two ends of line segment [0, 1], with a fit cost t. In our model, the “fit cost” represents the disutility of a mismatch due to the relative preference of a consumer for one content provider over another. Because we assume for analytical tractability purposes that there are two content providers, the “fit cost” of any consumer located within the line segment at a point x with respect to the content provider located at 0 is given by tx, and this represents how much that content provider is less than ideal for that consumer (conversely, (1 – x)t denotes the lack of fit with the content provider located at 1).

The choice of the particular modeling construct—the Hotelling model—implies that, for the purposes of our analysis, the content providers create content that are substitutes for each other. A BSP will preferably want to integrate itself with a content provider that generates content that complements its own (e.g., integrating local news with a search engine), which will leave the rival independent content provider at a disadvantage. Note that the strategic tensions for the BSP to deviate from net neutrality arise only if the content providers are competitors. It is entirely possible that the content from the two providers do not compete with each other. In the latter scenario, there is no incentive for the content providers to pay the BSP for preferential delivery, and in turn the BSP would not have any incentive to deviate from net neutrality.<sup>5</sup>

As is common in the online world nowadays, we assume that the content providers provide their content to the consumers for “free” and get compensated indirectly<sup>6</sup> from “a revenue-generating component somewhere in the value chain” [33]. This revenue model follows how many researchers, from economists such as Hal Varian [33] and Paul Krugman [26] and IS researchers such as Eric Clemons [25], think digital content would be paid for as their marginal cost is driven toward zero. The content providers are compensated when the consumers interact with advertisers and other revenue-generation mechanisms [9, 14, 36] on the content providers’ Web sites. Examples of such revenue-generation mechanisms include banner advertisements, affiliate revenues, rental of subscription lists, sale of aggregate information, licensing, live events, listing, paid inclusion, cost per install, getting users to create content for free, streaming audio and video advertising, and application programming interface (AP I) fees [45]. We capture this revenue model of the two content providers through two separate variables that represent the average revenue generated (from all sources) per packet requested by the end consumer, $r _ { \mathrm { H } }$ and $r _ { \mathrm { L } }$ , where, without loss of generality, $r _ { \mathrm { { H } } } > r _ { \mathrm { { L } } }$ . These two variables denote the revenue rates of the two content providers respectively per packet for content; in other words, these two parameters denote the average rates at which the requests for content from the consumers provide revenues to the content providers from myriad third parties that want to reach these consumers. As is observed in real life, content providers differ in their ability to get the “right” consumers for their respective advertisers (and other revenue sources) and therefore end up charging different advertising (and other) fees.<sup>7</sup>

We denote the revenue-generation rate of the vertically integrated BSP by $r _ { \mathrm { B S P } }$ and the revenue-generation rate of the independent content provider C, where $\mathrm { C } \in \{ \mathrm { H } , \mathrm { L } \}$ by $r _ { \mathrm { { C } } } .$ . Thus, $r _ { \mathrm { B S P } } = r _ { \mathrm { H } }$ and $r _ { \mathrm { c } } = r _ { \mathrm { L } }$ if the BSP vertically integrates with H, and $r _ { \mathrm { B S P } } = r _ { _ \mathrm { L } }$ and $r _ { \mathrm { c } } = r _ { \mathrm { H } }$ if the BSP vertically integrates with L. Therefore, depending on the BSP’s choice, $r _ { \mathrm { B S P } }$ can be either greater than or less than $r _ { \mathrm { c } }$ . The competition between content provider C and the BSP is driven by the fact that a larger consumer base will lead to greater advertising revenue.

## Broadband Service Provider

As in Cheng et al. [5], we assume a monopolist BSP that provides Internet access as well as its own content to the end consumers. While the monopoly assumption is a simplification in some locations, it should be noted that, unlike in many other countries, in the United States the extent of competition in the local broadband services market is very limited, to the point that in many places a single BSP is often a de facto monopolist [12, 21]. Some of the factors leading to this scenario are the high switching costs induced by long-term service contracts and by incompatible broadband technologies between cable and phone companies. Further, many customers do not qualify for digital subscriber’s line (DSL) broadband services from phone companies because they exceed the three-mile distance limit from the phone company’s nearest switching office, making the cable operators the only feasible BSPs in several local markets [40]. Thus, in addition to providing the benefit of making the analysis tractable, the assumption closely reflects the reality of local broadband services in the U.S. market. The BSP can provide its content either by providing indigenous content or as the result of a vertical integration with a content provider; the exact mechanism for this integration might be achieved through an outright merger between the two firms or through a strategic alliance.<sup>8</sup> Figure 1 shows a schematic of the market structure of the model. The BSP charges the consumers a fixed fee F for Internet access and potentially charges content provider C a per packet fee $p$ for preferential delivery of ${ \mathrm { C } } { \mathrm { : } }$ data packets if net neutrality is not enforced. In this context, we note that the technology to discriminate packets and streamline Internet traffic has been available at a minimal fixed cost, and we therefore assume that there is no additional expense incurred by the BSP to implement a mechanism that enables preferential delivery of content [5]. The $\mathrm { B S P } \mathrm { \ ' } _ { \mathrm { S } }$ capacity is denoted by µ.

## Consumers

Consumers request content from either the vertically integrated BSP or content provider C. We model the congestion in the network after Bandyopadhyay and Cheng [1] and Mendelson [34], and accordingly, consumers’ request for data packets follows a Poisson process with arrival rate λ. The gross valuation consumers receive is denoted by V(λ). Consumers face a delay cost due to network congestion during the data transmission process. As noted in the aforementioned literature, we assume an M/M/1 queue to model the data transmission service provided by the BSP under net neutrality. Then the time that a data packet spent in the system is $1 / \mu - \lambda$ (with net neutrality enforced—that is, when no packet has priority over another) and the corresponding delay cost is $d / \mu - \lambda$ , where d is the delay parameter that captures the unit cost of delay for consumers waiting for the content to arrive from the content providers. As discussed earlier, the consumers are uniformly distributed on [0, 1] in terms of their preferences for content (as shown in Figure 2), with an associated fit cost parameter t.

For an arbitrary consumer $\tilde { \mathbf { x } } \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ , the fit cost associated with deviation from his or her ideal content is tx] if the consumer chooses the $\mathrm { B S P 7 s }$ content and $t ( 1 - \tilde { { \mathbf { X } } } )$ if the consumer chooses content from provider C. Then the utility function for the $\mathrm { B S P ^ { \circ } s }$ consumers under net neutrality is

$$
u _ {\mathrm {NN\_ {B} SP}} \left(\tilde {x}\right) = V \left(\lambda\right) - t \tilde {x} - \frac {d \lambda}{\mu - \lambda} - F.\tag{1}
$$

The utility function for content provider C’s consumers under net neutrality is

$$
u _ {\mathrm {NN\_C}} (\tilde {x}) = V (\lambda) - t (1 - \tilde {x}) - \frac {d \lambda}{\mu - \lambda} - F.\tag{2}
$$

In the absence of net neutrality, we use a two-class priority queue to model the BSP’s data transmission service. The detailed congestion cost and the corresponding utility functions for consumers under no net neutrality will be discussed in the fourth section.

We define two indicator functions as follows to represent whether the BSP would prioritize its own content and whether content provider C would pay for the preferential delivery:

$$
I _ {\mathrm{BSP}} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1, & \text { the   BSP   prioritizes   its   own   content } \\ 0, & \text { the   BSP   does   not   prioritize   its   own   content } \end{array} \right.\tag{3}
$$

$$
I _ {\mathrm{C}} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1, & \text {content provider C pays for the preferential delivery} \\ 0, & \text {content provider C does not pay for the preferential delivery.} \end{array} \right.\tag{4}
$$

The timing of the games in both the indigenous content model and the strategic partnership model are shown in Figure 3. The indigenous content model is represented as a three-stage game (Figure 3a). In stage 1, the broadband provider announces F and $p .$ In stage 2, the BSP decides whether to give its own content preferential treatment and content provider C chooses to “pay” or “not pay” for the preferential delivery. In stage 4, consumers choose content from either the BSP or content provider C. In the strategic partnership model, we endogenize the BSP’s decision to establish a strategic partnership with one of the existing content providers, therefore the strategic partnership model can be represented as a four-stage game. In stage 1 of the strategic partnership model (Figure 3b), the BSP decides (1) whether to integrate with a content provider and (2) if so, integrate with which provider. In stage 2, the broadband provider announces F and p. In stage 3, the BSP decides whether to give its own content preferential treatment and content provider C chooses to “pay” or “not pay” for the preferential delivery. In stage 4, consumers choose content from either the BSP or content provider C. Given this particular timing, the strategy of the BSP is to calculate its maximum profit under different scenarios and then, depending on the underlying parameter values (see the Appendix for a list of the various parameters and variables used in the text), choose whether to observe net neutrality, or if the regulatory environment allows the BSP to do so, choose to prioritize its own content and (for a fee) the content from provider C to the end consumers. Thus, the monopolist BSP can calculate what would be the profit under different scenarios and then choose the particular pricing strategy that will maximize its profit for a given set of parameter values. In the third and fourth sections, we analyze these different scenarios by solving the BSP’s profit-maximization problems under different regulatory regimes (net neutrality and no net neutrality) and pricing arrangements. Note that there is only one pricing decision under net neutrality (the access fee that the BSP charges the consumers), while under no net neutrality, the BSP can charge both the consumers and content provider C (charging the latter for the service that prioritizes the delivery of its content to the end consumers).

## Indigenous Content Model

## Net Neutrality

With net neutrality in place, the BSP is forbidden from providing the service of and charging for preferential delivery of data packets. Then the marginal consumer who is indifferent from the BSP and content provider C can be determined by $u _ { \mathrm { N N \mathrm { ~ B S P } } } ( x _ { \mathrm { N N } } ) =$ $u _ { \mathrm { N N \_ C } } ( x _ { \mathrm { N N } } )$ , that is,

$$
V (\lambda) - t x _ {\mathrm{NN}} - F _ {\mathrm{NN}} - \frac {d \lambda}{\mu - \lambda} = V (\lambda) - t (1 - x _ {\mathrm{NN}}) - F _ {\mathrm{NN}} - \frac {d \lambda}{\mu - \lambda},\tag{5}
$$

which implies $x _ { _ { \mathrm { N N } } } = 1 / 2 . ^ { 9 }$ Therefore, the demand for the BSP and content provider C are both 1/2. The resulting Internet access fee is

$$
F _ {\mathrm{NN}} = V (\lambda) - \frac {t}{2} - \frac {d \lambda}{\mu - \lambda}.
$$

The BSP’s revenue consists of both Internet access charge from consumers,

$$
V (\lambda) - \frac {t}{2} - \frac {d \lambda}{\mu - \lambda},
$$

and advertisement revenues from advertisers, $( ( 1 / 2 ) \lambda r _ { \mathrm { { B S P } } } )$

## No Net Neutrality

Without net neutrality, the BSP has the option to provide a preferential delivery for data packets from content provider C and the BSP. Depending on whether the BSP prioritizes its own content and whether C pays for the preferential delivery, there are four different outcomes:

•	 Outcome 1: C does not pay for priority delivery of its own content, and content from neither provider is prioritized;

•	 Outcome 2: the BSP prioritizes its own content at the expense of that of C, which does not pay the priority delivery fee;

•	 Outcome 3: the BSP prioritizes C’s content for a fee so that the latter’s content is prioritized at the expense of its own; and

•	 Outcome 4: C pays the BSP so that the BSP does not prioritize its own content over that of C (in other words, content from both providers receive the same priority).

Note that all these outcomes are under the “control” of the BSP—for example, if it is charging C for priority delivery, the BSP can create a contract that specifies whether it will in turn not prioritize its own content (i.e., Outcome 3), or whether the payment from C merely ensures that C’s packets are not relatively “deprioritized” with respect to its own (which is Outcome 4). We note further that, a priori, none of the outcomes can be ruled out because, based on the values of the different parameters, any one of them might generate the highest profit for the BSP.

Outcome 1: The BSP does not prioritize its own content and content provider C does not pay for the preferential delivery $( \mathrm { I } _ { _ { B S P } } = \mathrm { I } _ { _ { C } } = O )$

Outcome 1 is essentially the equilibrium equivalent to net neutrality. The corresponding marginal consumer $x _ { 1 }$ is determined by $u _ { _ { \mathrm { B S P 1 } } } ( x _ { _ 1 } ) = u _ { _ { \mathrm { C 1 } } } ( x _ { _ 1 } )$ —that is,

$$
V (\lambda) - t x _ {1} - F _ {1} - \frac {d \lambda}{\mu - \lambda} = V (\lambda) - t (1 - x _ {1}) - F _ {1} - \frac {d \lambda}{\mu - \lambda},\tag{6}
$$

which implies $x _ { 1 } = 1 / 2$ . Under this scenario, the BSP’s problem is

$$
\begin{array}{c} \max _ {F _ {1}, p _ {1}} \pi_ {\text { BSP1 }} = F _ {1} + \frac {1}{2} \lambda r _ {\text { BSP }} \\ \text { such   that } u _ {\text { BSP1 }} (\tilde {x}) \geq 0, 0 \leq \tilde {x} \leq x _ {1} \\ u _ {\text { C1 }} (\tilde {x}) \geq 0, x _ {1} \leq \tilde {x} \leq 1 \\ \pi_ {\text { BSP1 }} - \pi_ {\text { BSP2 }} \geq 0 \\ \pi_ {\text { C1 }} - \pi_ {\text { C3 }} \geq 0, \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

where the first two constraints are consumers’ participation constraints and the last two are constraints that ensure that the BSP and content provider C choose this outcome over other possible outcomes. Thus, the BSP compares its profit under Outcome 1 with that under Outcome 2 (where the BSP prioritizes its own content over that of C). To consider the analogous constraint of content provider C, meanwhile, the BSP will need to compare ${ \mathrm { C } } { \mathrm { : } }$ profit under Outcome 1 to that under Outcome 3—when the BSP prioritizes C’s content at the expense of its own.

Some algebra shows that regardless of parameter values, the constraint for the BSP can never be satisfied. In other words, if content provider C does not pay for the preferential delivery, the BSP is always better off prioritizing its own content (see Table 1 for a comparison of the $\mathrm { B S P 7 } \mathrm { s }$ profits under the different scenarios). Therefore, Outcome 1 is not an equilibrium unless the BSP is prohibited through regulation from differentially prioritizing its own content.

We end this section with a discussion of the implications of Cases A and B. Under Case A , the BSP prioritizes its own content at the expense of its competitor, which prefers not to pay for that privilege. The BSP thus gets an advantage over its competitor without the requirement of a comparable rent; this is something that should definitely be of interest to a policymaker deliberating on this issue because such an arrangement may be tantamount to unfair competition. Under Case B , we have an outcome that looks counterintuitive at first sight: the BSP deliberately deprioritizes its own content with respect to its competitor in exchange for a fee. As the discussion in the previous paragraph indicates, this outcome is plausible: the BSP gains more from the fee from its competitor (which has a higher market share) than would have been generated from the advertising revenue of its own content (and commanding the same market share) by delivering its own content with the same priority as that of its competitor.

## The Welfare Effect of Net Neutrality

Th e different sc enarios in th e previous s ec tion, one under a net neutrality regime and four under no net neutrality, list exhaustively the various pricing/prioritization strategies of the BSP. Depending on the specific values of the parameters, the BSP can calculate the exact values of the optimum profit under each scenario and decide on the best pricing strategy F and p correspondingly. We have already observed that given the choice, the BSP will never opt for abiding by the principles of net neutrality; in other words, unless enforced, net neutrality will never be a natural outcome of the game. It is imperative, therefore, from a regulatory perspective to find out whether it is in the best interests of the other players (the content providers and the consumers), as well as in terms of the total social welfare, to regulate enforcement of network neutrality. This section is devoted to that discussion—we discuss the effect of net neutrality (or its abolishment) on the payoffs to the three players and the overall welfare. Proposition 2 summarizes the findings:

Proposition 2 (The Welfare Effect of Net Neutrality): Under Case A where

$$
r _ {\mathrm{C}} <   r _ {\mathrm{BSP}} - \frac {t}{\lambda} \big (1 - 2 x _ {3} \big)
$$

and Case B where

$$
r _ {\mathrm{C}} > \left(\frac {1 - x _ {3}}{1 / 2 - x _ {3}}\right) r _ {\mathrm{BSP}} + \frac {t}{\lambda} (1 - 2 x _ {3}),
$$

both consumer surplus and social welfare are higher under no net neutrality than under net neutrality.

Under Case C where

$$
r _ {\mathrm{BSP}} - \frac {t}{\lambda} (1 - 2 x _ {3}) \leq r _ {\mathrm{C}} \leq \left(\frac {1 - x _ {3}}{1 / 2 - x _ {3}}\right) r _ {\mathrm{BSP}} + \frac {t}{\lambda} (1 - 2 x _ {3}),
$$

both consumer surplus and social welfare are the same under net neutrality and no net neutrality.

Case C is in turn divided into two subcases, C1 and C2, as discussed in the previous section. Table 2 also shows the result of comparing the magnitude of these output variables under the different scenarios of no net neutrality to that under net neutrality, and in all the cases, we establish how the expressions compare regardless of input parameter values.

As can be seen from Table 2, the consumers’ access fees are lower with no net neutrality than with net neutrality under Cases A and B and unchanged under Case C. The corresponding consumer surpluses are higher under Cases A and B as compared to net neutrality and unchanged under Case C. Similarly, the total social welfare with vertical integration increases with no net neutrality under Cases A and B as compared to net neutrality, while it is unchanged in Case C.

The vertically integrated BSP’s profit is always higher under no net neutrality, and therefore the BSP will always prefer no net neutrality over net neutrality.

The expressions in Table 2 establish that the content provider C has either the same profit (Case B ) or is worse off under no net neutrality. Interestingly, content provider C has the same profit in Case B that it has under net neutrality, even though it has a higher market share than under net neutrality—this is so because the BSP is able to extract the additional surplus from C completely through the priority access fee. In Case A , content provider C has a lower market share than under net neutrality, and consequently a lower profit. In Case C, content provider C and the BSP have the same market share as under net neutrality, but as a result of the priority access fee, content provider C’s net surplus is lower than under net neutrality.

Commentators on net neutrality have argued that the vertically integrated firm has an unfair competitive advantage over the pure-play content provider, since the former can effectively act as a gatekeeper who can control the experience of the end consumers when they consume the content. ## Conclusion

Th e is ue of net neutrality h as wide implic ations for the ways that online content will be created, delivered, and consumed in the near future. Because the local BSP has control over the infrastructure that delivers this online content to end users, it can effectively regulate the choices of the end consumers if the principle of net neutrality is abolished. This is possible because the “pipe” by which the content is delivered to the end users has limited bandwidth, and therefore prioritizing some specific content for delivery automatically leads to increased delay for the rest of the nonprioritized content. Even though a lot of literature exists that analyzes the various facets of the net neutrality problem, most of the analyses focus on the legal and regulatory perspectives of the debate. In contrast, the research by Cheng et al. [5] and Economides and Tag [13] represent some of the few papers that concentrate on the economic issues surrounding the debate, and analyze them rigorously within an analytical framework. This paper contributes to that stream of economic literature by looking into the incentives of the various players involved and analyzing them using a game-theoretic model. Specifically, we look into the issue of vertical integration; if a BSP’s service is vertically integrated with that of a content provider, will the vertically integrated firm have enough market power to negatively affect the social welfare?

We find that the answer crucially depends on whether the BSP is integrated with the more- or less-effective content provider (effectiveness in this case being defined as the ability to generate advertising revenue from the consumer base). If the vertically integrated firm is relatively less effective in generating revenue from its content, we find that for a range of parameter values, the social welfare decreases as compared to a situation when there is no vertical integration. On the other hand, social welfare can actually increase if the BSP is vertically integrated with the more-effective content provider. But in either case, the competing content provider is often left worse off (and is never better off). In order to decide on an effective policy that regulates vertical integration in an online environment, policymakers therefore will need to balance the priorities of increasing social welfare to that of ensuring effective competition. Allowing a BSP to form a strategic relationship with the more-effective content provider might result in an increase of the social welfare in the short run. However, this decision might have the less desirable outcome of lowering the level of competition in that particular market segment in the long run, which in turn might lead to less desirable result for the society as a whole.

Because ours is a model of the real-life environment, we tried to abstract only the salient features that would become important under the lens of the net neutrality debate. It is indeed possible that a BSP (say, Cox Communications or AT &T) signs up with a content provider such as Google, and that would leave a rival content provider such as Yahoo! in a much worse position. It is also possible that Cox does not have a preferred provider for, say, online music, which does not compete with Google or Yahoo! (the same consumer might conduct a search and also listen to music, but these are distinct activities that happen in different points in time). In such a situation, priority delivery of Google’s content will affect the content from the online music providers Napster and

Rhapsody in exactly the same way, and thus, relative to each other, the prioritization of Google’s searches will not affect either Napster or Rhapsody in any way.

As in any other modeling setup, the results are influenced by the specifics of the model. For example, the delay cost is assumed to be linear in the time a data packet spends in the system. Changing the delay cost function may impact the equilibrium outcome: consumers might be very forgiving of small delays, but get increasingly impatient as the delay interval gets larger (in other words, the delay cost is a convex function of time). Intuitively, such a result would exacerbate the move toward a content provider that is paying for priority delivery of its content and increase the area under Case A or Case B , though qualitatively the results would perhaps not change. But such a conjecture should definitely be explored in future research. A similar outcome can be expected if the revenue-generation rate is assumed to be positively correlated with market share. Even though (as we discuss earlier) such a phenomenon is not generally observed in real life, this could still be relevant in the future, and therefore should be an area of further research. We also assume that the service capacity is an exogenous parameter, but future research can look at the effects of vertical integration in the long run where the service capacity becomes a decision variable for the BSP, and explore whether the competing content providers are driven out of the market. In such a situation, especially when the consumers’ intrinsic valuation $V ( \lambda )$ for the content is low but the content providers value them highly (i.e., either one or both $r _ { \mathrm { c } }$ and $r _ { \mathrm { B S P } }$ are high), one can intuitively infer that the content providers might have an incentive to indirectly subsidize the consumers; in other words, in order to extract the surplus from the content providers, the BSP might intentionally lower the access price it charges the consumers at the other side of the platform (and possibly in some cases, even lower it to zero). This might have the less-desired effect of driving the pure-play content provider completely out of the market. Again, such intuition will need to be confirmed by rigorous subsequent research. Finally, we assume that there are no benefits of synergy that can occur as a result of the vertical integration between a content provider and the BSP—that is, $r _ { \mathrm { { B S P } } } = r _ { \mathrm { { C } } }$ , where $\mathrm { C } = \{ \mathrm { H } , \mathrm { L } \}$ . One possibility for future research is to look at scenarios where $r _ { \mathrm { B S P } } > r _ { \mathrm { C } } .$ . Our research examines the various effects of vertical integration in the presence and absence of net neutrality on social welfare in the short run. Other possible areas of exploration include extending our analyses to the duopoly case of two competing BSPs or allowing for different types of online content from different providers.
