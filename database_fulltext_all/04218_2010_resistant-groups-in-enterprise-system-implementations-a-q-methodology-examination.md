## Abstract

This paper is an initial investigation into types of user resistance and the management strategy expectations of users in a mandatory adoption setting. Despite its relationship to adoption, relatively little is known about user resistance. User resistance is investigated in the Enterprise System (ES) environment because the complexity and richness of ES leads users to manifest a large range of resistant behaviors and beliefs. Using Concourse Theory and Q-methodology, ES users are interviewed followed by the development of a Q-sort questionnaire, which was distributed to ES users. The results reveal eight user groups and address the management strategies preferred by each group. The results have implications for both research in the field of user resistance and adoption, and practitioners involved in system implementation.

## Introduction

he Enterprise System (ES) market has grown rapidly<sub>and is estimated to be at \$47.7 billion by 2011</sub> (Jacobson et al., 2007) since there are many benefits to organizations who choose to enable organization change through ES implementations (Markus and Tanis, 2000). However, despite the widespread use of ESs in organizations today, there are still many ES implementation failures. For example, Maurer (2002) found that the reason for low return on investments for ES implementations is user resistance. One study found that more than half of system projects fail (LLC, 2002) while another found only a 16% success rate (Group, 1994). Another study even considers user resistance to be ‘at the root of many enterprise software project failures’ (Hill, 2003: 1). Other studies have also revealed how user resistance is a major source of ES implementation failures (Robey et al., 2002; Umble and Umble, 2002; Barker and Frolick, 2003). Furthermore, a report on 186 companies that implemented a large system found that resistance is the second most important contributor to time and budget overruns and is the fourth most important barrier to implementation (Cooke and Peterson, 1998).

The ES environment has two key characteristics that make it fertile grounds for an investigation of user resistance and patterns among types of users. First, ESs typically require mandatory usage throughout the organization. Usage is mandatory on account of how the level of system integration demanded in an ES environment requires the participation of all areas of the organization in order for the ES to succeed (Amoako-Gyampah and Salam, 2004). A second characteristic is that it is often easier to reengineer business processes to model ES processes rather than vice versa (Bosilj-Vuksic and Spremic, 2005). Users are affected because of their deep involvement in the extensive and complex reengineering processes at work during an ES implementation. Thus a user not only is forced to use the system, but the user’s job often is altered to comply with the new ES processes.

In response to a growing awareness that system users are not homogeneous, previous research has begun to explore the existence and identity of different groups of users. For example, one previous study found that perceptions of technology and adoption rates vary among types of users (Jurison, 2000). Another also examines different types of users and found that there are differences among stereotyped groups (Zhang and Han, 2005). Previous studies have also identified sets of user patterns among Internet users (Ranchhod and Zhou, 2001), and derived profiles of types of users in a recommendation system (Chen and Chen, 2005).

Two research questions are proposed to better understand the groups comprising users with similar resistance perspectives and resistance behaviors, as well as the management strategy expectations that these user groups have. In the systems implementation environment, the unique characteristics of their respective viewpoints will be examined.

1. What are the types of users that resist in ES implementations?

2. What are the management strategies identified by these types of users that will be most effective in minimizing the level of resistance?

Although user resistance has been identified as one possible form of non-adoption by the technology adoption literature (Venkatesh et al., 2003), it is unclear whether user adoption and user resistance are at opposite ends of the same continuum, or whether they are separate concepts that focus independently on positive and negative aspects of system implementation and usage. It seems reasonable to expect that, in any given system environment, not all system users have adopted the system willingly, and not all non-users have resisted. Furthermore, research about the relationship between project management strategy and user resistance is practically non-existent. Therefore, it seems necessary to undertake research to develop an understanding of the nature of user resistance that may subsequently serve as a basis for research concerning effective management strategy.

The nature of the research questions leads to the adoption of theory and methods that are capable of revealing the richness of the ES resistance phenomenon. Concourse Theory (Stephenson, 1978, 1986a, b) is a theory of communicability aimed at defining the substance of a discourse with the language used by the participants in the discourse themselves, and will guide the examination of the research questions. In the context of this study, the anticipated outcome will be a rich interpretation of ES resistance and management strategy based on the natural dialogue and discourse of ES users.

There are several contributions of this study. One contribution of this study is a better understanding of why users resist an ES, the behaviors exhibited by users, and management strategies to minimize user resistance. A second contribution is the understanding of types of resistant users, such as an understanding of how user resistance manifests itself through behaviors within groups of users. Research on resistant groups is either very scarce, or perhaps even non-existent, yet an understanding of resistant users is key to understanding how to mitigate user resistance. This research explores resistant groups and their characteristics, and sets the groundwork upon which future theories can be built. A third contribution is an understanding of the management strategies perceived by users to be the most effective in minimizing the level of user resistance. Through a better understanding of these strategies, managers may be able to both proactively prevent user resistance as well as reduce the level of user resistance once it has occurred. To accomplish these goals, the paper will report on ES user feedback regarding implementation-related management strategies.

This research will proceed first by reviewing the literature on user resistance, management strategies, and Concourse Theory. According to the methods and procedures prescribed by Concourse Theory, a concourse on the subject of user resistance and associated management strategies will be defined as a basis for developing a set of Q-sort items that will be administered to a representative sample of ES users. Data will be gathered and analyzed using the Q-sort and Q-factor analysis procedures.

## Literature review

## User resistance to an ES

There is an extensive body of research that has focused on system acceptance (Davis, 1989; Mathieson, 1991; Taylor and Todd, 1995; Venkatesh et al., 2003). These studies have consistently found relationships among beliefs, attitudes, behavioral intentions, and usage behavior, and typically focus on the initial decision about whether or not to use a system. However, far fewer studies have examined user resistance. To clarify this term, user resistance is defined in this paper as ‘users’ opposition to system implementation.’ There are some studies that have examined one or more reasons for user resistance. For example, De Jager (1994) addresses job insecurity, Keen (1981) addresses the loss of power, Marakas and Hornik (1996), Gosain (2004) address misalignment between the organization and the system, and O’Leary (2000) addresses the user interface and process changes. Other studies discuss multiple reasons that affect user resistance (Hirschheim and Newman, 1988; Shang and Su, 2004). Furthermore, models of user resistance have been proposed (Lapointe and Rivard, 2005; Bhattacherjee and Hikmet, 2007). This literature helps to provide readers with a better understanding of why user resistance occurs. However, types of users resisting have not been examined in previous studies.

One of the characteristics of the ES environment that contributes to user resistance is the reengineered processes. ESs are not needed for reengineering, but one main benefit of an ES is the process reengineering that occurs as the technology is implemented. Employees can be greatly affected by the job transformation caused by the ES implementation. This transformation is often difficult (Alvarez and Urla, 2002), which suggests that users have values, work habits, and dilemmas that carry over and challenge the new system. This readjustment usually causes a temporary reduction in performance (Hill, 2003) which, if left unresolved, can cause a much greater problem (Jiang et al., 2000). Standardized modules are used and often only partially customized in order to minimize initial implementation costs and future upgrade costs. Managers are discouraged from making modifications to these standardized modules unless they are absolutely necessary since every initial customization performed also requires customization for every upgrade. Rather, managers generally choose to reengineer processes to make them more efficient, often requiring changes in reward structures and job tasks.

One previous study (Ross and Vitale, 2000) describes how resistance took place in many forms since some users’ jobs significantly changed, some lost power, and most had to unlearn as well as relearn. Essentially, an ES implementation requires organizational change, which often alters the tools, skills, rewards, tasks of the job, organizational structures, and even beliefs and values. There have been multiple studies that have examined the impact of users resistance on ES implementation failures (Robey et al., 2002; Umble and Umble, 2002; Barker and Frolick, 2003).

Furthermore, not all ES user resistance negatively affects reengineering efforts. Sometimes, valuable feedback can be obtained through an examination of user resistance, particularly as it pertains to the preferred management strategies of resistors. It is possible both for managers to learn healthy means to address resistance and for ES users to add value to the success of the ES implementation.

## Management strategies to address user resistance

Whether or not the employees are aware of the effects of their user resistance, managers and system implementers must still address the issue in a manner that produces favorable results. Previous research (Aladwani, 2001) supports the need for management to proactively and constructively deal with user resistance rather than reacting when it arises. However, this requires management to understand the nature of user resistance and take appropriate steps, such as appropriately marketing the ES to employees (Aladwani, 2001). It is important for management to have strategies in place to minimize the negative effects of user resistance. Without adequate strategies, it is quite possible for management to errantly search for resisters, punish the compliers, and promote the uninvolved. The other extreme would be for management to take no action to address resistance, which could also lead to problems.

There have been a variety of studies which have looked at critical success factors for ES implementations (Gupta, 2000; Rao, 2000; Willcocks and Sykes, 2000; Nah and Lau, 2001; Akkermans and Van Helden, 2002; Stratman and Roth, 2002). Each of the studies cited in the previous sentence describe at least 10 critical success factors. Some of these factors are management strategies that help to minimize user resistance. Jiang et al. (2000) is a study that specifically looks at management strategies, and identifies 20 general strategies that are based on the resistance to change literature. Managers can use this list of general strategies as a checklist for various types of systems. However, as with most checklists, all the items are not applicable to every environment and one finding was that system type affects the management strategies employed (Jiang et al., 2000).

As described in the previous paragraphs, studies have identified important issues that managers can use to minimize user resistance. Through the identification of critical success factors, managers are better prepared to implement strategies that impact parts of the implementation. However, research that examines the types of management strategies desired by users is minimal or non-existent.

## Concourse Theory

Concourse Theory proposes that the natural structure of discourse in a domain exists in states of complementary communicability (Stephenson, 1978, 1986a). Any given state of communicability is defined by the shared communications or beliefs of its members. For example, an analysis of the debate on the subject of how to train MIS Ph.D. students revealed three distinct perspectives held by faculty members (Tractinsky and Jarvenpaa, 1995). The three perspectives are each defined by the shared beliefs of the MIS professors who constitute that particular perspective, and as such represent a ‘state’ of beliefs about how best to train MIS Ph.D. students. Collectively, the states define the structure of discourse in the domain.

Concourse Theory (Stephenson, 1978, 1986a, b) proposes both a method (Q-methodology) and a means (Q-sort) of organizing a concourse (the discourse about a domain of interest) according to the self-referent beliefs held by the participants in the concourse. The natural structure of the concourse is revealed through the statistical analysis and interpretation of how the participants organize the concourse according to their own points of view. Since the unit of analysis is the concourse itself rather than people or organizational groups or units, Concourse Theory is capable of revealing the naturally existing structure of the discourse in any given domain of interest. As such, Concourse Theory is one of many approaches that are compatible with grounded theory (Glaser and Strauss, 1967). Hence, Concourse Theory naturally lends itself to the study of undeveloped areas of knowledge, as is the case with ES user resistance and its associated management strategies.

The results should benefit both researchers and practitioners: previous research points out that Q-sort is particularly suited for either exploring or validating both positivist and interpretivist conceptions within IS research (Thomas and Watson, 2002), and that Q-methodology can complement a project manager’s set of methodologies for understanding the perceptions of stakeholders (Brown, 2002).

Previous research, however, has largely ignored the encompassing significance of Concourse Theory, preferring instead to focus almost entirely on the Q-methodology and the Q-sort, which are the method and measurement prescribed by Concourse Theory (Stephenson, 1978, 1986a, b). There are several such examples of IS research that employ either the Q-methodology or the Q-sort. IS researchers have used the Q-sort to establish the existence of organizational subcultures and their relationship to Decision Support System (DSS) user satisfaction (Kendall et al., 1987), competencies of software engineers (Turley and Bieman, 1995), attitudes of systems analysts (Dos Santos and Hawk, 1988), and metaphors in the language of IS that may be used to increase the effectiveness of systems development (Kendall and Kendall, 1993). The Q-methodology has also been employed to investigate managerial decision-making about the deployment of technology throughout the firm (Tractinsky and Jarvenpaa, 1995), support structured training for information technology (IT)

professionals (Wingreen et al., 2009), investigate the relative importance of IT management issues (Gottschalk, 2001; Pimchangthong et al., 2003), compare information systems between organizations (Dos Santos, 1989), and compare the work climate of an IS organization to those in other industries (Ryan and Schmit, 1996).

Since Concourse Theory offers a philosophy for how to interpret the results of a Q-sort and its subsequent Q-factor analysis, any study that focuses entirely on Q-methodology or Q-sort to the neglect of Concourse Theory potentially suffers from a threat to the interpretation of the results. There are several advantages for using Concourse Theory and Q-methodology in this paper for addressing the research questions. First, in the context of this study, richness of user resistance is expected to be manifested on account of the richness of the ES implementation environment. Concourse Theory predicts that this richness will be manifested through the respondents, which for this study are the ES users. Second, the goal of Q-methodology is to uncover patterns of thought rather than discover what percentages of people think certain ways (Valenta and Wigger, 1997). Furthermore, the purpose of Q-methodology is to understand individuals and groups, and is therefore appropriate for the research questions in this study. Through the use of Concourse Theory and Q-methodology, this paper will be able to better examine the types of resisters and their desired management strategies. The analysis and interpretation of the results will adhere to the guidelines and philosophy proposed by Concourse Theory (Stephenson, 1978, 1986a, b).

## Methodology

## Q-methodology and Q-sort

In the context of this study, Q-methodology and the Q-sort are prescribed by Concourse Theory to reveal the various types and structure of user resistance and the management strategies naturally evident in the communicability among ES users. States of communicability are operationalized by the participants themselves as they arrange the Q-sort statements according to their own unique points of view (Stephenson, 1986a, b, 1987, 1988). Since each statement is sorted in relationship to all others in any given sort, a Q-sort represents the individual’s coherent point of view on the subject. A Q-factor analysis correlates individual points of view into factors that represent the types of viewpoints on the subject, and hence reveals the naturally existing structure of discourse (Brown, 1980; Stephenson, 1986a, b, 1987, 1988). As such, Q-methodology stands at the intersection of qualitative and quantitative research methods. This characteristic that makes it possible to appropriate the most effective aspects of both approaches; specifically, the ability to make empirically based generalizations while maintaining a greater level of phenomenological richness (Wingreen et al., 2009).

The most significant difference between Q-methodology and more common survey and questionnaire methods is that all elements of a Q-sort are dependent on all others due to ranking. The interdependence of elements in a Q-sort is the operational means by which each individual’s coherent point-of-view is captured (Brown, 1980). On the other hand, one of the basic assumptions of a questionnaire or survey is that each measurement is made independently of all others. This substantially differentiates the Q-methodology and Q-sorting from cluster analysis and profile analysis, its closest relatives among correlational analytical methods, despite the claims that some make to the contrary (Brown, 1980; Thomas and Watson, 2002). In theory, similar results could be obtained by cluster analysis if all respondents were to somehow agree among themselves as to how they ranked with respect to each other on each variable before they recorded their individual measurements (Brown, 1980).

In summary, Q-methodology includes a quantitative analysis that is lacking in most qualitative and interpretive methods (Stephenson, 1986a, b, 1987, 1988), yet it upholds the rich story told in the native language of its participants, a trait generally found with case-based research or other grounded theory methods (as in Eisenhardt, 1989, 1991). Specifically, Q-factor analysis generates empirically derived factors and factor scores that associate each person with each factor/type revealed in the analysis. This is an improvement over the descriptive and non-parametric statistics that are generally associated with case-based research and other grounded theory methods. This is an important point, since it enables Q-methodology to generate the same level of richness that is possible with case-based research while working with the larger sample sizes (sometimes referred to as P-sets in Q-methodology) associated with empirical field research. In other words, Q-methodology allows researchers both to make generalizations comparable to those obtained in empirical field research, such as surveys, while maintaining a level of phenomenological richness that is comparable to casebased interpretive research (Wingreen et al., 2009).

## Instrument development and data collection

The Q-sort instrumentation was developed according to the guidelines delineated by previous research (Brown, 1980; Thomas and Watson, 2002). The concourse was defined using transcripts of a focus group of 11 ES users and 34 individual interviews of ES users from three different organizations, which each lasted an average of 45 min. The focus group and interviews revolved around the experiences the users had during the ES implementation and sought to understand the reasons for user resistance, the resistant behaviors exhibited, and the management strategies used to minimize user resistance. A representative sample of 29 Q-sort statements was drawn from the transcripts to represent reasons for user resistance and resistant behaviors, and eight statements to represent the management strategies preferred to minimize user resistance (Appendix A shows the Q-sorts used in the questionnaire). Following previous research, open-ended questions were added to each of the Q-sorts in order to have the respondents provide an explanation for why they sorted the statements as they did (Brown, 1980). This provided qualitative information to assist with the interpretation of the Q-factor analysis.

A pilot test was first conducted on a small convenience sample of ES users all within one organization in order to obtain feedback from respondents about the length, content, and structure of the questionnaire. For this pilot data collection, 110 ES users received questionnaires and a total of 35 users responded. A convenience sample was also used for the main data collection. For the main data collection, web searches were conducted to find ES user group listserves as well as ES user group organizations. An email was sent to three ES user group listserves as well as 317 ES user groups seeking representatives who would be willing to participate in the study by agreeing to distribute the questionnaire to 15–20 users within their organization. Only organizations that had rolled out a system less than 3 years ago were included in the data collection. A total of 24 representatives from these user groups agreed to distribute questionnaires. All but two of these representatives received a packet of 20 questionnaires and business reply envelopes along with instructions on distributing the questionnaires. The other two representatives received the questionnaire via email. Several weeks after sending out the questionnaire, a follow-up email was sent to each of these representatives. It was thus determined that the representatives distributed a total of 354 Q-sort instruments to ES users, 128 of which were returned, demonstrating a 36.2% response rate from ES users who actually received the questionnaire. However, since a total of 480 questionnaires were sent out to user group representatives, there was a 26.7% overall response rate. ## Reliability and validity

The importance and role of validity in Q-methodology research has been the subject of a growing volume of debate. Construct validity, and hence also convergent and discriminant validity, as they are usually understood do not apply to Q-methodology, since Q-methodology focuses on correlations between people rather than variables. Previous theorizing on the subject points out that individuals’ responses are at issue rather than the operational definition of variables, and thus ‘The concept of validity has very little status [in Q-methodology] since there is no outside criterion for a person’s own point of view’ (Brown, 1980: 174–175). Although it has been suggested that a comparable Q-analysis and R-analysis suggests some degree of validity (Brouwer, 1992–1993), most Q-methodology research has treated construct validity as irrelevant since the methodology is striving to understand the relative points of view of respondents. One study argues that the reliability and validity of Q-methodology lies in the data rather than the measure and that ‘ascertaining construct or predictive validity are inappropriate and irrelevant’ (Dennis, 1988: 413). In this respect, Q-methodology is related strongly to qualitative research for its assumption that there is no substitute to a respondent’s point of view (Dennis, 1988). In obtaining a person’s viewpoint, ‘since there can be no validity without reliability, a demonstration of the former [validity] is sufficient to establish the latter [reliability] (Lincoln and Guba, 1985: 316).

As the goal for field surveys and questionnaires is typically to obtain a representative sample of a population for any given set of variables, for Q-methodology the goal is to obtain a representative sample of Q-sort statements from the concourse on the subject. Previous research has demonstrated that the highest probability of obtaining a representative sample of Q-sort statements is accomplished by deriving the statements from literature on the subject, interviews with people involved with the concourse, and feedback from domain experts regarding the content of the Q-sort instrument (Dennis, 1988). These criteria were satisfied in this study by (1) generating a set of Q-sort statements based on a review of literature and interviews with domain experts, (2) obtaining feedback from participants in the concourse of user resistance and management strategy from ES users, and (3) domain experts revising the Q-sort instrument. Also, since the interpretation of user types is based on the Q-factor analysis, the factor scores will also be provided so that each reader on their own may validate the researchers’ interpretation based on the same data.

## Analysis

Response bias was examined through a comparison of two sets of subsamples: early vs late respondents and respondents vs non-respondents, based on respondent demographics. Bias related to the ordering of the Q-sort statements, a form of common method variance in Q-methodology, was examined by randomizing the order of Q-sort statements for two different versions of the instrument and comparing the two groups with t-tests. These procedures revealed that there were no significant differences between any of these groups. Furthermore, all respondents were guaranteed anonymity and confidentiality. Since the questionnaires were mailed back in a business reply envelope without any identifying information, it ensured that respondents were kept confidential.

The Q-sorts were analyzed using PQMethod, software that is specifically designed to analyze Q-sort data, and is often preferred by researchers using Q-methodology. PQMethod provides comprehensive statistical support for Q-method studies, including choices of principal components analysis, Q-factor analysis, and related analytical options (Table 2). The Q-sorts for each respondent were combined and following previous research, the whole was analyzed using centroid Q-factor analysis with Varimax rotation (Thomas and Watson, 2002). Centroid Q-factor analysis extracts factors based on an analysis of the between-person correlation matrix. Since the factors are formed from inter-correlated groups of Q-sorts, each factor represents a ‘type’ of person. Inasmuch as the Varimax rotation method produces orthogonal factors, it maximizes a function that evaluates both the differentiation between user types and the homogeneity within user types. In the context of this study, the procedure operationally defines types of user resistance that are both internally consistent and unique in their relationships to one another.

Factors were retained for further examination based on three criteria: eigenvalues, interpretability of the factor, and the researchers’ judgment over whether the factor contributes significantly to our understanding of user resistance and associated management strategy (Brown, 1980; Thomas and Watson, 2002). Generally speaking, a factor is retained if its eigenvalue is greater than one, although it is not the absolute cut-off value in the selection of factors (Brown, 1986). It is also desirable that a factor be both interpretable and theoretically significant, as determined by the researchers’ best judgment, because it is possible both to extract a statistically significant factor that is uninterpretable and theoretically insignificant, and to fail to extract a theoretically significant factor using purely statistical criteria (Brown, 1980: 43).

## Results

The results are reported according to the guidelines of Q-methodology, which recommends that the analysis and interpretation of the Q-sort should contain (1) factor loading arrays; (2) normalized factor scores; and (3) the statement(s) on which arrays load (Brown, 1980; Thomas and Watson, 2002). With this information, the readers are able to check and replicate the researchers’ logic, thus minimizing any errant effects of the researchers’ judgment on the interpretation of factors.

Group 1 is characterized by an absence of resistant behaviors, and thus will be termed ‘Non-resistant group.’ This group identified various reasons for resistance and management strategies to minimize user resistance, but was the only group that did not exhibit any resistant behaviors.

Group 2 is characterized by the more resistant behaviors than any other group, and thus will be termed ‘Resistant group.’ Six of the seven behaviors highlighted are active behaviors, with only one behavior that is passive (procrastination). From management’s perspective, this is the group that is most resistant. In order to minimize the resistance, the top-three management strategies identified by this group are training, incentives, and a clear plan.

Group 3 is the first of two groups, along with Group 5, that is characterized primarily by the resistant behavior of complaining, and thus will be termed ‘Complainers 1.’ To minimize the complaining, management can provide better feedback, support, training, and a clear plan.

Group 4 is the first of two groups, along with Group 8, that is characterized by the resistant behavior of impatience, and thus will be termed ‘Impatient 1.’ This group identifies that better communication, a clearer plan, and management expertise would have been the most useful management strategies.

Group 5 is termed ‘Complainers 2,’ since it is also characterized primarily by complaining, as in Group 3. Group 5 is distinguished from Group 3 by their belief that training was not poor, and their preference for management strategies involving incentives, expertise, and customizations instead of provision of training, management support, and a clear implementation plan.

Group 6 is characterized by impatience, turnover intention, and actual turnover (quitting) as the most representative behaviors, and thus is termed ‘Quitters because of their intention to quit the job or actual turnover. This group identified management communication, training, and incentives as the most important management strategies that should have been implemented better.

Group 7 is characterized by the resistant behaviors of complaining and using the old system, and is thus termed ‘Passive Resisters’ because they complain and use the old system rather than confront a problem. This group identified five management strategies and had system complexity and technical problems as the top reasons for user resistance.

Group 8 is characterized by impatience as the top resistant behavior, and is thus termed ‘Impatient 2,’ accompanying Group 4. However, Group 8 is characterized by different reasons for user resistance and management strategies.

To further understand each of these groups, the qualitative portion of the questionnaire was analyzed to determine if these groups made sense based on the supporting qualitative data. The qualitative portion comprises three questions asking the reasons for why the respondents chose the most and least representative statements. Each of the eight groups had respondent statements that supported the results of the quantitative analysis shown in Table 3. Appendix B provides examples of qualitative quotes from each of the eight groups.

An additional analysis was conducted to examine if any of the demographic variables might have a statistically significant effect on the user group. The purpose of this post hoc analysis is to examine if any groups were characterized by any particular demographic information. Thus, an ANOVA was conducted to determine if there are demographics that have significant effects between factor groupings. Table 4 shows the result of this test, that no statistically significant effects were found at alpha ¼ 0.05, indicating that none of the groups have statistically significant differences from the demographics of the overall questionnaire. Thus a group such as the ‘Non-resistant group’ or the ‘Resistant Group’ did not have any specific demographic characteristics that distinguished them from other groups. The lack of demographic effects allows the interpretation of Groups 1–8 to be more easily generalizable to the larger population of ES users and implementations.

In order to examine the reasons for user resistance, the resistant behaviors, and the management strategies in more detail, z-scores were calculated to show which best characterized the implementation experiences of all the respondents. As shown in Table 5, the additional workload was the most significant reason for user resistance, followed by a lack of fit, technical problems, and changed jobs. In regards to resistant behaviors, challenging the management plan was the most representative of ES users’ experiences, followed by impatience, complaints, and then trying to use the old system. For management strategies, a clear concise plan was the most desirable management strategy for users. The second most desired strategy is for the managers to have more expertise in the system and in rolling out the system. The third most desired strategy is better top-down communication.

## Discussion and conclusion

This study examines the types of users, focusing on the characteristics of users, the types of resisting users, and the desired management strategies identified by these groups. There were eight groups that were examined, two of which had a greater degree of resistant behaviors. Due to the lack of other studies examining groups of resistant users, there are no other studies to which results can be compared. There clearly are groups of users that emerge from the analysis, which is consistent with previous studies that suggest types of users exist. This study sets the groundwork upon which future research can be built, showing that resistant groups exist, and describes the resistant groups.

From a manager’s perspective, knowing that various groups exist in an ES implementation can lead to strategies that better meet the needs of the various groups. For example, each of the eight groups identified in the results had a different set of reasons for user resistance. To some groups, a lack of input was important while to other groups, the uncertainty was important. Therefore, depending on the employees, some may want to be on a planning committee while others do not; others need some computer training classes while others do not; and some want to have more top-down communication while others do not mind having only minimal communication.

The management strategies that were shown to be the most desirable to users are a clear plan, management expertise, and top-down communication. Although each group has different preferences, these three were shown to be the most important overall to users. Managers should also be aware of the reasons for resistance that were most often present during implementations. The top-five reasons for ES implementation resistance, in order, are additional workload, lack of fit, technical problems, changed job, and system complexity. If possible, managers should try to minimize the potential problems that arise from these areas. For example, the problem of lack of fit could be minimized through spending more time to find the best system suitable to the organization and have organizational change management in place to alter any necessary processes prior to the system implementation.

As brought forth through the concourse development and the results, this study expands on previous research by providing a better understanding of why users resist an ES, the behaviors exhibited by users, and management strategies to minimize user resistance. Furthermore, this study has identified what management strategies are most desired by users, which should help to minimize the level of user resistance. This study also has explored resistant groups, providing information on various groups and the management strategies desired most by these groups. Not only do these contributions address areas not examined by previous research, but also they provide information about types of resistant groups upon which future research can build.

Despite the collection of various demographic data, one surprising finding was that there were no respondent demographics identified that differentiated the groups of users. Although this could be due to insufficient statistical power, the implication is that both resisters and nonresisters exist from all demographic backgrounds. For example, age, gender, education level, and years with employer do not affect the level of resistance or the user group that best fits an employee.

One potential limitation with using Q-methodology is that Q-methodology is more concerned with the degree to which the concourse is represented rather than the general population of people. The relative proportions of types of people in the general population are usually unknown, since the unit of analysis is the concourse and its factors rather than individuals or groups of individuals. The percentage of variance explained by a Q-factor, therefore, is an indication of the percentage of the total number of Q-sorts that comprise that factor. However, if the respondents are also representative of the general population of people, then generalizations may additionally be made with respect to the relative proportions of each factor in the general population, in this case ES users. Based on both the development and analysis of the Q-sort instrumentation and the characteristics of the respondents in the study, we believe that this research also satisfies the dual criterion, and generalizations may be made about both the concourse and its factors, and the general population of ES users.

Another potential limitation is the generalizability since a convenience sample was used. Packets of questionnaires and business reply envelopes were distributed to members of various ES user groups. The users who received the questionnaire from these user group members were not randomly selected among the general population; rather, they were people known to the user group members who may have filled it out as a favor to the user group member. The result is that certain groups may have been underrepresented, such as small businesses that do not have a user group member. Thus, although the respondents represented many different positions within many different organizations, it may not be representative of the overall population. To minimize the impact of this limitation, user groups from various ES vendors were selected in order to represent a wide variety of businesses that implement an ES.

The richness of the ES environment provides a fullbodied picture of user resistance and associated management strategy. This study reveals both the existence and identity of various types of end-user resistance and their respective preferred management strategies. Concourse Theory and the Q-methodology provide theoretical and methodological foundations from which to interpret the meaning and structure of resistance types that are most likely to be encountered by managers, and a foundation for subsequent research on the subject. The results are of interest both to practitioners who are involved in system implementation and researchers involved in user resistance and adoption.
