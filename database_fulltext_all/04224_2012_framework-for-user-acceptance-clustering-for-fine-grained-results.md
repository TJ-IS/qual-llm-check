# Framework for user acceptance: Clustering for fine-grained results

Pieter Devolder <sup>a,</sup>\*, Bram Pynoo <sup>a</sup>, Bart Sijnave <sup>a</sup>, Tony Voet <sup>a</sup>, Philippe Duyck <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Ghent University Hospital, Belgium <sup>b</sup> AZ Nikolaas, Belgium

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 4 June 2009 Accepted 4 July 2011 Available online 1 June 2012

Keywords: Technology acceptance FFM TRI UTAUT Clustering Big Five

## A B S T R A C T

In an attempt to uncover intra-group behavior similarities, we developed an open multi-level framework for understanding the process of technology acceptance by its users. We partitioned our population into groups by clustering at several levels and then for each level it was divided into subgroups with a measurement layer added to uncover subgroup influence. Thus, by intersecting the resulting clusters of the set of models, the population was divided into subgroups that have similarities in the factors measured by the cluster layer models. Subsequently we tested our framework in a university hospital setting; personality and prior technology background models were used in clustering via the Five Factor Model and the Technology Readiness Index. UTAUT was used in the measurement layer. Our hypothesis that the subgroups have differing degrees of explained variance and different predictors was confirmed. Our framework was open, because any model that results in a taxonomy of the population can be used to obtain meaningful clusters.

## 1. Introduction

Measuring user acceptance of technology is a way of determining the user’s intentions toward using a new technology. Implementers could benefit by knowing the potential pitfalls of adopting a new way of working and anticipate to avoid excessive implementation costs.

Technology acceptance models can be used to determine which factors are adaptable and changeable so that value is realized. Models that successfully predict user behavior are thus of interest to all potential users of a new technology.

These factors have led to work such as that of Legris et al. [6], who suggested the incorporation of organizational and social factors into TAM-based models and Pynoo et al. [9], who showed that dynamic effects over time influenced changes in the factors, partly due to a rise in experience level of the users.

Questions about the foundations of TAM have also been raised and research [10] has been proposed to discover its underlying concepts. However, users of IT exist who (based on e.g., their personality type, job description, etc.) display a different behavior of acceptance, especially when smaller subgroups co-exist with larger ones; apparently the larger subgroups influence the smaller ones and the smaller subgroup’s influence is likely to be ignored, though present; this diminishes the power of the overall model.

We believe that the variance in the dependent variable for the subgroup was absorbed in the overall variance of the entire population. Therefore, we proposed a generic framework that could act as a population divider to dissect this variance.

Our research question was therefore:

Does subgrouping the overall population of technology users allow us to determine the predictors that are essential for describing a particular subgroup’s behavior?

## 2. Framework definition

The framework we proposed was a generic open one in which different models could be incorporated to form an overall analysis model for technology acceptance. It had two layers one for clustering and the other for measurement.

The purpose of the clustering layer was to cluster entities into subgroups of the input population, while the measurement layer used the subgroups and subjected each to the model that it used.

Fig. 1 shows how the population is filtered into subgroups based on the characteristics of each respondent (e.g., Filter 1 clusters according to type of image, Filter 2 on color).

The framework can be seen as a process through which all subject data must flow in order to become meaningful information. Our first aim of the cluster layer was to obtain a close to normal distribution of the input variables of the measurement model so that statistical boundary conditions for the analysis were better met. Conceptually, we tried to reduce the variability in the subpopulations with a reduction of the variability caused by the constructs of the cluster. Due to the cluster layer, a more homogeneous group of people could be examined so that the variance explained by the measurement model would be more meaningful for the target group. The variance explained by the measurement model thus became more interesting as variability was diminished.

Our final aim was to pinpoint which groups of people (based on criteria defined in the cluster layer) existed within the overall population that had different reactions to the measurement model. Different predictors for different subgroups could then enhance the specificity of measurement models and ensure that they were useful IT management tools, rather than ad hoc instruments.

## 3. Framework implementation instance

The implementation of the framework involves the choice of both the cluster and measurement layer models. The aim of our example was to assess technology acceptance among the nursing staff of a university hospital. In this instance of the framework we wanted to make sure that there was a distinction between a users’ individual traits and beliefs as opposed to a mere technology implementation context.

While implementing technology the aspects that are within the realm of influence of the technology implementers are chiefly found in the implementation context. It would, therefore, be interesting if specific actions could be defined for subgroups with similar behavior in technology implementation.

The measurement layer thus defined the actions that could be taken for certain subgroups. The cluster layer had to funnel similar types of individuals into a subgroup.

## 3.1. Defining the measurement layer

The core of the framework’s measurement layer was UTAUT, which identifies which constructs overlap between prior technology acceptance models while keeping the number of scale questions limited.

## 3.2. Defining the cluster layer

3.2.1. Theoretical addition of personality and technology background Behavior patterns have been found to be affected by the personality of the subject and the person’s setting. In a study of how users conducted themselves when searching for information,

Heinstrom [5] found that personality traits, as described by the Five Factor Model, interacted with context-related factors, and that they jointly affected information seeking behavior. In the field of technology acceptance, Devaray et al. [1] found that personality factors could predict users’ acceptance beliefs and attitudes. Research in the IS-field has acknowledged the effect of individual traits; e.g., personality has been found to affect personal innovativeness in IT [8].

## 3.2.2. Personality models for the cluster layer

Carl Jung’s work on individual’s personality led to the Five Factor Model known as the Big Five, consisting of Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism.

A major drawback of assessing personality traits is the large number of questions that must be included in the research questionnaires. One solution to this is in issuing questionnaires in which the scale is reduced to a reliable short questionnaire (e.g., the Ten Item Personality Inventory [4]), thereby allowing for personality assessment where administering the complete questionnaire would be too much.

## 3.2.3. Technology background models for the cluster layer

Recent research has shown that some highly used attitude scales have become outdated [3], so we chose to incorporate the technology readiness index (TRI). The idea of integrating technology acceptance research and technology readiness was first suggested by Lin et al. [7] in their TRAM, which integrated the Technology Readiness Index and TAM.

## 3.3. The TRI-FFM-UTAUT model

Our model incorporates a two-dimensional cluster and measurement layer concept with:

\- The cluster layer consisting of:

 The Five Factor Model (FFM) (for describing the personality context).

 The TRI (for describing the technology beliefs).

\- The measurement layer consisting of the UTAUT model (for describing IS-specific beliefs).

We posited that the technology acceptance of subjects with different personality types cross-linked with different types of technology experience (determined by FFM and TRI) would have different degrees of explained variance (Hypothesis 1) and be predicted by other constructs by their position in the technology experience-personality type plane (Hypothesis 2).

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Setting

The technology under scrutiny by our method was the electronic patient record (EPR) used in the Ghent University Hospital. The system has been under development for over 12 years with new functionality added in a continuously evolving improvement/learning cycle. The hospital has more than 1000 beds and is the second-largest single campus hospital in Belgium. The population selected for our study was all nursing personnel using the EPR. The population was compiled from data from the personnel services cross-linked with data of the nursing department recording actual users of the software within the population.

A total of 528 questionnaires were sent out to the population and 204 were returned resulting in a net response rate of 39% (the total number of nursing staff was around 1700 but only about 30% used the EPR). Further additions to the software will make it applicable to a broader range of nursing tasks.

## 4.2. Operationalization of our model

We opted to administer all scales of the three models of our research model in order to be able to analyze each model separately. This resulted in a questionnaire that collected data on the following items/scales:

\- Demographic information

 Date of answering the questionnaire

 Gender

 Department or ward within the hospital

 Age

 Duration of EPR use

\- 36 questions making up the TRI-scales

\- 31 questions making up the UTAUT-scales

\- 10 questions making up the Ten Item Personality Inventory, a brief measure of the Five Factor Model

\- Unstructured space in which to comment on both study and the EPR.

In our study we chose to probe for the following constructs associated with technology acceptance:

\- attitude toward using the IS – ATT

\- behavioral intention – BI

\- self-reported use – USE

The questionnaire was constructed utilizing the scales found in the three models (TRI, UTAUT and TIPI) with an added question regarding the self-reported use.

## 4.3. Administration of the questionnaire

Questionnaires were administered on paper. Each member of the target population was sent a copy with a letter introducing the study and stating its relevance for those using the software. Questionnaire delivery and return were accomplished by using the internal mail service of the hospital. All questionnaires were sent out on June 2nd, 2008 and the final return date was set as July 31st, 2008.

## 4.4. Visual analogue scale

The questionnaire utilized visual analogue scales. It provided the subjects with a line anchored from ‘‘completely disagree’ to ‘‘completely agree’’. We asked the users to mark the bar to indicate their response. This method was chosen to provide a spontaneous response from the subjects. Each entry was measured and coded as a percentage by measuring the distance of the 0-point of the line up to the mark made by the respondent. Thus it was the equivalent of a 101-point Likert scale.

## 4.5. Pre-test

In order to administer the questionnaires in the hospital staff, it was necessary to translate all scales into the native language of the respondents. Therefore, all scales were translated into Dutch. To make sure that the questionnaires were understandable and unambiguous, a pre-test was executed among respondents who were not taking part in the survey; it was distributed to 16 employees belonging to the Informatics Department during May, 2008. Their responses were collected and appropriate changes were made to two items in the questionnaire.

## 4.6. Results and analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS16. Factor loadings necessary to compute convergent and discriminant validity were extracted using AMOS 6.

## 4.7. Reliability and validity

Convergent and discriminant validity were checked: convergent validity (CV) was assumed to be achieved once the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) exceeded its normally accepted 0.5 threshold. Discriminant validity was achieved if the squared crosscorrelations between constructs were shown to be smaller than either of the constructs’ AVE.

Convergent validity was computed for all scales with more than two items in the scale. It ranged between 0.30 and 0.79. Convergent validity values are shown in Table 1: Convergent validity. The lower AVE value of FC can be explained, since it covers a broad scope of external conditions that influence IS acceptance. Similarly, SI is known to be composed of two different dimensions in social influence: peer-influence and superior-influence (which may cause a lower value in CV [2]). None of the TRI-scales attained the 0.5 level indicating that the validity of these constructs was questionable. Regardless of this, TRI was utilized for its intended purpose, i.e., installing a typology among the population about their technology readiness.

Discriminant validity was shown to exist between all constructs, with two minor exceptions:

1. FC (AVE = 0.37) and EE (AVE = 0.67) while their squared correlation was 0.42.

2. Insecurity (AVE = 0.38) and discomfort (AVE = 0.30) from the TRI-model had a squared correlation of 0.36).

## 4.8. Application of the clustering layer

## 4.8.1. Cluster analysis of Five Factor Model (FFM)

Table 2 shows the definition of the Block-taxonomies for FFM. A ‘–’ sign means average values for this measure, a ‘down arrow’ indicates relatively lower values for this measure while an ‘up arrow’ indicates relatively higher values for this measure.

A taxonomy of three groups can be found in populations when the FFM is administered. Other taxonomies have been suggested but the one utilized here was chosen as it has been replicated in numerous studies. Using our data, we investigated whether this taxonomy could be replicated. Mean values of the constructs of the Five Factor Model were computed for each respondent. Threemeans cluster analysis was subsequently used on these variables.

Resilients are adjusted to function well on all factors, i.e., high scores on extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness and openness combined with a low score on neuroticism. Undercontrollers generally score low on agreeableness and conscientiousness together with being neurotic. Overcontrollers, on the other hand, score high on agreeableness and conscientiousness but low on extraversion.

## 4.9. Application of the measurement layer

UTAUT was next applied in the measurement layer on the subgroups defined by the cluster layer. Then multiple regression analyses are performed on these subgroups for all three dependent variables. Three sets of analyses were run. First, we executed two one-dimensional cluster layer definitions where TRI and FFMsubgroups were used as population for the UTAUT-model. Following the one-dimensional cluster, we combined both clusters to obtain a more fine-grained view of the cluster.

## 4.9.1. No clusters

The first rows of Tables 5 and 6 show the overall UTAUTregression results for the entire population. Regressions were performed with USE, BI, and ATT as dependent variables in order to answer the question:Which variable is best suited to describe technology acceptance in a medical environment?

The model on USE was not significant whereas the model on BI resulted in a significant model with no viable significant predictors. The overall model on ATT showed that both performance expectancy (PE) and effort expectancy (EE) were strong significant predictors of technology acceptance.

## 4.9.2. TRI cluster

When incorporating the TRI cluster layer into the model, the dependent variables Use and BI produced no viable results, but a different result emerged when looking at ATT. Table 5 shows regression results for subgroups based on TRI. Both explorers and pioneers were predicted by the expected performance of the technology (PE), regardless of the effort (EE) needed to use it. The beta regression coefficient for explorers showed that the PE effect was approximately half that of pioneers. Skeptics and paranoids showed significant prediction on the EE construct with skeptics being affected more strongly. To a lesser degree and with significance level $p < 0 . 1$ , PE can facilitate both groups technology acceptance and provide facilitating conditions for paranoids. Laggards show highly significant beta coefficients on both PE and EE. The negative coefficient for FC implies that it can be counterproductive in stressful conditions.

## 4.9.3. FFM cluster

Table 6 shows either non-significant models or explained variance levels (adj. $R ^ { 2 } )$ that are below par for the dependent variables USE and BI. Analysis of ATT on the other hand showed that subgrouping on personality differed from the results of the nocluster situation.

Resilient subjects were most affected by EE followed by PE. This was also found to be true for overcontrollers. The only difference between the two groups existed in the social influence (SI) factor for resilients whose SI was only significant at $\texttt { a } p < 0 . 1$ level. However, the relative importance of EE and PE switched for undercontrolling types, with PE becoming a very strong indicator of technology acceptance. Facilitating conditions were not a significant predictor when subgrouping for personality.

## 4.10. Combined FFM-TRI clusters

When combining the two cluster groups, the subject count in the resulting subgroups decreased and jeopardized regression (see Table 7). Nevertheless, regressions were performed for all subgroups. Significant models were shown in Table 8 together with predictors that were of influence for the subgroup.

Resilient explorers were not affected by either performance or effort expectancy toward the new technology. The only predictor close to significant was SI $( \beta _ { \mathrm { S I } } = 0 . 4 0 , ~ p = 0 . 1 1 )$ . Pioneers were mostly described by their PE value. Nonetheless, when examining the fine-grained detail in the data, we observed that different types of pioneers existed. Resilient pioneers can be compared to resilient explorers with high SI coefficients $( \beta _ { \mathrm { S I } } = 0 . 6 2 , p = 0 . 0 3 )$ whereas undercontrolling pioneers were not influenced by their social environment. Their technology acceptance is best predicted in terms of PE. Overcontrolling pioneers have a different tendency when social pressure mounts; their attitude toward utilizing the system tends to deteriorate $( \beta _ { \mathrm { { S I } } } = - 0 . 4 4 0 , ~ p = 0 . 0 6 )$ . With this subgroup the negative effect of additional SI is comparable to the positive effect of PE $( \beta _ { \mathrm { P E } } = 0 . 5 1 , p = 0 . 0 1 5 )$ . ## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. TRI-FFM-UTAUT model

## 5.1.1. Results

Different types of people do respond differently in the process of adopting new technology, validating both Hypothesis 1 and 2. As a result, changing or adapting the method of addressing these people according to their subgroup may be an effective ploy.

This model allows organizations that implement new technology to assess which subgroups are important, making it possible to target them on issues that encourage them toward using a new technology.

Our results also showed that if groups are not subdivided, using the method of promoting the use of technology can have a positive effect on one subgroup but a negative effect on another.

The results of our TRI-FFM-UTAUT model were interesting for subgroups that may not accept a new technology, e.g., a laggard group may never adopt a technology unless they are forced to do so.

## 5.1.2. Managerial implications

The results of our model suggested that organizations should adapt the technology implementation process to the individual user. However, as treating each member of staff separately makes the system complex, our model splits the staff into subgroups which could be treated separately. Then our model becomes a viable management tool by showing which groups need specific attention; thus serving as a filter that directs specific training to those who best benefit from it.

## 5.2. Cluster layer–measurement layer framework

A limitation of the framework and consequently in every model utilizing it can be found in the potential non-orthogonality of the cluster layer constructs. In an ideal situation, changing factors in one cluster layer should not affect factors in another layer. However, small deviations of the level of orthogonality will not present a problem for cluster production. Large deviations of orthogonality, i.e., two cluster layers measuring essentially the same constructs could lead to large differences in subgroup counts and thus render the clustering layer useless.

## 6. Conclusion

An open framework was constructed allowing us to split users into subgroups displaying similar behavior toward technology acceptance. It consisted of multiple clustering layers which divided the population. The measurement layer uncovered meaningful influences of specific subgroups.

An instance of the framework was presented and tested in a university hospital setting. We incorporated personality factors and technology background to assess how subgroups devised on these models behaved under UTAUT. As hypothesized, the subgroups had different degrees of explained variance and were predicted by different sets of constructs.

This implies that populations in organizations that are undergoing technological change should be addressed in a personalized way so that the new technology is accepted. Our framework and model could then act as a strategic tool for people responsible for IT choice and its introduction into the organization.
