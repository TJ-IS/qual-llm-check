# An activity theoretic analysis of the mediating role of information systems in tackling climate change adaptation Helen Hasan,\* Stephen Smith<sup>†</sup> & Patrick Finnegan<sup>‡</sup>

\*Australian Health Services Research Institute, University of Wollongong, Wollongong, New South Wales, Australia, email: hasan@uow.edu.au, <sup>†</sup>School of Computing, Macquarie University, Sydney, New South Wales, Australia, email: stephen.smith@mq.edu.au, and <sup>‡</sup>UNSW Business School, UNSW Australia, Sydney, Australia, email: p.<sup>fi</sup>nnegan@unsw.edu.au

Abstract. This paper demonstrates that information systems (IS) researchers and practitioners can make a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution to the grand challenge of sustainability in light of global climate change. In doing so, the paper takes a novel perspective by going beyond the dominant emphasis in the Green IS literature on climate change mitigation to focus on climate change adaptation. To demonstrate how IS researchers and practitioners can engage with the grand challenge of sustainability, we report the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of an investigation into the role of IS in climate change adaptation programmes of the government of New South Wales, Australia. Canonical action research, informed by activity theory, proved to be an appropriate methodology for this investigation by combining iterative collaborative engagement and rigorous scholarly re<sup>fl</sup>ection. Activity theory has previously been successfully used in IS research as a framework for inquiry and description but not for prediction. This raised questions, addressed in this study, about whether or not activity theory could be used to guide interventions and make sense of their impact. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal how activity theory provides an appropriate balance between scope and detail to accommodate the complex processes of planning and implementing climate change adaptation programmes. We conclude that while climate adaptation is complex, activity theory, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally <sup>fi</sup>ve dynamic dimensions for deep sense-making, can inform interventions in climate change adaptation projects. Most signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, we demonstrate that IS experts can make a positive contribution to addressing one of the most important grand challenges of our time.

Keywords: climate change adaptation, Green IS, activity theory, grand challenges, canonical action research

## INTRODUCTION

Winter & Butler (2011) claim that ‘IS research is undervalued, at least in part, because as a community we fail to engage the full range and scale of problems to which our work and knowl edge is relevant’ (p. 99). Instead, we have historically focused on narrow research agendas and are particularly prone to studying familiar and tractable problems. As one of the grand challenges of our time, sustainability in light of global climate change is a complex problem that demands research that is holistic, interdisciplinary and innovative. This can be challenging for information systems (IS) researchers, as it rejects the dominant reductionist methodologica paradigm (cf. Walsham, 2012). Nevertheless, numerous IS researchers have turned their attention to the issue of climate change, spawning an area of investigation referred to as Green IS. The focus of Green IS research has been on the potential of IS to contribute to efforts that mitigate climate change (e.g. Watson et al., 2010; Melville, 2010; Derick, 2010; Elliot, 2011; Jenkin et al., 2011; Vom Brocke et al., 2012; Loeser et al., 2013). Climate change mitigation is typically concerned with the reduction of carbon emissions, energy ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, recycling or technology in novation (Aldy et al., 2010), focusing on ‘technological change and substitution that reduce resource inputs and emissions per unit of output’ (IPCC, 2007, p. 84).

In addition to attempting to mitigate climate change, there is a need for society to adapt to climate change. The context for adaptation is the belief that it may already be too late to prevent some effects of climate change (IPCC, 2014) and that governments, organizations and citizens must engage in actions that ensure sustainability in light of such changes (Ayers & Dodman, 2010; Bedsworth & Hanak, 2010; Tessa & Kurukulasuriya, 2010). Climate change adaptation thus refers to initiatives and measures to reduce the yulnerability of natural and human systems against actual or expected climate change effects’ (IPCC, 2007, p. 76). Climate change adap tation activities are aimed at protecting citizens over decades into the future (Adger et al., 2005) and manifest in many ways including long-term planning (Bedsworth & Hanak, 2010; Lisø, 2006), technology transfer (Tessa & Kurukulasuriya, 2010), emergency management (Francisco, 2008), population movement (Mimura, 1999) and refugee management (Docherty & Giannini, 2009). However, climate change adaptation has largely been ignored in the Green IS literature. Where adaptation is mentioned, it is almost exclusively with respect to the adaptation of organizational cultures to the restriction of imposed mitigation efforts (e.g. Bengtsson & Ågerfalk, 2011; Seidel et al., 2013).

This paper responds to the grand challenge of sustainability in light of global climate change, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the call by Watson et al. (2010) for ‘IS researchers…to demonstrate how the transformative power of IS can be leveraged to create an ecologically sustainable society’ (p. 23). To do so, we address the question of how IS researchers and practitioners can make a positive contribution to climate change adaptation by seeking to determine the potential of IS to impact, support and transform the planning and execution of climate change adaptation activities. We conducted a canonical action research (CAR) study involving the Climate Change Working Group (CCWG) of the government of New South Wales (NSW), Australia. The CCWG was formed in 2009 to drive the NSW Action Plan on Climate Change, which mandates the need to ‘prepare NSW for unavoidable changes in our climate’ (DECCW, 2010, p. 1). The adaptation activities of the CCWG focused on a wide range of both chronic problems (e.g. sea-level rise, temperature increases, population management and food production) and critical threats (e.g. <sup>fl</sup>ooding, storm, <sup>fi</sup>re and heat waves) that may arise from climate change.

The link between the research and problem-solving cycles of the action research was provided by one of the authors who, as a member of the CCWG, was charged with advising and reporting on IS implications of and requirements for adaptation programmes.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst phase of the study was to determine if, as believed, climate change adaptation should be considered a complex problem. To do so, complexity theory was used as our instru mental theory (cf. Davison et al., 2012). The results revealed that, while mitigation activities can be regarded as complicated, adaptation activities are often truly complex (Kim & Kaplan, 2006; Snowden, 2002). This is because adaptation activities both depend on extensive planning for the uncertain and turbulent high-velocity conditions anticipated in the future and involve compli cated modelling using large and diverse data sets. Complicated systems, although composed of many intricate parts, can be understood through careful examination so that their future be haviour can be predicted. Complex systems, on the other hand, are ‘composed of populations of interacting entities where the overall system behaviour is not prede<sup>fi</sup>ned but rather emerges through the interactions of its entities’ (Kim & Kaplan, 2006, p. 37).

The second phase of the study aimed at resolving the complexity inherent in climate change ad aptation through the lens of the Engeström activity framework (Engeström, 1987). This lens, adopted from activity theory (Leontiev, 1981), focuses on contextual tool-mediated activity. We chose activity theory as our focal theory (cf. Davison et al., 2012) because of its holistic coverage of the phenomenon of interest and because adaptation, by its essential nature, involves action and change. Activity theory has previously been successfully used in IS research as a framework for inquiry and description but not for prediction. This raised questions about whether or not activity theory could be used to guide interventions and make sense of their impact beyond activity theory’s traditional reliance on the Engeström framework. We <sup>fi</sup>rst applied the Engeström activity framework in a manner consistent with previous use (e.g. Chen et al., 2013) to analyse the role of IS in providing tools to support activities. However, we uncovered the limitations of this approach in dealing with the complexity of climate change adaptation. Following an extensive review of the use of activity theory in disciplines that leverage Vygotsky’s (1978) original work in the cultural historical tradition, we enhanced our focal theory with <sup>fi</sup>ve dynamic dimensions to identify and explain some of the less tangible and emergent features of climate change adaptation activities. Doing so provided an appropriate balance between scope and detail to accommodate the complex processes of planning and implementing climate change adaptation programmes. We conclude that while climate adaptation is complex, activity theory, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally <sup>fi</sup>ve dynamic dimensions for deep sense-making, can inform interventions in climate change adaptation projects. Most signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, we demonstrate that IS experts can make a positive contribution to addressing one of the most im portant grand challenges of our time.

## THEORETICAL GROUNDING

This section presents the instrumental and focal theories used in our study. These, respectively, are complexity theory and activity theory. Within a CAR study, an instrumental theory is used to provide an explanation of the phenomenon of interest in order to improve understanding of the problem. Once the problem is understood, a focal theory is used to provide the intellectual basis for solving the problem (Davison et al., 2012).

## Complexity theory

It is important to distinguish complex phenomena from the merely complicated, as ‘when you make the complicated simple, you make it better, but when you make the complex simple, you make it wrong.’ (Gray, 2009). To assist with this diagnosis, complexity theory provides ways to identify those problems that are complex by exploring their characteristics through a com plexity lens.

Complexity theory is concerned with the behaviour, over time and space, of systems or networks where the number of interconnected and interacting components is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large that a complete description of all the relationships between them is impractical and of no assistance in understanding the behaviour of the system (Cilliers. 1998). Characteristics of complex sys tems include multiple interacting dimensions, connectivity, interdependence, feedback, explora tion of the space of possibilities. self-organization. co-evolution. historicity where past behaviours randomly affect future directions, emergence, non-linearity, equilibrium, chaos and path dependence (Mitleton-Kelly, 2003; Kauffman, 1993; Kauffman, 1995). In the presence of these characteristics, even small differences in initial conditions can yield widely diverging outcomes, thereby rendering long-term prediction impossible (Hilborn, 2004). Furthermore, be cause of the presence of complex interdependencies between components of the phenomenon, the effort to solve one aspect of a complex problem may reveal or create other problems (Mitleton-Kelly, 2003).

Information systems development can be conceptualized as a complex system characterized by the emergence of co-evolutionary, self-organized behaviour, structure and order through the interaction of interconnected autonomous agents in a time-paced rhythm balancing at the edge of chaos (Vidgen & Wang, 2006). Drawing on concepts of complexity science and theory from Kauffman (1993; 1995), Holland (1995; 1998) and Benbya & McKelvey (2006) identify changing user requirements, changing organizational needs, changing external competitive conditions, increased interdependencies among the involved individuals, organizations and technologies and the rapid evolution of IS and information technology (IT) as sources of complexity in IS development. Thus, and of particular relevance to our study, stakeholders may hold contradictory but valid views of a complex problem and how it should be solved. Consequently, complex problems are not ‘solved’ in the usual sense of the term but are resolved (or dissolved) through syn thesis (Rittel & Webber, 1973).

On this basis, we identify four categories of the characteristics of complexity that we use to explain and understand the complexity of climate change adaptation programmes:

i Multiple interacting elements: Characteristics include large, complex, adaptive systems with interdependent elements and feedback loops, human actors and evolving technical tools.

ii Distributed con<sup>fl</sup>icted lines of authority: Characteristics include distributed control, autonomous agents and the challenges of synthesizing the agendas of multiple stakeholders.

iii Unpredictable dynamics and emergence: Characteristics include emergence, non-linearity, self-organization, historicity and the unpredictable effects of changed initial conditions. All relate to an indeterminate future.

iv Contradictory requirements: Characteristics include incomplete, contradictory and changing requirements that prohibit the setting of precise goals and measurable outcomes in the planning process.

## Activity theory

Once a phenomenon is diagnosed as complex, an action research study must apply appropriate non-reductionist methodologies and theories that retain the complexity of the problem throughout the investigation rather than attempting to simplify it as would be the case with a complicated problem. Activity theory, rooted in the Russian cultural historical psychology of the 1920s (Vygotsky, 1978), has the holistic and systemic capacity to cope with complex phenomena involving mediated human activities in dynamic and diverse contexts (Engeström, 2000; Blackler et al., 2000; Kaptelinin & Nardi, 2006; Timmis, 2014; Kuutti, 1996).

An activity is a purposeful, mediated, contextual dialectic relationship between subject and object or, put more simply, a person or group of people (subjects) using the affordances of tools (physical or psychological) as they work at something (the object) to achieve some purpose within a community (Kuutti, 1991, 1996; Kuutti & Virkkunen, 1995; Hasan et al., 1998, 2001; Blackler & Regan, 2009,).

The Engeström activity system, or framework (Figure 1), shows how work activity, in addition to being mediated by tools, is also affected (mediated) by (i) other members of a community, (ii) the rules associated with the activity and (iii) the division of labour (roles) related to the activity. It also distinguishes the outcome of an activity, both intended and unintended, from its object or purpose. Indeed, one of the key aspects of an activity theoretic analysis, and one of the most challenging, is acknowledging the cultural–historical context within which each activity occurs (Engeström, 2001).

In recent decades, digital technology has attracted the attention of activity theorists as a too that has transformed human activity and has itself been transformed as those human activities have evolved (Meloche & Hasan, 2008; Kaptelinin, 1996). Activity theory has also gained the attention of IS researchers, who appreciate its <sup>fi</sup>t with the mediating role of IS in most human activity. There are several collections of papers on the use of activity theory in IS (Bertelsen & Bødker, 2000; Nardi, 1996; Whymark & Hasan, 2005; Hasan et al., 1998, 2001, 2003), and some have attempted to provide guidelines for its future application (Grace, 2012; Quek & Shah, 2004; Kaptelinin & Nardi, 2006; Crawford & Hasan, 2006). However, with a few exceptions (e.g. Allen et al., 2013; Crawford & Hasan, 2006; Kuutti, 1996), the application of activity theory within IS studies has followed a traditional reductionist approach to the design, develop ment and evaluation of ICT-based systems (e.g. Waycott et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2013; Hasan & Gould, 2001; Hasan & Pfaff, 2012; Korpela et al., 2000; Kuutti & Vikkunen, 1995).

A typical use of activity theory in IS research is that displayed by Chen et al. (2013) in their post-analysis study of emergency management. Using Engeström’s (1987, 1999) framework (Figure 1) as their unit of analysis, they identify key activities and describe those activities using the elements of the Engeström framework. They add environment/context and timeline as ele ments and report those elements as an extension to activity theory. While these elements represent an extension to the Engeström framework, they are consistent with the original Vygotskian cultural–historical tradition, which directs that context and history (the timeline) are essential to the nature of an activity.

While the Engeströmian approach to activity theory which is dominant in IS research may be appropriate for well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned, completed projects and bounded situations, the work of Chen et al. (2013) and others suggests that other aspects of the cultural–historical tradition, which have become embedded in the interpretation of activity theory in other disciplines, are required to deal with more complex situations. In order to develop an appropriate focal theory to cope with the complexity of climate change adaptation activi ties, we found it necessary to include <sup>fi</sup>ve such aspects, which we refer to as dynamic di mensions of activity theory. These are as follows:

i the hierarchical representation of an activity (Leontiev, 1981),

ii the implication of the dialectic nature of the subject–object relationship (Vygotsky, 1978; Kaptelinen, 2005),

iii the two-way nature and three types of tool mediation (Hasan et al., 1998),

iv learning in the zone of proximal development (ZPD) (Vygotsky, 1978) and

v the tertiary tensions and contradictions between different instantiations of an activity over time (Engeström, 1999).

## The hierarchical representation of activity – zooming in and out

According to Leontiev (1981), activity is a holistic, high-level, usually collaborative construct, not to be confused with more everyday uses of the word ‘activity’ in English. An activity sits at the top of a hierarchy above goal-oriented actions and underlying operations (Figure 2). The Leontiev hierarchy is dynamic in two ways. Routinization and automation can move an activity downward, making it an action or operation. Conversely, a breakdown in an activity can move an operation upward to be a conscious action or a purposeful activity. This dynamic aspect of the hierarchy is of relevance to our work and is referred to as zooming in and out (Mursu et al., 2007).

From an activity theory perspective, a complex situation can be depicted in terms of motiveoriented activities. This contrasts with a great deal of complicated empirical research that focuses on what Leontiev would categorize as goal-oriented actions, ignoring the higher level of activity where motives and purpose are evident. What is one person’s activity can be another person’s action or even operation. This difference in perspective can be used to zoom in and out and understand, for example, why the grand organizational vision, so important to the executives, does not have much meaning for those lower down, or why decisions of the government ignore the perspectives of individual citizens.

## The subject–object dialectic

The core-de<sup>fi</sup>ning concept of activity has evolved through the work of Vygotsky (1978), Leontiev (1981) and Engeström (1987) to be the dialectic relationship between subject and object. The object of an activity has a dual (dialectic) status; it is both a projection of the human mind onto the objective world and a projection of the world onto the human mind (Katelinin, 2005). In this dialectic relationship, the subject learns and grows, while the object is interpreted, and subjectively reinterpreted, by the subject in the on-going conduct of an activity. Therefore, all stakeholders have their own subjective understanding of the object of an activity, distinct from the obvious objective view, and they continually revisit that understanding over time (Kaptelinin, 2005).

For acceptable outcomes of complex problems, such as climate change adaptation, those charged with making decisions need to appreciate the diverse and changing subjective views of stakeholders. When dealing with complex decisions, data can be found and manipulated to support any position in the climate change debate, and there are many instances of the misuse of information extracted from biased views of the data. A dialectic synthesis of opposing subjective interpretations of information is thus important in helping the individual rebalance con-<sup>fl</sup>icting views.

The two-way, three-dimensional view of mediation between tools and activities Most IS studies using activity theory focus on the mediation of an activity by digital tools (Chen et al., 2013). They rarely appreciate that mediation is a two-way concept where the capability and availability of tools mediate what can be performed, and tools, in turn, are modified as more is demanded from them. Tools thus evolve to hold the historical knowledge of how the community behaves and is organized (Crawford & Hasan, 2006). This is particularly powerful when the tools are computer based, as the evolution of these tools occurs at an unprecedented rate (Kaptelinin, 1996). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, three types of tools mediate human activity:

• primary (material) tools: artefacts, instruments, machines, computers, and so on;

• secondary (psychological) tools: language, signs, ideas, models, and so on; and

• tertiary (socio-cultural) tools: cultural systems, environments, contexts, communities, virtua spaces, and so on (Hasan et al., 1998).

Primary tools produce changes in the material object, psychological tools in<sup>fl</sup>uence the psyche and behaviour of subjects (Verenikina & Gould, 1998) and socio-cultural tools set boundaries on activities due to the culture, environment and context (Hasan et al., 1998). The latte are represented on the Engeström framework as ‘community’. However, Vygotsky’s (1978) original position treated all types of tools as mediators, as they all in<sup>fl</sup>uence the way activities unfold and evolve as they mediate activities. This richer two-way, three-dimensional view of too mediation is ideal for investigating complex problems such as climate change adaptation, which are characterized by long timeframes and uncertainty.

## The zone of proximal development

The concept of the ZPD is widely used in education and child development. It represents the zone between what a learner is currently able to do, or know, as a result of their level of educa tion or development and what they are able to do with appropriate help or facilitation. It origi nated with the work of Vygotsky (1978) and was developed by Engeström (1987) and Engeström & Sannino (2010). The ZPD is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the distance between the actual develop mental level as determined by independent problem solving and the level of potential develop ment as determined through problem solving under adult guidance, or in collaboration with more capable peers’ (Vygotsky, 1978, p. 86). It is an indication both of developmental potentia and the limit beyond which attempts at development have diminishing returns.

One of the biggest challenges of resolving complex problems such as climate change adaptation is the education of stakeholders about the complex and contradictory aspects of the problem. As different stakeholders endeavour to obtain support from others during planning, decision-making and execution, they need to educate others in order to bring them along with them. The ZPD is a powerful tool in this context, as it shows how far and how fast people can be educated. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the ZPD is an indication of both where learning is possible and the limits on how far stakeholders are prepared to go for change.

## Tensions and contradictions within and between activities

Most IS studies using Engeström’s activity framework acknowledge the extensive treatment that he has given to tensions and contradictions within and between activities (e.g. Engeström, 1987, 2001). It is widely accepted that contradictions are a cultural–historical force that destabilizes activities, leading to constantly evolving and transforming activities (Cole & Engeström, 1993). Engeström’s in<sup>fl</sup>uential concept of expansive learning (Engeström & Sannino, 2010) describes how contradictions shape the temporal path of an activity. The resolution of contradictions and tensions within and between activities acts as a driver of change and devel opment. As shown in Figure 3, contradictions can be found and understood at four levels:

i primary, or level 1, contradictions within each constituent component of an activity, often within the exchange value and the use value of each;

ii secondary, or level 2, contradictions between the constituents of an activity;

iii tertiary, or level 3, contradictions between the object/motive of the dominant form of the ac tivity and the object/motive of the more culturally advanced form of the activity; and

iv quaternary, or level 4, contradictions between an activity of interest and its neighbouring activities.

Information systems studies have predominantly focused on level 4 tensions between activities (e.g. Kuutti & Virkkunen, 1995; Hasan & Suratmethakul, 2005), with human computer interaction studies focusing on the level 2 contradictions between the tools and the activity (e.g. Nardi, 1996). However, the dynamic nature and long-time horizons of complex problems such as climate change adaptation suggest that we should pay more attention to level 3 contradictions. This is a particularly interesting area where IS can contribute because of its long history of studying the evolving nature of systems based on continually changing technologies. For example, can we envisage the technologies that will be available to us over the future decades when the current plans for future adaptation will be enacted?

## Research design

This study addresses the question of how IS researchers and practitioners can make a positive contribution to climate change adaptation by seeking to determine the potential of IS to impact, support and transform the planning and execution of climate change adaptation activities. From the beginning of the study, we realized that identifying the potential of IS in this context would not be straightforward, as (i) the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld has not extensively engaged with societal grand chal lenges, (ii) while prior work suggests that climate change adaptation is a complex problem, ev idence for such complexity is largely unsubstantiated and (iii) as a discipline, IS has not routinely dealt with complex problems. Consequently, questions arose regarding the suitability of theories used in IS research to inform the work of IS researchers and practitioners on climate change adaptation should the phenomenon prove complex as envisaged.

Given the conditions we faced, we considered a grounded approach that would allow us to iterate between theory and practice, in the context of real-world climate change adaptation problems, to be suitable. We thus turned to CAR. CAR is a form of applied research that is iterative, rigorous and collaborative (Davison et al., 2004. 2012). Through informed interventions CAR combines theory and practice to develop a solution to a practical problem that is of value to the people with whom the researchers are working, while at the same time developing theoretical knowledge of value to a research community (Kock et al., 1997; Coghlan, 2001).

The dual-outcome perspective means that CAR is embedded within a practical context that is to be explained and changed. Within this context, an ‘unstructured <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment’ takes place, where researchers act as change agents to improve practical outcomes and describe new insights that result from the change (Chiasson et al., 2008). Our research design was guided by the <sup>fi</sup>ve principles of CAR developed by Davison et al. (2004), namely, (1) the researcher–client agreement, (2) the cyclical process model, (3) theory, (4) change through action and (5) learning through re<sup>fl</sup>ection.

There was an explicit researcher–client agreement in that one of the authors (hereafter referred to as the embedded researcher) was formally co-opted to the CCWG. In addition to holding an adjunct research position, this author was an information systems of<sup>fi</sup>cer with the NSW government at the time. The members of the CCWG, although not familiar with the term CAR, agreed with, and understood, our research objective and approach. Although they were not aware of the data analysis methods, the CCWG members provided data (documents and interviews) for analysis and received the output of our analysis through reports to the CCWG. As a result of these reports, they acknowledged the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance and the challenge of providing the information and IS needed to enact their climate change adaptation plans.

The cyclical process mode/ of CAR includes one or more cycles, of each of the following five stages: (1) diagnosing (identifying or de<sup>fi</sup>ning a problem), (2) action planning (considering alternative courses of action for problem solving), (3) action taking (selecting and executing a course of action), (4) evaluating (studying the consequences of the action) and (5) specifying learning (identifying general learning)’ (Mathiassen et al., 2012). The research design thus involved communication between the linked dual imperatives of CAR: the problem-solving interest cycle and the research interest cycle (McKay & Marshall, 2001).

The study began shortly after the formation of the CCWG in August 2009 and continued until 2012 when the CCWG was disbanded because of a change in state government. During this time, the CCWG formally met eight times, and these meetings were used to structure the action research into seven cycles consisting of planning, implementation and evaluation activities, as outlined in Table 2. Prior to these cycles of change through action and learning through re<sup>fl</sup>ection, the complexity of climate change adaptation was objectively diagnosed using the four characteristics described earlier in the paper. Much of this diagnosis was based on documentation, observations and processes. We thus sought to <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence of complexity based on established theory rather than asking informants whether things were complex.

As discussed in the previous section, complexity theory was used as the instrumental theory to diagnose the problem, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the complexity inherent in climate change adaptation activities. Also, as previously discussed, activity theory as our focal theory informed action plan ning and intervention. We began with a typical use of activity theory in IS research using Engeström’s (1987, 1999) framework (Figure 1). However, upon re<sup>fl</sup>ection on the usefulness of this approach, we applied the <sup>fi</sup>ve dynamic dimensions of activity theory in order to appropriately guide interventions and make sense of their impact. This more <sup>fl</sup>exible approach allowed us to evaluate the usefulness of activity theory as a focal theory in the context of the interven tions that we designed and move beyond inquiry and description towards prediction.

## FINDINGS AND ANALYSIS

For presentation purposes, the reporting of the analysis in this paper focuses on two climate change adaptation activities chosen, as exemplars, from among the CCWG projects. Climate change adaptation activities fall into one of two broad categories: those that deal with the long-term challenges of chronic problems and those involving preparation for an anticipated increase in the frequency and severity of crisis events. The exemplar activities were chosen to represent one chronic issue (sea-level rise) and one crisis issue (heat waves). The choice of these two activities was reinforced by the fact that they were given high priority at every CCWG meeting as ‘passive hazards such as sea-level rise due to global warming and immediate hazards such as <sup>fl</sup>ood, storm, <sup>fi</sup>re and heat waves’ (CCWG minutes July 2011). Both rely on input from the global science of climate change and require government attention at a local level, but neither regularly grabs the media spotlight. These activities involved a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant number of projects at different levels from those of global concern and general science down to the concerns of individual citizens. The importance of these two adaptation activities has also been recognized by Australian research on rising sea levels along the country’s vast coastline (Vasey-Ellis, 2009) and heat-related effects including reduced life expectancy (Huang et al., 2012).

Sea-level rise is an insidious chronic issue of which the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to sell to the public, as the projected sea-level rise in 2050 is likely to be of the order of 40 cm, reaching 90 cm by 2100. What is not readily understood is that on top of the higher sea level, extraordi narily high tides and/or severe storm events can be catastrophic. The Department of Land rep resentative on the CCWG told us that people do, however, understand that the re-zoning of <sup>fl</sup>ood-prone land impacts property values, increases insurance premiums, results in tougher building regulations for new dwellings approvals and may force owners of existing dwellings to make expensive changes or move.

According to one report (NCCARF, 2010), some local councils in NSW have encountered challenges from citizens when planning sea wall erection or landowner relocation as a response to risks related to climate change. For example, Byron Bay Shire Council in northern NSW, when attempting to implement a strategy for sea level rises, passed a proposal to restrict sea defences being erected on private properties. A subsequent court battle ensued, and the Council lost the case (NCCARF, 2014).

Heat waves are of concern, as, from projections about climate change, NSW will increasingly experience more frequent and more severe extreme temperatures. Such heat waves are critical public emergencies that cause more deaths than <sup>fl</sup>ood and <sup>fi</sup>res combined (Coates et al., 2014) but are less dramatic, as there is less spectacular property damage. The young and elderly are most at risk from extreme heat, with the elderly being most vulnerable as some may not be able to afford air conditioning and often live alone. There are currently no reliable systems that iden tify and monitor all those at risk.

As we have discovered, adaptation activities have long planning horizons. This has meant that one of the <sup>fi</sup>ve principles of CAR, change through action, should be evaluated over the long term. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, some of the recommendations included in our <sup>fi</sup>nal CCWG report have taken some time to follow through, exacerbated by a change of state government in 2011. However, since the 2015 re-election of this government, progress has been made on some of the recom mendations. AdaptNSW (2015) contains an interactive map of climate projections from 2020 to 2079, an extensive collection of information on sea level rise along the NSW coast and public awareness tools concerning heat-related issues. We are still hoping for more opportunities where IS skills and resources could be applied to support community consultation and participation in decision-making using social media.

Members of the CCWG who were interviewed said that, while the individual dataset may be suit able to the collecting agency, it might not be valid when combined with other data from other agencies. There were also issues of power structures related to data ownership. Federated Information Exchange Agreements were introduced in 2009 by the NSW state government to enable agencies to share data. However, members of the CCWG explained that such agreements are insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient to enable the inter-agency data exchanges within the NSW state government and with external orga nizations, such as the BOM and State Emergency Service, necessary for the long-term planning and management required for climate change adaptation. This was a critical outcome for the practice of the CCWG and justi<sup>fi</sup>ed the action research approach from the perspective of the working group.

Not all challenges faced by the CCWG could be met through the provision of quality information and IS. One CCWG advisor asked, ‘Are we better off, if we just make a guess about sealevel and temperature rises and plan for these changes rather than waiting for more reliable climate modelling?’ Another from the Department of Environment and Climate Change questioned, ‘Who is responsible for <sup>fi</sup>xing or reversing climate change?’, indicating that the whole purpose of the CCWG was brought into question. ## CONCLUSION

In this paper, we encourage IS researchers to apply their knowledge and expertise to the grand challenges now facing society, of which sustainability is one. We suggest that the complexity of grand challenges has deterred such research in the past and propose that there is a need to develop suitable theoretical frameworks to underpin the investigation of complex phenomena and complex problems.

We make the case that there is now irreversible climate change and that there is an imperative for adaptation, which therefore should be recognized, along with mitigation, as a legitimate topic within the <sup>fi</sup>eld of Green IS. By conducting a CAR study of the activities of the CCWG of the State Government of NSW, we have demonstrated that IS tools and techniques create useful boundary objects and artefacts that make positive contributions to climate change adaptation efforts. Testing our <sup>fi</sup>nd ings against criteria derived from the literature on complexity theory, we con<sup>fi</sup>rm that climate change adaptation is inherently complex and show that IS researchers and practitioners can make a posi tive contribution to climate change adaptation activities.

In dealing with the complexity challenge, we have demonstrated that activity theory, enhanced with additional dimensions not traditionally recognized by IS researchers, can be useful in both guiding interventions and understanding their effects when investigating complex phenomena. From the expansive activity theory analysis, we conclude that climate change adaptation involves both top-down (i.e. government-driven) and bottom-up (i.e. local agency and individual) actions that necessitate individual level information on citizens. Consequently, government agencies need to better engage with a wide variety of community groups and citizens. Communication between all stakeholders needs to account for their motivations and subjective perceptions of the future impact of climate change. Such communication should also include public education within the ZPD of citizens. Prioritization and funding models should be adjusted to accommodate the uncertainty and long planning horizons of adaptation activities. In addition, inalienable assumptions about desirable outcomes and the needs of future generations need to be challenged as part of decision models utilized for adaptation activities. This will necessitate the redesign of information reporting and decision support systems.

Prevailing IT management practices that over-emphasize operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and the preservation of current manifestations of government agencies and citizen engagement are likely to impede the type of engagement strategies necessary to address climate change adaptation. In particular, conventions on data storage and sharing, as well as policies on emerging IT platforms such as social technology, will need to be redesigned, so as not to impede the development of appropriate adaptation and crisis management strategies.

Guided by the <sup>fi</sup>ve principles of CAR (Davison et al., 2004), the research design adopted for this study proved to be highly effective at allowing us to explore both context and action over a long pe riod of time. From the client’s perspective, the researchers’ dedication to the grand challenge of cli mate change alerted the members of the CCWG to the importance of their work and was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant motivation factor (see the subject–object dialectic entry in Table 8). The two-way, three-level mediation between activities and tools entry in Table 8 describes how our interventions informed the CCWG on the use of IS to support complex adaptation projects underway at the time. The subsequent NSW government’s AdaptNSW programme (Table 9) has followed through on many of our recommendations, con<sup>fi</sup>rming the Change Through Action principle of CAR.

The researchers have relied heavily on the Learning through Re<sup>fl</sup>ection principle of CAR in providing actionable reports to the clients and the potentially generalizable <sup>fi</sup>ndings on how IS can play an important role in grand challenges The extensions to the traditional IS applications of activity theory (Table 8) helped us to better theorize the dynamic dimensions of complex phe. nomena than would have been possible with the approach to activity theory currently dominant in IS research. We have thus presented a novel use of activity theory that other researchers may follow. We believe that we could not have achieved the results we report here if we did not follow CAR principles with the unique opportunity afforded by the embedded researcher’s position and his deep understanding of the organization.

We believe that the work has implications for how researchers should conceptualize their studies of IS in the context of climate change adaptation. In this context, future Green IS research needs to do the following:

i Recognize that the potential of IS for adaptation will depend on the ability to identify, understand and resolve the tensions within and between adaptation activities, particularly those in volving multiple government agencies, citizens and other stakeholders;

ii Encompass the different subjective perspectives on the purpose of activities and recognize that such perspectives can, and also must, change as these activities evolve;

iii Acknowledge that the mediating power of IS tools will depend on the resolution of lower-level data issues, such as data consolidation. While such issues were a key feature of IS research in the past, they have recently disappeared from the IS agenda.

iv Address the emerging relationship between IS tools and the adaptation activities that they serve to mediate. However, it is important to understand that future generations of IS tools for adaptation activities will be shaped by the conduct and outcomes of current activities.

v Ensure that multiple appropriate levels of granularity are included in studies of adaptation ac tivities in order to appreciate the complex multi-level dynamics that exists in this domain.

We make the claim that there is a measure of generalizability in our approach because of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the case and the abstraction of activity theory concepts rarely used in IS into our focal theory. Future research engaging societal grand challenges may be able to make similar improvements to other theories used in IS.
