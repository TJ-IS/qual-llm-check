# The infological equation extended: towards conceptual clarity in the relationship between data, information and knowledge

William J. Kettinger<sup>1</sup> and Yuan Li<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

Clearly defined relationships between core concepts in our field are the bedrock for building a cumulative tradition. No concepts or relationships could be more core to the Information System (IS) field than: data, information, and knowledge. Even though several models have been developed to depict the relationship between these core concepts, none provides a completely satisfying solution to resolve problems in understanding information processing and in guiding IS research and practice. In response to the limitations in existing models, a knowledge-based theory of information is extended from Langefors’ (1973) infological equation, suggesting that information is the joint function of data and knowledge. Specifically, the proposed theory describes data as the measurement or description of states, whereas knowledge outlines the relationship between concepts underlying those states. Information, representing a status of conditional readiness for an action, is generated from the interaction between the states measured in data and their relationship with future states predicted in knowledge. Following this logic, different forms of IS are conceptualized as the embodiments of knowledge domains capable of transforming specific categories of data into information for business operations and decision-making. The proposed model helps address controversies in previous studies and provides guidance for further research. ## Introduction

Many companies today are grappling with the ‘right’ approach to managing knowledge via Information Systems (IS) and database technology; but what does knowledge mean to these companies and how is it related to information and data? These questions have been under debate since the beginning of knowledge management (KM) research (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Sutton, 2001; Shah et al., 2007). To many people, knowledge is a more valuable form of information, which is in turn a more valuable form of data (Grover & Davenport, 2001). Such a ‘data-informationknowledge’ hierarchy is very popular in the IS field (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Martz & Shepherd, 2003). Despite the great appeal of this hierarchy, other models depict totally different relationships, such as the reversed ‘knowledge-information-data’ hierarchy (Tuomi, 1999) and the infological equation proposed by Langefors (1973). Unfortunately none of these models has delivered a completely satisfying solution in defining these concepts and their relationship allowing for consistent and generalized use. As a result, confusions still exist, causing difficulties in IS research and practice (Mingers, 1996). Practitioners complain that the enormous expenditures on KM aimed at delivering ‘knowledge’ rarely meet company expectations (Davenport & Prusak, 1998), and scholars worry that the incapability to distinguish knowledge from information and data makes KM a buzzword or a recycled concept (Spiegler, 2000). Years have passed, but the situation has not changed fundamentally.

To resolve the conflict between the models, we need to develop a better understanding of the concepts of data, information, and knowledge and their relationship. By comparing the strengths and weaknesses of several alternative models and searching reference disciplines for the intellectual basis of these concepts, we propose a revised model from Langefors’ (1973, 1980) infological equation, recognizing that information is the joint function of data and knowledge. We argue that data refer to the measurement or description of facts or states, and knowledge represents the relationship between concepts underlying those states. Information, representing a status of conditional readiness for an action (MacKay, 1969), is generated from the interaction between data and knowledge. We name this model a knowledge-based theory of information. Based on this theory, different forms of IS are conceptualized as the embodiments of knowledge domains capable of processing specific categories of data into information for business operations and decision-making. We illustrate that this theory has the promise of resolving conflicts in previous studies and guiding further IS research to increase the business value of IS practice.

To support our viewpoint, we first compare several representative models of the relationship between data, information, and knowledge, showing their distinctions and limitations. We then briefly review issues associated with the definition of these core concepts in order to uncover the intellectual basis of their relationship. The knowledge-based theory of information is then offered, providing a more parsimonious and robust depiction of the relationship between these core concepts. Finally, the implications of the theory for IS research and practice are discussed.

## Comparison of existing models

Data, information, and knowledge are the most fundamental concepts in the IS field (Hirschheim et al., 1995). Usually data refer to facts about objects or events, information is processed data or a message that makes a difference or informs, and knowledge is framed experience used to evaluate or incorporate new experiences (Davenport & Prusak, 1998). Several models depicting the relationships between these three concepts have received the most attention (Spiegler, 2000; Alavi & Leidner, 2001):

\- Model 1, which we call a value chain model, is the dominant ‘data-information-knowledge’ hierarchy.

According to this view, data are the description of objects or events; information is data that is processed (e.g., classified, summarized, and transferred) to add meaning and value within a certain context; and knowledge is a high-value form of information, or information that is distilled from particular context and can be generalized to other contexts (Grover & Davenport, 2001; Martz & Shepherd, 2003).

\- Model 2, which we call a materialization model, is a reversed ‘knowledge-information-data’ hierarchy (Tuomi, 1999). This model suggests that data are created from information and information is derived from knowledge. It depicts a materialization process of conversion from knowledge to structured information and then to data, whereby knowledge is articulated via the latter two within a specific context. According to this model, data do not become information after the addition of meaning; instead, data are created from information by putting the latter into a predefined data structure that determines its meaning. Furthermore, information is knowledge that is made explicit, for example, embedded in IS.

\- Model 3, which we call an interactive model, suggests that information is produced from data and knowledge, that is (data & knowledge)-information. An early thought leader in this camp was the Swedish scholar, Bo¨rje Langefors. Langefors (1973, 1980) proposed an infological equation, I ¼ i(D, S, t), suggesting that information I is the interpretation i that a person makes of a message D based on his/her pre-knowledge or receiving structure S during a specific amount of time t. Similarly, Drucker (1988) argues that information is data endowed with relevance and purpose and converting data into information requires knowledge. Although some KM researchers have adopted this perspective (e.g., van der Spek & Spijkervet, 1997), overall this model has not been as widely recognized as the value chain model within text books or among IS practitioners.

Significant distinctions exist between these models, and arguably each model has its strengths and weaknesses. Model 1 helps to explain the roles of information in knowledge creation (Nonaka, 1994), whereas Models 2 and 3 explain the replication of information transferred between persons (Langefors, 1973; Tuomi, 1999). Models 1 and 2 are limited in explaining such processes as electronic brainstorming where knowledge is created via the iteration with information. Although Model 3 has strengths over Model 1 in addressing this problem, it has other limitations. For example, in Langefors’ infological equation the terms information and knowledge are very often used interchangeably and information is confus ingly defined as knowledge of particular facts (Langefors, 1980). Thus, it seems that none of the three models as outlined above provide a completely satisfying solution.

Two important controversies account for distinctions between these three models. The first controversy is that the competing models have different definitions and treatment of information. Some support a meaningful view of information and suggest that information is data or a message endowed with meaning, so that meaning is inherent to information (Drucker, 1988). Others advocate a meaningless view and argue that information is an attribute of a message, and meaning is generated from information by the person who receives it (Dretske, 1981; Mingers, 1995). In addition, as summarized in Table 1, the meaning of information differs across studies that have been carried out at different levels (Stamper, 1987; Mingers, 1995). For instance, scholars in telecommunication research focus primarily on the symbolic level of information and emphasize the objective measures of the amount of information (Shannon, 1948), whereas scholars in computer-aided decision making research focus more on the semantic and pragmatic levels of information and emphasize its subjective meaning (Miranda & Saunders, 2003). None of the three models above explicitly addresses these levels. Interpretation of information from different levels has caused problems in understanding across IS research (Mingers, 1996).

The second controversy is the obscure relationship between information and knowledge. Scholars commonly treat information as the basis or necessary material of knowledge (Nonaka, 1994; Martz & Shepherd, 2003), which is built upon the separation of meaning from information and the suggestion that knowledge is created from information conveyed in a message (Dretske, 1981). Others disagree, saying that information is neither necessary nor sufficient to produce knowledge (Machlup, 1980, 1983), and some even argue that knowledge is the basis of information (Langefors, 1980; Drucker, 1988). Interestingly, the infological equation, which presents a clear relationship between the two concepts, continuously treats information as a specific type of knowledge and often uses them interchangeably (e.g., Langefors, 1980, p. 17).

The existence of these controversies has a common basis. Several influential writings (Langefors, 1980; Stamper, 1987; Mingers, 1996) show that studies on information in the IS field have a strong semiotic tradition (the study of signs; see Stamper, 1973), which analyzes data as signs that represent or signify information. The infological equation, for instance, states that data do not contain information; instead, data are a collection of symbols or signs that are used to represent information, and even knowledge (Langefors, 1980). Although semiotics is helpful in explaining the meaning of information in a message or sentence, we find it inadequate to clarify the relationship between all three constructs, especially when knowledge is expressed in statements for communication. Let us consider the following three statements:

– S1: ‘There remain 17 pieces of article type A’. (Langefors, 1980)

– S2: ‘It is raining’. (MacKay, 1969; Hirschheim et al., 1995)

– S3: ‘All swans are white’. (Popper, 1992)

In their corresponding literature, S1 is used as an example of inventory data, S2 is example of an information statement, and S3 is a typical example of knowledge. The question is, since all of these statements are collections of signs, are they all data? And when a person speaks each statement, does he or she possess knowledge of each? Finally, is information conveyed or included in each statement? From a semiotic perspective, all these statements can be analyzed as data (or signs) conveying information, although in practice they might be best termed as data, information, and knowledge, respectively. The existing models (1, 2, and 3) do not provide a consistent answer; a better solution is needed.

## Issues in defining the core concepts

Before we introduce our recommended approach, a brief review is in order to define each of the core concepts.

## Issues in defining data

Data have long been recognized as a core factor in IS and have been generally defined as the measure or descrip tion of objects or events (Checkland & Holwell, 1998). Numerous classical writings offer comprehensive reviews of the research on data (Hirschheim et al., 1995; Checkland & Holwell, 1998), so we will not duplicate them here. However, there is one issue that we feel is important enough that we must explore: the distinction between data and signs; both of which have been used equivalently by many scholars (Langefors, 1980; Mingers, 1995). We will use a related term, codes, to show the distinction. Codes are a given set of signs in computers that are standardized and encoded with the corresponding rules of mapping to the signs (Hirschheim et al., 1995). Codes can be used to encode not only data but also knowledge, such as the knowledge base in Expert Systems or KM Systems, and decision models in Decision Support Systems. Signs, then, are not restricted to representing information but also data and knowledge, as illustrated in the three statements above (S1, S2, and S3).

## Issues in defining knowledge

Studies on knowledge and KM are conducted in several discourses (Schultze & Leidner, 2002). In this research, we take the normative discourse and depict knowledge as a set of rules produced by human societies, such as the condition-action pairs that specify a law-like relationship. In IS research and practice, knowledge can be of several different types, such as know-why, know-how, and knowwhen (Alavi & Leidner, 2001), and it is typically expressed in some formal structures, such as production rules (e.g., the IF-THEN statements), knowledge frames, knowledge maps, and knowledge networks (Turban, 1995; Marakas, 1999). The above-mentioned statement S3, for example, IF a bird is a swan, THEN it is white

It is a tautology to define knowledge as what is known; a widely accepted definition is Plato’s justified true belief of the relationship between concepts, from which other practical definitions are derived (Davenport & Prusak, 1998; Shah et al., 2007). A key distinction between knowledge and belief is that the truth claims of knowledge have been approved by some qualified elite and are taken for granted at the present time for practical purposes (Hirschheim et al., 1995). Considering the three examples mentioned above, we see that S3 meets the standards, assuming that no evidence has been found to reject the assertion; otherwise we need to refine S3.

Difficulties exist in judging whether S1 and S2 are also knowledge statements, which is of critical importance to understanding knowledge and its distinction from data and information. To answer these questions, we utilize research in other fields, including the philosophy of Popper (1992). In his study on scientific inquiry, Popper discusses a particular type of statements, which he calls strictly or purely existential statements, or there-is statements, for example, ‘There are black ravens’. Such a statement, although we believe it to be true, is not knowledge. Popper explains from his objectivism point of view that this statement cannot be rejected or falsified by another statement of an observed event, or simply new facts. For instance, someone may say (or observe) that ‘there are brown ravens’; this, however, does not conflict with or reject the previous statement. Both S1 and S2 are of this type, and none of them is knowledge.

Although Popper’s philosophy may not be agreed on by all, we find the discussion on there-is statements valuable in distinguishing knowledge from non-knowledge, and especially data. A data statement, such as S1, is also believed to be true and can be justified; nevertheless, it cannot be treated as knowledge. Explained in another way, a piece of knowledge should be generalizable and applicable to evaluate new experiences (Davenport & Prusak, 1998). If a statement is about the facts of some existing objects or events, such generalization is not selfevident. Therefore, we add generalizability and verifiability to the various standards of knowledge and use it as a necessary condition to judge a knowledge statement.

## Issues in defining information

Of the three concepts, information is perhaps the one that bears the most controversies. Information is usually defined as data processed into a form that has meaning to the user and is of real or perceived value in current or prospective actions or decisions (Davis & Olson, 1985). Such a definition is challenged by scholars who question how meaning and data interact to produce information (Mingers, 1995, 1996). Problems also exist in other fields where different information theories have been developed with little consensus achieved (Capurro & Hjorland, 2003). Although Shannon’s (1948) communication theory (describing information as uncertainty reduction based on a message or a statement) is a common basis of many studies. it works best at the empiric or symbolic level (i.e., how much information is carried) but not at the semantic and pragmatic levels (i.e., what information is carried). Since a statement can be used to represent data or knowledge, it begs the question: how is such a statement related to information and uncertainty reduction?

To answer this question, we introduce an important concept, information content. Systematically analyzed by MacKay (1969), information content is the measure of the amount of information in a message, and it is proportional to the uncertainty reduction (or less probability) of the message. If a message is more informative than another, it suggests that it has more information content. For instance, the statement ‘There remain 17 pieces of article type A’ is more informative, and therefore has more information content than another statement ‘There remain some pieces of article type A’, since the former reduces the uncertainty about the inventory level more than the latter. Similarly, the statement ‘It is pouring’ is more informative than the statement ‘It is raining’. This concept is also helpful in analyzing knowledge statements. For instance, Popper (1992) in studying the testability of knowledge suggests that the amount of information conveyed in a theory, or its empirical content, increases with its degree of falsifiability (or less probability). Compare these two statements, ‘All swans have light color’ and ‘All swans are white’: both contain information content because of the reduction in uncertainty about the color of swans, although the latter is more informative.

The above analysis further shows that studies on information purely from a communication or semiotic perspective are not sufficient in analyzing its full meaning and distinction with other concepts, as data and knowledge can also be expressed in statements with information content. A more robust definition of information is needed to match its daily use; MacKay’s (1969) definition seems to come closest to meeting this requirement. MacKay (1969) defines information as ‘the state of conditional readiness’, or ‘the selective function on the range of the recipient’s states of conditional readiness for goal directed activities’. According to this definition, a statement may contain different information and cause different behavioral intentions under different contexts. For instance, the message ‘It is raining’ may warn someone to stay at home or fetch an umbrella before going out (Hirschheim et al., 1995), depending on the context. The notion of ‘selection from a range of possible states’ implies that such a definition can be applied to other levels of information, from symbolic states of signs to pragmatic states of behavior. At the symbolic level, it complies with communication theories if the states refer to machine status; at the pragmatic level, it conforms to the use of information in business operations and decisions if the states represent possible actions.

## Summary of the definitions

The preceding review examines the definitions of data, information, and knowledge; it also highlights the distinctions between data and signs, knowledge and existing facts, and information and information content. Based on the review and previous comparison, we conclude that neither Model 1 (Value-adding) nor Model 2 (Materialization) appear satisfactory, since a linear model is incapable of explaining the existence of multiple meanings of a message. As discussed above, Model 3 (Interaction), as embodied in Langefors’ infological equation, seems to have the most promise in explaining the meaning of information. However, several problems outlined below prevent us from entirely accepting Langefors’ original equation as an optimal solution:

\- First, in the infological equation, data are treated as signs representing information, which is not completely accurate. The consequence is that a system of information (i.e., infology) is still operationalized via a system of data (i.e., datalogy; Langefors, 1973, 1980) despite the relationship and distinction built in the equation.

\- Second, information is defined as a specific type of knowledge of particular facts. This issue poses the question of whether the infological equation is really about the relationship between the three concepts or just between data and information.

\- Third, knowledge is less explicitly analyzed in the infological equation. Although the terms ‘receiving structure’ and ‘pre-knowledge’ are used, these terms lack accuracy, and it is not clear how knowledge is related to the interpretation process.

\- Additionally, a time element is included in the infological equation indicating when the data are collected and how long they are valid. Although time is important, it is not necessary to be included in the theory since it is a matter of data quality and can be better analyzed as a sub-issue in the latter, together with other factors which may influence data and its interpretation such as spatial and contextual aspects (Agerfalk & Eriksson, 2004).

Possibly because of these limitations, the infological equation has not been well embedded in the core of contemporary IS research. Additional research is needed to leverage the strengths of the equation while sharpening its theoretical relationships to address its cited limitations.

## Proposition of a knowledge-based theory of information

Building on the infological equation’s interactive relationship between data, information, and knowledge, we propose a revised model, suggesting that information is the joint function of data and knowledge, and lower level information is used as input to produce higher-level information. We name this model a Knowledge-Based Theory of Information, or KBI (i.e., knowledge-based information) theory. We argue that data specify the precondition or input values of a circumstance, based on which information is produced to enable the decision or selection of action under that circumstance. Knowledge, then, is the framework or the process through which information is produced from data. To better present the theory, we first briefly illustrate the essence of this theory, and then provide more detailed explanation of the core concepts and their relationship.

A typical way of expressing knowledge is using the condition-action pair, which specifies what action may be taken or what future event may occur when a particular condition exists. For example, the business rule ‘IF the inventory is low, THEN new material should be ordered’ indicates that when the condition ‘the current inventory of 17 items of article type A is low’ exists, the action ‘ordering more articles of type A’ should be selected. Such a selection may or may not be made, depending on the actual inventory level measured in data and the business rule depicted in knowledge; in other words, the information ‘to order or not to order’ is contingent on both data and knowledge.

Figure 2 illustrates the relationship between data, knowledge, and information in the KBI theory. In this model, data represent the measurement or description of the existing conditions, such as ‘It is raining’ or ‘There remain 17 pieces of article type A’. The availability of such data matches the condition clause in the knowledge structure, so that the potential action is to be taken or a future event may happen. When this happens, we say that the initial messages deliver the information of grabbing an umbrella or ordering new items of A.

The interactivity between data, knowledge, and information has the same structure as in Artificial Intelligence (AI) research. For instance, Turban (1995) shows a general AI structure, where the initial states are converted into goals through procedures. Such a structure is very close to the relationship depicted in the KBI theory, since, as analyzed above, data refer to the description of the states, information is the readiness for the goal (e.g., to order or not to order), and knowledge is the framework or process (e.g., the business rule) through which the readiness is triggered by the preexisting state.

It is further argued that for the same data, different knowledge applied will produce different information. Using the same example, when the definition of ‘low inventory’ varies or other decision rules or conditions are considered, the corresponding condition-action pair would differ, resulting in different information from even the same data (i.e., the inventory level). Generally speaking, this happens when different decision models or knowledge frameworks have been used to produce information from the same data. For example, we may need to check the short-term requirement of article type A in order to decide to order or not, and if there is no demand on article type A, the statement ‘There remain 17 pieces of article type A’ contains no useful information. Therefore, both knowledge and data are variants in this relationship.

## KBI theory’s core concepts

Because of the controversies in the definition and usage of each concept in the previous models, it is necessary to provide a clear definition in order to better understand these concepts and their relationship. First, we define data as:

Data are the measure or description of objects or events, usually referred to as a set of interrelated data items that measure the attributes of the objects or events.

This definition is consistent with definitions commonly used in many other IS studies. Statement S1, for instance, is an example of data since it is the measure of the inventory level of a particular item. Statement S2 is also data because it is the description of the current weather condition. Both statements are of the ‘there-is’ type, that is, about the facts of some existing objects or events. Many other examples can be found in various IS literature, such as a person’s weight or the cost of a business plan. It should be noted that the term ‘attributes’ is emphasized in the definition (e.g., as in Entity-Relationship modeling) because only attributes of the objects or events are directly measured or described, and not all aspects of an object or event may fall within the interest of the information user.

In addition to the unstructured data statements such as S1, data can also be represented in more structured ways such as databases or data tables. For instance, an inventory table with columns and rows may be used to show the inventory levels of several items. Such a structured approach increases the data management efficiency and is a major endeavor in data modeling research (Hirschheim et al., 1995). No matter which approach is applied, the same data are measured or described, although differences exist in the particular signs used to represent the data (e.g., sentences vs tables). This further supports our earlier assertion that data are different from signs.

Contrary to the infological equation, the time element is not included in the KBI theory, nor is it included in the definition of data. Although time is an important factor in data collection since the attribute value of an object or event may change over time, we view this as a technical issue in data modeling rather than a logical concern in the KBI theory. This can be resolved more conveniently by adding a time dimension in the data set, such as in data warehousing and in On-Line Analytical Processing. It should be noted that other factors, such as the location (i.e., spatial dimensions) where an object is measured, the reference system of measurement, and scales used, may also influence data collection and processing. These important factors are overlooked in the infological equation and should be handled in the same way as time is. As all these factors are about data quality, we suggest not including them in the relational model for parsimony purposes.

Data alone do not yield information for an action or decision, as it has no inherent association with the possible consequences of an action beyond the existing condition. In fact, uncertainty may not be substantially reduced in making a selection. In order to generate information and reduce uncertainty for an action or decision, knowledge is needed. Based on the commonly accepted definition, we state:

Knowledge is justified true belief of the relationship between constructs.

This definition has four implications. First, knowledge is a belief and such a belief should be generalizable to and verifiable by additional evidence. Second, knowledge must be true or approximate the truth under certain circumstances and is taken to be beyond questioning for practical purposes. Third, the truthfulness of knowledge must be justified or approved by some qualified elite, which differs it from other unjustified beliefs. These three aspects have been widely discussed in previous studies, including epistemology and IS research (Hirschheim et al., 1995). Finally, knowledge is the belief of the relationship between constructs, such as the mean-end pairs or the condition-action pairs, which could be at either the contextual level or the theoretical level (Berthon et al., 2002). An isolated, singular construct may not be treated as knowledge because of the lack of relationships with other constructs.

We have mentioned earlier that knowledge can be represented in several different forms, although the production rules (i.e., the IF-THEN pairs) are the most straightforward. Statement S3, for instance, is an example of knowledge and can be re-phrased as ‘IF a bird is a swan, THEN it is white’. Other examples include:

S4: ‘IF the inventory level of an item is lower than the safety stock, THEN new pieces should be ordered’.

– S5: ‘IF it rains and you do not want to get wet, THEN you should take an umbrella before going out’.

In addition, although a purely existential statement (or a there-is statement) is not knowledge, it does not conflict with the case that such a statement can be embedded in another universal statement or knowledge statement. For instance, consider the following statement:

S6: ‘IF the inventory level of article type A is lower than 20, THEN more items of article type A should be ordered to prevent shortage’.

S6 is also a knowledge statement, even though it contains a data item (20) and implies a data statement ‘the safety stock of article type A is 20’. It is knowledge because the content is generalizable and verifiable, meeting the necessary conditions discussed above.

Other forms of knowledge, such as knowledge frames, knowledge maps, and knowledge networks (Turban, 1995), share the same structure as the production rules. The difference is the complexity of the relationships in the other forms, where multiple relationships exist in a single knowledge structure. All these knowledge forms are describing the relationships between constructs, and in certain situations they can be converted into a group of individual production rules depicted in Figure 2.

The KBI theory suggests that information is the function of data and knowledge. Given the definitions of data and knowledge and the above review on information research by MacKay (1969), we offer the following definition of information:

Information is the meaning produced from data based on a knowledge framework that is associated with the selection of the state of conditional readiness for goal-directed activities.

This definition is consistent with the spirit of definitions of information in traditional IS research, and it suggests that meaning is an inherent attribute of information. Meaning, according to some scholars (e.g., Gray et al., 1985), refers to the interpretation assigned to an experience. Information scientists such as MacKay (1969) further elaborate the meaning of a message as its selective function on the recipient’s range of states of conditional readiness. Both indicate that a selection, or judgment, or uncertainty reduction, is triggered by information, which complies with communication research and is also generalizable to semantic and pragmatic levels. For instance, statement S1 together with S6 means that ‘new pieces of article type A should be ordered’, or more precisely, ‘at least 3 pieces of article type A should be ordered to meet the safety stock requirement’. Similarly, statement S2 informs the person who hears it to ‘grab an umbrella’, given that S5 is known. For both cases, a state of conditional readiness is selected, although the corresponding actions may not have been taken.

To further clarify the definition of information, we address the following two issues:

First, information must be eventually based on data, which we regard as the primary source of information; it can also come from a secondary source, that is, other people’s information. The awareness of this ‘secondary’ source of information is very important, and it is an important extension of the KBI theory beyond the essential concept that information is the function of data and knowledge. Often, information processing is not directly based on a person’s own or other persons’ observed facts, but rather on their interpretation of the observations they make; that is, the ‘data’ a person receives might be actually information processed and communicated (von Hayek, 1937). When this happens, it might be that the possibilities of multiple meanings within a data set are restricted by the sender’s processing based on his/her own knowledge and, in this sense, data received have the characteristic of information. The information sent, or data received, will undergo a re-interpretation process through which the intended meaning is recovered. Despite these differences regarding interpretations and re-interpretations, the essence is the same, that information is the meaning of data.

Second, the production of information from data needs knowledge, and when knowledge varies, so does information. This is the essence of the KBI theory, as it addresses the question of how data are converted into information. It also helps explain why different information may be produced from the same data and further calls attention to knowledge when misinterpretation happens. For instance, we have shown above that the information ‘to grab an umbrella’ is produced based on statements S2 and S5. However, when the person who hears the message (S2) applies a different piece of knowledge because of different goals, for example:

– S7: ‘IF it is raining and you do not want to get wet, THEN stay at home’.

then different information will be generated, which, in this case, is to ‘stay at home’.

The interaction between data and knowledge, from which information is produced, is further illustrated in Figure 3. This figure shows that, even with the same data collected, different knowledge applied will produce different information. It therefore highlights the issue of ‘fit’ between data and knowledge, that is, in order to produce the needed information for an action, knowledge must fit the data provided, or vice versa.

A second issue recognized by such scholars as Schultze & Leidner (2002) is the tendency of IS research to assume that knowledge always has positive implications for organizations. KBI theory avoids this pitfall by indicating that when knowledge is misused, information can be misinterpreted, resulting in unintended organizational consequences. In essence, it is the job of knowledge managers to create situations where the ‘right’ knowledge is available depending on the information requirements of the tasks at hand (Becerra-Fernandez & Sabherwal, 2001).

The above discussion on interpretations and perceptions does not imply that the content of knowledge and the corresponding interpretation process are subjective. We suggest that both are objective or at least inter-subjective, given that the same knowledge is shared by different interpreters. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the use of knowledge varies by person, as learning and accumulation of knowledge differs from one to another, so that the interpretation is bound within a person’s knowledge domain or rationality. Additionally, the application of knowledge is influenced by a person’s values or goals. An important role of IS is therefore to augment the IS user’s capabilities in dealing with multiple sources of information for better operations or decisions.

## KBI’s general information processing model

The relationship between knowledge, data, and information described in the KBI theory provides the basis of understanding information processing in IS. The examples described above are simplified situations, where only a single data statement containing a single data item and a single knowledge statement containing a simple business rule are involved, from which a particular piece of information is produced. We call this situation the element model. In practice, a complete IS built upon a single element model rarely exists, as information is often produced from other information rather than pure raw data (as we discussed above, information can also be based on other people’s manipulation of the data). For instance, in organizational decision-making, higher-level managers make decisions based on information provided by lower-level emplovees rather than raw data collected at the lower levels. In these situations where information is processed in a hierarchical or nested manner, the singular processing relationship or element model, where information is the dependent variable, is not sufficient and calls for refinement.

To address the seemingly paradoxical relationship between data and information, and to explain more complicated information processing behaviors of IS, we extend the fundamental logic described above to common situations and propose a General Information Processing Model, suggesting that all information-processing models can be decomposed into a system of element models, each containing its own data elements and knowledge framework. From a system perspective (Simon, 1960), it claims that no matter how complicated the information processing may be, it can always be operationalized in a system (e.g., network or hierarchy) of element models of the interaction between data and knowledge. In this hierarchy, lower-level information is produced from a subset of data and knowledge and then used as input to produce higher-level information with other knowledge elements; when ‘information’ exists in a data set, it only means that part of the data has been pre-processed. For instance, the statement ‘only 17 items of A remain’ produces the information that ‘at least 3 pieces of article type A should be ordered’, which is then used as input to processing orders. Therefore, we extend our earlier definition of data to include the pre-treatment on whole or part of the data set.

The KBI theory and the multiple levels of information We mentioned earlier that controversy exists between multiple levels information (see Table 1); a theory of the relationship would not be convincing if this controversy is not resolved. For instance, a person may write on a note I t i s r a i n i n g. What information is generated from these symbols (or handwritings) at each communication level, and what are the corresponding data or knowledge? The KBI theory can provide consistent answers to these questions, whereas none of the previous models can. Although originally developed from a pragmatic perspective, the KBI theory can be consistently extended to the other levels of communication, as shown in Table 2. At the lowest empiric or symbolic level, data refer to the signs used to represent the words, knowledge is the rules of sign transmission and statistical properties, and information is the string ‘I t i s r a i n i n g’ recognized from the signs. This string is then processed at the syntactic level through knowledge of linguistics, yielding the sentence ‘It is raining’. At the semantic level, this sentence further yields the information about the weather condition as being raining but not snowing, and the corresponding knowledge is semantics. Finally at the pragmatic level the message yields the information that ‘you should stay at home’ based on the receiver’s knowledge of S7. This example shows that, at different communication levels, combinations of data and corresponding knowledge yield information to be processed at other (higher) levels. What remains the same is the relationship of data, knowledge, and information as depicted by the KBI theory.

It is noted that not all these levels are relevant in certain communication environments. For instance, while the statement ‘I admit it, I killed him’ may be used in English class for grammatical and syntactical purposes, it does not convey the information at a semantic level that ‘I am the killer’; nor will it result in, from a pragmatic and practical perspective, that ‘I will be arrested’. Furthermore, if this statement is spoken to a non-English-speaking person, even the syntactic and symbolic meanings are lost, and the message becomes pure noise. Similarly, someone may tell you ‘The winner of the boat race was a woman’. This statement may result in different pragmatic information: if you are interested in the boat race and expect a male winner, you may feel surprised to hear the message and are eager to know who she is; on the contrary, if you are not interested in the boat race, you may not even pay attention to this message.

The above analysis shows that the KBI theory is a useful approach to analyzing the use of information at each level. As IS researchers, our primary interest in analyzing information processing is at the pragmatic or practical level; specifically, how information is related to the selection of actions in operations or decisions, which has a direct impact on IS design. Information at other levels is the focus of such disciplines as information science, computer science, and electronic engineering. The KBI theory may represent a unifying mechanism to start to tie research across these levels and disciplines.

## The KBI theory and the creation of knowledge

Another controversy in dealing with the relationship between the core concepts, as mentioned earlier, is the obscure relationship between information and knowledge. Given the assertion in the KBI theory that information is produced from knowledge, the question is: What are the roles of information in knowledge creation? Some may suggest a circular relationship, such as knowledge – information – (new) knowledge; however, we argue that this circularity is fallible. To show the problem of this circularity and the promise of our theory, we draw upon research from an evolutionary perspective of knowledge creation (e.g., Li & Kettinger, 2006). According to this perspective, knowledge creation is an evolutionary process, where tentative knowledge is generated from the variation of existing knowledge and then selected based upon information produced. During the process, information of all sorts does not determine the direction of variation of the knowledge, but the selection of tentative knowledge (for further clarification see Li & Kettinger [2006] and the discussion around their Figure 4 on p. 606).

We use Figure 3 to illustrate the process of knowledge creation and the roles of information during the process. Suppose we are solving a new problem by developing new solutions. For this problem, data are collected to measure the current problem state. Such data do not ‘inform’ what to do, as no applicable knowledge exists that can be applied to suggest an action and solve the problem. We then need to generate alternatives, for example, Knowledge\_1 and Knowledge\_2, each predicting a future state of the problem. The predicted future states, such as ‘problem is solved with Knowledge\_1’ or ‘problem is not solved with Knowledge\_2’ or alike, inform that Knowledge\_1 is successfully created whereas Knowledge\_2 should be rejected. In this case, information is produced from the knowledge (although tentative) and the data, confirming the KBI theory. If neither Knowledge\_1 nor Knowledge\_2 succeeds, it informs that another alternative should be generated, and according to the evolutionary view, the new alternative or new knowledge cannot be ascertained by earlier trials or the output information, suggesting that the circular view is problematic. Instead, the new alternative should be generated via ‘imaginative conjecture’ (Weick, 2006) and then selected based on the output information.

An evolutionary view does not imply that pre-existing information has no impact on knowledge creation; for instance, someone may inform that Knowledge\_3 (or some alternatives) would be suitable for the problem. When such information is presented, it already conforms to the KBI model as the information is generated from the application of Knowledge\_3 in the problem context. Therefore, pre-existing information can play a similar selective role for Knowledge\_3 as that for Knowledge\_1 and Knowledge\_2. Such a pre-selection process entails the same logic of KBI, further suggesting that the circular view is inaccurate.

## Discussion and conclusion

We developed a Knowledge-Based Theory of Information to define and clarify the relationship between data, knowledge, and information. We demonstrate that information is produced from the interaction between data and knowledge, and low-level information is used as input to produce high-level information. Specifically, data refer to the measurement or description of existing states, and knowledge stands for the relationships between concepts underlying those states. Information, representing a status of conditional readiness for goaldirected action, is generated from the interaction between the existing states measured in data and their relationships with future states or actions predicted in knowledge. In other words, information is the selection of future states or courses of actions based upon the occurrence of particular facts. This theory adopts the interactive point of view as best typified in the infological equation and pioneered by the early IS luminary, Bo¨rje Langefors. Recognizing the significance of this earlier work, we believe we offer a valuable extension by overcoming its limitations and sharpening the conceptual definitions and relationships. Specifically, KBI highlights the distinctions between data and signs, knowledge and facts, and information and information content, which have been confused in past research. As summarized in Table 3, the KBI theory extends the infological equation by better addressing four critical relational problems.

The IS field should enhance existing tools to measure the effectiveness of information produced from data and knowledge and its use for business decisions. Understanding KBI theory should allow researchers and practitioners to better recognize and measure data and knowledge, and the process through which information is processed for action. The following illustrates several examples of KBI applications.

## Applying the KBI theory: an example in computer-mediated communications (CMC)

In CMC research, especially those built upon the Media Richness Theory (Daft & Lengel, 1986), there are two frequently asked questions: (1) Why are there different interpretations (or information) of the same message in communications? and (2) How do we design and implement IS to effectively facilitate CMC? Early empirical studies attempting to answer the questions have produced mixed results (Daft et al., 1987; Markus, 1994; Dennis & Kinney, 1998). We suggest, based on Table 1, that the problem has its root in taking either a mechanistic view (signal contains information) or semantic view (i.e., information is determined by the user and only emerges at the destination) of information. The former seeks to replicate the signal sent at the destination of the communication channel, thus requiring the same framework-of-reference (MacKay, 1969) or machine knowledge for interpretation. For human communication, however, as no two persons possess exactly the same knowledge, they may have different anticipations for, or interpretations of, the same message (Ackoff, 1958); thus, information conveyed in the same message before and after the communication may differ. The keys to resolving the conflicts are to specify the level of research in CMC studies and more importantly to measure the knowledge framework that is used in interpreting the messages.

In addition, the intention and action of the actors involved in information exchange also matter. From the sender’s perspective, messages carry more meaning than just facts about reality; they may also carry the sender’s intentions and beliefs and are used to influence the receiver (Agerfalk & Eriksson, 2004). On the other hand, the information receiver does not always accept the information passively; instead, he or she may actively produce meaning out of the message (Lee, 1994; Miranda & Saunders, 2003) by re-interpreting the message based on his or her own knowledge or experience (Langefors, 1973). Such a process generates different information that is not uniquely determined by the communication channel (Miranda & Saunders, 2003). This distinction suggests that knowledge and additional facts that are asymmetrically distributed between the sender and the receiver should be shared in order to effectively deliver the information and facilitate communication, and such knowledge sharing would build a common platform for mutual understanding.

For scholars involved in CMC system design, the KBI theory suggests that emphasis should be put on the design of dynamic and human-centric systems that are able to accommodate users’ knowledge and preference in interpreting data and messages. Such a system should take into consideration not only what is interpreted from the messages but also how and why. For instance, the recent development in Semantic Web (Tyrvainen et al., 2006) enables users to use semantics rather than hard key words to actively gather information from the Internet to fit their own demand. Such an improvement increases the effectiveness of information communication of the websites. Similarly, to improve communication effectiveness between CMC users, mechanisms should be presented to recognize the differences in their knowledge frameworks and reinforce the common pattern in information processing in order to stimulate the ‘intelligent cooperation’ between users (Polanyi, 1966).

## Applying the KBI theory in KM

KBI theory has promise in guiding innovative KM by putting knowledge at the core of a social-technological network. Under this approach knowledge is managed via its informational attributes, that is, what knowledge is relevant in a given condition. These informational attributes are data sets that describe or codify knowledge content and are managed in enterprise content management systems (Tyrvainen et al., 2006) via database technology. To make this happen, pieces of knowledge should be first codified by attribute values (e.g., who, when, where, how, and why, etc.), saved in a knowledge base, and managed via meta-knowledge (Shah et al., 2007). For example, we may encode several customer service techniques based on their distinct effectiveness in dealing with specific customers groups; when an employee needs to find an appropriate technique to service a particular customer, he or she can search in the knowledge base to find the piece of knowledge that best matches the group to which the customer belongs. Following this approach, knowledge may be managed via

IT (e.g., Sutton, 2001), similar to other traditional, datadriven information management approaches.

## Applying the KBI theory in broader IS research and practice

As KBI theory depicts how information is produced from raw data and knowledge, it essentially illustrates the generic process and underlying structure of information processing applications (Davis, 1974). To provide a holistic view of the different IS applications, we adapt Ein-Dor’s (1986) early conceptualization of IS as knowledge repositories, and envision IS as the embodiments of knowledge domains that are designed to process specific categories of data to produce needed information. Different forms of IS are determined by particular information requirements and the corresponding demand on data and knowledge to deliver such information. Therefore, the degree of sophistication of an IS reflects the complexity in modeling data and knowledge in the design and deployment process.

Along with other IS theories and frameworks, KBI theory can help find better answers to IS questions. For instance, the KBI theory can be applied to Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989) to help explain why users perceive an IS to be useful: an IS is useful when the data are timely and accurate, knowledge embedded in the system is understandable and valuable, and the output information is illuminative. It can also be applied to Adaptive Structuration Theory (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994) to prescribe how the functionality of IS can be faithfully appropriated in organizations. Since organizations are knowledge-based, it is therefore important for the knowledge embedded in IS to be compatible with organizational knowledge; otherwise, information produced from IS is not accepted by the organization, resulting in the abandonment of the system. ## Limitations and future research directions

Limitations exist in the newly developed KBI theory, which require further refinement in future research. First, the proposed relationship of information as a function of data and knowledge has not been empirically tested; the theory is constructed and verified purely on logic. Empirical research should be conducted to test the theory with proper measurement of the three core concepts. A possible way is to conduct a lab experiment, where subjects are given some data sets or messages and apply a knowledge framework to infer the meanings of the data sets or messages. Second, only a high-level conceptual model is proposed. Further research could be conducted to explore the practical meaning of the relational model, and develop some directly applicable models or guidelines for specific contexts or types of IS. This can be carried out at individual, group, and organizational levels to test the strength of the model and stability across levels. Third, we only briefly described the relationship between information and knowledge creation based on the KBI theory and other qualitative research. Future research is needed to fully explore this process and provide empirical evidence of whether this model provides enhanced understanding of the knowledge creation process compared with other competing models, such as Model 1. Finally, as the KBI theory is focused on the underlying logic of information processing in IS, follow-up studies could be extended to analyze the influence of IS on environmental factors (Orlikowski & Barley, 2001), which may be promising in clarifying the contradictory organizational consequences of many IS studies (Robey & Boudreau, 1999).
