# How Hypertext Links Influence Consumer Perceptions to Build and Degrade Trust Online

KATHERINE J. STEWART

KATHERINE J. STEWART is an Assistant Professor of Information Systems at the R.H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland at College Park. She received her Ph.D. from the University of Texas at Austin in 2000. Her current research focuses primarily on trust in virtual settings and organizational issues related to the development of open source software.

ABSTRACT: This paper suggests and empirically supports the propositions that a link between two organizations’ Web sites will have simultaneous effects on trust in both the link sender and the link recipient, and that these effects result from interactions among the reputation of the link recipient, trust in the link sender, and the perceived relationship of the linked organizations. The study finds that the perceived relationship caused by a link leads to positive effects for the less reputable of the linked organizations, but negative effects for the more reputable organization. These effects are exaggerated or attenuated depending on the reputation of the organization that sends the link. The effect of presenting the link as an advertisement or a link to a partner was also examined, but no effect was uncovered, raising the question of how organizations may effectively differentiate links on their Web sites.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: entitativity, hypertext links, reputation, trust, trust transfer.

RESEARCH HAS EMPHASIZED THE IMPORTANCE OF TRUST in evaluations of companies and information encountered online [17, 36, 42]. Trust is important because of the difficulty of confirming another’s identity, the likely information asymmetry between buyers and sellers, and the necessity of sharing sensitive personal information [3, 5]. While these hurdles may exist in offline settings, information systems (IS) researchers note they are exacerbated by the technology mediation of online interactions. For example, in offline settings, physical proximity may aid trust building, whereas one of the supposed benefits of online transactions is that they can be conducted without the need for physical proximity.

Many studies have demonstrated the importance of trust to outcomes in e-commerce [34, 35] and several factors have been shown to influence such trust. These include characteristics of the trustor such as trust propensity, age, gender, and experience [46,

50], as well as characteristics of the Web site and Web site owner including company size and reputation [26] and Web site design [35]. While studies of Web site design begin to address factors more specific to the online context, most of the factors studied have been directly translated from the offline setting to the online setting, where they have been shown to have similar effects.

Given that technology mediation is a critical part of the challenge of building trust online, it is important to understand how different uses and implementations of the technology may influence trust to complement the understanding built from studying how offline trust antecedents are applicable. Building such an understanding may help organizations create a more effective online presence. Motivated by the lack of work on understanding the role of technological factors in this domain and the potential benefits of doing so (discussed, for example, in [39]), this research examines how the use of hypertext links, a core feature of the technology of the Internet, may influence trust in a business-to-consumer (B2C) setting.

While past work has focused on the significant problem of building initial trust online (e.g., [36]), less attention has been paid to how trust may be maintained and how organizations may avoid losing trust. As organizations become more successful at creating initial trust and consumers become more trusting of the overall online context, the issue of potential trust degradation will become more important. Thus, in addition to examining the role that hypertext links play in building trust, this study also examines how they could lead to reductions in trust.

The goal of this paper is to develop and test a model that simultaneously considers positive and negative effects to companies that send links (linkers) and companies that receive links (linkees). In addition to exploring negative effects, the study seeks to build on prior work in three ways. First, prior work has focused on effects to the linker or the linkee, generally assessing effects on one of the linked organizations only (e.g., [41, 46]). This paper builds on that research by developing the argument that links may lead a consumer to concurrently adjust trust in both the linker and the linkee. Second, prior work has tended to focus on direct effects on trust—for example, showing that perceived similarity to a trusted site [46] and the technological savvy of the consumer [33] have direct positive effects. The model and empirical test developed in this paper add to that work by proposing and supporting interaction effects on trust, providing a more detailed understanding of the impact of some of the factors that have been shown to have direct effects in past research. These interaction effects rest on the extent to which a link causes a consumer to perceive a relationship between the linked entities; thus, a third contribution of the study examines two factors influencing the strength of the effect of links on perceived relationships. These factors are the reputation of the linker and the nature of the link in terms of indicating either close relationships or more arm’s-length interactions. Prior work has focused extensively on the role of reputation in trust building (e.g., [12, 26]), but that focus has been on explaining how the reputation of a trust target directly influences trust in that target. This research adds to the prior work examining reputation effects by exploring how the reputation of an organization affects the perception of the relationship between that organization and another and thereby influences trust in both. In other words, this study examines how the reputation of a partner may influence the trust in an organization beyond how trust may be affected by that organization’s own reputation.

## Literature Review and Hypothesis Development

MANY AUTHORS HAVE ARGUED THAT TRUST is crucial for e-commerce, and there have been several excellent reviews of the myriad conceptualizations of trust (cf. [17, 36, 37]). One of the major distinctions made in this work has been among trusting beliefs, intentions, and behaviors (cf. [34]). The present study focuses on trusting beliefs as the trust construct most likely to be directly affected by cognitive processes initiated by links [46] and by the reputation of linkers and linkees [34]. For the purpose of developing hypotheses, the study employs McKnight and Chervany’s broad definition of trusting beliefs as meaning that “one believes that the other party has one or more characteristics beneficial to oneself” [34, p. 46]. Such beliefs may include assessments of benevolence, integrity, ability, honesty, and other related attributes [30, 37]. The paper adopts the broad definition, rather than focusing on a particular subcomponent, because cognitive processes that influence trust are expected to apply to a wide range of trusting beliefs [37].

Two other constructs of importance in the research are reputation and the perception of the relatedness of two organizations. Reputation reflects a consumer’s overall assessment of an organization based on information about its past behavior with regard to honesty, concern for customers, professional conduct, ethics, and standards [4, 12, 26]. Perceptions of the relatedness of two or more organizations reflect the extent to which entities are psychologically grouped together [7]. Such psychological groupings have been found to be based on the perceived interaction of the group members [10] or on their perceived similarity to one another [52]. A perceived relationship in this study is thus intended to encompass relationships that extend from either social groupings or categorical groupings. While these are two psychologically different kinds of groupings, both will be argued to have the same effects for the same reasons; thus, they are not distinguished in the hypothesis development below but they are measured as distinct subconstructs.

## Past Findings Regarding Influences of Hypertext Links on Trust

Prior work has suggested and demonstrated positive relationships between links and many judgments related to trusting beliefs regarding the linker. These include credibility [14, 16], legitimacy and caring [53], and trust [41]. Park et al. [41] suggested that links increase trust by making sites appear more comprehensive, and Freeman and Spyridakis [16] argued that such comprehensiveness enhances credibility by allowing people to relate the information on one site with another.

In addition to positive effects of linking that may accrue to the linker, research has suggested that there are positive effects of linking to the linkee based on the idea that links represent the embeddedness and social capital of the linkee [40]. Palmer et al. [40] suggested links may be seen as a stamp of approval given to the linkee by the linker. Consistent with this idea, Stewart [46] argued that links cause trust to be transferred from trusted organizations to unknown linked organizations because a link creates a perception of a relationship between the linker and the linkee that influences the way information is processed about the linkee. Based on these studies, linkees benefit as the number of links they receive from others increases [40]; however, the impact of any one link received decreases as the number of links sent by a particular linker to different linkees increases [46].

In contrast to the positive effects described above, less attention has been devoted to understanding or examining potential negative effects of linking. However, there is some anecdotal and empirical evidence that links may have negative effects on trust in one or both link partners. For example, Fogg and colleagues [14, 15] suggested that consumers sometimes judge links as detriments to credibility, based on their judgment of the linked site, and that such negative results may accrue to either the linker or the linkee. Warner [51] suggested that consumers who have a bad experience with a linked site may reduce their trust in the site that sent them there.

As summarized above, most explanations of effects of links on trust have incorporated the importance of the relationship between linker and linkee. Stewart [46] used literature on social perceptions to argue that effects of links result from cognitive processing on the part of consumers, and empirically demonstrated that a link on a Web site enhanced the perceived relationship between the linker and the linkee. Following in this vein, this paper develops a model that centers on the role of the perceived relationship between linker and linkee in determining the simultaneous effects that a link may have on trust in each. Theories regarding effects of perceived relationships are well developed in the social psychology literature.

## Social Perception Theories as a Basis for Understanding the Influence of Hypertext Links on Trusting Beliefs

Cognitive balance theory (CBT) [22, 23] laid the basis for a great deal of research on understanding the valences of relationships. CBT discusses two types of relations between individuals or entities, one of sentiment and one of unit formation. Both are relevant to understanding the effects of links on trusting beliefs. Sentiment refers to the way a person feels about or evaluates another person or object; thus, consumer trusting beliefs in an organization is a kind of sentiment relation. Unit formation refers to whether or not a person perceives that two people or objects in some sense belong together (e.g., organizations A and B are in the same group), and prior work has shown that a hypertext link may influence the perception of a unit relation [46]. The main tenet of CBT is that sentiment and unit relations will tend to be balanced, where balance refers to both the actual state of relations among entities and to the psychological state of individuals involved in or observing those relationships.

Dyads are balanced when all relationships between two people or objects are either positive or negative [23]. This explains why a link between two organizations’ Web sites may be seen to imply a positive relationship between them. Triads are balanced when all relationships among actors in the triad are positive or when one is positive and the other two are negative. For example, if a consumer perceives two organizations to be business partners (a positive unit relationship) and the consumer distrusts both organizations (i.e., has a negative sentiment relationship to each organization), then the triad is balanced. If one relation is negative and two are positive, the triad is unbalanced because an individual has dissimilar relations to similar targets. For example, if a consumer trusts organization A, perceives that organizations A and B are partners, and distrusts organization B, then the consumer may experience some cognitive dissonance [13] because of his or her conflicting sentiments toward two related entities. People are motivated to reduce such dissonance [23], which may be done by changing their sentiment relationship to one of the objects or by changing their perception of the unit relationship between the objects [1].

More recent research has built on CBT by considering the continuous nature of unit relationships and examining how the degree of a perceived relationship influences the processing of information about groups and group members [31, 32]. Crawford et al. [8] argue that as multiple members of a group are encountered, an overall impression is formed such that information that was specific to one group member becomes associated with others. Traits associated with individuals in a group are abstracted to the group level and may later be applied to other individuals in the group, implying that the judgment of a trait of one member of the group will be positively correlated with the judgment of that trait in another member of the group. For example, if one member of a group is judged to be highly intelligent, other members of the group may later be judged to be highly intelligent also. Thus, the first proposition examined in the study is that a perceived relationship between two organizations may result in adjustments in trust judgments about both organizations. This effect would be expected to be stronger the higher the perceived relationship of the group members, as such perceptions result in more homogeneous trait and behavioral inferences [9]. Thus, the second proposition explored in the study is that adjustments to trust judgments will be moderated (rather than directly affected) by the strength of the perceived relationship between the organizations.

As explained above, CBT suggests that the perception of a relationship between two organizations should result in a decrease in the difference in the levels of trusting beliefs in the organizations. In order for the levels of trusting beliefs in two organizations to become more similar, one of two things must happen. If adjustments to the trusting beliefs in the linker and the linkee are both positive, then they must be different in magnitude (i.e., the adjustment to the less trusted organization must be larger). Alternatively, the difference in trust levels will be decreased if the adjustment to the more trusted organization is negative. While some prior work suggests effects will be positive to both linker and linkee (e.g., Park et al. [41] suggest links between lesser known and better known sites enhance the trustworthiness of the lesser known site and also benefit the better known site by making it appear more comprehensive), other evidence suggests that links could have negative effects [14, 15]. Leveraging Crawford et al.’s [8] discussion of the abstraction of group traits suggests that trusting beliefs in one link partner will be increased and trusting beliefs in the other decreased. This may occur because group traits are based on individual member judgments, and if one group member is associated with high trust-inducing traits and another with lower trust-inducing traits, the abstracted group trait may fall at an intermediate level. When individual members of the group are then judged, those judgments may tend toward the intermediate level of trust rather than the level originally associated with the individual. Thus, H1 and H2 suggest that the level of trusting beliefs assigned to the member with higher trust-inducing traits will be decreased under what it would be if the member was not in the group. Likewise, the level of trusting beliefs assigned to the member with the lower trust-inducing traits will be increased above what it would be outside the group. To consider which member of the group is likely to elicit higher levels of trusting beliefs prior to this process, it is necessary to consider what trustinducing traits are relevant.

Many traits may affect which of two link partners is more or less trusted before the adjustment process described above occurs. The combination of these traits in determining the level of trusting beliefs prior to any adjustment is captured in what has been variously discussed as knowledge-based, familiarity-based, or prediction-based trust [12, 17]. Gefen et al. [17] argue that familiarity influences trust in e-vendors because it affects the ability of trustors to predict the actions of the trust target and their potential confusion about target policies. For familiarity to result in positive levels of trusting beliefs requires that the knowledge or experience that underlies the familiarity be positive. For example, familiarity must allow the trustor to predict that the target will act in a way that is beneficial rather than detrimental to the trustor. Reputation captures this combination of the amount and quality of past experience with an organization, and reputation has been shown to be an important antecedent to online trust [26, 42] as well as to trust in other settings [4, 12]. When a consumer views a site with a link and makes a judgment about the linking site’s trustworthiness, it is therefore the reputation of the linked site that may cause an adjustment in trusting beliefs in the linker.

Hypothesis 1: Trusting beliefs in an organization sending a hypertext link (the linker) will be affected by the interaction of the reputation of the linked organization (linkee) and the perceived relationship between linker and linkee such that (a) when the linker is more reputable than the linkee, a stronger perceived relationship will result in lower trusting beliefs in the linker, and (b) when the linker is less reputable than the linkee, a stronger perceived relationship will result in higher trusting beliefs in the linker.

When the consumer follows the link and interacts with the linkee Web site, it is not only the reputation of the linker that will affect trusting beliefs in the linkee but also the adjusted level of trusting beliefs that has resulted from the immediately preceding interaction with the linker. In other words, a typical sequence of interaction may be that a consumer experiences the site sending a link and then follows the link to interact with the linkee. In this case, the effect on trusting beliefs in the linker may depend on the consumer’s preexisting knowledge of the reputation of the linkee. However, the effect on trusting beliefs in the linkee should reflect trusting beliefs in the linker [46], such beliefs including both effects of reputation as well as the immediate salient experience on the site.

Hypothesis 2: Trusting beliefs in an organization receiving a hypertext link (the linkee) will be affected by the interaction of trusting beliefs in the organization sending the link (linker) and the perceived relationship between linker and linkee such that (a) when trusting beliefs in the linker are high, a stronger perceived relationship will result in higher trusting beliefs in the linkee, and (b) when trusting beliefs in the linker are low, a stronger perceived relationship will result in lower trusting beliefs in the linkee.

The effects hypothesized above depend on the strength of the perceived relationship between a linker and a linkee. It is therefore important to understand what factors affect the strength of that perception. Previous work on the influence of perceived relationships on assessments of group members has generally focused on groups in which members may be observed together but the choices behind their associations are not displayed (e.g., coworkers assigned to a project, members of a labor union [32]). Prior studies of the effect of perceived relationships on trust have also generally either ignored issues of the nature of the relationship or focused only on situations where the more reputable trust target clearly chose to associate with the less reputable trust target (e.g., in Uzzi [49], a trusted business associate voluntarily recommended an unknown target).

Does it make a difference if a link is sent from the less reputable target to the more reputable target rather than the reverse? Freeman and Spyridakis [16] suggested that links may act as cues when users are engaged in peripheral processing of information on Web sites, and Brase [6] argued that perceptions of relationships are affected by the strength of cues. Thus, the question may be rephrased: Are links sent by more reputable organizations representative of stronger cues to a relationship than links sent by less reputable organizations? Reputable organizations are more credible organizations [18, 25], and the literature on source credibility, both generally [2] and as specifically applied to Web sites [16], has demonstrated that information from more credible sources has a greater impact than information from less credible sources.

Anderson [2] conceptualized credibility as a weight that amplifies the value of information from a source, and Freeman and Spyridakis [16] demonstrated that credibility of a Web site was positively related to intended use of information from that Web site. Prior work has demonstrated that a link may be seen as containing information about the relationship between the linked organizations [46]. When this information comes from a reputable source, its weight in determining user judgments should be higher than when it comes from a less reputable source, and the link is therefore likely to have a stronger impact on the perception of a relationship.

Hypothesis 3: The reputation of the linker will have a positive effect on the perceived relationship between linker and linkee.

## Link Types

The presentation of links utilized on the Web may vary widely. Thus, in addition to which partner sends a link, another relevant question is whether qualitatively different links may have different kinds of effects on consumer perceptions. In discussing the perception of groups, Lickel et al. [32] point out that any collection of individuals, ranging from those with seemingly little relation to one another (such as people in line at a bank or people in the audience at a movie) to those with seemingly strong relations to one another (such as members of a family), may be considered as a group. Different kinds of groups vary in the extent to which members are perceived as related. Members of intimacy groups and task groups are perceived as most strongly related, followed by groups based on social categories and loose associations [32]. Some factors that distinguish between these kinds of groups are their permeability (the ease with which members may enter and leave the group) and duration (the length of time over which the group exists). More strongly related groups are less permeable and have longer duration than less strongly related groups. Sherman et al. [43] suggest that the perception of group type precedes and influences the processing of information about group members.

The nature of the link between two organizations may serve as an indicator of the type and degree of interaction they share and thereby influence how the organizations are perceived. Two common kinds of hypertext links displayed on Web sites are links between organizations sharing business affiliations, such as partnerships, and links that represent advertisements. Organizations linked in alliances or partnerships might be perceived as highly related because such associations may involve high levels of embeddedness [49] or the sharing of resources [19]. Such links may imply cooperative and coordinated behavior and shared objectives, which Brase [6] suggests are hallmarks of strongly related groups. In contrast, an advertising link may be seen to represent a less strongly related grouping because any organization that has the resources to pay the fee may have an advertising link sent to it. An advertising relationship may involve relatively few shared objectives and little coordination, characteristics that Brase [6] notes would be associated with relatively weaker groups. Such a relationship may therefore be more representative of a loose association—easily entered into and easily disbanded—whereas a partnership linkage may represent a task-focused or intimacy-based type of grouping.

Hypothesis 4: A link representing a partnership will lead to a higher perceived relationship of linker and linkee than a link representing an advertisement.

## Method

AN EXPERIMENTAL STUDY WAS CONDUCTED to test the hypotheses. Subjects were assigned the task of shopping for a laptop computer. This product category was chosen because it is one in which trust is expected to be important due to price, quality, and service concerns. During the experiment, subjects visited two Web sites. To manipulate reputation, one site was a reputable computing magazine that was expected to enjoy a high level of familiarity and trust. The second was the site of a vendor of laptop computers. This site was modeled on real vendors’ Web sites; however, a fictitious company name and logo were used to be certain that the organization would be unknown to subjects and therefore be expected to elicit a lower level of trust based on reputation. The type of link between the sites was varied such that subjects saw either no link, a link labeled as an advertisement, or a link to an organization labeled as a partner. Links employed the organization logo of the linkee and appeared on the right side of the home page of the linker (Appendix A shows how the link manipulation was implemented on the vendor site). The position of the more reputable and less reputable sites as linker and linkee was randomized.

## Measures

As explained above, the nature of the link between sites and the reputation of the sites were manipulated rather than measured. Trusting beliefs were measured using Likert scale items from prior work [26, 46]. Based on and using scales from Stewart [46], perceived relationship was operationalized using two constructs: the extent to which the organizations were seen as having a business relationship with each other (perceived business tie) and the extent to which the linked organizations were perceived as being similar (perceived similarity).

Many factors may influence a consumer’s perceptions of an organization and its Web site. These include characteristics of the consumer such as technological experience [27, 33] or level of comfort with the online context [48], characteristics of the organization such as whether or not it also has a brick-and-mortar presence [44, 46], and characteristics of the Web site such as its level of security or quality of design [29, 35, 47]. Factors related to the companies and their Web pages were experimentally controlled in this study, and perceived ease of use and graphics quality were each measured using a seven-point Likert scale item to allow some statistical control as well. Subjects were randomly assigned to conditions to avoid potential bias based on consumer characteristics, and statistical control of such characteristics was provided using data collected on age, gender, and income, which have been shown to be related to familiarity with and trust in the channel and attitudes toward shopping (cf. [50]). Experience with computers and the Web may also be important because more experienced users may be more discerning in their evaluations of Web sites, more comfortable using the Web to make a purchase, or have stronger preexisting notions about the product category used in the study. Four measures captured experience: the number of months the subject had been using the Web (Web months), the subject’s technical work experience measured in months (IT work experience), the frequency of Web use (Web use), and the subject’s frequency of online purchases (online purchases). Measurement of subject’s propensity to trust companies encountered on the Web were included because the trust literature indicates that propensity may be an important factor determining trusting beliefs in a target [37]. All survey items are included in Appendix B.

## Subjects

Subjects were recruited using fliers posted in the vicinity of a U.S. university and advertisements placed in the university newspaper. They were offered a 1 in 50 chance to win a \$100 lottery, and recruiting materials provided a URL to participate. The study was conducted entirely online. Upon logging in to the experiment, subjects were randomly assigned to one of the experimental groups. One hundred ninetythree subjects completed at least the first questionnaire in the experiment. However, some failed to finish the experiment by either dropping out after completing the first questionnaire or proceeding directly to the end to enter their name in the lottery. Two subjects indicated that they thought they had previously heard of the fictitious company. One subject took over 1,600 minutes to complete the experiment (the maximum excluding this subject was about 55 minutes). These subjects were dropped from the data set, and the analyses were conducted on the remaining 165 subjects. Subjects ranged in age from 18 to 43, with a mean of 22 years. Approximately 29 percent reported having completed at least a bachelor’s degree. Approximately 48 percent were male. Additional descriptive statistics are included in Appendix C.

## Procedure

After agreeing to a consent form and viewing instructions, subjects completed a survey measuring their Web use, Web months, online purchases, and propensity to trust

Web sites. Subjects were then directed to one of the sites. They were asked to look up and record information on each site in order to engage them in the study. On the magazine Web site, they were asked to determine the three criteria that would be most important to them personally in purchasing a laptop. On the vendor site, they were asked to select a model that they might be interested in purchasing and provide the model number and price. After viewing each site, subjects completed a survey measuring trusting beliefs in that site and control variables related to the site.

Subjects moved from the first site to the second either by clicking on the link on the site or, for those who viewed the sites without links, by clicking a link in an instruction bar maintained in all conditions in a frame at the top of the browser. After they finished with both sites, subjects completed instruments measuring perceived business tie and perceived similarity between the sites. Manipulation check questions asked if subjects had heard of each organization prior to the study, if they recalled seeing a link, and, if so, if the link had been labeled as an advertisement or partnership. Subjects then completed a survey to collect data on demographic control variables. Last, subjects viewed a debriefing page explaining in greater detail the purpose of the study.

## Analysis

Analysis proceeded in four stages. First, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to verify that random assignment appeared to be successful, and manipulation checks were conducted to ascertain whether subjects were able to recall what kind of link they saw and to differentiate the more and less reputable sites. Second, measurement properties of scale items were assessed using factor analysis and Cronbach’s alpha. Third, hypotheses were tested using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). Finally, because results of the hypothesis tests indicated that the two constructs used to reflect perceived relationship behaved differently, a supplemental analysis was conducted to further examine the roles played by the two constructs.

## Supplemental Analysis

Because different effects were uncovered for the two constructs representing perceived relationship, a supplemental analysis was conducted to further explore the relationship between the two constructs so as to help refine the research model. This analysis was designed based on the social psychology literature regarding the perception of groups. In this literature, Hamilton and Sherman [21] speculated that perceptions of interaction among group members may increase other aspects of their perceived relationship, including similarity. Thus, once the existence of a group is perceived based on interaction cues, members within it may tend to be perceived as being more similar to one another. Lickel et al. [32] found a positive correlation between perceived interaction and perceived similarity, and Lickel et al. [31] suggest that it may be based on the interaction of targets, which may be easily observed, that people assess the extent and nature of their relationship with one another. Based on this work, an ANCOVA examined the effect of perceived business tie, which represents the interaction between linker and linkee, on perceived similarity. Independent variables entered in this ANCOVA were the same as those used to test H3 and H4, with the exception that perceived business tie was entered as an independent rather than a dependent variable. The effect of perceived business tie was significant at $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ and the R-square increased from 0.24 to 0.37 compared to the model shown in Table 3. Other effects were essentially unchanged except that the statistical significance of the age control variable was reduced to $p < 0 . 1 0$ . Based on results of hypothesis tests and the supplemental analysis, the model in Figure 1 was modified in Figure 4 to show the relationships supported by the research.

## Discussion

RESULTS WERE GENERALLY SUPPORTIVE of the main arguments in the paper. Links were found to spur a bidirectional process resulting in effects to both a linker and a linkee. These effects were moderated by the perceived relationship between the companies, and they were positive or negative as predicted by the hypotheses. While the hypotheses focused on the high-level construct of perceived relationship, the empirical operationalization and test examined two facets of that construct, and the pattern of results indicated that those two facets may be of differential importance: effects on the linker rested on perceived similarity, while effects to the linkee resulted from perceived business tie. In addition to the moderating role played by the constructs, perceived business tie was observed to have a direct positive effect on trusting beliefs in the linkee. Further, the linker reputation had a direct effect on perceived business tie, but not on perceived similarity, which was instead found to be influenced by the perceived business tie.

## Practical Implications

The study demonstrates that trust effects from links occur to both link partners. In this study, there were positive effects to an unknown organization, supporting the implication that less reputable organizations may effectively build trust by exchanging links with reputable partners. However, there were simultaneous negative effects to a reputable organization. Prior studies [20, 46, 49] had not examined potential effects to the more trusted member of a dyad, and such effects may be of particular practical importance in the context of the WWW, where well-known organizations are soughtafter link providers. The findings imply that reputable organizations may be damaged by links from unknown organizations and may therefore benefit from taking steps to monitor and control such links. Similarly, because they may be damaged by sending links to less reputable sites, organizations should weigh this possibility against the possible benefits of linking, such as advertising revenue.

## Theoretical Implications

In addition to considering effects to both linker and linkee and demonstrating that effects may be negative, the study results suggest further refinement to the theoretical model of how the structure of online links may affect consumer perceptions by placing perceived relationship in a moderating rather than a mediating role. Results further suggested that perceived similarity is a mediator between perceived business tie and effects on trust, helping to clarify the causal chain from links to trust outcomes. The fact that different facets of perceived relationship played different roles in this process suggests that future work might be expanded to explore other facets of perceived relationships beyond the two included in this study. In particular, research on perception of social relationships suggests that perceptions of interdependence, common goals, and common outcomes may be other facets that could play important roles [32].

While the reputation of the linker was found to play a determining role in the strength of the perceived business tie, the presentation of the link as an advertisement or a partnership did not have a statistically significant effect. Given that the manipulation was modeled on how some organizations actually do present links and that it was shown to have a statistically significant effect on subjects’ answers to the manipulation check question, this in itself is an interesting result from the study. It may imply that the efforts some organizations take to differentiate kinds of links on their Web sites may not be effective. In particular, looking at the pattern of incorrect responses to the link-type manipulation question revealed that the highest number of incorrect responses (44 percent) was among subjects who saw a partnership link but identified it as having been an advertisement. It could be that even among those who recalled the wording of the link identifying it as a partner, some perceived this as just another kind of advertisement. Future work might explore what actions organizations can take to differentiate in the minds of consumers the relationships they present on their Web sites.

In addition to those mentioned above, the results indicate at least two other interesting avenues for future research. In this study, any link placed on the site had an effect. Future work should seek to establish the boundaries of the model by testing a wider variety of link types in order to see if there may be some ways of presenting links that will avoid effects. It is also important to investigate the resiliency of link effects. This study examined only relatively immediate effects on trusting beliefs in the two organizations. It remains to be seen whether these effects are long lasting, or whether they may fade with further interaction between the consumer and the organizations.

## Limitations

There are limitations related to both the theoretical conceptualization of the model and the empirical test. As noted above, both considered only relatively immediate effects on trusting beliefs in the two targets, and prior work has differentiated swift trust development from trust that develops over long periods of time [38]. Similarly, both took a relatively coarse view of the effects of reputation by conceptualizing and operationalizing it in a dichotomous fashion. Future work should seek to examine whether the effects that result from the relative differences between high- and low-reputation partners persist across a wider range of absolute levels of reputation. As with all experimental studies, this one is most useful in arguing for the causal relationships among the variables at the cost of generalizability to less controlled settings where many more factors may vary.

The sampling technique may have resulted in some selection biases (e.g., overrepresented students who had time and interest to participate), which could also limit the generalizability of the results, as could the choice of context. For example, it is uncertain the extent to which results can be generalized to evaluations of relationships among different kinds of organizations (e.g., government and nonprofit organizations). While it is important to understand effects of links to and from information sites, the interpretation of links placed on information sites such as magazines could be different from the interpretation of links to and from other kinds of sites. For example, links to vendors might be seen to reduce the objectivity of information provided, which could be another reason that trust suffers when such links are present. This may also have contributed to the lack of differences between the effects of advertising and partnership links, and it is possible the hypothesized difference could be found when the linker or linkee are different kinds of organizations.

While the conceptualization of trusting beliefs in the hypothesis development was left intentionally broad, because the theory on which the hypotheses are based has been shown to be broadly applicable to many different kinds of sentiments and relationships, the measurement was fairly narrow and did not attempt to tap all facets of trusting beliefs. The scale to measure trust was chosen to maintain consistency with the most closely related prior work, and to avoid the burden placed on subjects by longer scales. The drawback is that it does not allow testing whether there may be different effects to different types of trusting beliefs (e.g., ability versus integrity). However, McKnight et al. [35] demonstrate very high correlations among subconstructs of trusting beliefs, ranging from 0.77 to 0.90, which seems to imply that similar effects may be expected across measurement scales.

## Conclusion

ESTABLISHING AND MAINTAINING TRUST on the WWW is a difficult task [24]. This paper sought to extend the online B2C trust literature by exploring in greater detail how different presentations of hypertext links may have different effects on trust in the linked organizations. The experimental study supported the main contentions in the paper that links have simultaneous effects on both linkers and linkees; that these effects result from interactions among the reputation of the linkee, trusting beliefs in the linker, and perceived relationship of the linked organizations; and that the effect is positive to the less reputable organization but negative to the more reputable organization. These effects were strengthened or attenuated depending on the reputation of the organization that sent the link; however, no effect was uncovered for presenting the link as an advertisement or as a link to a partner. Overall, the study provides a firmer basis for understanding the means by which links affect trust, while raising several questions about how organizations may effectively value and differentiate links on their Web sites to exercise greater control over these effects.
