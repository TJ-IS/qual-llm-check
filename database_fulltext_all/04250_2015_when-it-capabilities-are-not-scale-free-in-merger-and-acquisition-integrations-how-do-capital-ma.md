# When IT capabilities are not scale-free in merger and acquisition integrations: how do capital markets react to IT capability asymmetries between acquirer and target?

Hüseyin Tanriverdi<sup>1</sup> and Vahap Bülent Uysal<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

In mergers and acquisitions (M&A), a primary objective of acquirer is to integrate IT resources of the target with its own. IT M&A integration is assumed to create synergies, which in turn increase shareholder wealth by making the value of the merged firm greater than the sum of the standalone values of the two firms. In this study, we challenge this assumption and argue that IT M&A integration does not always lead to greater value creation. Prior research on IT M&A integrations indicates that IT resources are often not scale-free in M&A: that is, they do not transfer easily and costlessly from an acquirer to its target or vice versa. In fact, IT M&A integration can destroy value rather than create it when IT resources are not scale-free. We theorize about the contingencies under which IT M&A integration can create value for shareholders of acquirers. We test our hypotheses in a sample of 549 M&A transactions between 1998 and 2007. We find that, on average, capital markets react negatively with M&A announcements of acquirers whose IT capabilities are superior relative to those of the targets. The superiority of the acquirer’s IT capabilities signals that the acquirer is likely to rip and replace IT resources of the target. This IT M&A integration approach increases risks of disruption to target’s operations and revenue growth. Capital markets take such risks into account and reduce the stock price of the acquirer. One contingency that reduces the negative reactions of capital markets is industry relatedness of target. In a same-industry acquisition, an acquirer and its target have similar operating models, competitive dynamics, and regulatory context. Thus, ripping and replacing weaker IT resources of the target with superior IT resources of the acquirer creates expectations of more efficient operation, engenders positive stock price reactions, and increases shareholder wealth. Another contingency that reduces the negative reactions of capita markets is the acquirer’s track record in profitable growth. A profitably growing acquirer that has superior IT capabilities increases the confidence of capital markets that it can minimize potential disruption risks of IT integration, continue its profitable growth pattern with newly acquired target, engender positive stock price reactions, and create shareholder wealth. These findings indicate that IT M&A integration does not always lead to greater value creation in M&A. The study makes a contribution by identifying the contingencies under which IT M&A integration creates wealth for acquirer’s shareholders. ## Introduction

Firms undertake mergers and acquisitions (M&A) with various motivations such as increasing their scale of operation and reducing costs; entering new markets, acquiring new customers, and exploiting their growth potential; gaining access to new products, technologies, and knowledge; taking out competitors; or avoiding being acquired themselves. An enduring puzzle in M&A research and practice is that despite the prevalence of M&A activity, returns to shareholders of acquirers around the time of M&A announcement is, on average, 0 or slightly below 0 (Andrade et al, 2001; King et al, 2004). A meta-analysis of the empirical M&A studies concludes that existing empirical M&A research has not clearly identi<sup>fi</sup>ed organizational mechanisms that create shareholder wealth (King et al, 2004). IS researchers recently joined the quest to identify shareholder wealth-creation mechanisms in M&A and started to examine the roles of IT capabilities and IT M&A integrations (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011). They hypothesize that strong IT M&A integration capabilities could contribute to shareholder wealth creation by integrating business processes, products, and services of acquirer and target, realizing synergies, improving business operations, and thereby making the value of the merged <sup>fi</sup>rm greater than the sum of standalone values of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms. An initial empirical test of this hypothesis shows that IT M&A integration capability of acquirer is positively associated with shareholder wealth creation (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011).

In this study, we seek to better understand the conditions under which IT capabilities and IT M&A integrations create or destroy shareholder wealth in M&A. Unlike prior studies, we do not assume that strong IT capabilities and IT M&A integration can always create shareholder wealth. We recognize that IT resources of merging <sup>fi</sup>rms are often not scalefree, that is, they do not transfer easily and costlessly from acquirer to target or vice versa. We argue that IT M&A integration can disrupt operations of target and lead to wealth destruction rather than creation when IT resources of merging <sup>fi</sup>rms are not scale-free. We examine how capital markets view IT capability asymmetries between acquirer and target in forming forward-looking expectations about the acquirer’s IT M&A integration approach and synergy realization potential, and how they adjust the stock price of the acquirer accordingly in reacting to new M&A announcements. We theorize about the contingencies under which IT M&A integration could create shareholder wealth.

We start with the premise that M&A is a market for corporate control. Firms that do not have strong organizational capabilities fail to add value to their assets and become acquisition targets for others. IT capabilities are an important class of organizational capabilities. Business value of IT literature has shown that IT capabilities enable <sup>fi</sup>rms to integrate their internal business units, newly acquired businesses, and achieve cross-business synergies (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011); connect with external business partners, exchange physical goods, information, and <sup>fi</sup>nances with them and create business value in interorganizational relationships (Rai & Tang, 2010; Rai et al,

2012); increase organizational agility (Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011); proactively sense and respond to competitive dynamics (Chi et al, 2010); improvise in turbulent competitive environments and achieve competitive advantages (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010). Thus, IT capability asymmetries between acquirer and target are likely to affect how the merger creates or destroys shareholder wealth.

The theory of market for corporate control and the resource-based view (RBV) of M&A assume that superior capabilities of acquirers are scale-free. That is, acquirers can transfer their superior capabilities to targets without having to endure much dif<sup>fi</sup>culty or incur signi<sup>fi</sup>cant costs. However, the IS literature on IT M&A integrations indicates that integrating IT capabilities of acquirer and target in M&A is notoriously dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and costly (Merali & McKiernan, 1993; Johnston & Yetton, 1996; Wijnhoven et al, 2006; Mehta & Hirschheim, 2007; Henningsson & Carlsson, 2011; Tanriverdi & Du, 2011a, b). These dif<sup>fi</sup>culties indicate that IT capabilities are often not scale-free in M&A.

We hypothesize that the superiority of the acquirer’s IT capabilities can motivate the acquirer to impose its IT capabilities onto the target as well and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase the risks and costs of disruptions to the target’s operations and revenue growth. The expectation of such disruptions can lead to negative stock price reactions to the M&A announcement, and lower the wealth of the acquirer’s shareholders. However, in a same-industry acquisition, where an acquirer and its target have similar operating models, competitive dynamics and regulatory context, ripping and replacing IT capabilities of the target with strong IT capabilities of the acquirer can create the expectation of a more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient operation. This can in turn lead to a positive stock price reaction, and thereby increase the wealth of the acquirer’s shareholders. In addition, a pro<sup>fi</sup>tably growing acquirer that has superior IT capabilities can increase the con<sup>fi</sup>dence of the capital markets that it can minimize potential disruption risks of IT M&A integration, continue its pro<sup>fi</sup>table growth pattern with the newly acquired target as well by transferring its superior IT capabilities, engender a positive stock price reaction, and create wealth for its shareholders. We <sup>fi</sup>nd empirical support for these ideas in a sample of 549 M&A transactions between 1998 and 2007.

The study contributes to the business value of IT and M&A literatures by explaining why IT capabilities are not scale-free in M&A integrations; why IT capability asymmetries between acquirer and target create notorious IT M&A integration challenges; and why IT M&A integration does not always create shareholder wealth. The study also makes a contribution by identifying contingencies under which the superiority of acquirer’s IT capabilities relative to those of the target can create shareholder wealth in M&A transactions.

## Theoretical background

The market for corporate control theory posits that <sup>fi</sup>rms compete for ownership and control of businesses (Manne,

1965). If the current owner of a business does not have the capabilities to add value to the assets of the business then the assets underperform, the business becomes undervalued, and it eventually becomes an acquisition target. This argument is consistent with the RBV of M&A, which posit that <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior capabilities seek opportunities to deploy the excess capacity of those capabilities to new uses in newly acquired businesses (Robins & Wiersema, 1995). The M&A deals seek to transfer superior capabilities of acquirers to targets, and redeploy poorly managed assets of targets toward more pro<sup>fi</sup>table uses in acquirers (Capron & Pistre, 2002).

As noted above, IT capability is an important class of organizational capability for governing a business well and adding value to its assets (Bharadwaj, 2000; Chi et al, 2010; Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010; Rai & Tang, 2010; Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011; Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011; Rai et al, 2012). The logic of the market for corporate control perspective suggests that the differences in IT capabilities between an acquirer and its target could drive M&A transactions over and beyond the effects of previously studied drivers of M&A activity and M&A performance (e.g., merger waves, deal, target, and acquirer characteristics). That is, <sup>fi</sup>rms that have weak IT capabilities could fail to add value to their assets, and hence they could face higher risk of being acquired by other <sup>fi</sup>rms that have superior IT capabilities. By building on the logic of the RBV, it is also plausible to expect that <sup>fi</sup>rms possessing superior IT capabilities are more likely to look for acquisition opportunities where they can redeploy those capabilities to targets whose IT capabilities are relatively weaker.

When the deal closes and the two <sup>fi</sup>rms are combined under the governance of the acquirer, the acquirer will seek to share resources, exploit cross-<sup>fi</sup>rm synergies, and realize the expected value-creation potential of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms (Coff, 1999). But, at the time of the M&A deal announcement, the two <sup>fi</sup>rms are not operationally integrated yet. There is uncertainty about whether the acquirer will be able to exploit the expected value-creation potential of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms. To document the expected valuation of investors on the acquisitions, previous studies widely use the event study methodology which is built upon the idea that capital markets take into account new information released by the deal announcement as well as existing information about industry, deal, target, and acquirer characteristics (Brown & Warner, 1980, 1985; Moeller et al, 2004). Thus, the capital markets’ reaction surrounding the M&A announcement makes a forward-looking estimation about how much value the combined entity (acquirer and target) is likely to create after the deal closes and the two <sup>fi</sup>rms start operating as one entity. That is, the adjustments in the stock price of the acquirer re<sup>fl</sup>ect the capital markets' forward-looking expectations about the change in shareholder wealth in response to M&A. We follow these studies and posit that the capital markets are likely to take into account IT capabilities of acquirer and target in estimating the shareholder wealth effects of a newly announced M&A. To isolate the effects of IT capabilities, we control for alternative drivers of shareholder wealth such as the characteristics of industry, deal, target, and acquirer.

## Hypotheses development

## The role of IT capability asymmetries between acquirer and target

The theory of market for corporate control and the RBV of M&A reviewed above assume that superior capabilities of acquirers are scale-free. That is, superior capabilities of acquirers can be redeployed to targets with little or no cost. The values of the capabilities do not diminish as a result of their application over the expanded scale and scope of the combined <sup>fi</sup>rm’s operations (Levinthal & Wu, 2010). These assumptions are often not valid for transfer of IT capabilities between acquirers and targets in M&A for two reasons. First, the pervasive IT integration challenges and failures observed in M&A integrations indicate that IT capability transfers are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and costly (Merali & McKiernan, 1993; Johnston & Yetton, 1996; Wijnhoven et al, 2006; Mehta & Hirschheim, 2007; Henningsson & Carlsson, 2011; Tanriverdi & Du, 2011a, b). A survey by PricewaterhouseCoopers <sup>fi</sup>nds that about 83% of acquirers are not able to integrate IT (Shay, 2002). Second, operating models of the acquirer and target are often different. The challenge in transferring IT capabilities is not simply about redeploying the excess productive capacity of one <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT capability to the same use in the other <sup>fi</sup>rm. Rather, it is often about redesigning and recon<sup>fi</sup>guring the IT capabilities, operating models, and business processes of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms to ensure that the IT capabilities are applicable in the expanded scale and scope of the combined <sup>fi</sup>rm. Thus, IT capabilities are often not scale-free in M&A integrations.

IT M&A integration projects seeking to redeploy superior IT capabilities of the acquirer to the target are often much more complex and larger in scope than regular IT projects. The requirements of regular IT projects are often relatively well understood. In the M&A context, IT requirements of the combined entity (acquirer and target) are not understood well enough until business functions of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms come together and make integration decisions. The two <sup>fi</sup>rms start to learn about each other’s businesses and IT environments in detail only after the deal closes. Before the deal closes, the acquirer has only limited access to the data room and employees of the target to conduct high-level IT due diligence (Vielba & Vielba, 2006). Even if both <sup>fi</sup>rms have very well documented and optimized operating models before the deal, the merger requires changes in both <sup>fi</sup>rms’ operating models to enable integration (Allen et al, 2002). Thus, in the short run, the focus of IT integration is often on relatively simple IT infrastructure connectivity (e.g., secure connectivity, e-mail migration, desktop support migration etc.) and back-of<sup>fi</sup>ce function integration (e.g., payroll, HR, <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting). Business decisions on the integration of the core value-adding functions of the <sup>fi</sup>rms such as R&D integration, product integration, and sales force integration spread over time.

Until those decisions are made, the requirements for IT application integration and IT capabilities are not known. Furthermore, IT integration projects encompass almost all layers of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT infrastructures and business applications. Accordingly, they touch large numbers and types of stakeholders in the business and IT functions of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms. They entail multiple objectives that often con<sup>fl</sup>ict with each other: for example, complete the IT integration quickly in the compressed time frame of the merger, at the lowest possible cost, without disrupting the target’s business, and enable the target to realize revenue growth objectives set by the acquirer.

When the target has IT capabilities that are comparable to those of the acquirer, the acquirer often limits the IT integration to basic connectivity in the IT infrastructure and the back-of<sup>fi</sup>ce functions. It leaves the core valueadding functions of the target alone to minimize disruptions in operations and revenue growth. If IT capabilities of the target are weaker, acquirer is tempted to rip and replace them with its superior IT capabilities. As noted, this approach implies a major change in the management process for the target and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increases the risks and costs of disruptions to the target’s business operations, revenues and growth opportunities. When valuing a newly announced acquisition, capital markets are likely to account for the possibility of such disruptions. The expectation that the imposition of the acquirer’s superior IT capabilities onto the target is likely to cause disruptions can lead to negative stock price reactions, and hence lower the wealth of the acquirer’s shareholders.

## <sup>H1:</sup> Capital markets are likely to react negatively to M&A announcements of acquirers whose IT capabilities are superior to those of the targets.

In the <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis, we have focused on explaining why superior IT capabilities of the acquirers relative to those of the targets are likely to lower the wealth of the acquirers’ shareholders, on average. We are yet to explain how this overall relationship may be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed under different contingencies. In our second and third hypotheses, we identify two important contingencies where superior IT capabilities of the acquirer can engender positive stock price reactions to new M&A announcements and create wealth for acquirers’ shareholders.

## The contingency of synergistic same-industry acquisitions

The differences in operating models and in competitive and regulatory environments of an acquirer and its target require different IT capabilities. Thus, these differences limit the scale-free feature of IT capabilities in M&A integrations. One contingency that might address this limitation is the industry-relatedness of the target. If the target is operating in the same industry as the acquirer, then the two <sup>fi</sup>rms share the same institutional environment (Powell & DiMaggio, 1991). They are subject to the same competitive dynamics (Dess & Beard, 1984). They serve the same customer base. They employ similar skills and know-how in serving the customers. Thus, the merger holds high synergy potential. The two <sup>fi</sup>rms may have differences in operating models. But it is easier for the acquirer to impose its superior IT capabilities onto the target because the customers, competitive and regulatory environments of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms are the same. Asymmetries in the IT capabilities of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms are re<sup>fl</sup>ections of how well they organized for competing in the same environment.

The main strategic rationale behind the same-industry acquisitions is to achieve economies of scale through the consolidation of similar operations of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms, and creation of operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies and synergies in the combined <sup>fi</sup>rm. If the target’s IT capabilities are weaker for operating in this industry, ripping and replacing them with the superior IT capabilities of the acquirer is likely to result in more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient operations. The risk of disruptions in operations and revenues of the target is lower in the same-industry acquisitions because the IT capabilities of the acquirer are already supporting similar operations and customers.

Since IT capabilities are more scale-free in same-industry acquisitions, the superiority of the acquirer’s IT capabilities signals to the capital markets that the acquirer is more likely to transfer its superior IT capabilities to the target, add more value to the target’s assets, increase the scale and scope of its operations, and achieve operational synergies without disruptions. These expectations, in turn, are likely to engender positive stock price reactions to the announcement of the M&A.

<sup>H2:</sup> In M&A announcements in which acquirer and target are from the same industry, capital markets are likely to react positively to the announcements of acquirers whose IT capabilities are superior to those of the targets.

## The contingency of acquirer’s track record in pro<sup>fi</sup>tability and growth

Another key contingency that could affect how capital markets react with a new M&A announcement of an acquirer that has superior IT capabilities is the track record of the acquirer in pro<sup>fi</sup>tability and growth. There is often a major trade-off between the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability and growth objectives of acquisitions. Some acquirers emphasize growth at the expense of pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. They acquire targets to add new products to the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s portfolio of offerings. An expanded product portfolio can enable the acquirer to meet multiple product needs of its existing customer base. It can also open up new market possibilities and expand its total addressable space of customer markets. Thus, the acquirer can grow its revenues. However, when the acquirer makes the trade-off in favor of growth, imposing the superior IT capabilities of the acquirer onto the target and integrating the core value-adding functions of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms such as R&D, production, and sales could be highly disruptive to the target’s operations and revenue growth.

Leaving the target autonomous is not desirable because operational costs of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms can grow faster than the revenues and hurt the long-run pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of the combined <sup>fi</sup>rm. Thus, despite their superior IT capabilities, acquirers that have a track record of only growth without a simultaneous track record of pro<sup>fi</sup>tability are not likely to receive signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive stock price reactions to their new M&A announcements.

Some acquirers emphasize the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability objective of acquisition at the expense of the growth objective. They seek to squeeze as much ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains and cost synergies out of the target as possible. They give much less priority to growing the revenues of the combined <sup>fi</sup>rm. The ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains and cost savings improve the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of the combined <sup>fi</sup>rm, but they do not offer long-run growth potential. Thus, despite their superior IT capabilities, acquirers that have a track record of only pro<sup>fi</sup>tability without a simultaneous track record of growth are not likely to receive signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive stock price reactions to their new M&A announcements.

The ultimate desired end-state of acquirers in M&A would be pro<sup>fi</sup>table growth. Pursuing both the growth and the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability objectives simultaneously implies high levels of IT integration in the combined <sup>fi</sup>rm. The integration of IT infrastructures and applications of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms is one of the most important precursors to the integration of business operations, products (Zollo & Singh, 2004), brands, pricing policies, sales structures, and customer services of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms (Homburg & Bucerius, 2006). IT integration enables the exploitation of potential business synergies between the two <sup>fi</sup>rms by enabling the exchange of relevant R&D and operations knowledge, customer knowledge, and managerial policies, processes, and practices across the businesses at a large scale (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011). For reasons discussed above, however, such high levels of IT integration signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase the risks and costs of disruptions, not just to the target but also to the acquirer’s operations. However, an acquirer that already has a track record of pro<sup>fi</sup>table growth can reduce these concerns for capital markets. The superiority of the

IT capabilities of a pro<sup>fi</sup>tably growing acquirer can increase the con<sup>fi</sup>dence of the capital markets that the <sup>fi</sup>rm will be able to continue its pattern of pro<sup>fi</sup>table growth with the newly announced acquisition as well by overcoming the challenges of high levels of IT integration. Thus:

<sup>H3:</sup> Capital markets are likely to react positively to M&A announcements of profitably growing acquirers, whose IT capabilities are superior to those of the targets.

## Methods

## Sample and data sources

In testing our hypotheses, we focus on large domestic acquisitions undertaken by large <sup>fi</sup>rms in the United States. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally; we obtain completed acquisitions that are listed in the Securities Data Company’s (SDC) M&A database as a merger or an acquisition of majority interest with transaction value greater than US\$100 million between 1998 and 2007. We obtain accounting information on acquirers and targets from the Compustat database. We extract stock returns from the CRSP data set. Finally, we obtain data on our corporate governance controls from the Investor Responsibility Research Centre (IRRC) database.

As detailed in Table 1, we conduct a multi-way match across all data sources and apply standard data cleaning practices of prior M&A studies to form our sample. This process results in a <sup>fi</sup>nal sample of 549 acquisitions by 369 <sup>fi</sup>rms. In the <sup>fi</sup>nal sample, the average sales of acquirers is \$10.526 billion (SD = \$17.659 billion), whereas the average transaction value of the acquisitions is \$3.144 billion (SD = \$7.231 billion). In comparison, the average sales of the entire population of acquirers in the SDC database during the same timeframe is \$3.694 billion (SD = \$11.467 billion), whereas the average transaction value of the acquisitions is \$892 million (SD = \$4.749 billion). These descriptive statistics indicate that our sample contains large acquisitions of large <sup>fi</sup>rms.

## Dependent variable

Cumulative abnormal returns (CAR) We use the event study methodology (Brown & Warner, 1980, 1985) to measure announcement period shareholder wealthcreation effects of a newly announced M&A. The event study methodology assumes that capital markets are ef<sup>fi</sup>cient. In reacting with the news of a new M&A announcement, investors process and incorporate into the stock price of the acquirer the new information in the announcement as well as existing information about acquirer and target characteristics such as IT capabilities of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms, and the two <sup>fi</sup>rms’ potential to create synergy with each other (McWilliams & Siegel, 1997). We seek to isolate the effects of IT capabilities on announcement period shareholder wealth-creation effects of M&A by controlling for previously studied factors of shareholder wealth creation in M&A such as synergies.

We compute the abnormal returns (AR<sub>i</sub>) of an acquirer i in a 3-day ‘event window’ [ 1,+1], surrounding the formal announcement date of an acquisition (day 0) by examining deviations of the acquirer’s actual returns $( R _ { i } )$ from the expected normal returns $( R _ { n } )$ had it not undertaken the acquisition:

$$
A R _ {i} = R _ {i} - R _ {n}\tag{1}
$$

In choosing the event window, we keep in mind that small event windows may miss early market reaction, whereas large event windows may capture information− unrelated to the event (Haleblian & Finkelstein, 1999). Following Moeller et al (2004), we cumulate the abnormal returns over a 3-day event window [ 1,+1]:

$$
C A R _ {i} (- 1, 1) = \sum_ {t = - 1} ^ {1} (A R _ {i t})\tag{2}
$$

The standard event study methodology computes− expected normal returns $( R _ { n } )$ of the <sup>fi</sup>rm in equation (1), from an estimated market model for the <sup>fi</sup>rm in an ‘estimation period’ such as 200 trading days [ 205, 6] before the event window (Brown & Warner, 1980, 1985). This approach assumes that there is no other acquisition in the estimation period. In samples of ‘frequent acquirers’ that conduct multiple acquisitions per year, one or more acquisitions may fall into the estimation period and confound the computation of the expected normal returns. To address this issue, prior studies drop the estimation period approach for samples of frequent acquirers. Instead, they use alternative proxies for the expected normal returns, such as the value-weighted market returns or the equally weighted market returns (Haleblian & Finkelstein, 1999; Fuller et al, 2002). Since our sample contains some frequent acquirers, we set the expected normal returns of an acquirer, $R _ { n } ,$ equal to the value-weighted market returns.

We also conduct robustness analyses using the conventional estimation period approach used in the standard event study methodology. The results remain intact in this methodology reported in Model 8 of Table 3.

## Independent variable

Relative IT capability To capture asymmetries between IT capabilities of acquirer and target, we <sup>fi</sup>rst obtain measures of the strengths of IT capabilities of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we use a publicly available data source that assesses IT capabilities of <sup>fi</sup>rms. Every year, Information Week (IW) magazine invites <sup>fi</sup>rms to submit application packages for listing consideration in their annual IW500 list. IW asks a panel of IW experts and industry peers of the applicants to assess whether IT capabilities of the applicant <sup>fi</sup>rms are above average in their respective industries. This assessment is not simply about IT investments of <sup>fi</sup>rms into generic IT applications and services provided by IT vendors. It is about how the <sup>fi</sup>rm con<sup>fi</sup>gures and customizes those generic IT resources to its speci<sup>fi</sup>c business, and how the <sup>fi</sup>rm achieves technological, procedural, and organizational innovations with those IT resources. If a <sup>fi</sup>rm is selected into the IW500 list, it is considered to have strong IT capabilities. Thus, we measure the strength of IT capability of an acquirer (target) in our sample by assessing whether or not the <sup>fi</sup>rm was listed [1 = strong IT capability] in the IW500 list [0 = weak IT capability] in the year before the acquisition (IW Dummy). From this measure, we construct a ‘Relative IT Capability’ measure for the acquirer by subtracting− the target’s IW Dummy from the acquirer’s IW Dummy. The relative IT capability measure takes on the value of [ 1] if the target is in the IW500 list (stronger IT capability), but acquirer is not (weaker IT capability); [0] if both <sup>fi</sup>rms are in the IW500 list or both <sup>fi</sup>rms are absent from the list (comparable IT capabilities); and [+1] if the acquirer is in the IW500 list (stronger IT capability), but target is not (weaker IT capability).

Prior studies in IS have used IW500 membership as a measure of IT capability of <sup>fi</sup>rms (e.g., Bharadwaj, 2000; Santhanam & Hartono, 2003). Recent studies have also shown that this measure is conceptually and empirically more valid than alternative IT capability proxies such as IT spending and IW500 rank (Wang & Alam, 2007; Muhanna & Stoel, 2010). IW500 membership as a measure of IT capability is particularly relevant for longitudinal studies such as ours because it is consistently available across all years of the study from the same source. It is also a publicly available data source that capital markets can use to form opinions about IT capability status of public <sup>fi</sup>rms. In our sample, 29.7% of the acquirers make the IW500 list. In comparison, only 5.1% of the targets enter the IW500 list despite the fact that the targets in our sample are also large companies (average transaction value = \$3.144 billion). This pattern con<sup>fi</sup>rms our theoretical expectation that IT capabilities are likely to be asymmetrically distributed among acquirers and targets.

It also attests to the importance of studying whether and how the asymmetries between IT capabilities of acquirers and targets make a difference in shareholder wealth effects of M&A.

## Control variables

Prior M&A research examined various alternative predictors of CAR in measuring shareholder wealth effects of new M&A announcements. Those predictors can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into characteristics of (a) deal, (b) industry, (c) target, and (d) acquirer. We include these characteristics as control variables in our study to be able to isolate the effects of IT capabilities on CAR and rule out potential alternative explanations to our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. We also control for factors that are likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the independent and dependent variables of the study simultaneously to minimize potential endogeneity concerns.

## Deal characteristics

Method of payment Prior studies indicate that the market reacts differently to stock and cash offers in M&A (Myers & Majluf, 1984). Since stock offers provide options like payoffs in uncertain situations, cash offers can signal that the acquirer has more certainty and con-<sup>fi</sup>dence about the synergy potential of a target (Travlos, 1987). Accordingly, prior studies <sup>fi</sup>nd that the market reacts positively with cash offers (Travlos, 1987; Fuller et al, 2002). To control for the method of payment, we include a dummy that takes on a value of [1] for all-cash offers and [0] otherwise.

Hostile offer Successful merger integration and the realization of expected value-creation potential of the merger require the cooperation of the target’s management team. Since a hostile offer is likely to reduce the willingness of the target to cooperate, it can signal challenges in realizing value-creation potential of the merger. Schwert (2000) shows that hostile offers destroy shareholder value. Thus, we control for hostile offers. We follow Moeller et al (2004) and include a hostile dummy that takes on the value 1 if the deal is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as hostile in the SDC database, or 0 otherwise.

Multiple bidders If multiple potential acquirers bid for the same target, a bidding war may ensue. As bidders increase their bids and in<sup>fl</sup>ate the price paid for the target, the highest bidder wins the bid, but it destroys value for its shareholders due to overpayment (Barney, 1988; Harrison et al, 1991; Capron & Pistre, 2002). Thus, we control if there were multiple bidders for the targets in our sample. We add a ‘multiple bidder’ dummy that takes on the value of 1 if there are multiple bidders identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the SDC database, or 0 otherwise (Moeller et al, 2004).

Relative size As the size of target increases relative to the size of acquirer, there could be greater synergy exploitation opportunities for the acquirer (Capron, 1999). But the increasing relative acquisition size could also pose greater integration and assimilation challenges. We measure relative acquisition size by computing the ratio of the transaction value of the target to the book value of the acquirer’s assets.

## Industry characteristics

Industry M&A liquidity M&A dynamics in an industry can in<sup>fl</sup>uence acquisition decisions and returns of acquirers. We control for the liquidity of the market for corporate assets within an industry by including the Industry M&A liquidity measure which is computed as the sum of acquisitions values for each year and three-digit SIC code divided by the total assets of all Compustat <sup>fi</sup>rms in the same three-digit SIC and year (Schlingemann et al, 2002). This variable controls for the intensity of M&A transactions in an industry and the extent to which the industry is going through an M&A wave in a given year.

## Target characteristics

Industry relatedness of target To capture the extent to which a target has synergy-creation potential with acquirer, we include the most commonly used measure of synergy in M&A literature, that is, the industry relatedness of the target (King et al, 2004). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we use a dummy variable to capture if the acquirer and target operate in the same three-digit SIC industry [1] or different [0] industries (Moeller et al, 2004). As our second hypothesis explains, same industry acquisitions enjoy higher structural similarities and synergy-creation potential.

Market-to-book ratio of target Growth opportunities of the target <sup>fi</sup>rm are re<sup>fl</sup>ected in stock prices and indicate the potential to generate value for the shareholders (Myers & Majluf, 1984). We control for the marketto-book ratios of target to account for its potential to increase value of the merged <sup>fi</sup>rm. We compute marketto-book ratio of acquirer as the market value of equity plus book value of debt over book value of assets.

Pre-acquisition IT capability of target The strength of the pre-acquisition IT capability of the target could determine acquirer’s IT integration approach and challenges. Thus, we include the pre-acquisition IT capability of the target (IW dummy of target) as a control. As explained above, we compute the relative IT capability of the acquirer with respect to the pre-acquisition IT capability of the target.

## Acquirer characteristics

Firm size Large acquirer size is considered to be a source of managerial hubris and overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence (Moeller et al, 2004). Moeller et al (2004) attribute poor acquisitions of large <sup>fi</sup>rms to managerial hubris at these <sup>fi</sup>rms. Thus, we control for size of the acquirer. We measure <sup>fi</sup>rm size as the sales of the acquirer at the end of the <sup>fi</sup>scal year before acquisition announcement date.

Market-to-book ratio of acquirer Growth opportunities of an acquirer may also in<sup>fl</sup>uence market reaction to acquisition announcements. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, <sup>fi</sup>rms with better growth opportunities may transfer these growth opportunities to the target <sup>fi</sup>rm, which may consequently increase shareholder value (Capron & Pistre, 2002). We compute market-to-book ratio of acquirer as the market value of equity plus book value of debt over book value of assets.

Book leverage of acquirer Jensen (1986) argues that managers may make negative net present value investment choices that bene<sup>fi</sup>t them personally at the expense of shareholders when they have access to free cash <sup>fl</sup>ows. A potential solution to this agency problem is to increase the leverage of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Leverage limits managers’ access to free cash <sup>fl</sup>ows and motivates managers to improve <sup>fi</sup>rm performance since they would risk losing their jobs if the <sup>fi</sup>rm falls into <sup>fi</sup>nancial distress (Masulis et al, 2007). These arguments have been used to suggest that managers of leveraged <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to make valueincreasing acquisitions (Uysal, 2011). Thus, we control for leverage by computing the ratio of total debt to total assets (Moeller et al, 2004).

Prior acquisition experience of acquirer Acquirers have great potential to learn from their prior acquisition experiences, but research shows that they generally fail to realize this potential (Hayward, 2002). Billett & Qian (2008) argue that experience in undertaking acquisitions could lead to overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence regarding managers’ abilities in creating value out of the acquisitions. To account− − for these factors, we control for prior acquisition experience of acquirers. We compute a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s total number of acquisitions in the past 3 years (from t 3 to t 1) before the event and take the natural log of the total to ensure normal distribution of this variable.

Prior <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of acquirer Ex post performance differences could be an artifact of systematic ex ante performance differences, not the true impacts of acquisitions per se (Park, 2003). Prior empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior prior <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance tend to achieve better acquisition performance (Morck et al, 1990). Thus, we control for the acquirer’s prior <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. We compute the acquirer’s ratio of earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization to total assets (EBITDA/TA) at the end of the <sup>fi</sup>scal year before the acquisition.

GIM index Anti-takeover provisions adopted by boards limit the possibility of being taken over; thereby, they empower managers while weakening the shareholders rights. The asymmetric effects of anti-takeover provisions on managers and shareholders may generate opportunities for managers to make poor acquisition decisions. Following this intuition, Masulis et al (2007) show that <sup>fi</sup>rms with low shareholders rights make poor acquisitions. As in Masulis et al (2007), we use the GIM index constructed by Gompers et al (2003). This index is the total number of anti-takeover provisions adopted by the <sup>fi</sup>rm, and a high number of GIM index indicates ‘high management power’ or the ‘weak shareholder rights’ (Gompers et al, 2003; Masulis et al, 2007).

Pro<sup>fi</sup>tability and growth track records of acquirers We generate four categories of historical pro<sup>fi</sup>tability and growth patterns for assessing if acquirers have track records of pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, growth, or both in the 3 years before a new M&A announcement in our sample. Speci-<sup>fi</sup>cally, we classify an acquirer as ‘High Growth’ <sup>fi</sup>rm if its average market-to-book ratio in the trailing 3 years is greater than the mean market-to-book ratio of its industry peers. Similarly, we classify an acquirer as ‘highpro<sup>fi</sup>tability’ <sup>fi</sup>rm if its average EBITDA/TA in the trailing 3 years is greater than the mean EBITDA/TA of its industry peers. These two classi<sup>fi</sup>cations in turn allow us to generate four categories of <sup>fi</sup>rms: (a) high growth and high pro<sup>fi</sup>tability; (b) high growth and low pro<sup>fi</sup>tability; (c) low growth and high pro<sup>fi</sup>tability; and (4) low growth and low pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. In our regression analyses, we use the low growth and low pro<sup>fi</sup>tability category as our base case.

Period effects Finally, we include year dummies to account for potential period effects due to macroeconomic factors.

## Discussion and conclusions

How IT capabilities of acquirer and target affect shareholder wealth in M&A is an important but understudied research question (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011). This study advances our understanding of this question by studying how capital markets view IT capability asymmetries between an acquirer and its target, how they interpret the asymmetries to form expectations about the acquirer’s IT M&A integration approach and synergy-creation potential, and how they adjust stock price of the acquirer in new M&A announcements and affect shareholder wealth.

Consistent with the theory of market for corporate control and the RBV of M&A, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that there are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant asymmetries between IT capabilities of acquirers and targets in M&A transactions. In the population of M&A transactions, acquirers tend to have stronger IT capabilities whereas targets often have weaker IT capabilities. However, contrary to the assumptions and propositions of these theories, IT capabilities are not scale-free. Acquirers that possess superior IT capabilities face notorious challenges and costs in transferring them to the targets. Thus, the predictions of these theories about the performance consequences of the superior capabilities do not always hold. We articulate the conditions under which superior IT capabilities of acquirer create shareholder wealth.

On average, the capital markets react negatively with the superiority of acquirer’s IT capabilities. This negative reaction is mainly due to the concern and expectation that the acquirer will rip and replace the target’s IT, impose its own IT onto the target, and in the process signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly disrupt the target’s operations, revenues, and growth. Put differently, on average, capital markets do not view IT capabilities as scale-free. On the contrary, they expect acquirers to experience signi<sup>fi</sup>cant dif<sup>fi</sup>culty and cost in integrating their IT with those of the targets. This <sup>fi</sup>nding implies that IT M&A integration does not always create shareholder wealth, a <sup>fi</sup>nding that challenges the received wisdom in IT M&A integration research.

However, there are two key contingencies that modify and reverse the expectations of the capital markets and lead to positive stock price reactions to M&A announcements. One contingency is the industry relatedness of the target. In a same-industry acquisition, an acquirer and its target enjoy higher structural similarities and synergy potential. Capital markets expect IT capabilities to be closer to scale-free because of the structural similarities of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms. Investors react positively with M&A announcements of acquirers that possess superior IT capabilities because they expect those IT capabilities to transfer to the same-industry targets relatively easily and without major costs. A second contingency is the acquirer’s track record in pro<sup>fi</sup>table growth. Even when IT capabilities are not scale-free and present major transfer challenges and costs in M&A, capital markets may expect the acquirer to overcome the IT integration challenges if the acquirer has a track record of pro<sup>fi</sup>table growth and superior IT capabilities. They expect a pro<sup>fi</sup>tably growing acquirer to add value to the target by exploiting its superior IT capabilities and continue its pro<sup>fi</sup>table growth pattern. Interestingly, the main effect of the acquirer’s track record in pro<sup>fi</sup>table growth is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. This implies that capital markets do not take new M&A performance of a pro<sup>fi</sup>tably growing acquirer for granted. They also look for the presence of superior IT capabilities to gain con-<sup>fi</sup>dence that the acquirer will be able to continue its pro-<sup>fi</sup>table growth pattern with the new acquisition. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings uncover some of the contingencies under which IT M&A integration creates shareholder wealth.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the returns to the acquirer’s shareholders also make important contributions to the broader M&A research. The cumulative <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the M&A studies show that the average announcement period returns to shareholders of the acquirers are slightly negative or 0 (Andrade et al, 2001; King et al, 2004). In addition, prior studies <sup>fi</sup>nd that in samples of large target acquisitions of large acquirers, acquirers end up destroying shareholder wealth on a massive scale (Moeller et al, 2004, 2005). Our study also uses a similar sample of large target acquisitions of large acquirers. But it shows that a subsample of acquirers, namely those who have superior IT capabilities and track records of pro<sup>fi</sup>table growth are able to create shareholder wealth even in large acquisitions.

Overall, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that, in valuing a newly announced M&A, capital markets take into account IT capability asymmetries between acquirer and target. They interpret these asymmetries to form forward-looking expectations about the acquirer’s IT M&A integration approach and synergy-creation potential. Contrary to the received wisdom in IS research, superior IT capabilities do not always ensure shareholder wealth creation in M&A.

## Implications for practice

Business and IT executives ought to recognize that superior IT capabilities of acquirers are not always scale-free and that they cannot be transferred to targets without major risks and costs. Replacing IT capabilities of targets with superior IT capabilities of acquirers could be tempting for IS leaders of acquirers. However, if the IT capabilities are not scale-free, this approach could cause signi<sup>fi</sup>cant disruptions in business operations and growth prospects of the target and lead to the destruction of shareholder wealth. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that IT capabilities are likely to be closer to scale-free in acquisitions from the same industry.
