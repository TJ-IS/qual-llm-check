# Designing consumer interfaces for experiential tasks: an empirical investigation

John D. Wells<sup>1</sup>, William L. Fuerst<sup>2</sup> and Jonathan W. Palmer<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Washington State University, WA, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>University of Kansas, U.S.A.; <sup>3</sup>College of William & Mary, U.S.A.

## Abstract

In the early adoption phase of business-to-consumer (B2C) ecommerce, the tasks that proved most conducive to online consumer interaction typically were goal-directed, being clear in sequence and structure. A key challenge in ecommerce is the ability to design interfaces that support experiential tasks in addition to goal-directed tasks. Most of the ecommerce research on interface design, however, has focused on goal-directed tasks and has not addressed experiential tasks. Based on the literature from interface metaphors and mental models, this paper explores the use of tangible attributes derived from the physical business domain as a technique for designing an interface that effectively supports experiential tasks. A laboratory experiment was designed and conducted to test the impact of two types of interfaces and business domain familiarity when completing an experiential task. Because consumers need to retain and recall information to evaluate products/services or to make brand associations, retention/recall of information was measured on both the day of the treatment and after a 2-day lag. Results revealed that the interface based upon the business domain metaphor stimulated higher levels of retention and recall of information and thus provided the desired support for experiential tasks. Further, users with weaker domain familiarity showed the greatest improvement in retention and recall, particularly after a 2-day lag, when using the interface with the business domain metaphor design. ## Introduction

The growth of the WWW and related electronic commerce applications have extended consumer behavior research beyond the offline, traditional brick-n-mortar environments to online, virtual environments (Burke, 2002). From an online consumer interaction perspective, early research points to search motives (i.e., tasks) as a means to predict consumer behavior and presents a dichotomous contrast of online consumer tasks: goal-directed vs experiential (Hoffman & Novak, 1996). Goal-directed tasks possess an inherent structure that guides consumers when they are engaged in linear, search-directed activities. An example of a goal-directed task is when a consumer searches for specific product information such as price or quantity-in-stock. Conversely, an experiential task is less structured and supports non-linear, non-search-directed activities. An example of an experiential task is when a consumer browses for fun or to obtain general information that would normally be gathered via direct experience with the business domain. Given that most ecommerce research has focused on designing interfaces for goaldirected tasks, a key challenge is the design of interfaces to support experiential tasks (Alba et al., 1997).

When electronic commerce first emerged, researchers pointed to interface design for consumers as an important research issue (Lohse & Spiller, 1998). As a result, information systems (IS) researchers have provided a necessary and useful foundation for studying consumer interface design. An examination of consumer attitudes and expectations (Jarvenpaa & Todd, 1996) has led to the identification of web usability criteria (Nielsen, 1999; Chau et al., 2000) and, subsequently, the validation of instruments to measure these criteria (Agarwal & Venkatesh, 2002; Palmer, 2002). Yet, the majority of this research assumes a unilateral focus on structured, goaldirected tasks. To date, little is known regarding the issues associated with the design of consumer interfaces that support experiential tasks.

While the need to design interfaces that support experiential tasks has been the topic of recent preliminary research (Li et al., 2002; Jiang & Benbasat, 2003), there are currently no suggested design techniques or approaches for creating an interface that may support such tasks. The primary goal of designing a consumer interface that can support experiential tasks is the ability to convey a ‘virtual’ experience of the business domain that acts as a surrogate for direct, real-world experience (Li et al., 2001). This paper describes the use of an interface metaphor as a technique that may prove effective for designing this type of consumer interface. An interface metaphor uses an understandable source domain that facilitates the understanding of a more complex target domain (Lackoff & Johnson, 1980; Baecker et al., 1995). For instance, the desktop metaphor used folder and paper icons (i.e., source domain) to facilitate the understanding of an operating system (i.e., target domain). With respect to experiential tasks, using the business domain as the source domain of an interface metaphor may be an effective technique for designing a consumer interface that supports experiential tasks and provides the consumer with a ‘virtual’ experience of the business domain.

Ideally, a consumer interface should not only be able to support different types of tasks but also a large, heterogeneous consumer base. With a large consumer base comes a variability in user skills and background (Pitkow & Kehoe, 1996) not generally found in traditional workforce IS users. A well-designed interface should be able to bridge the gap between users with varying levels of domain-specific knowledge (Hoffman & Novak, 1996). When considering experiential tasks, variability in user expectations is often dictated by the user’s prior experience in the business domain (Alba et al., 1997). Thus, with the emergence of the consumer as the interface user (Koufaris, 2002), a need exists to understand how the design of the interface potentially affects user perceptions and performance.

The research presented in this paper has two primary objectives. The first objective is to identify a technique for designing consumer interfaces that can support experiential tasks and to test the effectiveness of this type of interface. The second objective is to understand the impact of the consumer’s familiarity with the business domain when processing experiential tasks via a consumer interface. The relevant literature for this study comes from two primary theoretical sources: interface metaphors and user mental models, and is framed within existing research on consumer choice and information presentation. Using this literature as a theoretical foundation, specific hypotheses are proposed that explore how consumers interact with interface metaphors and how they perform when executing experiential tasks. A laboratory experiment was conducted to test these hypotheses and is described in the research methodology section. An analysis of the results is then presented. Finally, contributions, limitations, and future work are discussed.

## Theoretical foundations: consumer interface design

Consumer interfaces are different from traditional computer interfaces aimed at intra-organizational working professionals. Not only are user characteristics associated with consumer interfaces more heterogeneous (Pitkow & Kehoe, 1996), but the tasks executed via such interfaces possess characteristics that are specific to the purchasing process (O’Keefe & McEachern, 1998). This section provides a theoretical framing for studying consumer interfaces designed for experiential tasks as well as the impact of consumer heterogeneity on this type of interface. First, early consumer choice and information presentation research is briefly reviewed. Next, the contrast between goal-directed and experiential task characteristics is presented along with a definition of an experiential task.

## Consumer choice and information presentation

Early consumer choice literature initially focused on how information presentation affects a consumers’ ability to store and process information (Bettman & Kakkar, 1977; Bettman, 1979) and, subsequently, the ability to retain and recall relevant information (Johnson & Russo, 1984). In an electronic commerce domain, these same information presentation issues apply as organizations must effectively present information to facilitate the tasks within the consumer choice process: need recognition, information search, evaluation, purchase, and postpurchase service (O’Keefe & McEachern, 1998). Experiential tasks can emerge at various stages across the consumer choice process, particularly the evaluation stage, implying a need to present carefully information to support the characteristics of the consumer’s task.

Prior to the emergence of ecommerce, MIS research on information presentation focused on issues associated with the tables vs graphs dichotomy (Lucas, 1981; Benbasat & Dexter, 1986a, b; Dickson et al., 1986; Jarvenpaa and Dicksen, 1988). One key insight from this early work was the need to qualify the task for the given mode of information presentation (Dickson et al., 1986). Subsequent research examined the representational fit between task types and information presentation and found that a congruence between information presentation and task type improved user performance (Vessey, 1991; Vessey & Galletta, 1991). Another key issue that emerged from this research stream was the effect of interface user characteristics (Lucas & Nielsen, 1980), which has been established as an important factor for designing effective user interfaces (Aykin & Aykin, 1991).

The MIS information presentation literature provides a foundation for designing ecommerce interfaces from two perspectives. First, integrated forms of textual and graphical information presentation can be designed to support specific task characteristics. Second, a consumer’s familiarity with the interaction domain can be explored as an individual difference that may affect how different interface structures support experiential tasks.

## Experiential tasks

One of the primary distinctions between goal-directed and experiential tasks is that experiential tasks are considered to be more hedonistic, rather than utilitarian (Hoffman & Novak, 1996; Wolfinbarger & Gilly, 2001). For example, an experiential task such as casual browsing is considered fun or playful (Wolfinbarger & Gilly, 2001) while a goal-directed task such as product comparison focuses on efficiency and deliberate action (Hoffman & Novak, 1996).

Compared to goal-directed tasks, experiential tasks are relatively unstructured as they rely on non-directed search activities and non-linear navigational choices. For instance, a consumer who browses a web site to gather general information about an organization is neither guided nor bounded by the nature of the task; they simply navigate in a direction of their choosing. Conversely, a goal-directed task such as searching for product specifications will direct the consumer to certain content categories on the web site.

While goal-directed tasks are effective for processing search attributes associated with indirect observation of a product/service, a key distinguishing characteristic of experiential tasks is the processing of experiential attributes associated with the direct observation of the product/ service (Alba et al., 1997). Search attributes such as price and quantity-in-stock do not require the direct observation of the product/service being evaluated and are easily accessible via consumer interfaces. Experiential attributes, however, do require the direct observation of the product/service and typically include the evaluation of product/service characteristics such as color, size, and shape.

Mathwick et al. (2002) argue that the inherent properties of the task dictate how a consumer will perceive the informational attributes associated with the task. Goaldirected tasks typically possess a hierarchical or linear structure that tends to rely on analytic processing, such as when consumers evaluate products/services based on specific decision criteria. Conversely, experiential tasks point to perceptual processing as consumers process information intuitively without deliberate calculation. An example of perceptual processing of an experiential task is the information a consumer gathers by experimenting with various product configurations (e.g., trying on different clothes).

## Hypothesis development and research model

The use of consumer interfaces for experiential tasks has been the focus of recent academic research. From a marketing perspective, Li et al. (2001) qualify direct observation of product attributes mediated via information technology (IT) as ‘virtual’ experience rather than direct observation or experience. The use of IT-mediated 3-D advertising has been observed to have an effect on product knowledge and consumer attitudes (Li et al., 2002). MIS researchers have started to explore the impact of ‘virtual’ experience characteristics such as vividness and interactivity on consumer attitudes and intentions (Jiang & Benbasat, 2003). These studies provide evidence that the design of consumer interfaces for experiential tasks is an important and relevant issue. However, we have just begun to scratch the surface with respect to understanding the underlying dynamics that contribute to the design of this type of consumer interface.

## Interface metaphors

A possible avenue for identifying and designing a consumer interface that can support experiential tasks is the use of interface metaphors. The essence of metaphor is the use of an understandable source domain that facilitates the understanding of a more complex target domain (Lackoff & Johnson, 1980; Baecker et al., 1995). When faced with the challenge of facilitating user understanding of a text-based operating systems (OS) for the personal computer, the designers of the first successful graphical OS (e.g., Apple) turned to a design technique that used familiar, intuitive objects to facilitate user interaction, the desktop interface metaphor. Simply put, users can better understand and remember the features of an OS if the interface metaphor is representative of the task domain. To present a user with an accurate, understandable representation of a target domain (i.e., applications and data), most computer interfaces possess metaphoric attributes from a source domain to facilitate this process. Thus, an interface metaphor is considered to be ‘designed interface actions, procedures, and concepts that exploit specific knowledge that users have of other domains’ (Carroll et al., 1988).

While academicians in the area of human–computer interaction (HCI) have been active in exploring the issues surrounding interface metaphors, most of this research has addressed conceptual (Carroll & Thomas, 1982; Carroll & Mack, 1985; Dieberger & Frank, 1998), methodological (Madsen, 1994), and usability issues (Erickson, 1990; Kay, 1990). Some empirical research exists (Golovchinsky & Chignell, 1997; Vaughan, 1998; Kim, 1999), but it has not focused on the use of interface metaphors to support different types of tasks, nor has it focused on the use of metaphors to support ‘virtual’ experiences.

Currently, a common design technique for hypermedia environments is the book or frame (i.e., table of contents) metaphor (Davies et al., 1991), which is used heavily in electronic commerce websites. The book metaphor uses familiar concepts such as chapters/sections as a source domain to organize content associated with a business domain by logical categories, hereafter referred to as a categorical content interface (see Figure 1a). The categorical content interface is conducive to goal-directed tasks because it: (a) has a utilitarian orientation, (b) structures search activities through linear navigational choices, and (c) effectively presents search attributes. Examining the three steps/screens in Figure 1a reinforces this assertion. The first screen assumes that the consumer wants their exposure to the business domain to be organized into logical informational categories, a more utilitarian orientation. The second and third screens limit the consumer to just one informational category, which is representative of a linear navigational structure. The third screen presents product information (i.e., search attributes), but no experiential context such as where the consumer can expect to find these products within the business domain.

The common, categorical content interface appears to have worked effectively for goal-directed tasks, but may prove ineffective when used for experiential tasks or when the consumer does not have the domain familiarity required to comprehend the information categories presented. This type of interface also runs the risk of violating some basic interface metaphor design principles (Madsen, 1994). These principles recommend using tangible, recognizable objects (Baecker et al., 1995) that reside within the problem domain (Carroll et al., 1988). While the book metaphor uses tangible, recognizable objects (e.g., chapters), these objects are not derived from the business domain (e.g., the product/service being experienced). Thus, the business (product/service) domain may prove to be a better source domain for the design of consumer interfaces that are more effective at supporting experiential tasks.

The characteristics of an experiential task often represent a scenario in which the consumer would like to ‘virtually’ experience the business domain (e.g., product/service) (Alba et al., 1997; Li et al., 2001). We posit that the actual business domain is a logical source for an interface metaphor that is meant to convey a ‘virtual’ experience and provides a tight coupling between the task and business domains. Spatial attributes from the business domain can also be easily presented through such an interface, hereafter referred to as a virtual domain interface (see Figure 1b), conveying a more comprehensive representation of the business domain.

The virtual domain interface is conducive to experiential tasks because it: (a) has hedonic benefits, (b) supports non-linear navigational choice, and (c) effectively presents experiential attributes. Examining the three steps/screens in Figure 1b reinforces this assertion. With respect to hedonistic benefits, the three screens provide the consumer with the flexibility to mouse-over and click on any interactive object they choose, providing both textual and graphical information. Also, the structure of this interface provides more flexibility for consumer browsing, which is representative of its nonlinear navigational structure. The spatial layout of all screens, and specifically the second screen, provides additional spatial domain attributes and an orientation that allows the consumer to better experience the business domain. According to Erickson (1990), an interface metaphor is most effective when it contains a structure that is visually representative of the task domain. As a result, a user’s retention/recall of information conveyed through an interface is enhanced, which is considered important for a number of consumer-related tasks (Johnson & Russo, 1984). Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: When performing an experiential task, a virtual domain interface will enable a user to retain and recall significantly more information when compared to a categorical content interface.

While the design of the interface is a key determinant for effective consumer interaction, individual characteristics can also affect how users perceive and interpret a consumer interface (Jahng et al., 2002). Because the success of an interface metaphor depends on providing a user with an effective source domain, a consumer’s prior

## Domain familiarity

Mental model theory provides a sound foundation for exploring the impact of domain familiarity as a moderating influence on a given problem domain (Gentner & Stevens, 1983). Norman (1990) defines a mental model as ‘models people have of themselves, others, the environment, and the things with which they interact’ and states that ‘people form such models ‘through experience, training, and instruction’ (p. 17). Further, Norman (1990) frames the concept of mental models into three different areas: the design model, the user’s model, and the system image. The design model is a conceptual model that the designer possesses of the system. The system image is the actual physical structure of the system. Finally, the user model is the user’s conceptual view (or mental model) of the system. Mental model theory stresses the importance for the design and user models to be highly convergent (Norman, 1990). Simply stated, the system designer must be able to create functionality that is congruent with the user’s view of the system, making the user’s perception of the task domain an important design consideration and, for the purpose of this research, an instantiation of a user’s mental model.

Gillan et al. (1995) observed some key differences between users who possess strong domain familiarity (SDF) and those who possess weak domain familiarity (WDF). Most notably, SDF users can effectively interpret a wider range of metaphors while WDF users prefer a metaphor that is a more direct representation of the problem domain. SDF users have an advantage over WDF in two areas: (1) a mental model built via real-world experiences is considered stronger than one built via an IT-mediated experience (Biocca, 1997) and (2) an SDF user, as compared to a WDF user, can effectively interpret indirect representations or various metaphors of the problem domain (Gillan et al., 1995). Based on this logic, it is posited that SDF users will have a distinct advantage over WDF users regardless of the mode of interface. Therefore, we offer the following hypothesis for understanding the relative strength of a user’s domain familiarity as it relates to retaining and recalling information presented in a consumer interface:

Hypothesis 2: When performing an experiential task, a user who possesses strong familiarity with the business domain will retain and recall significantly more information when compared to a user with weak domain familiarity.

This research suggests a parallel between the key components of metaphor theory (i.e., source and target domains) and mental model theory (i.e., design model and system image). Further, a parallel can be drawn between the user model in mental model theory and the consumer’s perception of the business domain as a source domain in metaphor theory. These theories suggest performance differences when consumers use different metaphors in support of an experiential task and when there is variation in consumer familiarity with the business domain.

## Relationship between the interface metaphor and domain familiarity

The first two hypotheses focus on the main effects of interface metaphors and domain familiarity, respectively. However, additional insight may be gained from the potential interaction between the type of interface and a user’s domain familiarity.

As stated earlier, Biocca (1997) contends that an ITmediated experience is still inferior to actual real-world, physical experience, which will produce a relatively weaker mental model when compared to a mental model that is a product of real-world interactions. However, recent research does show that an IT-mediated ‘virtual’, but realistic experience may act as an effective surrogate for direct observation of the business domain (Li et al., 2002) as well as building a viable mental model (Biocca, 1997). This research finding suggests that regardless of whether a user’s mental model was built via real-world or virtual experience, a consumer’s ability to retain and recall business domain information is enhanced. Thus, the retention and recall gap between the SDF and WDF user when interacting with the virtual domain interface is expected to be relatively small. Conversely, the SDF user’s mental model when interacting with the categorical content interface will prove much more beneficial compared to a WDF user who does not possess a strong mental model of the business domain. Further, the categorical content interface does not provide a ‘virtual’ experience of the business domain and leaves the WDF user with the same weak mental model. This suggests that the retention and recall gap between the SDF and WDF user when interacting with the categorical content interface will be relatively large. Thus, we offer the following hypothesis that a user’s domain familiarity will influence the effect that the type of interface (categorical content vs virtual domain) will have on information retention and recall.

Hypothesis 3: When performing an experiential task, the positive effect of domain familiarity will be greater on information retention and recall for users interacting with a categorical content interface as compared to a virtual domain interface.

While some statisticians take the viewpoint that a significant interaction nullifies the relevance of main effects (Meyer, 1991), others argue that the nature of the interaction determines whether main effects can be interpreted. Rosnow & Rosenthal (1991) state that ‘plots that are not X-shaped are graphs not of 2 - 2 interactions but of interaction plus something else’ (p. 575). Jaccard (1998) makes this distinction more apparent by labeling X-shaped plots as ‘crossover’ interactions and plots that are not X-shaped, but contain unparallel lines as ‘ordinal’ interactions. Based on the interface metaphor and mental model literature, we are not predicting a crossover interaction, but rather an ordinal interaction, and thus can interpret both the main and interaction effects. ## Research methodology

An experiment was conducted to investigate the impact of interface metaphors and domain familiarity on the user’s ability to retain and recall information when executing an experiential task. The independent variables were mode of interface and domain familiarity. All subjects were asked to execute the same experiential task. The dependent variable consisted of user information retention and recall and is considered a key performance indicator in the context of this research study given the effect on consumer-related constructs such as decision-making and brand associations.

## Problem domain

The problem domain for this study was a hypothetical vacation resort that was based on typical resort businesses. The use of a hypothetical vacation resort was attractive because the possibility of a subject having specific domain knowledge about a particular resort was eliminated. However, the ability to test the domain familiarity effect still exists because subjects can have either a lot or very little experience with vacation resorts in general. Currently, most resort organizations present consumers with various forms of information on the internet including room rates, recreational activities, and reservations. Given the goal of this research, two types of interfaces were developed, a categorical content interface (Figure 1a) and a virtual domain interface (Figure 1b). Users of both interfaces were presented with the same open-ended, experiential task, to learn as much about this resort as possible.

## Independent variables

Mode of interface To ensure that the same information was presented in both interfaces, an information repository was created to store the text and graphics used in the interfaces. An information inventory checklist was used to make sure that the same text and graphics from the central repository were included in each interface. The primary difference in the interfaces was interface structure, with one using experiential domain attributes and the other using more informational domain attributes. These two interfaces represent the mode of interface independent variable (see Figure 2).

The design principles presented by Erickson (1990) and Madsen (1994) were utilized to create a virtual domain interface that incorporates objects from the designated problem domain (see Figure 1b). Experiential, tangible objects from the source domain, a vacation resort, were used to facilitate the user’s understanding of the target domain, resort information. To illustrate, an interactive map of the resort was designed that would allow the user to click on building structures that would, in turn, present context-specific information.

The other interface was a categorical content interface (see Figure 1a) and was designed using established HTML design principles that are often applied in industry (Vora, 1998). This particular interface used a book or frame metaphor as a source domain, which is common in ecommerce web interfaces. The primary difference in the categorical content interface, when compared to the virtual domain interface, was that information was organized by informational categories (i.e., logical information groups) instead of the physical business domain.

Domain familiarity Consistent with prior mental model research (Satzinger & Olfman, 1998; Shayo & Olfman, 2000; Smith-Jentsch et al., 2001), the domain familiarity variable was operationalized by testing the accuracy of a subject’s mental representation of the general problem domain, vacation resorts; prior to the administration of the experiment. A questionnaire was designed to assess a subject’s past experience with vacation resorts and measure the accuracy of his/her mental representation. After subjects completed the questionnaire, a quantitative score was calculated. Based on the overall mean (m ¼ 39.37), subjects were placed in either the strong or weak domain familiarly groups if their score was greater/ less than one standard deviation from the mean. Subjects scoring a 22 or lower were placed in the WDF group while subjects scoring a 56 or higher were placed in the SDF group. Also, only subjects who had been to an actual resort (i.e., real experience) were eligible for the SDF group, while only subjects who had never been to an actual resort (i.e., no experience) were eligible for the WDF group. ## Dependent variable

The dependent variable for this study was a measurement of the amount of information retained and recalled by the user. Information retention was chosen because it is considered to be a primitive, fundamental requirement for consumer information processing (Johnson & Russo, 1984), particularly as it relates to information presentation and consumer choice (Bettman & Kakkar, 1977; Bettman, 1979). From an IT research perspective, the information retention/recall performance measure has been used in past information presentation studies, particularly the tables vs graphs literature (Umanath & Scamell, 1988; Umanath et al., 1990).

As information retention/recall is affected over time, due to effects such as interference (Jenkins & Dallenbach, 1924), information retention and recall was tested directly after the experiment and after a 2-day lag period. Ashcraft (1994), using the Ebbinghaus (1885/1913) forgetting curve, illustrates that retention is significantly affected over a 2-day period. Given the typical time delay between consumer information gathering tasks and an actual purchase, the inclusion of a 2-day re-test seemed relevant for the context of this study.

## Instrument validation

A pilot study with 39 subjects was administered to test the experimental procedures and validate the research instrument. Results from the pilot study were used to refine the instrument and to assess both the content validity and reliability of the measurement instrument. Without being exposed to either interface, 14 subjects were asked to complete the questionnaire, which contained questions about the problem domain, a hypothetical resort. The average number of questions answered correctly was 0.58 (less than 1 out of 30 questions) and most of the answers that were correct were the product of random guessing, thus demonstrating that the subjects were limited in their ability to intuitively answer the questions.

The remaining 25 subjects were exposed to the virtual domain interface to validate the two forms, Form A and Form B. Fourteen subjects were asked to complete Form A and then Form B (2 days later). The remaining 11 subjects were asked to complete Form B and then Form A (2 days later). Using Pearson’s product–moment correlation, the reliability of the subjects’ information retention/recall performance across the two forms was calculated to be 0.85 and 0.82, respectively. Based upon these results, the two instruments were considered to be comparative measures of the subject’s level of information retention/ recall.

## Power analysis

When calculating power, it is important to identify the largest and smallest anticipated effect sizes across all main and interaction effects (Murphy & Myors, 2004, p. 72). In this study, the main effects, particularly the effect associated with domain familiarity, are expected to be relatively small. Conversely, since we are expecting an ordinal interaction (H3), the effect size associated with WDF subjects is expected to be relatively large.

Owing to the lack of a published, relevant effect size for this type of study, the desired sample size for this experiment was calculated using a standardized effect size. Murphy & Myors (2004) use a percentage of variance (PV) coefficient to specify a standardized effect size, which is considered a ‘general approach’ to power analysis (p. 25). Further, the PV value can be crossreferenced to other effect size coefficients such as t (t-test) and f (ANOVA) (Murphy & Myors, 2004, p. 52). To strike the appropriate balance between detecting a statistically significant difference that has pragmatic value, we chose a medium effect size (PV ¼ 0.1), which corresponds to d ¼ 0.5 and f ¼ 0.25. Further, the effect size for an interaction is expected to be larger than the main effect, thus a PV ¼ 0.15 was chosen for this effect, which strikes a balance between a medium and high effect size.

Using the One-Stop F Calculator software (Murphy & Myors, 2004), a sample size of n ¼ 74 was calculated using Power ¼ 0.80 and a PV ¼ 0.10 for the main effects. Next, a sample size of n ¼ 48 was calculated using Power ¼ 0.80 and a PV ¼ 0.15 for the interaction effect. Since this sample size was derived using a cell mean comparison, the sample had to be doubled to n ¼ 96. Thus, a sample of n ¼ 96 is necessary to detect relevant, significant differences for both main and interaction effects.

## Experiment

The subjects for this study were undergraduate students who were enrolled in an introductory management information systems course at a major university. A total of 104 subjects participated in the experiment. Although

265 subjects were eligible for the study after the pre-test screening (see Table 2), the final sample size can be attributed to two factors. First, participation in the study was strictly voluntary, which contributed to subject attrition. Second, because this study was a balanced design and the number of SDF subjects was considerably less than the number of WDF subjects, eight WDF subjects who participated after the last SDF subject were discarded.

Based on their domain familiarity, either weak or strong, subjects were randomly assigned within each group to either a categorical content or virtual domain interface. Next, each subject was provided with the following experiential information-browsing task:

You and a group of your friends and/or family are planning a vacation to an all-inclusive resort. This group has requested that you go to the website for a particular resort and check it out. You will be meeting them for dinner later tonight to make your report. Keep in mind that your overall goal is to convey to your friends and/or family what type of experience they could expect by going to this resort. Therefore, be sure to approach this informationbrowsing task from a broad-minded perspective.

Each subject was given exactly 10 minutes to execute the task. Upon completion of the task, a questionnaire (i.e., Form A) was administered to measure the subject’s information retention and recall. After a 2-day lag, a different instrument (i.e., Form B) was administered to test for the persistence of information retention. The instruments contained information that was similar in format and complexity, but different in context to avoid a learning effect.

## Mode of interface (H1)

This hypothesis was designed to test the effect that mode of interface has on a subject’s ability to retain and recall information when performing an experiential task. For the same-day test and re-test, respectively, the average scores for subjects in the virtual domain interface group were 16.98 and 12.08 while the average scores for subjects in the categorical content interface group were 12.60 and 7.5. A significant difference was found between the amount of information retained by subjects in the two mode of interface groups $( P { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , thus the hypothesis was supported for both tests.

## Domain familiarity (H2)

This hypothesis was designed to test the effect that a subject’s domain familiarity has on his/her ability to retain and retrieve information. For the same-day test, the average score for subjects in the strong domain familiarity group was 15.50 and the weak domain familiarity group was 14.06, revealing a weak difference $( P { = } 0 . 0 7 )$ . The re-test produced a wider gap, with the strong domain familiarity group averaging 10.73 as compared to the weak domain familiarity group at 8.85. Thus, the hypothesis was supported (Po0.01) for the retest.

## Interaction between mode of interface and domain familiarity (H3)

This hypothesis was designed to test the potential ordinal interaction between the mode of interface and the subject’s familiarity with the business domain. For the same-day test, a weak difference was observed (P ¼ 0.08) with the re-test indicating no support for an interaction between the mode of interface and domain familiarity $( P { = } 0 . 2 1 )$ . Further analysis and potential explanations for these mixed results are provided in the following section.

## Summary of results and post hoc analysis

The results of the same-day information retention questionnaire strongly support one of the three hypotheses (H1) with H2 and H3 receiving weak support. For the re-test questionnaire, two of the three hypotheses (H1, H2) were strongly supported with H3 losing the weak support observed in the same-day test. A summary of these results is presented in Table 6.

The loss of weak support for H3 in the re-test warrants further analysis. Based on the interface metaphor and mental model literature used to derive the hypotheses, the primary source for an interaction effect was posited to be WDF users who interacted with a categorical content interface. The data indicate a trend in this direction (see Table 7), but as stated in the results section, the interaction indicated weak significance in the same-day test and proved insignificant in the re-test. Referring to Tables 4 and $5 ,$ one can see that the domain familiarity main effect becomes more pronounced in the re-test (Pvalues of 0.07 and 0.009, respectively), which weakens any support for a possible interaction (see Figure 3). One possible explanation is that the mental model generated by WDF users after viewing the virtual domain interface is less robust than the mental model generated from real experiences (SDF users) and thus weakens over time. When viewing the data from another perspective (see Figure 4), one can see the magnitude of the mode of interface main effect, which does not change between the same day and retest results (P-values of 0.0001 and 0.0001, respectively).

## Contributions

Theoretical and conceptual contributions This study developed design guidelines for interfaces that support experiential tasks based upon mental model theory and metaphor theory and suggests a convergence between the models used in these two areas of research. From a mental model perspective, Norman (1990) stresses the importance of coupling the design and user models when designing a system. For experiential tasks, it seems logical for the designer’s view of the system, the interface, to closely resemble the business domain that the user is trying to understand. From a metaphor theory perspective, this would suggest that the interface metaphor (i.e., source domain) used should be closely linked to the task domain (i.e., business domain). Thus, the coupling of the user’s knowledge of the task domain with the source domain in the interface metaphor is consistent with the coupling of the design and user model in mental model theory. This study provides evidence that for experiential tasks, there is a close parallel between business/source domains and design/user models when facilitating a user’s understanding of the target domain and system image, respectively.

This study also has interesting implications for the HCI and web usability literature. In HCI circles, an infamous example of the ineffective use of interface metaphors was the Southwest Airlines web site (Nielsen, 2000). In the early days of eCommerce, Southwest Airlines created an interface that used an ‘airline ticket counter’ metaphor (see Figure 5a). However, from a web usability perspective, the use of metaphor in this case was criticized because certain interface features did not map well to the metaphor (Nielsen, 2000). An example of this poor mapping is the use of a picture to represent a message from the CEO, which users found to be unintuitive and confusing. A few years later, Southwest Airlines transitioned to an interface that was deemed more intuitive and easier to understand (see Figure 5b).

This research study presents an interesting contrast that explains one of the key reasons why Southwest’s use of the ‘airline ticket counter’ metaphor failed. The current study has shown that a business domain interface metaphor facilitates experiential consumer tasks. However, Southwest Airlines’ consumers did not need to have a ‘virtual’ experience to purchase tickets. Most of the tasks that airline consumers execute in purchasing tickets are more goal-directed, which explains the success of the second Southwest Airline web site. There is, however, one aspect of purchasing tickets that does have strong experiential attributes, seat selection. Thus, many airlines/travel portals are providing a ‘plane layout’ metaphor for seat selection. This result re-affirms one of the basic tenets in the interface metaphor literature – an interface metaphor is most effective when it contains a structure that is visually representative of the task domain (Erickson, 1990).

Practical implications This study suggests several directly actionable approaches for practitioners seeking effective consumer interfaces. The results from this research demonstrate that interfaces can be designed that effectively support experiential tasks. Tasks with experiential, perceptual attributes benefit from an interface design approach that is based on tangible objects from the business domain. Organizations can select interface designs based upon market segmentation analysis of the consumer tasks performed on organizational web sites. If an organization sees a need to support and facilitate experiential tasks via its web site, the experiential design principles presented in this paper can prove useful.

As one can speculate, the cost of creating a virtual domain interface will generally exceed the cost of creating a categorical content interface. Both the nature of an organization’s business domain and the characteristics of the consumer target market will dictate when it is economically feasible to invest in an experiential consumer interface. For example, a vacation resort may find it useful to provide prospective consumers with a virtual tour of the service domain. However, the benefits gleaned from investing in a quality virtual domain interface are often tied to the characteristics of the consumer target market, and the consumers’ domain familiarity is a key characteristic. Suppose a vacation resort’s strategy is one that targets seasoned resort travelers or repeat consumers. Based on this type of strategy, a consumer’s existing familiarity with the business domain weakens the need for a virtual domain interface as most online consumer interaction will favor search attributes such as vacation package prices and availability. However, if presenting experiential attributes via an IT-mediated interface can convey product/service knowledge that results in successful consumer acquisition, then such an investment is worth considering. As web development tools continue to advance, the costs of developing virtual domain interfaces will lower, and organizations can consider the option of developing interfaces that support both types of tasks and thus dynamically tailor the web site based upon the user.

## Limitations

Limitations of this research include the experimental setting, which has both positive and negative implications. On the positive side, a laboratory study allows researchers to control potentially confounding variables. In this study, a pure categorical content and a pure virtual domain interface were designed and tested. However, hybrid interfaces that can effectively support both goaldirected and experiential task types are likely to be more realistic. Also, experiential tasks are not necessarily applicable to all product and service contexts. Lastly, this study investigated the use of virtual domain interfaces with only experiential tasks. The impact of virtual domain interfaces on the performance of goal-directed tasks was not investigated.

## Future research

The results from this study provide a foundation for future research in the area of consumer interface design. The nature of consumer tasks will dictate the type(s) of interface metaphors that are effective for facilitating user interaction. As stated in the limitations section, we polarized the types of interface metaphors in this study and limited our focus to an experiential task. Yet, the practical reality is that organizations need to support a wide variety of consumer tasks, including both goaldirected and experiential.
