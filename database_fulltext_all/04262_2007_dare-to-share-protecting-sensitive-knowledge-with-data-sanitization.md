# Dare to share: Protecting sensitive knowledge with data sanitization

Ali Amiri <sup>⁎</sup>

Department of MSIS, College of Business, Oklahoma State University, Stillwater, OK, 74078, USA

Received 21 August 2005; received in revised form 17 August 2006; accepted 21 August 2006 Available online 2 October 2006

## Abstract

Data sanitization is a process that is used to promote sharing of transactional databases among organizations while alleviating concerns of individual organizations by preserving confidentiality of their sensitive knowledge in the form of sensitive association rules. It hides the frequent itemsets corresponding to the sensitive association rules that contain sensitive knowledge by modifying the sensitive transactions that contain those itemsets. This process is guided by the need to minimize the impact on the data utility of the sanitized database by allowing mining as much as possible of the non-sensitive knowledge in the form non-sensitive association rules from the sanitized database. We propose three heuristic approaches for the sanitization problem. Results from extensive tests conducted on publicly available real datasets indicate that the approaches are effective and outperform a previous approach in terms of data utility at the expense of computational speed. The proposed approaches sanitize also the databases with great data accuracy, thus resulting in little distortion of the released databases. We recommend that the database owner sanitize the database using the third proposed hybrid approach. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Data mining; Sensitive knowledge protection; Data sanitization; Data utility

## 1. Introduction

Tremendous advances in collection and processor technologies have resulted in the development of very large transactional databases. Data mining of these databases has attracted the interests of many researchers and practitioners alike. Proper data mining can provide an edge to an organization over its competitors by for example improving customer service and reducing costs [6,12,16]. In particular the discovery of association rules from the database can provide invaluable information on customer purchasing patterns that can lead to better business decisions. Association rule mining is mostly known in the area of market basket analysis to capture relationships present among items in a transactional database. However, it has also been successfully used in other areas such as fraudulent tax declaration detection [25], path access patterns in the World Wide Web [8], and image recognition [19,20]. Despite its benefit in modern business and organizational environment, association rule mining can also pose a threat to privacy and security in a database sharing environment when precautions are not taken in its implementation. In such an environment, it is imperative to ensure that not only private sensitive data such as identifiers and names are masked but also sensitive knowledge hidden in the database in the form of association rules is not disclosed.

To illustrate the need to safeguard sensitive knowledge in the form of sensitive association rules, Oliveira and Zaiane [17] give the example of a number of companies that decide to share their databases of customers purchasing activities and perform association rules mining on the databases for their mutual benefits. However, some of these companies do not want to reveal strategic patterns in the form of sensitive association rules to the other companies. They want to transform their databases in order to prevent the discovery of such rules. As another illustration, consider the case of some banks that want to share their databases for their mutual benefits of gaining a competitive edge over their competitors. The databases contain data about customers (e.g., gender, education, and marital status), and their accounts (e.g., checking and savings accounts and CDs) and possibly other closely related information such as family data (e.g., children, their ages and educations). However, individual banks want to protect their lucrative customer segments by concealing strategic patterns and trends hidden in the database in the form of sensitive association rules. Each bank wants to transform its database in such a way that these rules are concealed.

The dilemma for the database owners/administrators (such as in the above two illustrations) is clear. If the organizations intend to collaborate by sharing data, it is necessary to allow users to mine non-sensitive association rules in the shared database to the largest extent possible without disclosing sensitive knowledge in the form of sensitive association rules. The challenge in a publicly available database environment is to perform the transformation in such a way that sensitive knowledge is protected while the utility of the released databases is maximized. Data utility is measured by the ability to discover non-sensitive knowledge in the form of non-sensitive association rules available in the original database from the released database. Data sanitization is a process that is recently proposed to promote sharing of data among organizations while alleviating concerns of individual organizations by protecting their sensitive knowledge in the form of sensitive association rules. It is very important to recognize that data sanitization concentrates on protecting one specific type of knowledge, which is knowledge in the form of sensitive associations rules. Other types of knowledge such as classes, segments/clusters, and linear/non-linear relationships are outside the scope of data sanitization, and therefore these types require other protection techniques. Data sanitization, initially introduced in [2], is the process of transforming the original database into the released one. The released database is called the sanitized database. Data sanitization seeks a balance between privacy/sensitive knowledge protection and data utility/non-sensitive knowledge discovery. It hides the restrictive/sensitive association rules that contain sensitive knowledge by deleting some items from the transactions that contain the restrictive rules. This process is guided by the need to preserve the ability to mine/ discover non-restricted association rules from the sanitized database. As explained later in the paper, it is extremely important, however, to note that data sanitization is usually implemented by concealing the sensitive frequent itemsets corresponding to the sensitive association rules in the released database. This is the common approach adopted by the overwhelming majority of researchers [1,2,14,16,17]. It is also important to note that data sanitization, as a term, was also used to refer to “the process of deliberately, permanently, irreversibly removing or destroying the data stored on a memory device” [27]. Obviously, this is not in the scope of the current paper.

A closely related process to data sanitization is known as data perturbation [15,21,22]. Data perturbation seeks to protect individual confidential data values (such as identifiers and income) in the database from disclosure to inhibit individual record identification while allowing users to access accurate aggregate statistics (such as means, correlations, etc.) from the perturbed database. Data perturbation aims to maintain confidentiality of sensitive data values while maximizing the utility of the perturbed database in the form of providing accurate responses to queries involving aggregate and statistical information. The common approach of data perturbation techniques is to add random noise to the confidential data values. Data perturbation differs from data sanitization in several major ways. First, data perturbation seeks to protect individual confidential data values while data sanitization seeks to protect sensitive knowledge hidden in the database in the form of sensitive association rules. Second, data utility of the perturbed database is measured by the ability of users to obtain accurate aggregate statistical information such as means and correlations; whereas data utility of the sanitized database is measured by the ability of users to discover the non-sensitive association rules. Third, data perturbation is ideal for cases in which data is numerical and data sanitization is suitable for databases in which data is item-based or categorical. Forth, most data perturbation techniques make assumptions about the distribution of the database such as multivariate normal distribution [15] whereas data sanitization techniques make no assumption about the distribution of the database.

Atallah et al. [2] considered the problem of data sanitization to limit the disclosure of sensitive rules by decreasing the support of their corresponding frequent itemsets below a certain limit with little side effects on the non-sensitive rules. They showed that the problem is

NP-hard and proposed a heuristic to solve the problem. Verykios et al. [24] suggested hiding sensitive rules by replacing certain actual values in the database with “false” values. This method can however create serious problems and misleading conclusions when the sanitized database is mined. Saying et al. [23] suggested replacing the actual values with “unknown” to overcome these problems. Oliveira and Zaiane [16] suggested efficient algorithms to sanitize the database. The algorithms require two scans of the database regardless of the number of sensitive rules to hide. The first scan is used to build an index file for efficient retrieval of the data and the second is used to sanitize the database. Oliveira and Zaiane [17] proposed a more efficient algorithm, called the Sliding Window Algorithm (SWA), to sanitize the database in one scan. They presented computational tests that showed that SWA outperforms other algorithms found in the literature in terms of concealing all sensitive frequent itemsets corresponding to the sensitive association rules and data utility of the released database. In this paper, we propose three sanitization approaches that outperform SWA in terms of maximizing data utility of the released database, which is the ultimate objective of data sanitization, however at the expense of computational efficiency/speed.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we define the data sanitization problem, along with useful related concepts. We describe our three sanitization approaches in Section 3. Section 4 discusses the data used in conducting computational experiments, and analyzes the results of these experiments and Section 5 concludes.

## 2. Definitions and problem description

The problem of association rule mining was first introduced by Agrawal et al. [1]. The problem can be defined as follows [2]. Let $I { = \{ i _ { I } , i _ { 2 } , i _ { 3 } , . . . , i _ { m } \} }$ be the set of items and $D$ be a database of transactions, where each transaction t is a set of items such that $t \subset I .$ Each transaction has a unique identifier, called TID. An itemset X is a subset of items, i.e., $X \subset I . \mathrm { A }$ transaction t supports an itemset $X { \mathrm { i f } } X \subset t . { \mathrm { ~ A } }$ frequent/large itemset is an itemset that is supported by at least a specified number of transactions. An association rule is an implication of the form $X \Rightarrow Y$ where $X \subset I , Y \subset I ,$ $X \cap Y \neq \phi . { \mathrm { ~ A ~ } }$ rule $X \Rightarrow Y$ holds in the database with confidence $c \ { \mathrm { i f } } \ { \frac { | X \cup Y | } { | X | } } \geq c$ where |Z| is the number of transactions in the database that support (i.e., contain) itemset Z. Similarly, a rule $X \Rightarrow Y$ holds in the database with support s $\operatorname { i f } { \frac { | X \cup Y | } { n } } \geq s$ where n is the number of transactions in the database. In the remainder of the paper, each association rule has a support at least equal to a threshold s set by the database owner.

Association rule mining is usually implemented in two phases: (1) frequent itemsets are identified based on the support s set by the database owner, and (2) association rules with confidence c set also by the user are derived from the frequent itemsets.

We consider two types of association rules: sensitive/ restricted and non-sensitive/non-restricted association rules. Sensitive association rules are rules that contain sensitive knowledge showing strategic patterns and trends. Non-sensitive association rules are rules that contain non-sensitive knowledge showing general, nonstrategic patterns and trends. Data sanitization is the process of transforming the database into a new database that hides or removes sensitive association rules while preserving the discovery of non-sensitive association rules. Data sanitization seeks a balance between privacy/ protection and knowledge discovery/data utility of the database. The main idea behind data sanitization is to decrease the support of the sensitive rules in the sanitized/ released database with the minimum side effects on the support of non-sensitive rules in the sanitized database. It is very important. however to point out that data sanitiza: tion is usually implemented by concealing the sensitive frequent itemsets corresponding to the sensitive association rules. This is the common approach adopted by the overwhelming majority of researchers [1,2,14,16,17]. For example, the frequent itemset corresponding to the association rule $X \Rightarrow Y$ is the itemset $X \cup Y .$ Thus, the specific data sanitization problem that we address in this paper is defined by Atallah et al. [2] as follows. Given the source/original database D over the set of items $I ,$ the set of all frequent itemsets $F ,$ the set S of sensitive itemsets to be hidden, the set N of non-sensitive itemsets, and the minimum support threshold s, the problem is to transform the database D into a database $D ^ { \prime }$ so that all sensitive itemsets become hidden in $D ^ { \prime }$ (i.e., their supports in $D ^ { \prime }$ are below the minimum support threshold s) while the number of non-sensitive itemsets that can be mined/ discovered from $D ^ { \prime }$ (i.e., their supports in $D ^ { \prime }$ are at least equal to the minimum support threshold s) is maximized. As pointed by Menon et al. [14], hiding an itemset is relative to the minimum support level s used by the database owner to mine the itemsets, which is usually private knowledge. That is, hiding an itemset requires dropping its support below this particular threshold value s for the itemsets (this value could be different for each itemset). As a result, anyone mining the database at a minimum support threshold lower than this value could discover a hidden sensitive itemset, along with a large number of other itemsets. As also pointed by Menon et al. [14], the database owners would generally select their threshold values such that they are comfortable with these sensitive itemsets being potentially exposed if the sanitized database is mined at lower values for the minimum support levels.

The database D over I is shown in Table 1 (a). The supports of all possible non-singleton itemsets are shown in Table 1 (b). Given a minimum support threshold s of 2 (50%), the set of frequent itemsets $F { = } \{ \{ \} { \_ { 1 } } { _ { 1 } }$ $\mathrm { i } _ { 2 } \}$ , {i , i }, {i , i }, {i , i }, {i , i }, {i , i , i }, {i , i , $\dot { 1 } _ { 3 } \} \}$ . Now suppose that the owner of the database has decided that the itemset $\{ \mathrm { i } _ { 1 } , \mathrm { i } _ { 4 } \}$ is sensitive and therefore needs to be hidden. There are a variety of ways to conceal this itemset (i.e., its support in the sanitized database $D ^ { \prime }$ is brought below the minimum support threshold s). For instance, transaction 1 could be sanitized to read $\{ \mathrm { i } _ { 2 } , \mathrm { i } _ { 3 } ,$ $\mathrm { i } _ { 4 } \}$ , or transaction 4 could be entirely excluded from the sanitized database.

Ideally, the sensitive itemsets should be hidden with minimal effect on the non-sensitive itemsets that could be mined from the sanitized database. Consequently, the objective of the data sanitization problem is to conceal all sensitive itemsets such that the number of nonsensitive itemsets that could be mined from the sanitized database is maximized.

## 3. Data sanitization approaches

The sanitization problem is known to be very complex, i.e., NP-hard [2]. Hence, we propose three heuristic approaches to solve the problem that outperform the existing SWA in terms of maximizing data utility of the released database. The first one, called Aggregate approach, involves reducing the support of sensitive itemsets below the minimum threshold s by deleting some transactions from the database while the number of non-sensitive itemsets with support smaller than the threshold s in the sanitized database is minimized. The second approach, called Disaggregate approach, involves modifying some transactions by deleting some items from these transactions so that in the sanitized database every sensitive itemset has a support less than threshold s and the number of non-sensitive itemsets that have support less than s is minimized. The third approach, called Hybrid approach, identifies first the transactions to sanitize using the Aggregate approach and then determines the necessary items to remove from those transactions using the Disaggregate approach. Next, we provide the detailed implementation of each approach.

## 3.1. The Aggregate approach

The approach is iteratively applied in a greedy fashion until the support of each sensitive itemset falls below the threshold s while the number of non-sensitive rules with support smaller than the threshold s is minimized. At each iteration of the Aggregate approach, the transaction whose removal from the database causes the decrease of the support of the maximum number of sensitive itemsets and the decrease of the support of the minimum number of non-sensitive itemsets is identified and removed from the database. The approach is made of the following two major steps.

Step 1: Initialization:

• The sanitized database $D ^ { \prime }$ equals the original database D.

• Determine the set $D ^ { C }$ of all sensitive transactions that support the sensitive itemsets.

• Determine the support $s _ { j }$ of every sensitive and nonsensitive itemset $j \in \boldsymbol { F } { \overset { \cdot } { = } } S \cup N$ (Recall that F is the set of all frequent itemsets, S is the set of sensitive itemsets, and N is the set of non-sensitive itemsets, and that initially $s _ { j } \geq s \forall j \in F )$

• The set S<sup>¯</sup> of sensitive itemsets in $D ^ { \prime }$ with support $\geq s$ equals the set of all sensitive itemsets S and the set $\bar { N }$ of non-sensitive itemsets in $D ^ { \prime }$ with support $\geq s$ equals the set of all non-sensitive itemsets $N .$

Step 2: While there is still a sensitive itemset with support $\geq s ( \mathrm { i . e . , } \overline { { S } } \neq \phi )$ do the following:

1. For each transaction k in $D ^ { C }$ do

• Determine the number $( b _ { k } )$ of sensitive itemsets in ${ \overline { { S } } } \ ( { \mathrm { i . e . } }$ , with support $\geq s )$ that are supported by $k$

• Determine the number $( a _ { k } )$ of non-sensitive itemsets in ${ \overline { { N } } } ( { \mathrm { i . e . } }$ , with support $\geq s )$ that are supported by by $k$

• Determine the ratio $f _ { k } \frac { b _ { k } } { a _ { k } } i f \ a _ { k } { > } 0 \ e l s e \ f _ { k } = b _ { k }$

2. Select the transaction $k ^ { * } \in { \cal D } ^ { C }$ such that $f _ { k } { } ^ { * } = { \mathrm { m a x } }$ $\{ f _ { k } : k \in D ^ { C } \}$

3. Update:

• Remove the transaction $k ^ { * }$ from $D ^ { C }$ and from $D ^ { \prime }$

• Reduce the support of every itemset $j { \in } \overline { { S } } \cup \overline { { N } }$ that is supported by $k ^ { * }$ by 1; i.e., $s _ { j } : = s _ { j } - 1$

• Remove every sensitive itemset from $\bar { S }$ if its support $< \mathbf { s }$

• Remove every non-sensitive itemset from $\bar { N }$ if its support $< \mathbf { s }$

The computational complexity of the Aggregate approach is $\bar { O } ( | F | ^ { 2 } | D ^ { C } | )$ ).

## 3.2. The Disaggregate approach

This approach modifies the sensitive transactions in the database individually, instead of deleting them as in the Aggregate approach, by deleting some items from them until the support of every sensitive itemset falls below the threshold s while the number of non-sensitive itemsets whose supports fall below the threshold s is minimized. The Disaggregate approach is similar in structure to the Aggregate approach. At each iteration of the Disaggregate approach, a sensitive transaction and an item in the transaction are identified such that the removal of the item from the transaction causes the support of the maximum number of sensitive itemsets and the support of the minimum number of non-sensitive itemsets to drop. The approach is made of the following two major steps.

## 3.3. The Hybrid approach

This approach is a combination of the previous two approaches. Initially, the Aggregate approach is applied to identify the set of sensitive transactions to remove from the database. But, instead of deleting those transactions from the database, the Disaggregate approach is applied to only those transactions by deleting some items from them until the support of every sensitive itemset falls below the threshold s while the number of non-sensitive itemsets whose supports fall below the threshold s is minimized. The computational complexity of the Hybrid approach is still $\mathrm { O } ( | \bar { F | } ^ { 2 } | I | D ^ { C } | )$ in the worst case.

## 4. Computational experiments and results

The three proposed sanitization approaches and SWA were coded in Borland Delphi and run on an Intel Pentium III with 512 MB of RAM running Windows NT at 1.8 GHz. Extensive computational tests were conducted on real datasets. In this section, we describe these datasets and analyze the results.

## 4.1. Description of the real datasets

One of the outcomes of the first workshop on Frequent Itemset Mining Implementations (FIMI 2003) was the creation of the FIMI repository (http://fimi.cs. helsinki.fi/) that contains real datasets made available by participants. Out of these, we selected a retail market basket dataset from an anonymous Belgian retail store reported in [7], two sets (bms1 and bms2) containing click-stream data from Blue Martini Software Inc., used for the KDD Cup 2000 and described in [26], and two datasets (chess and mushroom) from the Irvine Machine Learning Database Repository [5]. All these datasets are used in [14]. They have varying characteristics in terms of the number of items, number of transactions, and average transaction lengths; this information is shown in the first four columns of Table 3.

Following [14], we used support levels that are roughly around the median of the ranges provided in [10] to generate the frequent itemsets. These values varied from 0.05% to 80% of the number of transactions, with the sparser databases having lower minimum support levels, and the denser ones having higher levels. (The density of a database is measured by the average transaction length divided by the number of items.) The support levels and number of itemsets are shown in the last two columns of Table 3. 150 frequent itemsets were initially selected at random as sensitive for the retail database, and 20 initial frequent itemsets were also selected randomly for the other databases. The final number of itemsets for a database was obtained by ensuring that every superset of a sensitive itemset is also sensitive.

## 4.2. Results and analysis

The results of applying the three proposed sanitization approaches as well as SWA are shown in Table 4. All four approaches conceal all sensitive itemsets in the sanitized database; but their performances in terms of data utility of the sanitized databases they create vary. Recall that data utility is measured by the ability to discover non-sensitive itemsets available in the original database from the sanitized database. In other words, the number of non-sensitive itemsets that are concealed during the sanitization process should be minimized. The column “Data utility” in Table 5 represents the percentage of the non-sensitive itemsets that are not concealed and therefore can be mined from the sanitized database. The higher this value, the better the sanitization approach. The Disaggregate approach is the most effective approach in maximizing data utility of the sanitized database. The Aggregate, Hybrid, and Disaggregate approaches sanitize the databases with an average data utility of 45.82%, 78.44%, and 79.60%, respectively. The outperformance of the Disaggregate approach is expected as it operates at the item level within each transaction in the database. The Hybrid approach performs almost equally well. This indicates that the Aggregate approach picks the “right” transactions to sanitize and the Hybrid approach picks the “right” items to remove from these transactions. The SWA has the lowest performance as it sanitizes the databases with the lowest data utility of 27.73% on average. The poor performance of the SWA compared to our three proposed approaches can be explained mainly by the extreme emphasis of the algorithm on the efficiency (i.e., speed) of concealing the sensitive itemsets with little regard to the number of non-sensitive itemsets that are hidden in the process. Indeed, the “victim” item to be removed from a transaction is selected by SWA solely on the basis of the number of sensitive itemsets that become hidden, i.e., data utility in terms of the number of non-sensitive itemsets that can be mined from the sanitized database is not used as the guiding objective of the sanitization process even though data utility is stated as the maximization objective of SWA and the hiding of all sensitive itemsets is just a constraint in the problem to be enforced by the end of the sanitization process. The outperformance of the three proposed approaches over SWA in terms of maximizing data utility is achieved, unfortunately, at the expense of computational speed. However, the fact that the sanitization problem is not solved repeatedly in real time alleviates the problem of low efficiency of the three approaches. Nevertheless, with the growing sizes of transactional databases, the issue of efficiency should be addressed in future research. The three proposed approaches are still preferred to an exhaustive search for the optimal solution through branch and bound for example. Indeed, results of additional computational experiments (not reported here) clearly show that the state-of-the-art commercial optimization software CPLEX [9], implementing a branch and bound approach, fails to generate even feasible solutions to small instances of the problem (with just a couple of hundreds of transactions) after running for more than 24 hours or 86400 seconds.

A data sanitization approach is primarily evaluated based on its ability to hide sensitive itemsets with minimal effect on data utility in the form of non-sensitive itemset discovery. In this study, we use an additional criterion to evaluate the performance of the data sanitization approaches, namely data accuracy. This criterion can be measured at two levels — transaction and item [16,17]. At the first level, accuracy can be viewed as the percentage of transactions that are accurate in the sanitized database, i.e., left unsanitized. A transaction is accurate in the sanitized database if and only if every attribute value in the transaction is accurate. At the second level, accuracy can be viewed as the percentage of frequencies of the items that are not deleted from the sanitized database, i.e., the ratio. $\frac { \sum _ { i \varepsilon I } ( f _ { D } ( i ) - f _ { D ^ { \prime } } ( i ) ) } { \sum _ { i \varepsilon I } f _ { D } ( i ) }$ , where f (i) represents the frequency of item i in database X. It is worth re-emphasizing here that the primary goal of data sanitization remains to conceal all sensitive itemsets while the number of non-sensitive itemsets that can be mined from the database is maximized and that data accuracy information is provided for illustration purposes.

The performance of the proposed sanitization approaches with respect to data accuracy is also shown in Table 4. The results show that, on average, the Disaggregate and Hybrid approaches generate the most accurate sanitized database at the item level and the Hybrid approach generates the most accurate sanitized database at the transaction level. The Disaggregate approach generates the least accurate database at the transaction level. Indeed, the Aggregate, Hybrid, and Disaggregate approaches sanitize the databases with an average accuracy of 83.54%, 84.38%, and 80.78% at the transaction level and 81.37%, 98.39%, and 98.39% at the item level, respectively. These results are expected as the Disaggregate approach operates at the item level within each transaction in the database. Again, the good performance of the Hybrid approach indicates that the Aggregate approach picks the “right” transactions to sanitize and the Hybrid approach picks the “right” items to remove from these transactions. Unlike its poor performance with respect to data utility, the SWA performs very well with respect to data accuracy of the sanitized database with an average of 89.32% at the transaction level and 98.91% at the item level. This can be explained mainly by the attempt of the algorithm to hide the itemsets by sanitizing as few sensitive transactions as possible.

The density of the database seems not to have a significant impact on data accuracy of the sanitized database especially at the item level. For example, the average data accuracy at the item level for the three sparser databases (bms1, bms2, and retail) is 98.78% using the Disaggregate approach. On the other hand, the same measure for the two denser databases (mushroom and chess) is 97.24% using the same approach.

We conducted an additional computational experiment to explore the effect of the number of sensitive itemsets to conceal on data utility and data accuracy using the same database — retail database. The results are shown in Table 5. Clearly, the Disaggregate approach produces a sanitized database with the highest level of data utility as it hides the least number of nonsensitive itemsets in the sanitized database. The Hybrid approach performs almost equally well. The Aggregate approach reduces the utility of the sanitized database significantly when the number of sensitive itemsets in the database increases. The SWA sanitizes the databases with the lowest level of data utility. In general, as the number of sensitive itemsets to conceal increases, the data utility of the sanitized database decreases; this is to be expected — when a higher number of sensitive itemsets need to be suppressed, a greater number of transactions would need to be sanitized, resulting in concealing a larger number of non-sensitive itemsets. However, using either the Disaggregate or hybrid approach, the data utility of the released database remains very good (i.e., greater than 90%), favoring the fundamental decision of sharing the database. Moreover, data accuracy especially at the item level is also very good (i.e., more than 96% using either the Disaggregate or Hybrid approach), and it is lower at the transaction level (i.e., between 74.73% and 95.59% using the Disaggregate approach).

Based on the performance (in terms of data utility and data accuracy) and the efficiency (in term of CPU time) of the three proposed approaches and the Sliding Window Algorithm (SWA), the Hybrid approach seems to be the best choice to effectively sanitize a database even though it is slower than SWA.

## 5. Conclusion

An increasing number of organizations started to share their transactional databases for their mutual benefits. However, undesired discovery of sensitive knowledge can occur, thus reducing the incentive for these organizations to collaborate by sharing data. Data sanitization has emerged as a process used to promote the sharing of data among organizations while alleviating concerns of individual participants by preserving confidentiality of their sensitive knowledge in the form of sensitive association rules. It hides the sensitive association rules that contain sensitive knowledge by concealing the frequent itemsets corresponding to those rules. This process is guided by the need to minimize the impact on the data utility of the sanitized database by allowing mining nonsensitive knowledge in the form of non-sensitive association rules from the sanitized database. The problem of data sanitization is known to be very complex, NP-hard; hence we developed three heuristic approaches to solve the problem, namely the aggregate, disaggregate, and hybrid approaches. An empirical comparison study using several real datasets has been conducted. Results of the study show that the proposed approaches outperform the existing Sliding Window Algorithm (SWA) in terms of maximizing data utility, which is the optimization objective of data sanitization, at the expense, unfortunately, of computational efficiency or speed. However, the fact that the sanitization problem is not solved repeatedly in real time alleviates the problem of low efficiency of the three approaches. Nevertheless, with the growing sizes of transactional databases, the issue of efficiency should be addressed in future research. The proposed approaches sanitize also the databases with great data accuracy, thus resulting in little distortion of the released databases. Based on the performance (in terms of data utility and data accuracy) of the three proposed approaches and the Sliding Window Algorithm (SWA), we recommend using the Hybrid approach to effectively sanitize a database even though it is slower than SWA.

To consolidate the partnership between organizations for sharing databases, these organizations may need to make tradeoffs between hiding sensitive itemsets and revealing non-sensitive itemsets, where it might be acceptable to divulge some sensitive itemsets if hiding them results in the hiding of a large number of the nonsensitive itemsets [14]. A member of this partnership would likely be interested in a sharing arrangement only when the utility of the shared databases is reasonable and exceeds a minimum level that could be stated in the sharing arrangement [14]; if not, the member may feel that there may not be significant benefits to sharing and may opt to drop out of the partnership. One way to address this issue is to incorporate a pricing scheme in the sharing arrangement, where the price of the database could be based on the utility of the shared data [14]. However, other factors need to be addressed as the utility of the database could be increased artificially by for example deleting all sensitive transactions from the original database [14]. Since data sanitization is used to promote sharing of data among a group of organizations, issues related to the dynamics and performance of groups in a GDSS are relevant in the context of data sanitization. Such issues include cooperation, trust, truthfulness, communication, free riding, etc. [3,4,11,13,18]. Various schemes are suggested to address these issues in groups sharing information to accomplish tasks assigned to them [3,4,11,13,18]. Even though the study of these issues and schemes is beyond the scope of our paper, this topic is very relevant in the context of database sharing and constitutes an important venue for future research.
