# A Delphi study of knowledge management systems: Scope and requirements

Dorit Nevo <sup>a,\*</sup>, Yolande E. Chan

<sup>a</sup> Operations Management and Information Systems, Schulich School of Business, York University, 4700 Keele Street, Toronto, Ont. M3J 1P3, Canada

<sup>b</sup> Queen’s School of Business, Queen’s University, Canada

Received 15 August 2005; received in revised form 25 January 2007; accepted 26 May 2007 Available online 25 July 2007

## Abstract

We empirically explored the roles and scope of knowledge management systems in organizations. Building on a knowledgebased view of the firm, we hypothesized and empirically tested our belief that more integration is needed between technologies intended to support knowledge and those supporting business operations. Findings from a Delphi study and in-depth interviews illustrated this and led us to suggest a revised approach to developing organizational knowledge management systems. <sup>#</sup> 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Knowledge management systems; Knowledge-based view; Delphi methodology; Interviews

The knowledge-based view of the firm [9,31] has changed the value and role of knowledge management (KM) in organizations. This change has the potential to lead to subsequent adjustments in the scope of KM systems (KMS) in organizations. In this paper, we empirically examined – using the Delphi method and semi-structured interviews – how top managers perceived their KMS. Our objective was to provide empirical evidence for emerging views of these systems in the firm.

## 1. Background

Knowledge has long been considered an important organizational resource, and its effective management is therefore crucial to success. Researchers have therefore studied KM in order to determine its contribution in managing and leveraging organizational knowledge.

A review of the KM literature shows the changing view of the focus of KM, resulting in different definitions (see, for example, Table 1) and different levels of technology support. In its early conceptualization stage, KM research focused on empowering the knowledge worker and providing support for organizations starting to adopt its methodology. On the technology side, the predecessors of KMS were executive information systems, decision support systems, and expert systems.

As KM gained popularity, the focus (in the mid to late 1990s) shifted into a practical approach—finding better ways to manage organizational knowledge.

Thus, many published definitions of KM were prescriptive, emphasizing specific activities of KM that could be captured, managed, or facilitated by IT. Consequently, KM definitions during the 1990s included lists of specific manageable activities, such as knowledge creation, identification, codification, sharing, reuse, and application (see [11]).

KMS studies also followed the trend, with systems commonly being defined as technologies that support four KM activities: knowledge creation, codification, transfer, and application [1]. In addition, early KMS studies often focused on codification, i.e., capturing and representing organizational knowledge using IT (e.g. [2,17,37]). Much of the KMS research during this time therefore focused on specific technology solutions, such as discovery, representation, or dissemination [8,27]. Examples of specific tools included intelligent systems, DSS, various types of knowledge repositories and directories, and collaboration tools (see Appendix A and [14] for a review of theory-driven KMS classifications). Market driven classifications presented a similar view, suggesting KMS categories such as knowledge repositories, knowledge discovery and mapping tools, e-learning suites, community builders, meta-search systems, enterprise knowledge portals, push-oriented systems, collaborations tools, and visualization and navigation systems.

Another key addition to KM research and practice during this period was the discussion of tacit and explicit knowledge, suggested by Nonaka [18], building on the work of Polanyi [20]. Explicit knowledge can be expressed using language or other formal representation and communicated easily but tacit knowledge is personal or hidden [39] and hard to formalize. Tacit knowledge can be technical – representing skills and crafts – or cognitive, referring to beliefs, ideas and mental models. The tacit/explicit distinction was perceived as especially relevant in the development

(b)

of KMS, resulting in a basic requirement that a KMS be able to manage, or at least support the management of, both tacit and explicit knowledge [21,32].However, this view also received criticism. Doubters noted that much KM research has taken a narrow view, overlooking important foundations such as law (knowledge privacy and protection), politics (knowledge control and dominance), and marketing (persuasion and knowledge asymmetries) [13]. Moreover, these same critics noted that KM research seldom considered the ‘‘dark side’ and how it could be used to suppress or distort knowledge to serve a specific agenda. Also, critics argued that any knowledge has both a tacit and explicit dimension. To make the explicit knowledge useful requires the tacit dimension, and vice versa [4,6]. Therefore, technology is incapable of capturing tacit knowledge, and without a context cannot fully support KM [15,36]. In fact, it is argued that attempting to capture knowledge and explicitly representing it in the KMS might lead to bounded communications, interpretation, and understanding [3].

Related to this, in recent years there has been a shift toward the knowledge-based view of the firm [30]. This view put knowledge at the center of the firm and argued that intellectual capital is the firm’s key asset. Acquiring, integrating, and leveraging individual firm members’ knowledge became the important KM activity. Thus, the current focus of KM research is on enabling competitive advantage and managing knowledge as an organizational resource, strongly integrated with the other resources [34,35] (also see Table 1). In this light, IT plays a revised role in supporting organizational KM [24]:

‘‘there is a growing realization that technical and social processes interact in complementarities to shape knowledge management efforts’’.

KMS therefore cannot be seen as a stand-alone application but should be integrated with all aspects of the business [10,28].

The authors therefore set out to provide empirical support for an integrated view of KMS and while this view was intuitive, it had previously been examined conceptually. Further, we built on the work of Ein-Dor and Segev who proposed an evolutionary approach to IS classification, based on the changes in attributes and functions designed into various types of systems. They said:

‘‘[i]nformation system types evolve by accretion of technologies. Thus, information systems of various types are essentially on a continuum of technology accretion. The point at which a set of technologies is considered distinct and requires a new name is somewhat arbitrary, and largely a matter of convenience. Thus, there are really two types of evolution involved in information systems—one, the evolution of technology and its use and the second, the evolution of our naming or labeling of particular combinations of technology. Perhaps the most important conclusion is that, too frequently, the labeling drives our perception of the field and its constituents rather than the content’’ [7].

Following this notion of evolution, our goal was to explore whether KMS was a need driven by use or whether it was merely a labeling artifact. If we could support the former, then we supported the existence of a KMS as a novel, stand-alone application; if the latter, then we provided support for the knowledge-based view of the firm and the argument that because of the supporting role of IS, the systems were de facto ‘‘KMS.’’

## 2. The Delphi study

The Delphi method attempts to obtain consensus from a group of experts using repeated responses of questionnaires and controlled feedback. The process used in our study involved a panel of top managers from 21 organizations. Participants were carefully selected from organizations in a large North American city. In selecting them, our criterion was to find managers involved with the KM initiative, and having authority over its KMS purchase; Fig. 1a shows their demographics by position in the organization. Delphi panel members were from organizations varying in size from 200 to 45,000 employees worldwide, and from several industries. Fig. 1b gives a breakdown of participating organizations by industry.

The majority of the organizations already had technology support for KM in place, either developed in-house or purchased from a vendor. These systems included various knowledge repositories, professional support systems (with embedded business logic and rules), computer-aided design tools, customer management systems, and communications tools.

Following the normal Delphi procedures, the Delphi study was carried out in two parts—a brainstorming phase to generate a list of KMS capabilities and a ranking phase to identify the importance of each. To supplement the process, upon completion of the study, in-depth interviews were conducted with panel members to gain an understanding of the issues pertaining to technology support.

During the initial round, panel members were emailed the question:

‘‘What are the most important functions and capabilities that should be embedded in the design of knowledge management systems?’’

After all responses had been received, two judges sorted the answers, aggregating related items into basic capabilities and creating a consolidated list of most desired capabilities. For example, the items ‘‘[the KMS] enables control over access rights,’’ ‘‘authenticates users,’’ and ‘‘creates back-up for stored knowledge’’ were aggregated under a more general ‘‘security’ capability. Agreement between judges was statistically measured using Cohen’s k and was good (k = 0.71). Once finalized, the list was sent back to panel members for review and validation. Based on the responses received, two items were dropped: ‘‘incorporates voice recognition’’ and ‘‘includes project management capabilities.’’ In addition, a small number of items were further aggregated. Following the creation of the list of capabilities, panel members were asked to rate the desirability of each item based on its importance. The members were told to divide 170 points among the 17 capabilities, allocating more points to capabilities that they felt were more important.

As the goal of a Delphi study is to attain consensus among its panel members, Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) was used to measure agreement in the rating of capabilities. Similar to other measures such as Spearman’s or Kendall’s rank-order correlation coefficient, W measures the strength of association among the ranking of items. The coefficient of concordance allows for multiple judges (rather than just two), a quality that makes it most suitable to test inter-judge reliability [29]. As a general guideline, scores closer to 1 represent a stronger consensus. Specifically, Schmidt et al. [26] proposed that strong consensus exists for $W \geq 0 . 7 ;$ moderate consensus for $W = 0 . 5 ;$ and weak consensus for $W < 0 . 3$

To compute the Kendall’s W measure, we first converted all ratings into rankings, allowing for ties. After the first round, the W indicated weak consensus. Panel members were therefore asked to revise their rating carefully and to provide an explanation for cases in which their rating differed substantially from the group’s average. After the second round, consensus had improved $( W = 0 . 5 1 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and detailed explanations were provided when individual ratings differed substantially from the group’s. It is important to note that, by removing the five panel members with the greatest deviation from the group’s average, we were able to obtain a strong consensus $( W = 0 . 6 7 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ among 80% of the panel members.

At this stage we ended the Delphi study and started follow-up telephone interviews with each panel member to obtain additional insights. We first discuss in more depth the findings of the Delphi study and then describe the interview process and findings.

## 2.2. Discussion

The Delphi study focused on identifying what panel members perceived to be the most important KMS capabilities. To understand these capabilities in the context of KMS, we classified them (see Table 3) according to the four KM activities that KMS generally supports (creation, codification, transfer, and application).

We first note that the Delphi study findings are close to these four activities. Nevertheless, the Delphi study diverged from this definition in three important ways:

\- Four of the 17 capabilities (adaptability, security, ease of use, and cost efficiency) were generally considered to be IS capabilities. Moreover, they were ranked highly, indicating that panel members viewed a KMS as an organizational IS.

## 3.1. Variety of IT used

At the start of each interview, respondents were asked to describe knowledge intensive activities in their organizations, and how these activities used technology. Knowledge intensive activities included: R&D, production, customer service, forecasting, business performance evaluation, employee management, and the documentation and improvement of business processes. Most organizations employed a variety of IS to support these activities, including advanced database applications, CRM systems, ERP systems, CAD tools, etc. Organizations also used tools more closely identified with KM such as intranets and business portals. Proprietary business software was also widely used (e.g. forecasting systems, dispatch systems, etc.).

## 3.2. KMS-related practices and issues

Overall our interviews indicated the use of a wide variety of practices and criteria to evaluate the success or failure of KMS. Table 4 shows the success factors with quotes from panel members. It is clear that many success factors were generic to IS and few targeted special characteristics of KMS.

Similarly, when asked the opposite question (what would be considered a failure of the KMS), respondents said that lack of system use was important:

‘‘If you set up some kind of knowledge database and three months later or six months later . . . you realize people haven’t used it, it’s a failure. It comes back to the whole purpose behind it is for people to use it to make their lives or their work easier.’’ [Vice President of Finances & Administration, Manufacturing Distribution, 45,000 employees]

Ineffectiveness was another important failure factor:

‘‘To the extent that we bring in a system and we spend a lot of money on it and people use it but we don’t get a sense that the business itself has been impacted . . . then that would be a failure.’’ [Vice President Human Resources, Manufacturing, 2000 employees]

‘‘If I looked at some of the communities that are posted—there isn’t a lot of information in, and I get the sense that some people have a community just because it’s the thing to do, as opposed to truly providing value.’’ [Director of Operations, Manufacturing, 18,000 employees]

Finally, technical features such as being slow system or difficult to implement were mentioned by some respondents.One aspect which we felt was uniquely related to KM was the measurement of KMS performance, which presented a challenge:

‘‘That is one of the big problems with knowledge management systems in general is that faced with the big cost, trying to come up with expected benefits and then actually measuring the benefits that you get out of the expenditure that you’ve made.’’ [Senior Vice President Finance, Distribution, 4600 employees]

Organizations used a variety of ways to measure KMS success, mostly through qualitative user feedback, system usage, and return on investment. Other measures included measuring the quality of the output of the system, measuring gains in market share, and qualitative customer feedback.

Finally, panel members were asked what benefits they expected to derive from their KMS. Table 5 gives a summary of representative responses to this. As with the success and failure factors, it presents a combination of generic IS expectations and some specific KM-related benefits.

In sum, data from the interviews presented a similar trend to that found in the Delphi study – a mix of IS and KM capabilities and practices melded together into companies’ desires from – and experiences with – KMsupporting technology.

## 4. Discussion

Our goal was to identify whether empirical support existed for the emerging knowledge-based view of the firm and the evolution of KMS. Using the qualitative Delphi method and follow-up interviews, we leveraged the knowledge and experience of 21 panel members who were actively involved in their organization’s KM initiatives. Two aspects of technology support for organizational KM were studied. First, what capabilities of a system were important to support knowledge intensive activities, and second, how organizations currently used technology to support these activities. Two types of evolutionary paths were examined—one driven by use (or need) and the other an evolution of labeling (i.e., naming a group of technologies). Building on our empirical findings we suggested that the latter is the case for KMS.

Pulling together the insights obtained in the study and interviews, we found support for the view for KMS as knowledge support systems, consisting of any organizational IS enhanced with KM capabilities. One question is whether this is a continuum from a traditional IS to a full KM system. Indeed, our results showed is that it was not at obvious that there was a single line and where it crosses from being an IS to a KMS. As noted in one interview:

‘‘I’m not quite sure . . . where a knowledge management system ends and a business system starts.’’ [Vice President of Operations, Manufacturing, 200 employees]

Moreover, focusing on the needs of organizations and how systems are used to support organizational KM, our results imply that organizational needs call for the integration of KM capabilities within every class of technology in the organization. Thus, KMS should not be created as stand-alone applications. This conclusion strongly supports the knowledge-based view of the firm and the integration of knowledge and business.

## 4.1. Limitations

Our study was not without limitations. Specifically it had a positivist orientation, and critical questions regarding negative aspects of KMS in organizations were not addressed. In addition, the size of the Delphi panel was small, although it was methodologically sufficient. The heterogeneity of panel members should also have been considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, only a moderate consensus was obtained in the Delphi study (although this might be attributed to the use of ratings rather than rankings).

Although we suggested that KMS represent an evolution of labeling rather than of use, there is clearly some benefit in continuing to study these systems as a separate class of technologies. Doing so promotes ongoing focused attention on developing technologies to support KM. However, our study suggests that this is no longer sufficient.

One important contribution of our study was in identifying and exploring KM capabilities that should be integrated with other business technologies.

Another contribution offered by our Delphi study was in identifying important KMS capabilities and matching them to contemporary definitions of KMS. By studying KMS as a technology class, as well as an integrated platform, new responses can be developed to KM-related challenges such as supporting tacit knowledge or ensuring the value and quality of knowledge.

## 5. Conclusion

This study focused on the need to think differently about KMS, in light of recent changes in the way that organizations view KM. The results of our qualitative analysis confirmed the appropriateness of the scope of current KMS support, but suggested that this should be more strongly integrated with the overall technology in the firm. Thus, rather than studying KMS as a separate entity, more focus should be given to placing KM support in context and integrating KMS with other technologies, creating integrated knowledge support systems—business technologies enhanced with KM capabilities. The contribution of our work is in identifying the problems and needs of organizations when implementing KM, particularly in identifying new issues of integration.
