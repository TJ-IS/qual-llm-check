## a b s t r a c t

Past studies show that only a small percent of organizations implement and enforce formal rules or informal guidelines for the planning, design and development, usage, modi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and disposition stages of spreadsheet models. Due to the lack of such policies, there has been little research on how companies can effectively govern spreadsheets throughout their life cycle. This paper describes a survey involving 43 participants from the United States, representing companies that were working on compliance with the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 (SOX) as it relates to spreadsheets for <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this survey describe speci<sup>fi</sup>c controls that organizations have implemented to manage spreadsheets for <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting throughout a spreadsheet's life cycle. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that there are problems in all stages of a spreadsheet's life cycle and we suggest several important areas for practice and future research.

## 1. Introduction

It is broadly accepted that errors are prevalent in spreadsheets [13,17]. Spreadsheet risk can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the likelihood of adverse operational or <sup>fi</sup>nancial consequences resulting from the use of a spreadsheet. To date, most spreadsheet research has focused primarily on understanding and mitigating spreadsheet risks associated with quantitative errors that occur during system development, an early stage of the system's life cycle. This line of research investigates and often suggests implementation of more formal software engineering techniques during the creation of a spreadsheet [4,9,13]. While there are de<sup>fi</sup>nite risks of developing an erroneous spreadsheet, there are additional and equally as serious risks associated with the maintenance and operational use of the spreadsheet throughout the later parts of its life cycle. We use a broad life cycle framework for our study, which includes planning, design and development, usage, modi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and disposition of a spreadsheet, in order to provide a lens for developing our survey and evaluating the results.

We surveyed 43 companies working on compliance with the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 (SOX) as it relates to spreadsheets associated with <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting. We chose to sample such companies because of the regulatory, <sup>fi</sup>nancial, and reputational importance of spreadsheets used for <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting under SOX. That is, for these spreadsheets that are associated with government regulation and are monitored by external auditors, we expect best organizational practices. We collected information about the controls and processes they have implemented in their organizations as well as the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties and challenges they have encountered. This paper presents the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this survey. While these <sup>fi</sup>ndings describe spreadsheets associated with <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting, the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of effective controls and processes are applicable to other key spreadsheets in an organization and therefore should be considered in developing best practices for IT governance. Similarly, there are areas where organizations fail to reasonably control the spreadsheets used in <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting, despite their efforts. We expect that all organizations will encounter the same challenges and dif<sup>fi</sup>culties that these organizations are facing, but many non-regulated companies will avoid addressing risk until there is an easy and effective way of dealing with the problem

The remainder of the paper is divided into <sup>fi</sup>ve parts. The next section provides an overview of the spreadsheet life cycle, controls, and the accountability SOX introduces that motivates organizations to implement controls and processes for spreadsheet development and use. A life cycle framework is proposed from which controls can be considered. The survey methodology is then discussed and the results of the survey are described and analyzed. Finally, conclusions and suggestions for practice and future research are made as we identify the areas where companies are struggling to effectively control spreadsheets.

## 2. Overview of the spreadsheet life cycle, controls, and SOX accountability

## 2.1. General background

Panko and Halverson [15] outlined a taxonomy of spreadsheet research issues as a three dimensional cube, in which one important dimension was the system life cycle stage. They de<sup>fi</sup>ned this life cycle as consisting of four stages: cell entry, draft of model, debugging, and operational. The <sup>fi</sup>rst three stages detailed their interpretation of the system development life cycle. Their review of previous research studies documented observations associated with auditing/monitoring, user controls, modi<sup>fi</sup>cations, and different party responsibilities in the operational stage. As a <sup>fi</sup>rst step in the outlined research plan presented in their paper, they created a separate taxonomy of development and testing error types for the system development life cycle stages [16]. Panko and Aurigemma [14] later revised this taxonomy, but noted the shortcoming that the system life cycle dimension was not addressed in either version; their studies did not examine a spreadsheet's ongoing use after development. In the taxonomy proposed by Rajalingham, Chadwick & Knight [20], errors that end-users can make, such as data entry errors or interpretation errors, as well as the user's intention to create fraud, were considered. This taxonomy represented a <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to de<sup>fi</sup>ne and classify spreadsheet risks during operational use. Since then, the increased emphasis on IT governance has led to the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of issues that contribute to the risk of a spreadsheet after its development, such as maintenance, documentation, version control, privacy issues, and reasonable separation of duties.

Basic spreadsheet programs lack the embedded logic and data controls necessary to prevent errors and misuse during operational use. Therefore, organizations need to apply manual or automated control processes to help mitigate spreadsheet risks by ensuring that appropriate tools are used to minimize, detect, and resolve errors throughout the entire life cycle. In general, end-users are resistive to attempts to control and restrict the development, sharing, and use of selfgenerated models. The challenge is to identify effective controls that can help an organization improve the integrity of its spreadsheets without the controls being prohibitively time-consuming or expensive to implement and without interfering with the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the spreadsheet medium.

Surveys show that most organizations have no formal policies to ensure the integrity of their operational spreadsheets [2,7,12,17]. Companies reported that while informal guidelines were common, formal guidelines existed in only about half of the organizations. Neither the formal rules nor the informal guidelines were usually implemented and enforced throughout the planning, development, usage, modi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and disposition stages of the spreadsheet life cycle, despite all of the literature on the prevalence of spreadsheet errors in organizations. One area where the corporate culture has changed is in <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting [22]. Sarbanes–Oxley regulations hold publicly traded companies in the U.S. accountable for implementing and evaluating their spreadsheet controls for <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting. The Public Company Accounting Oversight Board's (PCAOB) Auditing Standard 5 identi<sup>fi</sup>es the need for a combination of preventive and detective controls to prevent and detect errors or fraud in <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting [19]. In 2004, several surveys reported that 80–95% of U.S. <sup>fi</sup>rms use spreadsheets for <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting [13]. Thus, Sarbanes–Oxley regulations force many publicly traded companies to view end-user developed spreadsheet models that impact <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting similar to formal information systems used for <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting.

## 2.2. Background of SOX and controls

In the U.S., as a result of various <sup>fi</sup>nancial frauds and scandals over the past two decades, the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 (SOX) [23] initiated new policies, procedures, and disclosures in <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting for publicly held companies. For years ending on or after November 15, 2004, when external audit <sup>fi</sup>rms identify material weaknesses in a company's <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting process, a description of the weakness or de-<sup>fi</sup>ciency is documented in the company's annual 10-K report. Audit Analytics is a public company intelligence service that provides detailed research on over 20,000 public companies. Based on the companies included in their database, there were 113 10-Ks that recorded material weaknesses as the result of inadequate spreadsheet controls for 77 different companies between 2004 and the <sup>fi</sup>rst half of 2008 [9]. For example, in 2006, Design Within Reach Inc. was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as having the following material weakness: “Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, controls were not designed and in place throughout the year to ensure that access was restricted to appropriate personnel and that unauthorized modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the data or formulas within spreadsheets was prevented” [3].

With SOX, organizations are being held accountable for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time to provide documentation about the effectiveness of their spreadsheet risk management practices for the entire spreadsheet life cycle. In particular, the external audit <sup>fi</sup>rms focus on existing spreadsheets and identify control de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies primarily for the stages after the spreadsheet is developed. Senior executives should communicate an end-user computing policy to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the spreadsheet risk management requirements expected from the organization [18]. This policy must de<sup>fi</sup>ne effective processes and enact appropriate monitoring to ensure compliance with these processes. Most importantly, these processes need to encompass all stages of the system life cycle. From this policy, an operating model de<sup>fi</sup>ning accountability, roles and responsibilities, processes, controls, and control standards can be created [11]. Finally, the company should document implementation of the controls and processes outlined in the operating model. In order to do this, it is necessary to elaborate on what Panko and other spreadsheet researchers previously de<sup>fi</sup>ned simply as the “operational” stage.

## 3. Developing the spreadsheet life cycle framework

It is advisable for companies to adopt a framework as a foundation for developing policies and procedures for spreadsheet controls. Many companies and auditors have adopted Control Objectives for Information and Related Technology (CobiT) [6] to address IT compliance for SOX [1]. Other useful guidance for the development and assessment of spreadsheets also exists. The Institute of Internal Auditors recently issued a practice guide for user-developed applications (UDAs), which includes guidelines for controlling and auditing UDAs using a risk-based assessment of <sup>fi</sup>nancial, operational, and compliance materiality [5]. To manage spreadsheet risk, PricewaterhouseCoopers [18] proposes that organizations create an inventory of spreadsheets that are in the scope of SOX regulations, evaluate the use and complexity of each spreadsheet to assess the risk of <sup>fi</sup>nancial misstatement, determine the necessary level of controls for “key” spreadsheets, evaluate existing controls for these spreadsheets, and develop action plans to remediate control deficiencies.

General types of controls that can be considered for implementation include change controls, version controls, access controls, input controls, security and integrity of data, documentation, development life cycle, back-ups, archiving, logic inspection, segregation of duties, and overall analytics [18]. These control activities can be embedded within and across different life cycle stages of the spreadsheets. Panko [13] suggests a framework where control activities are classi-<sup>fi</sup>ed into three broad categories: preventive, detective, and corrective controls. Applying this framework assumes that the individuals associated with a spreadsheet throughout its life cycle can identify appropriate control activities from these three sets, and properly segregate responsibilities, to mitigate the risk of an error at each stage.

The accountability that SOX imposes makes it critical for companies to consider how these different types of controls should be implemented in their operations, and includes de<sup>fi</sup>ning who should be responsible for their implementation and for monitoring their effectiveness. An organization should clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ne the roles and responsibilities of different organizational stakeholders. This includes developers, business users, business owners (who are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the people responsible for having the spreadsheet developed), IT and IS security of<sup>fi</sup>cers, independent review groups, the accounting department, and internal auditors. It is often the case, however, that one person performs several roles, such as the business owner, developer, user and reviewer. In this context, Panko's [13] control category framework is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to apply.

Spreadsheets require different levels of control depending on their complexity and intended usage. Even as the levels of control vary, organizations must still consider all stages of the life cycle as they identify the appropriate controls given the risks associated with a particular spreadsheet. For purposes of this study, we chose to use a life cycle framework as the lens for developing our survey and evaluating the results. Prior spreadsheet research is focused primarily on the design and development of spreadsheets, stopping at the “use,” or “operation,” stage. Lawson et al. [7] are the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to outline a model for the later stages of the spreadsheet's life cycle, but they do not fully describe and develop the model. Therefore, in Table 1 we propose a <sup>fi</sup>ve-stage life cycle framework for this analysis. Given that a de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the operational portion of the spreadsheet life cycle has never been formally developed, we categorized it as involving three stages: usage, modi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and disposition. This more detailed categorization is still broad enough to allow for incorporation of other models that have been proposed in prior research and practice on control frameworks. For example, the following models can all be mapped into the <sup>fi</sup>ve stages outlined in Table 1: Lawson et al.'s [7] seven-stage model (designing, testing, documenting, using, modifying, sharing, and archiving); CobiT's [6] general objectives of planning and organization, acquisition and implementation, delivery and support, and monitoring and evaluating; risk assessment and monitoring guidance [5,18]; and Read and Batson's [21] six stage spreadsheet life cycle model. Similar to other models, Read and Batson's [21] last stage is “use,” and is focused on how the user can best take advantage of the spreadsheet, not on controls in this stage.

Table 1 summarizes the description of the activities performed in the life cycle framework for our study. We include the functions of monitoring and auditing spreadsheets as part of the usage stage. An interaction between usage and modi<sup>fi</sup>cation is necessary, as monitoring, auditing, and, generally, evaluation of spreadsheets may lead to modi<sup>fi</sup>cations, which will affect usage. Modi<sup>fi</sup>cation may also have a relationship with the design and development stage. For example, the CobiT model includes “managing change” as part of acquisition and implementation. We view archiving as relevant to the usage, modi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and disposition of spreadsheets. This also relates to record retention policies and laws [8].

## 4. Survey method and sample selection

We conducted an online survey of U.S. publicly traded companies to study how organizations de<sup>fi</sup>ne the roles and responsibilities of different stakeholders associated with controlling a critical spreadsheet. In particular, we investigated what these companies were doing to comply with SOX, what roles various stakeholders played, which stakeholders were responsible for ensuring processes were implemented and which processes organizations found most challenging to control. The survey questions were designed to elicit responses about material and/or critical spreadsheet applications used in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting process, where “material” and/or “critical” spreadsheet applications were de<sup>fi</sup>ned as being signi<sup>fi</sup>cant to the <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements and/or footnotes, and probably identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as “in scope” for purposes of SOX compliance. The survey included items related to the spreadsheet life cycle framework, with emphasis on the usage and modi<sup>fi</sup>cation stages (see Table 1). A long and a short version of the survey were developed. This was done to increase participation. The short survey provided an option to continue and complete the long survey.

We had several objectives in selecting our sample from the population of U.S. public companies. First, we wanted a sample of public companies that varied in size and industry. This was intended to provide a better opportunity to generalize to all public companies. Second, we desired from each participating company a person who had the necessary knowledge of SOX compliance and spreadsheet controls in their organization to complete the survey. This was intended to increase the reliability of the responses.

Several approaches were taken to elicit responses to meet our objectives. First, participants were sought by posting a link to the electronic survey on local LinkedIn groups associated with the Information Systems Audit and Control Association and the Institute of Internal Auditors. Second, a list of the 200 largest public companies in Southern California and a random sample of 130 companies listed in the S&P 1500 were called in an attempt to identify the contact name of a quali<sup>fi</sup>ed individual in the organization. After obtaining a speci<sup>fi</sup>c name and email address, an email was sent to the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 50 contacts who had expressed a willingness to participate. Finally, some personal contacts of the authors were emailed. The emails opened with the sentence, “You have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the person who is most knowledgeable about how spreadsheets associated with <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting for SOX compliance are being managed in your company” to emphasize the need for an appropriate person to complete the survey. The email also brie<sup>fl</sup>y described the study and provided a link to the online surveys. All participants were given a choice between the short and long versions of the anonymous survey. The sample obtained is described in the next section.

## 5.1. Description of final sample

There were a total of 43 respondents to our online surveys. The total sample population cannot be estimated due to the various approaches taken; therefore, no response rate can be calculated. Table 2 presents the 15 different industries represented in the sample. Manufacturing and entertainment had the largest number of respondents, representing 25.6% and 14.0% of the sample, respectively. The size of the companies responding, measured in assets, ranged from less than \$24 million to over \$100 billion, with about 74% of companies with assets greater than \$1 billion (see Table 3). Of the 28 respondents that provided their job title, the majority (18) were associated with internal audit, followed by SOX or General Compliance positions (6). In addition, 15 respondents were also the person responsible for SOX Compliance and/or spreadsheet controls in the organization. These results indicate that respondents were at an appropriate level of the organization and knowledgeable about controls over spreadsheets for purposes of this study.

There were 29 respondents who answered the long survey version, and 14 who answered the short version. Therefore, there was a maximum of 29 or 43 responses for items from the long survey and the total responses, respectively. In order to increase participation, participants were allowed to skip questions that they did not feel comfortable answering. Thus, the responses for some items are lower than the overall number of participants for the survey. Results are presented in percentages and/or numbers. Care is taken to show the total responses in each case.

## 5.2. Impact and outcomes of SOX

SOX emphasizes internal controls and the documentation of those internal controls. 25 out of 27 survey respondents (93%) report that spreadsheets used in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting process are of the same level or higher level of importance in the post SOX era. Due to the heightened risk and visibility for SOX spreadsheets, we expected that organizations would have reduced the number of spreadsheets used in <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting after SOX was effective. However, the number of spreadsheets that respondents reported using has not decreased, as 20 out of 26 respondents (77%) reported using the same number or more spreadsheets in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting process since the implementation of SOX. Similarly, we expected that after SOX, companies would increase the controls over spreadsheets used in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting process. Nevertheless, 23 out of 27 respondents (85%) indicated they did not have a computing policy speci<sup>fi</sup>c to spreadsheets, demonstrating lack of a comprehensive plan to mitigate spreadsheet risk. Our survey results further indicate that the operating controls over spreadsheets used in <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting are also still lacking in many areas including change management, version management, access control, and the development process.

The emphasis on internal controls for the purposes of SOX is to increase the probability of <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements that are materially correct. Critical weaknesses in controls over spreadsheets used for the purpose of preparing <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports have the potential to lead to public disclosure of weaknesses in controls by management and external auditors. Perhaps even worse, errors in spreadsheets used in <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting may cause material errors in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements. We asked two questions related to these possibilities. First, 48% (13/27 respondents) indicated that “internal reviews uncovered lapses or non-compliance with established protocols for spreadsheet controls.” Second, 26% (7/27 respondents) answered yes to the question, “Have internal reviews documented <sup>fi</sup>nancial statement errors related to spreadsheet errors?” It is possible that compensating controls or <sup>fi</sup>nal reviews by quali<sup>fi</sup>ed individuals may reduce the ultimate risk of material errors in <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements. However, the responses to these two items support the other <sup>fi</sup>ndings that stronger internal controls are needed in many corporations.

## 5.3. Self-identified control processes difficult to implement

We asked the 43 respondents to identify the top three processes in which implementing appropriate controls for critical spreadsheets used in <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting are most dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. As shown in Fig. 1, respondents were most concerned with change management, input controls, access control, and version management. They were most con<sup>fi</sup>dent with the backup process. Respondents proceeded to answer survey questions that supported their initial levels of concern or con-<sup>fi</sup>dence about these processes as detailed in the following paragraphs. The detailed items about each process provided more insight into the perceived strengths and weaknesses of the controls. For some processes, detailed responses were strongly correlated with the process respondents identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a top concern. For other processes, however, later survey responses suggested weaknesses in processes that were not identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by many respondents as a process in which implementing appropriate controls is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult.

Both large and small companies found that change management was one of the most dif<sup>fi</sup>cult processes to effectively control, collectively ranking as one of the top three choices for both large and small companies. When we ranked the top choice only, change management was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the most dif<sup>fi</sup>cult process for large companies to control and it tied with the development process as the second most dif<sup>fi</sup>cult process for small companies. Version management was also considered to be one of the more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult processes to control for both large and small companies. Neither large nor small companies identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the backup process as one of the more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult processes. There were some noticeable differences between the small and large companies in our survey sample, however. When asked to identify the most dif<sup>fi</sup>cult process to control, the smaller companies ranked the testing process <sup>fi</sup>rst, followed by a tie between change management and development processes. The larger companies did not consider the testing process a concern as zero respondents selected this as a top choice, and only 2/32 respondents (6%) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the development process as their top process that is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to control. Instead, large companies found that input controls and access control were the other two most dif<sup>fi</sup>cult processes to control, in addition to change management.

These observed differences may be due to how spreadsheets are developed, used, and shared throughout different size companies. In small companies, the business user may create and use a spreadsheet without formal testing or development procedures, but not share the spreadsheet with as many users. If this is the case, testing and development processes would be top concerns, whereas access controls and version management would be lower level concerns. However, larger companies with more business users and business owners who share spreadsheets may be more concerned with controls associated with change management, access control, and version management. It is dif-<sup>fi</sup>cult to infer reasons for these differences in the top self-identi<sup>fi</sup>ed control processes without having speci<sup>fi</sup>c questions in the survey asking how a particular spreadsheet is used in large versus small companies. The survey did not ask for information about speci<sup>fi</sup>c spreadsheets, such as the number of users or the complexity involved. Therefore, throughout the remainder of the paper, large and small companies are analyzed together. Given that large companies represented 74% (32/43 respondents) of our sample, the concerns of the large companies tend to dominate those of the smaller companies.

5.4. Planning, design, and development stages of the spreadsheet life cycle

The planning stage is the time when organizations should identify the needs, risks, and appropriate policies and procedures that should be applied to new spreadsheets. For example, organizational policies may allow very limited controls over simple spreadsheets that are developed by a user and have few risks associated with them. Alternatively, complex models that may have many users, or models that will be used for a series of time-critical important decisions, will require appropriate controls throughout the entire life cycle. Prior work summarizes model types as Complex, Simple, Time-critical, and Ill-defined in order to apply appropriate controls for the planning and design and development stages of the life cycle [21]. It is important to identify the model type, or at least characteristics of the proposed model, during the planning stage so that the resulting computing policy considers appropriate controls for the later stages of the life cycle as well as the design and development of the model. The design and development stage of the life cycle is the stage for which most prior research has been conducted [4,9,17,21]. We were interested in learning about the actual implementation of controls in this stage and the particular strengths and weaknesses identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by respondents. As previously discussed, the 11 small companies in our sample considered development and testing as signi<sup>fi</sup>cant processes of concern even though large companies did not. Based on responses to later survey questions, it appears that development and testing processes are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lacking in controls. Table 5 shows that an overwhelming number of respondents do not have formal development procedures for creating spreadsheets and they lack policies regarding design and documentation standards. Most respondents reported no policy to describe the skill requirements for the person responsible for developing the spreadsheet. But for those who do have such a policy in place, the accounting department was reported as the department responsible for ensuring that the policy is followed. Given that the spreadsheets in this survey relate to <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting, it is logical that the accounting department would ensure the policy dictating the skill requirements necessary for the spreadsheet developer is followed. Table 4 shows that 40% (10/25) of the respondents did collect information about the developer of the spreadsheet and 32% (8/25) of the respondents collect documentation about the type of testing done during the development process. In addition, survey results in Table 6 indicate that the required domain experience for spreadsheet developers is low, with slightly higher requirements for the developer's spreadsheet experience. McGill and Klobas [10] found that end-user developers with greater levels of spreadsheet knowledge develop spreadsheets of higher system quality. Thus, the distribution shown in Table 6 for the developer's required spreadsheet experience <sup>fl</sup>ags some development concerns for 12%–54% of the companies in our survey. McGill and Klobas's research does not consider the domain experience of the developer.

We also provided survey participants with a list of 15 controls or tools and asked them to identify those that they currently have effectively implemented in their organization, and whether they plan to implement controls not currently used in the future. Table 7 presents the results, which includes the fact that only 10.3% of the respondents mandated training for developers. The absence of formal development and training procedures is an issue, and this, coupled with the developers lack of accounting knowledge and spreadsheet experience when creating spreadsheets critical for the <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting process, has the potential to lead to serious errors in output.

The lack of a policy to describe the job title or skill requirements of the person responsible for reviewing the spreadsheet after new spreadsheets are developed continued to be an issue. However, the majority of respondents indicated that their review process did include an auditing review checklist or other protocol to describe the tests to be performed. Over one-third stated that their review process included a procedure that generates an audit trail. For those respondents who have these review processes in place, the accounting department and internal auditor were largely identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the department or person responsible for ensuring such a review process is followed. While the majority of respondents report having a formal review process, other controls and tools that could potentially improve or support the review process, such as independent review groups, external auditing software tools, and a spreadsheet computing policy stating design standards, were used in 25% or less of the responding companies (Table 7). Further adding to the concern within the review process is the level of the reviewer's domain experience. Table 6 shows that reviewers in our survey are required to have a strong spreadsheet background, but that, similar to developers, noticeably lower requirements are in place for domain knowledge. If respondents utilize the review phase as a compensating control, hoping to catch domain errors made in the development stage before they would impact the <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting process, it is important for reviewers to have a higher level of domain knowledge than is reported, especially given the low requirements for the developer's domain expertise.

## 5.5.2. Access controls

As shown in Fig. 1, access control was tied with version management as the third most dif<sup>fi</sup>cult process for implementing spreadsheet controls in <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting. PricewaterhouseCoopers [18] de<sup>fi</sup>nes access control as “limiting access at the <sup>fi</sup>le level to spreadsheets on a central server and assigning appropriate rights.” Password protection is also discussed as an access control. As revealed in Table 7, a majority of respondents had three controls classi<sup>fi</sup>ed under the PricewaterhouseCoopers category of access control in place. 76% of respondents had <sup>fi</sup>les secured in drives and server folders with limited access. This indicates that respondents have taken measures to limit access to spreadsheets. In addition to limiting access to the drives and folders, they also keep these areas well organized. 62% of respondents reported effective controls in logically structured directories and folders for business units, cycles, and type of spreadsheets. The third access control involves passwords. 55% of respondents require passwords to update spreadsheets.

Despite having effectively implemented the above access controls, untabulated results demonstrate that respondents continue to struggle with access controls in the later part of the spreadsheet life cycle. 86% of respondents (30/35) reported that there was no policy to describe the job title or skill requirement of the person responsible for using a newly developed spreadsheet. Similarly, when asked if they had a documented procedure to set up appropriate access levels for different identi<sup>fi</sup>ed users of the spreadsheet, 66% (23/35) answered no.

## 5.5.3. Back-up process

Companies surveyed identi<sup>fi</sup>ed processes in which it was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to implement controls in <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting. By not selecting a process, we interpret this to mean they feel more con<sup>fi</sup>dent with spreadsheet controls for that process. The back-up process was the least selected, thereby indicating the greatest level of con<sup>fi</sup>dence, which is supported by answers to additional survey questions. 89% of respondents (31/35) reported backing up critical spreadsheets on a regular basis, although the timeframe for “regular” was not de<sup>fi</sup>ned. The majority of respondents reported the IT department was responsible for ensuring that the backup procedures are followed.

## 5.5.4. Review and monitoring

We had few separate questions in the survey that made a clear distinction between review before usage and review during usage. However, the purpose of review and monitoring during the usage stage is different than a review performed before the model is placed in operation. In particular, monitoring is necessary during the usage stage to identify accidental user-errors (such as a number unintentionally entered over a formula or a formula erroneously copied by a user) and fraudulent user activities that are performed with the intention to deceive. The survey provides some insight into the review, monitoring, and auditing processes. As described above in the Planning, Design, and Development section, review processes and the existence of separate review groups seem to be limited. In asking about speci<sup>fi</sup>c controls or tools implemented, we found that 24% of respondents have independent review groups, and only 7% of respondents use third party auditing software (Table 7). As a result, the review and monitoring associated with this stage of the life cycle is most likely done by the user. McGill and Klobas [10] found that user spreadsheet knowledge had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive effect on decision-making accuracy as the user's knowledge can be used to overcome problems associated with a poorly developed spreadsheet design and can minimize accidental user-errors. Therefore, the high spreadsheet experience requirements observed for reviewers will help detect and minimize accidental user-errors if these reviewers are also the users of the spreadsheet (Table 6). The likelihood of identifying potential fraud decreases however with this lack of separation of duties.

## 5.6. Modification stage of the spreadsheet life cycle

When respondents were asked to identify the single most dif<sup>fi</sup>cult process to control, 23% of the 43 respondents selected change management. None of the top seven effective controls implemented by the survey respondents was a change management (or change control) as listed in the PricewaterhouseCoopers control categories. Given the high level of concern placed upon this process and the lack of reported existing controls, it is not surprising that 63% or more of the 35 respondents to the questions shown in Table 9 reported no documented procedures (including use of IT and built-in spreadsheet controls) to address issues related to change management. Untabulated results show that if a company did have a documented procedure in place, the accounting department was the department largely responsible for ensuring that the policy was followed. Two respondents listed their spreadsheet review group as the responsible department. 89% of respondents (31/35) reported they did not have a policy to describe the job title or skill requirement of the person responsible for changing the spreadsheet.

Version management was one of the top three processes of concern for implementing spreadsheet controls. Version management is obviously very dependent on the effectiveness of a company's change management process. If a company does not have a clear process for documenting and controlling changes, it will be hard for a company to identify new versions of a spreadsheet. Even when companies know that a new version of a spreadsheet exists, it is still dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to state whether companies are effectively implementing version management controls because our survey did not provide numerous options of controls in this area for respondents to select. The straightforward option proposed was third party tools for access, version, change, and archive support. Only 3.4% of respondents indicated they were effectively implementing this control (Table 7). The other less obvious option for a version management control was logically structured directories/ folders for business units, cycles, and type of spreadsheet, where <sup>fi</sup>les could be named and categorized based on their version. 62% of respondents indicated they are effectively implementing the control for logically structured directories/folders (Table 7). However, respondents may not be utilizing this as a version management control, at least not as a strong, stand-alone control for version management, given the level of dif<sup>fi</sup>culty they have expressed in controlling this process. If respondents are implementing other version management controls not on our list, these controls are not providing the respondents with a strong level of comfort in this area. 63% of respondents (22/35) stated they did not have a documented procedure to limit access to the most recent version of the spreadsheet (Table 9). Only 28% of respondents (18/25) collect information about the number of versions and identify the latest version of a spreadsheet (Table 4). It is not surprising that respondents ranked version management as a top level concern. Again, some of these dif<sup>fi</sup>culties may exist due to the problems companies are having with change management controls.

## 5.8. Future controls or tools to be implemented

In addition to those controls already implemented, Table 7 shows the percent of companies that plan to implement particular controls or tools in the future. For those respondents who stated future plans, the foremost future control respondents plan to implement is Excel Track Changes (37%), followed by formal review processes (32%). The formal review process was already in the top four internal controls or tools currently implemented. In fact, if the plans are reasonably accurate, the current top six controls or tools will be implemented in 76% or more of the responding companies in the future. However, given future plans, the only other controls or tools that would exceed a total of 50% in the future (currently implemented plus plans to implement) would be Excel Track Changes (54%) and independent review groups (50.4%). It is particularly troublesome that both a spreadsheet computing policy for stating design standards and mandated training for developers are low in current and planned implementation.

## 6. Conclusions and suggestions for practice and future research

The Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 has provided signi<sup>fi</sup>cant motivation for public companies to develop and tighten controls over spreadsheets used for <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting. There are potentially serious, negative consequences of poorly designed spreadsheets, including public disclosure of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant weaknesses in controls and materially misstated <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate that companies continue to use spreadsheets for <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting. However, even with such a strong incentive for companies to have effective controls, many weaknesses in controls exist. Formal policies and procedures are still lacking in most companies for most of the spreadsheet life cycle stages. More than half, and often most, of the companies report no policy in place to describe the required quali<sup>fi</sup>cations for individuals who develop, modify, review, or use spreadsheets. The results show that while individuals who develop and review spreadsheets have a reasonably high level of spreadsheet experience, their domain knowledge tends to be much lower than their spreadsheet experience. In the case of <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting, which can involve knowledge of complex accounting rules, this is of great concern. Though compensating controls may be in place at the <sup>fi</sup>nal review stage before information goes into <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports, stronger controls at earlier stages of the process would reduce the risk of non-compliance and errors. Further, spreadsheets associated with applications other than <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting are less likely to have compensating controls for developer and reviewer domain de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies, since external auditing and accountability are not present.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that practitioners can improve controls in several areas. More formal policies and procedures that set requirements for processes and expertise for domain knowledge and spreadsheet expertise are needed, particularly in the design and development and usage stages. The absence of controls in the modi<sup>fi</sup>cation stage was also particularly striking. A majority of responding companies had few policies and controls in place for change and version management. In general, many of the survey responses indicated a lack of policies and procedures for assigning responsibilities and accountability to speci<sup>fi</sup>c individuals (including appropriate credentials) in each stage. Companies should consider the importance of assigning responsibilities and separating duties as controls to prevent errors and to reduce opportunities for fraud. We note again that the weaknesses found in this study are for controls for spreadsheets used in an area that is highly regulated and visible. We would further suggest that practitioners consider and apply similar analyses to operational spreadsheets, in which errors may lead to poor business decisions.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest several important areas for future research. Though more research is now being done beyond the design and development stage, our results indicate that there are problems in all stages of a spreadsheet's life cycle. Attempts made by some organizations to control certain processes by an ad-hoc approach do not appear to be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient. In particular, effective controls for change management, access, and version management are seriously de<sup>fi</sup>- cient. In addition to identifying more tools for these processes, companies need more information about how to create policies that outline a cohesive, effective, and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient set of controls. Future surveys need to query organizations for more technical details about the various controls that are implemented and the types of spreadsheet that are being monitored. In our survey, despite the critical nature of these spreadsheets used in <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting, the cataloging of information about spreadsheets is uneven, at best. Table 4 reveals that less than half the responding companies collect information for spreadsheet inventories on 6 of the 11 items we listed. Only the purpose of the spreadsheet was routinely collected by nearly all companies. Understanding which items contribute to the risk of a proposed spreadsheet will help a company identify the information that they need to collect for a spreadsheet after the planning stage so that they can better monitor the effectiveness of the spreadsheet controls. Finally, further research comparing the impact of domain knowledge and spreadsheet expertise is also needed. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this line of research will help organizations plan and implement policies that impact training for spreadsheet developers, design review, change management, version control, and auditing of spreadsheet models in all application areas within their organizations.
