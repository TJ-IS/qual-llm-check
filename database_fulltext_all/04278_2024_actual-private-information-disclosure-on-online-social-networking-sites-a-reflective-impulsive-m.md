## Abstract

People disclose their private information on social networking sites (SNS) despite their concerns about privacy invasion—a phenomenon referred to as the privacy paradox. Extant studies explaining this paradox have primarily used privacy calculus theory, which assumes that individuals rely on reflective factors to make decisions. This theory can easily predict the intention of private information disclosure (PID) but lacks the power to explain actual PID. This study follows the reflective-impulsive model, which encompasses reflective and impulsive systems to explain actual PID. We collected data from multiple sources to test our hypotheses. We surveyed users of a leading SNS in China, retrieved the messages and photos disclosed by the survey respondents, and invited their SNS “friends” to evaluate this retrieved content to measure the respondents’ PID. Results support the research model as follows: First, in the impulsive system, immediate rewards lead to impulsiveness (compared to long-term social rewards that inhibit impulsiveness), which, in turn, influences PID. Second, in the reflective system, task-focused coping responses mediate the effects of privacy concerns and privacy self-efficacy on PID. Third, the effect of task-focused coping responses under the reflective system contrasts with impulsiveness—since the latter is not contingent on users’ privacy invasion experience. This study contributes to the information privacy literature by applying reflective-impulsive model to explain the actual PID of SNS users and by delineating the different contingent roles of users’ privacy invasion experience for both systems’ effects.

Keywords: Online Social Networks, Privacy Concerns, Task-Focused Coping Responses, Reflective-Impulsive Model, Impulsiveness, Private Information Disclosure, Privacy Invasion Experience

## 1 Introduction

Information privacy is a major concern for technology users in the information age (Acquisti et al., 2015). Users of social networking sites (SNS), such as Facebook and Twitter, have expressed concerns about recurrent privacy invasions on these platforms (Sheng et al., 2023). SNS users’ self-disclosed private information can easily fall into the hands of privacy invaders, such as cyberstalkers and cyberbullies (Wang et al., 2019). An increasing number of online privacy incidents, including identity thefts and online stalking, have been reported in recent years (Orden, 2019). Users are responding by blaming SNS providers for failing to safeguard their privacy and threatening to quit SNS. However, despite these concerns, many users continue to share significant amounts of private information on

SNS, including their home addresses and intimate photos (Li et al., 2020). This paradoxical discrepancy between SNS users perceiving privacy threats and nevertheless disclosing private information, known as the privacy paradox, is potentially harmful to SNS users and service providers (Mirza et al., 2021).

The privacy calculus model has been widely used to effectively explain the privacy paradox between users’ privacy concerns and their intention to disclose private information (Acquisti et al., 2015; Teubner & Flath, 2019). The model assumes that individuals are rational when dealing with privacy issues and that they thus thoughtfully weigh various rational and reflective factors to form a behavioral intention to disclose private information (Trepte et al., 2020). Previous studies have identified reflective factors such as benefits, privacy threats, and self-efficacy that lead to users’ intention to disclose private information on SNS (see Barth & de Jong, 2017, for a review). However, the information privacy literature has also pointed out that the privacy calculus model cannot sufficiently explain actual private information disclosure behavior. For example, Yu et al. (2020) conducted a meta-analysis study on previous information systems (IS) privacy literature. They confirmed that the effect size of privacy concerns on actual private information disclosure (PID) is small and insignificant (β = -0.063).

The privacy calculus model may not effectively explain actual PID because it assumes that users make decisions about sharing private information through a reflective process and does not capture the complexity and dynamics of users’ actual PID decision-making processes. Social networking sites are designed as instant and real-time communication technologies that provide users with rewards that can be quickly obtained. To rapidly obtain these rewards, people often use SNS and disclose information without taking the time to really think things through. With little deliberation time, SNS users will often make spontaneous and impulsive decisions (Strack & Deutsch, 2004). As a result, it is unsurprising to find that SNS users disclose private information (e.g., intimate photos) due to the failure to control their impulse to share their experience in real time, ignoring or underestimating the potential consequences. Anecdotal evidence suggests that impulsive decisionmaking has become increasingly common among a significant portion of SNS users. A survey of 342 undergraduate students found that most have impulsively disclosed information on Facebook (NDTV, 2017). It has been reported that the impulsive use of social media via smartphones even leads to the emergence of addiction-like symptoms, the so-called social-networks-use disorder (Wegmann et al., 2020).

This study applies the reflective-impulsive model (RIM) as an overarching theoretical framework to incorporate the impulsive system to explain actual PID. RIM adopts a dual-system perspective to incorporate two distinct processes of reasoning that determine individuals’ behavioral patterns—namely, the reflective system as a “cold” system, and the impulsive system as a “hot” system (Strack & Deutsch, 2004). Under RIM, the reflective system is rational and responsible for deliberate reasoning, whereas the impulsive system is irrational and responsible for quick decisions (Strack & Deutsch, 2004). RIM explicitly delineates an impulsive system to explain the actual behavior and provides a theoretical perspective for incorporating the reflective system (Deutsch et al., 2016).

By utilizing RIM to explain actual PID decisions on SNS, we can identify two potential opportunities for further research that could enhance our comprehension of the privacy paradox phenomenon. First, applying RIM implies an encompassing perspective on understanding the determinants of PID on SNS through impulsive and reflective psychological processes. Regarding the impulsive system, RIM typically identifies dispositional and situational factors that can trigger the system (Deutsch et al., 2016). This study can thus inform SNS practitioners about how users’ impulsive decisions are formed and how such decisions are fostered or inhibited through contextual and dispositional factors. In particular, previous RIM literature has suggested that individuals can be easily tempted by immediate rewards to form impulsive decisions (Woolley & Fishbach, 2017). Meanwhile, SNS users also attempt to improve their image through, for example, retouching their photos and carefully choosing captions, which eventually translates into long-term rewards, (Woolley & Fishbach, 2017). From the perspective of RIM, rewards are categorized into immediate and long-term rewards, each serving distinct functions within the impulsive system. This study investigates how the two types of rewards play out in the impulsive system to explain PID.

Within its reflective system, RIM helps elucidate how threat and efficacy appraisals trigger task-focused coping responses, leading to further risky behavior (Hofmann et al., 2008). Therefore, this study aims to reveal how individuals reflectively apply protection mechanisms to cope with their perceived privacy threats and why task-focused coping responses, in turn, lead to risky behavior on SNS. Research on the privacy paradox has found that higher perceived privacy threats generally reduce people’s PID (Barth & de Jong, 2017). However, privacy protection mechanisms offered by social media platforms, such as audience management tools, can allow users to gain control over perceived privacy threats by engaging in task-focused coping responses (Kaptein & Van Helvoort, 2019; Liang et al., 2019). Their perceived threats may dynamically change after engaging in coping responses and may affect PID decisions on SNS (Wang et al., 2019). Understanding how task-focused coping responses intervene in the relationship between perceived privacy threats and PID could yield valuable insights into the privacy paradox. That is, threats may not necessarily suppress PID if task-focused coping responses also play a role in decision-making.

Second, although the reflective and impulsive systems jointly influence users’ PID behaviors, the dominant system differs across different groups of users and task conditions (Cui et al., 2022). Understanding the boundary contingencies of reflective and impulsive systems on SNS helps better explain the privacy paradox for different groups of SNS users. We thus identify individuals’ privacy invasion experience as a key contingent factor. Many SNS users fall victim to privacy invasions (or are at least aware of the potential for such issues) (Choi et al., 2015). For people with or without privacy invasion experience, their reliance on dual systems can differ. Therefore, we examine the moderating role of users’ prior privacy invasion experience when investigating the effects of impulsive and reflective systems on the PID of SNS users.

To achieve these research objectives, we propose a research model that considers the influences of both reflective and impulsive systems on PID and explores the antecedents of the dual systems. To validate our proposed research model, we collected data from multiple sources, including data collected from a paired survey of SNS users and data about actual PID collected from SNS. For the antecedents of PID under RIM, we invited users of a leading Chinese SNS to participate in a laboratory survey. Regarding PID, we collected selfdisclosed messages from users on their SNS and asked their SNS “friends” to evaluate these messages. We used the paired survey design to minimize common method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003).

This study provides three key contributions to the literature by applying the novel theoretical perspective of RIM to explain PID and address the privacy paradox. First, we advance the present understanding of actual PID behavior by providing a theoretical account of the effects of an impulsive system on the PID of SNS users. Second, we expand on the conventional understanding of the trade-off between rewards and privacy threats by thoroughly identifying and reconceptualizing the antecedents that influence PID’s reflective and impulsive systems. Third, we contribute to the RIM literature by elucidating the contingencies when the two RIM systems are more or less effective in explaining actual PID. We examine the moderating effect of users’ prior privacy invasion experience and demonstrate how the mediation effect of task-focused coping responses on PID is moderated by privacy invasion experience.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Sections 2 and 3 review the theoretical foundation of our research, present our research model, and develop our hypotheses. Section 4 explains our research method, and Section 5 presents our data analysis and results. Section 6 presents our theoretical and practical contributions and discusses the limitations of our study.

## 2 Theoretical Background: RIM

## 2.1 Privacy Paradox: From Traditional Perspectives to RIM

When explaining the privacy paradox, most of the extant privacy literature assumes that people are “rational animals” who can identify the utility of PID and disclose what they believe is good for them. Previous studies have widely used privacy calculus theory as their dominant framework to identify factors processed in individuals’ reflective thinking (Dinev et al., 2006). The core idea of this theory is that if the benefits outweigh the potential costs of PID, individuals will have a positive intention to disclose private information (Trepte et al., 2020). The benefits of PID may be either general (i.e., perceived benefits of PID) or specific (i.e., social capital, enjoyment, and image management as benefits) (Li, 2011). As typical “costs” in the trade-off, privacy concerns refer to the concern about potential negative consequences associated with sharing information (see Smith et al. (2011) for a review).

Further studies based on the rational assumption adopt different perspectives to advance the understanding of the rational factors of PID. For example, protection motivation theory (PMT) focuses on how people evaluate privacy threats and cope with such threats (Cho et al., 2010; Liang & Xue, 2009; Youn, 2009). Recent literature has started questioning the “rational animal” assumption, introducing a “bounded rationality” perspective into the privacy literature. Essentially, people may not always make rational PID decisions to maximize the utility or benefits of their actions (Adjerid et al., 2018). Privacy-related decisions (especially PID decisions on SNS) are often made within one’s cognitive ability and time limits. Appendix A summarizes the theories and studies based on the “rational animal” or unbounded rationality assumption in explaining the privacy paradox.

As the rational system of privacy-relevant behavior has been well established, the irrational system regarding privacy-relevant behavior has been largely neglected in the extant literature. In behavioral research, the dualprocess perspective proposes combining rational and irrational systems to understand human behavior (Evans & Stanovich, 2013). The dual-process perspective suggests that human behavior is not based solely on analytic reasoning but also on associative thinking that can help individuals process information quickly and intuitively (Evans, 2008; Hofmann et al., 2009; Strack & Deutsch, 2004). The dual-process perspective describes associative thinking as fast, automatic, intuitive, and irrational, while rational thinking is characterized as slow, deliberative, and analytical (Evans 2008). Scholars generally agree that the two systems work in parallel to determine the behavior of human beings (Liang & Xue, 2021). In the same vein, neuroscience research has long validated that dual behavioral systems are relevant to two different brain regions and that both regions can actively influence behavior (Reyna & Brainerd, 2011).

Several dual-process theories have been developed to understand privacy-relevant behaviors (Barth & de Jong, 2017). Most of these dual-process theories are similar in depicting the rational system, whereby individuals reflectively analyze the benefits and threats in context. However, these theories differ in explaining the fast associative thinking system. For example, some argue that behavior results from the central processing of information and heuristics (Liang & Xue, 2021). Such dual information processing theories (e.g., elaboration likelihood model) are particularly applicable in contexts when people are required to deal with persuasion information (e.g., privacy setting warnings) (Gu et al., 2017). Appendix A presents the studies that have drawn on a dual-process perspective to explain privacy-relevant behavior.

This study relies on one of the dual-system models, i.e., RIM, to study actual PID. In addition to the deliberate reasoning system, RIM introduces the impulsive system representing the fast-reasoning system. The impulsive system is particularly applicable when explaining proactive behavior that involves immediate rewards (Strack & Deutsch, 2004). Previous studies have applied RIM to explain behaviors such as smoking and drinking that tend to provide excitement by providing immediate rewards (Hofmann et al., 2008) and other behaviors that provide immediate product acquisition, such as shopping (Van Gelder, 2013). In our research context, PID provides immediate rewards (e.g., enjoyment and communication convenience). We apply the impulsive system under RIM to explain PID on SNS.

## 2.2 Impulsive System

The impulsive system is responsible for automatic, quick, and effortless processing that relies on associations to generate impulsiveness for action (Deutsch et al., 2016). Through the impulsive system, an immediate reward associated with a behavior can trigger impulsiveness for the behavior automatically and quickly. Impulsiveness (also dubbed an impulsive behavioral tendency) refers to the intense and spontaneous behavioral tendency to gain immediate rewards (Rook & Fisher, 1995). Such a behavioral tendency involves acting without deliberate thinking across a range of situations (e.g., individuals “just want to do it” or “do it now and think about it later”)

(Stanford et al., 2009). SNS users who exhibit high impulsiveness may feel powerful urges to disclose private information without thinking (Rook, 1987; Whiteside & Lynam, 2001). They may even experience intense feelings (e.g., feeling “compelled” or “just wanting” to do something) (Rook, 1987) and may even feel anxious if they cannot take action (Verheul et al., 1999).

In the impulsive system, the perception of immediate rewards activates impulsiveness. Immediate rewards are rewards that individuals can obtain or experience without any delay in a specific context (Woolley & Fishbach, 2017). Most psychologists believe in the pleasure principle that human beings instinctively pursue immediate rewards because such rewards imply little waiting time and great pleasure (Woolley & Fishbach, 2017). In the hope of immediate pleasure, people’s perception of immediate rewards activates corresponding behavioral tendencies in their brains (Deutsch et al., 2016). Hence, people form an intense and spontaneous behavioral tendency to gain immediate rewards (Rook, 1987).

In addition to establishing the effect of immediate rewards in triggering impulsiveness, psychologists have sought to understand what can help people resist impulsiveness. The famous “marshmallow experiment” first highlighted the problem, revealing that providing a long-term but more valuable and enduring reward can help preschool students inhibit their impulsiveness (Mischel et al., 1989). Such rewards are the so-called delayed rewards (Green et al., 1994). The pleasure principle can also explain the effect of delayed rewards. If delayed rewards are significant enough, they will dominate people’s attention (Green et al., 1994). Some people will perceive that delayed rewards will bring them more pleasure in the future and will thus consciously control their impulsiveness towards behavior to reap this higher level of delayed gratification (Funder & Block, 1989). Further studies have found that delayed rewards can be applied in many contexts—for example, to help people curb their impulsiveness towards unhealthy behavior and risky investment behavior (Cheng et al., 2012; Woolley & Fishbach, 2016).

Immediate and delayed rewards are indispensable in the impulsive system, serving as situational stimuli and inhibitors. However, some individuals are likely to behave more impulsively than others. Dispositions also play an essential role in the impulsive system (Harvanko et al., 2019; Reynolds et al., 2006). For example, some people are more likely to take risks and are thus more likely to make impulsive investment decisions (Hertwig et al., 2019). People’s moneyspending disposition influences impulsiveness in shopping (Troisi et al., 2006). Individuals with impulsive dispositions will be more prone to spontaneously disclose private information without considering the long-term consequences.

## 2.3 Extending the Reflective System with Protection Motivation Theory

Within the framework of RIM, the reflective system revolves around how people respond to threats (Hofmann et al., 2008). The reflective system of RIM stems from the premise that individuals’ reasoning influences their behavior through a relatively slow and conscious deliberation process that requires deliberate efforts (Deutsch et al., 2016). In the reflective system, the potentially negative consequences of a behavior are activated. People need to spend sufficient cognitive effort to recall relevant information, evaluate threats, and further consider their ability to control them (Deutsch et al., 2016). As the impulsive system is characterized by limited cognitive capacity and tends to prioritize immediate reactions over careful analysis, people will exert minimal deliberative effort in considering the negative consequences of a behavior (Deutsch et al., 2016).

To explain the effects of threats and efficacy, Hofmann et al. (2008) incorporated PMT in RIM as the representation of the reflective system because PMT and the reflective system of RIM are primarily focused on the role of perceived threat and protection mechanisms in shaping protective responses. Meanwhile, both theoretical frameworks assume that people will react to perceived threats rationally and reflectively engage in protective responses to eliminate these threats. Specifically, PMT in the RIM literature describes deliberate threats and efficacy appraisals and identifies threats- and efficacy-relevant factors as typical reflective factors (See Hofmann et al. (2008) for a review).

The original purpose of PMT was to elucidate how other reflective factors contribute to the development of threats and how individuals respond to these threats (Rogers, 1975). For example, suppose individuals were confronted with the possibility of developing skin cancer, specific protection mechanisms, such as wearing sunscreen, could mitigate the likelihood of this potential hazard (Hofmann et al., 2008). PMT suggests that two cognitive appraisal processes, namely threat appraisal and coping appraisal, drive an individual’s adoption of protection response to cope with a potential threat (Maddux & Rogers, 1983; Rogers, 1975; Rogers et al., 1983). In the threat-appraisal process, individuals evaluate the vulnerability and severity of certain risk situations (Gao et al., 2018; Youn, 2009). In the copingappraisal process, individuals evaluate the effectiveness of recommended protection behaviors (i.e., response efficacy) and their ability to carry out the protection behaviors (i.e., self-efficacy). The two appraisal processes have been well-established in the information system literature to explain how people deliberately cope with threats to their privacy and security (Crossler, 2010; Liang & Xue, 2009, 2010; Liang et al., 2019; Mousavi et al., 2020). Thus, with its notions of threat appraisal and coping appraisal, PMT can offer valuable insights into the deliberate thinking process involved in PID.

## 3.1 Effects of Impulsive Factors

In alignment with the definition of impulsiveness in the prior literature, we define impulsiveness on SNS as a sudden, strong, and intensive behavioral tendency to disclose information on SNS (Rook & Fisher, 1995). Research has proposed that impulsiveness is an important predictor of technology use (Meerkerk et al., 2010). From this perspective, impulsiveness is felt prior to the performance of the behavior, leading to actual actions such as posting, sharing, and commenting on an SNS (Wang & Lee, 2020). For SNS users experiencing impulsiveness, their thinking about PID is unreflective and their decisions to disclose private information on SNS are often unplanned (Rook & Fisher, 1995; Schnauber-Stockmann et al., 2018).

When SNS users’ impulsiveness is aroused, they are less likely to deliberately evaluate the potential threats associated with their PID. Instead, they simply follow their impulsive desires, which naturally exist during their use of SNS, to disclose a large amount of private information (Yang et al., 2016). They may even feel anxious if they cannot disclose the information they want to share (Yang et al., 2016). Consequently, when their impulsiveness is elevated, SNS users will often be overcome by an intense desire to disclose personal information on an SNS (Rook & Fisher, 1995).

H1: The impulsiveness of SNS users positively affects their PID on SNS.

Immediate rewards promote people’s impulsive decisions, while delayed rewards prevent them from making impulsive decisions (Woolley & Fishbach, 2017). Accordingly, in the impulsive system of PID, we identify contextual immediate rewards as the stimuli and contextual long-term social rewards as the inhibitors of impulsiveness on SNS. People are immediately rewarded with convenience and enjoyment on SNS. SNS offers users many “one-click” features to disclose and share information. These features allow them to conveniently comment on others and build their circle of friends. They can also share their opinions or communicate as they please with their SNS friends. For example, SNS users may enjoy showing off or drawing attention to themselves by simply clicking the “post” button on a selfie. Or they may enjoy spreading gossip or joking with others by posting offhand comments.

SNS users become more impulsive when more immediate rewards are presented (Deutsch et al., 2016; Woolley & Fishbach, 2016). They experience pleasurable feelings when perceiving rewards they can reap immediately (Woolley & Fishbach, 2016). Pleasurable feelings help people release stress (Xu et al., 2014). Increased immediate rewards can tempt SNS users to seek immediate gratification to escape stress and reality (Beatty & Ferrell, 1998). SNS users can pursue excessive immediate rewards without deliberate thinking (Beatty &

Ferrell, 1998). Such users are more likely to ignore the potential negative consequences of their decisions and engage in impulsive behavior.

H2a: Convenience in communication as an immediate reward of PID positively affects impulsiveness.

H2b: Enjoyment as an immediate reward of PID positively affects impulsiveness.

In the context of SNS, long-term social rewards from engaging in PID are typically framed as delayed rewards in previous literature. PID characterizes image management and relationship building as long-term social rewards (Quinn, 2016). The pursuit of image management causes SNS users to invest cognitive efforts into planning the preferred branding of their online selves and executing the plan over an extended period (Zhang et al., 2023). Relationship building is a long-term social reward that is also acquired over time. To build relationships with other users, SNS users need to deliberately consider how to be palatable to other users through PID on SNS platforms, and that requires considerable time and effort (Wang et al., 2021).

When such long-term social rewards are perceived, SNS users will tend to be less impulsive in pursuing immediate gratification. People who perceive long-term social rewards form a sustaining motivation to plan for the content they intend to disclose on SNS (Bembenutty, 2022). When making decisions on SNS, they will consider their plan and think deliberately about whether their plan is well-executed or not. They will refrain from thoughtless and unreflective behaviors on SNS and seek to maintain a good image and relationship through carefully posting and commenting (Wang et al., 2021). Impulsiveness on SNS will thus be inhibited. We propose the following:

H3a: Image management as a long-term social reward of PID negatively affects impulsiveness.

H3b: Relationship building as a long-term social reward of PID negatively affects impulsiveness.

Much research suggests that impulsiveness on SNS is also affected by personal dispositions that vary with social contexts (Harvanko et al., 2019; Reynolds et al., 2006). With SNS offering ubiquitous social cues (Deng, Wang, Li, et al., 2022; Qiu et al., 2023), users may form different contextual dispositions following these social activities. Some users are more susceptible to these social activities than others. Susceptibility to social influence reflects users’ dispositional differences in relying on social cues or social activities to make decisions (Parsons et al., 2019). Individuals with a high level of susceptibility to social influence are susceptible to popular and trendy stimuli and elicit behavioral tendencies. In other words, SNS users who are more susceptible to social influence may be more prone to real-time social cues, such as tagging by friends and emerging trends shown on Trending Topics lists (Cinjarevic et al., 2011).

When faced with social cues on SNS, users who are susceptible to social influence will have a strong desire to conform to the expectations of their friends (Silvera et al., 2008). These desires make them more likely to immediately follow the herd without thoughtfully evaluating a particular action on an SNS (Posey et al., 2010; Xu et al., 2012). For example, when a social movement gains popularity on SNS, susceptible users may feel an impulsive desire to share their related experiences or opinions. As a result, they may impulsively disclose their preferences, hobbies, social relations, and personal life stories with their friends to fit in (Posey et al., 2010; Xu et al., 2012). For example, SNS users who are susceptible to social influence may impulsively post photos of a recent vacation or event to align with what their friends are posting, regardless of privacy threats. We propose that SNS users who are more susceptible to social influence tend to engage in more impulsive PID decisions on SNS.

H4: The susceptibility to social influence positively affects impulsiveness on SNS.

## 3.2 Effects of the Reflective System

The extant privacy literature has recognized privacy settings as the major protection mechanism that SNS platforms provide to address privacy threats directly (Lewis et al., 2008). In a reflective system, the behavioral response to applying protection mechanisms to directly cope with threats is labeled a task-focused coping response (also known as a problem-focused coping response) (Liang et al., 2019). Therefore, the task-focused coping response in this paper refers to users’ deliberate attempts to configure privacy settings, such as audience management, activity status (e.g., online or offline) management, and hiding their profiles from others to deal with privacy threats (Lewis et al., 2008). Coping responses to privacy threats can be non-task-focused. For example, people may adopt protection mechanisms to manage the stressful emotions triggered by perceived privacy threats, such as psychologically redirecting attention away from perceived privacy threats, and engaging in wishful thinking about threats (Liang et al., 2019). In contrast to configuring privacy settings, these protection mechanisms cannot effectively address privacy threats.

We argue that task-focused coping responses positively influence PID on SNS. The underlying logic of the risk compensation perspective can be incorporated into the reflective system of the RIM framework to understand the effects of task-focused coping responses on PID. Risk compensation refers to increased risky behavior after using effective prevention (Wang et al., 2022). After applying a privacy protection mechanism, individuals may become less careful in compensating for their coping efforts and thus engage in risk-taking behaviors, such as PID (He et al., 2019). Previous research has widely applied risk compensation to explain individuals’ behavior regarding threats (see Hedlund (2000) for a review). For example, Trogen and Caplan (2021) applied such a perspective to explain why COVID-19 vaccines promoted individuals’ intention to move.

Accordingly, SNS users who have configured privacy settings tend to believe that their privacy is already strictly protected. Such beliefs may reinforce their sense of confidentiality on SNS (Adjerid et al., 2018; Mousavi et al., 2020). With privacy settings configured, some users may even believe that privacy settings are designed to protect their privacy to the maximum extent and that large organizations are infallible (Acquisti & Gross, 2006; Cho et al., 2010). As a result, task-focused coping responses may lead SNS users to become less cautious and engage in risky behaviors. Such users may have misconceptions regarding confidentiality and may counterintuitively increase their PID. Adopting privacy protection mechanisms can also make users more willing to continue disclosing their personal information (Chen & Chen, 2015). We posit that SNS users with taskfocused coping responses are more likely to disclose private information than other users (Mousavi et al., 2020).

## H5: Task-focused coping responses positively influence PID.

In a reflective system, PMT is applied to explain taskfocused coping responses because task-focused responses are a protection response on SNS. PMT was developed to explain individuals’ protection response (Floyd et al., 2000; Liang & Xue, 2010). According to PMT, when long-term social rewards are active in users’ minds, they serve as reflective factors that motivate task-focused coping responses (Floyd et al., 2000; Mousavi et al., 2020). On SNS, the pursuit of long-term social rewards such as image management motivates users to adopt task-focused coping responses (e.g., audience management) to address privacy threats that could harm their image. Furthermore, the desire for relationship building can motivate individuals to employ task-focused coping responses. Individuals may control the accessibility of their information to other users, addressing privacy threats that could jeopardize their relationships with others. In summary, when individuals seek long-term social rewards like image management and relationship building, we posit that they will be motivated to adopt task-focused coping responses on SNS.

H6a: Image management as a long-term social reward of PID positively affects task-focused coping responses.

H6b: Relationship building as a long-term social reward of PID positively affects task-focused coping responses.

Privacy threats on SNS include a violator’s unauthorized access to and use of one’s private information (Zlatolas et al., 2015). In the privacy threats appraisal, people assess the two dimensions of privacy threats, namely, the vulnerability and severity of privacy threats (Youn, 2009). Privacy vulnerability represents the probability of the occurrence of a privacy threat (Cho et al., 2010). For example, SNS users evaluate their probability of falling victim to privacy threats, such as the possibility that their information on SNS will be accessed by an unexpected audience or be monitored by data collection agencies (Gao et al., 2018). Privacy concerns refer to individuals’ concerns about privacy threats, reflecting individuals’ apprehensions about the severity of privacy threats (Jiang et al., 2013). The appraisal of privacy threats demands significant cognitive effort to recall and evaluate relevant information about potential negative consequences. This process remains inactive in the impulsive system but becomes active in the reflective system, triggering a protective response.

Specifically, privacy threat appraisals motivate the protection responses of individuals on SNS (Floyd et al., 2000; Liang & Xue, 2010; Liang et al., 2019). When SNS users experience increased privacy concerns about privacy threats, they will be strongly motivated to seek effective approaches (e.g., configuring their privacy settings) to address their concerns as a way to regain control over their private information (Gao et al., 2018; Jeong & Kim, 2017). For example, if users are concerned about inappropriate access to their personal information, they can restrict access to personal information or opt out of certain data collection plans. In the same vein, when the perceived privacy vulnerability is high, such users become more likely to rely on various privacy settings on SNS (e.g., audience management) to minimize the probability of privacy incidents occurring. Summarizing the above discussion, we posit the following hypotheses:

H7a: Privacy concerns positively affect task-focused coping responses on SNS.

H7b: Privacy vulnerability positively affects taskfocused coping responses on SNS.

SNS users also evaluate specific privacy protection mechanisms in a process known as privacy coping appraisal. In the privacy coping appraisal, SNS users evaluate the response efficacy, that is, their confidence in the effectiveness of privacy protection mechanisms provided to avert privacy threats (Debatin et al., 2009). SNS such as Facebook frequently change their privacy protection mechanism for users, and users evaluate the mechanism’s effectiveness (Fox-Brewster, 2016). Users also evaluate privacy self-efficacy, which refers to individuals’ confidence in protecting themselves from privacy threats (Youn, 2009). SNS incorporate privacy protection mechanisms, but certain SNS make these mechanisms hard to follow, complex to understand, easy to dismiss, and burdensome to modify (Chen & Chen, 2015). However, people with sufficient SNS knowledge may have the confidence to successfully manage the obstacles presented by privacy protection mechanisms (Shu et al., 2011).

According to PMT, coping appraisal can also influence the adoption of task-focused coping responses (Liang et al., 2019; Posey et al., 2014; Vance et al., 2012). Users with high privacy self-efficacy are likely to proactively engage in task-focused coping responses because they have strong confidence in their privacy protection ability to overcome privacy threats and achieve the potential rewards (Xu et al., 2011). Users with low privacy self-efficacy may be more inclined to doubt their abilities, resulting in a lack of motivation to adopt task-focused coping responses. (Xu et al., 2011). High response efficacy can also motivate people to adopt task-focused coping responses because they believe they will allow them to reap the associated protective benefits (Ifinedo, 2012; Rogers et al., 1983). Users who do not believe that the privacy protection mechanism will effectively protect them (i.e., with low response efficacy) may perceive task-focused coping responses to be useless or unnecessary (Cho et al., 2010). As a result, they will not engage in such responses on SNS.

H7c: Privacy self-efficacy positively affects taskfocused coping responses on SNS.

H7d: Response efficacy positively affects taskfocused coping responses on SNS.

## 3.3 Moderating Effects of Privacy Invasion Experience

Relevant experience has been regarded as an appropriate moderator for dual behavioral systems in previous research (Rook, 1987). As people gain more experience, they gain a better understanding of the consequences of their behavior and thus develop stronger regulation skills to override the effects of impulsiveness (Hofmann et al., 2008). Research has shown that the effects of impulsiveness can be controlled by relevant experience (Brandt et al., 2019). In the reflective system, experience helps expose people to a wider variety of situations and information, activating stronger and more deliberate reflective processing (Daudelin, 1996). In summary, prior experience can serve as a “dual switch” that can alter the effects of dual systems.

The contingent role of prior experience in dual systems can be further explained from the cognitive resource perspective. Cognitive resources refer to individuals intelligence and task-relevant knowledge, which serves as a functional basis for the reflective system of behavior (Erev et al., 2008). Although the two systems can both be active in the brain, the relative strength of their effects mainly depends on individuals’ cognitive resources in an idiosyncratic context (Hofmann et al., 2008). When their cognitive resources are adequate, individuals tend to rely on the results of the reflective system (Hofmann et al., 2008). When their cognitive resources are deficient—for example, when individuals lack relevant experience or become impaired by drinking too much—they tend to rely on the impulsive system because of the reflective system’s dysfunction (Hofmann et al., 2008). Privacy invasion experience is the extent to which individuals have been victims of privacy invasions while using SNS (Cho et al., 2010). Privacy invasion experience can strongly influence the amount of cognitive resources needed for PID decisions because such experience introduces knowledge about the consequences of PID and increases the mental processing demands associated with weighing threats (Hennig-Thurau et al., 2004; Norberg et al., 2007).

We posit that privacy invasion experience strengthens the effect of task-focused coping responses on PID in the reflective system. People with prior negative experiences tend to be highly sensitive to threats and the corresponding protection mechanism because they do not want to experience the “nightmare” again (Hogarth & Einhorn, 1992). Thus, when making PID decisions, we posit that people with privacy invasion experience will have more cognitive resources at their disposal, leading to “cold” rational decision-making. Thus, they will rely more on the reflective system, i.e., the task-focused coping response. As a result, the effect of task-focused coping responses on PID will be stronger for people with privacy invasion experience.

H8: Privacy invasion experience positively moderates the effect of SNS users’ task-focused coping responses on PID. This effect is stronger for SNS users with a high level of privacy invasion experience compared to those with a low level of privacy invasion experience.

We speculate that the effect of impulsiveness on PID is also contingent on users’ prior privacy invasion experience. SNS users with considerable privacy invasion experience will be sensitive to the negative outcomes of PID SNS use. Negative experiences can alert users to the need to be deliberate when they feel driven by the impulsive system. For instance, Rook and Fisher (1995) found that negative experiences can serve as a wake-up call and motivate individuals to change their behavior. In addition, negative experiences can help people to learn and grow. Experiencing the negative consequences of impulsive behavior may make an individual more likely to reflect on their behavior and consider how they could have acted differently. Such experience can help them develop the ability to overcome their impulsive tendencies. We posit the following:

H9: Privacy invasion experience negatively moderates the effect of impulsiveness on PID. This effect becomes weaker for SNS users with a high level of privacy invasion experience compared to those with a low level of privacy invasion experience.

## 4 Research Methodology

## 4.1 Data Collection via a Paired Survey Design

We used a paired survey design to test our hypotheses. Data for the independent variables (IVs) and the dependent variable (DV) was collected from different sources to minimize common method bias (MacKenzie & Podsakoff, 2012). Weibo.com, a widely used Chinese microblog social platform, was chosen as the research context (Allegretti et al., 2015). Approximately 43.5% of internet users in China use Weibo, with 61.9% posting personal information and photos on the platform (CNNIC, 2017). Weibo allows users to follow others without asking for their consent. However, Weibo users configure their privacy settings to limit the audience of their posts. PID is a common practice on Weibo, making it a suitable platform for data collection. To recruit participants, an invitation was posted on the official Weibo account of a leading Chinese university.

We invited SNS users to take our survey in pairs. Specifically, two participants who were mutual “friends” on Weibo registered, came to the laboratory together, and completed the survey questionnaires (Allegretti et al., 2015). These participants were given two sessions to complete these questionnaires: a selfevaluation session and a peer evaluation session. In the former, each of the two participants in the pair individually provided self-reported answers to the questions related to IVs and control variables. Meanwhile, each participant was asked to carefully read the original messages posted by their friend for at least 10 minutes in the lab and judge the extent to which their friend had disclosed private information on Weibo. Essentially, the IVs and control variables were reported by one participant, while the DV was mutually measured by the Weibo friend, i.e., the participant’s survey peer.

We used a Weibo friend to assess each participant’s level of actual PID because, compared to an unknown third party, an SNS friend can reliably determine whether the messages posted by a participant are actually “private” or not. As reported in Appendix B, most of the paired friends were familiar with each other (mean of familiarity = 4.1/5), and most relationships were over a year old. These relationships ensured that the peers could accurately judge whether their Weibo friends were truly disclosing private information on Weibo. Weibo users might make jokes on Weibo or disclose information that seems to be personal but is actually untrue. Therefore, Weibo friends were used in peer evaluations to minimize the chances of counting “fake” PID when evaluating the PID level of an information disseminator.

To improve the accuracy of the peer evaluation, we gave participants clear instructions and examples of private information for the PID evaluations in order to provide the peers with an objective baseline to accurately evaluate the PID of their friends. The instructions consisted of the definitions of private information (i.e., information that includes personal relevance), the sensitivity level of different types of information (i.e., high-level, medium-level, low-level, and no privateness), and typical examples for different levels of privateness. These instructions were developed based on the results of a pretest with 44 samples. In the pretest, we investigated the perceived privateness of different kinds of information and typical SNS posts. For example, most SNS users believe that identifiable information (e.g., real names), contact information (e.g., address), health-relevant information, financial information, and job information are very private and sensitive.

To further ensure that the peers were accurately judging whether the participants were truly revealing personal information on Weibo, we used three coders. The coders read the instructions we provided, went through the participants’ past disclosed content, and coded the privateness level of the disclosed content. Then, we checked the correlations between the results of the external coders and the Weibo friends. We found that the evaluation of PID by the friends and coders were highly correlated (p > 0.4) with each other. When “friends” and “coders” were given the same instructions and baseline to evaluate PID, this resulted in similar interpretations of PID.

We initially recruited 228 respondents (i.e., 114 pairs) who completed the self- and peer evaluation sessions. The reverse items of several variables were then used in the questionnaire to check whether the participants were serious in answering the survey questions. Eight participants (i.e., four pairs) who provided inconsistent answers between the reverse and original items were excluded from the final sample (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Our final sample size was 220, of which 41.2% were women. The demographic information of the respondents is presented in Appendix B. These demographics are generally similar to the overall user demographics of Weibo users (CNNIC, 2017).

We analyzed early/late response bias to check for potential response bias (Etter & Perneger, 1997). We separated the early (first 25%, N = 55) and late respondents (last 25%, N = 55) into two groups. We calculated the mean differences of several key variables between the two groups by performing an independent sample t-test. The results in Table 1 show no significant differences in the demographic information, impulsiveness, privacy invasion experience, SNS usage, and privacy concerns of both groups, suggesting that response bias does not present a major concern.

## 5 Data Analysis and Results

We followed a two-step approach to test our hypotheses (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988). In the first step, we assessed the reliability and validity of our measurement model (see Section 5.1). Then, we performed a structural equation model analysis in the second step to validate the causal effects posited in our hypotheses (see Section 5.2).

## 5.1 Measurement Model

We conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to test the measurement model. CFA allows users to test the reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the scales employed in the study (Hair et al., 2014). We measured the model fit of our measurement model by using the following indexes: chisquare/degree of freedom (χ2/df), goodness-of-fit index (GFI), and comparative fit index (CFI) (Schreiber et al. 2006). According to these indexes, the first-order measurement model demonstrated a favorable performance (Bagozzi 2011; Bagozzi et al. 1991): χ2 = 581.172, χ2/df = 1.216 (>1 and <3), GFI = 0.875 (>0.85), CFI = 0.973 (>0.90).

We evaluated the reliability of the reflective constructs based on general guidelines to assess the psychometric properties of the measurement items (Byrne, 2013). We initially evaluated the reliability of our measurements using Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability (CR) (Byrne, 2013) and calculated the reliability of the eleven first-order latent constructs. All measurements, except for susceptibility to social influence, obtained reliabilities above 0.7 (Byrne, 2013). Susceptibility to social influence obtained acceptable reliability following previous research suggesting that Cronbach’s alphas and CRs exceeding 0.60 are acceptable for scales with five or fewer items (Byrne, 2013).

Specifically, in the impulsive system, impulsiveness significantly influenced PID. Therefore, H1 was supported. Convenience in communication as an immediate reward positively influenced impulsiveness, whereas the effect of another immediate reward, enjoyment, on impulsiveness was not significant. Namely, H2a was supported, but H2b was not. The effects of both long-term social rewards significantly negatively influenced impulsiveness. Therefore, H3a and H3b were both supported. ## 5.3 Post Hoc Analysis

We performed a nested model comparison in AMOS to test whether including an impulsive system, reflective system, and moderating effects improved the model fit. To compare the nested models, we generated Model A as the representation of the reflective system, Model B as the representation of the impulsive system, Model C as the representation of RIM, and Model D as the default mode (i.e., our research model). Models A and B were nested within Model C, whereas Model C (i.e., RIM) was nested within Model D.

Table 7 shows the results of the model comparisons. The χ2 of Model C (i.e., RIM) was significantly higher than that of Model A (without the impulsive system), indicating that the inclusion of impulsiveness significantly improved the model fit. We also found a significant difference between the χ2 of Models C (i.e., RIM) and Model B (without the reflective system), indicating that including task-focused coping responses improved the model fit. Furthermore, including the moderating effect of privacy invasion experience on task-focused coping and PID significantly increased the model fit. In sum, the impulsive system, the reflective system, and the moderating effects of privacy invasion experience all increased the explanatory power of PID.

To check whether RIM can complement the privacy calculus model and better explain the actual PID, we also applied the privacy calculus model as an alternative model to explain PID. Table 8 shows the testing results of the alternative model using CB-SEM. The positive effects of the immediate rewards were significant (p < 0.05) (Rosenthal et al. 1994), whereas the effects of privacy concerns and privacy vulnerability were not significant (p > 0.05). The R<sup>2</sup> of the alternative model was 0.080, indicating that this model performed worse than RIM. This result highlights the limitation of using privacy calculus to explain PID. In other words, the privacy calculus model has limited explanatory power for PID, and other factors or models beyond this model must be considered to explain PID.

## 6 Discussion

## 6.1 Discussion of Results

To explain the privacy paradox surrounding PID in the context of SNS, we applied RIM as an overarching theoretical framework to identify factors that resulted in users’ actual PID on SNS. The research model explained 15.6 % of the variance in the DV (i.e., PID). Specifically, we found that impulsiveness influences users’ PID. Even with significant privacy invasion experience, SNS users’ impulsiveness remained highly influential, leading to PID. We also found that communication convenience as an immediate reward and susceptibility to social influence can promote impulsiveness, while long-term social rewards can inhibit impulsiveness. The effect of the other immediate reward—enjoyment on impulsiveness was not significant. SNS users can gain enjoyment with their online friends both in the short and long term, meaning that enjoyment is not a typical immediate or delayed reward. Therefore, its effects on impulsiveness may be blurred. We also found that the effects of perceived privacy threats and privacy vulnerability, as control variables on impulsiveness, were nonsignificant. This implies that the perceived privacy threats and privacy vulnerability were inactive in the impulsive system. Taken together, including the impulsive system significantly improved both the model fit and the model’s explanatory power for explaining the privacy paradox. Our analysis results demonstrate that models based solely on the reflective system may be misleading because they do not differentiate between the two systems that drive people’s behaviors.

In the reflective system, long-term social rewards, privacy concerns, and privacy self-efficacy lead to task-focused coping responses. These relationships are not surprising, as previous literature has already established these effects regarding the reflective system (Krasnova et al., 2010; Liang & Xue, 2009; Rosen & Sherman, 2006). Furthermore, we confirmed the significant main effect of task-focused coping responses on PID and observed that this effect is more pronounced among users with a high level of privacy invasion experience. The mediating effects of taskfocused coping responses in the reflective system were moderated by the privacy invasion experience, suggesting a moderated mediation effect.

The effects of privacy vulnerability and response efficacy on task-focused coping responses were not supported. Previous studies reveal that coping and threat appraisal’s effectiveness is contingent on many factors. For example, Boss et al. (2015) found the effect of response efficacy may be ineffective. When the recommended coping responses reduce threats but do not reduce fear, users may consider a recommendation inadequate and search for alternate solutions (e.g., emotion-focused coping responses) to reduce their anxiety (Liang et al., 2019). Chen et al. (2021) proposed an explainable tipping point where perceived vulnerability may effectively motivate employees to execute recommended protection mechanisms. Therefore, we believe that certain contingent factors may impact the effectiveness of threat appraisal and response efficacy. We leave this issue for future research.

The moderation effect of privacy invasion experience on the relationship between impulsiveness and PID was not supported. A controversy exists in the literature surrounding the contingent role of prior negative experience in the impulsive system (Crawford & Melewar, 2003; Strack & Deutsch, 2005). Hofmann et al. (2008) suggested that relevant prior experience, including negative experiences, may not enter the impulsive system to moderate the effect of impulsiveness on health-related behavior (e.g., overeating). In the health-related behavior context, people experience urges, such as overindulging in alcohol or binging on foods with high-fat content at parties (Verheul et al., 1999). In such cases, individuals can easily respond to their impulsiveness; thus, the temporal rapidity of the response process may not allow individuals to recall any relevant experience to counteract their impulsiveness. PID on SNS may be similar to health-related behaviors in that impulsiveness may easily lead to actual PID behavior. With smartphones, SNS users can easily execute PID behavior in response to their impulsiveness. Their rapid response process may prevent prior related negative experiences from reducing the effects of their impulsiveness, leading to the insignificant moderating effects of privacy invasion experience on the relationship between impulsiveness and PID.

## 6.2 Theoretical Implications

This research is one of the first studies to apply RIM to explain users’ actual PID on SNS. By doing so, we contribute to the privacy paradox between privacy concerns and actual PID, a long-standing puzzle for information privacy researchers.

First, drawing upon RIM, this study provides the privacy paradox literature with an impulsive system, a complementary yet crucial theoretical lens in understanding the privacy paradox. Previous information privacy studies have primarily assumed the reflective system and used the privacy calculus model to explain the paradox between privacy concerns and users’ intention to disclose private information in online environments. However, the impulsive system can complement our human brain’s more deliberate and rational system and allows us to react quickly and instinctively to gain immediate rewards in situations. Therefore, the impulsive system also plays a significant role in influencing users’ PID decisions on SNS, which are inherently designed to offer immediate rewards and trigger impulsive choices. By systematically incorporating an impulsive system, we enhance the understanding of the privacy paradox: People will still disclose their personal information despite perceived privacy threats due to their inability to overcome impulsiveness. Our findings are consistent with those of the extant research showing that the impulsive system leads to actual technology use behavior despite potential adverse outcomes (Wang & Lee, 2020).

Second, we provide an encompassing perspective to categorize and reevaluate the determinants of PID through RIM. RIM provides a dual-process perspective that can be used to understand how dual systems work in the human mind. Against this background, we investigate the antecedents of the impulsive behavioral tendency in the impulsive system, i.e., dispositional and contextual antecedents, and antecedents of task-focused coping responses in the reflective system, i.e., threats and coping appraisals. Our findings imply that the impulsive system is a “hot” system, prompting spontaneous responses to immediate rewards and social cues. However, this system can be controlled through the perception of long-term social rewards. In comparison, the reflective system is a “cold” system, where people plan for long-term rewards, privacy protection mechanisms, and potential threats projected into the future. Although RIM provides a dual-process approach to understanding how dual systems work in the human mind, the RIM literature generally lacks consideration of contextual antecedents in the two systems. This study significantly contributes to privacy research by deepening the understanding of the underlying mechanisms of dual systems in privacyrelated contexts.

Third, our study changes the privacy paradox literature’s traditional view that perceived threats negatively influence PID and thus enhances the understanding of how privacy paradox happens in the reflective system. We incorporated coping responses into the reflective system to capture individuals’ coping with perceived threats. Coping responses can give users a misconception of confidentiality and counterintuitively increase their PID on SNS. Perceived privacy threats will intuitively negatively relate to PID. As shown in Table 3, the correlation between privacy concerns and PID is negative. However, with coping responses, SNS users may overestimate their privacy level and underestimate the effectiveness of their coping responses (Acquisti & Gross, 2006; Cho et al., 2010). Our study demonstrates that individuals will compensate for their coping efforts by engaging in risky privacy-relevant behaviors, such as PID (i.e., risk compensation process). Such a process happens on SNS, explaining why privacy threats can even positively lead to PID. This finding offers similar implications for contexts where people are provided with protection mechanisms but also strongly desire to engage in risky behaviors and is thus consistent with the risk compensation process identified in the information security (Wang et al., 2017) and health-related (Hofmann et al., 2008) contexts.

Finally, we explored how contextual individual differences impact the effects of reflective and impulsive thinking on PID decisions. Previous research on RIM has largely ignored boundary conditions for dual systems. Our study helps extend RIM by identifying negative privacy experience as an important contingency factor. Specifically, we found that prior experience with privacy invasion serves as a “dual switch” that can activate the reflective system more for some individuals. For people who have experienced privacy invasions, their reflective system and coping responses will play a bigger role in determining PID. The memory and knowledge gained from past privacy issues give such users more cognitive resources to reflect carefully on threats. Instead of reacting impulsively, such users are more able to respond to their reflective system. We thus reconcile the controversy in the literature regarding the moderating effect of experience in dual systems.

## 6.3 Practical Implications

This study offers several practical implications for practitioners who manage SNS. First, this study provides practitioners with a convenient and effective scale for measuring the impulsiveness construct on SNS, a managerially important concept that is nevertheless virtually ignored in current practice. Our findings demonstrate the powerful impact of the impulsive system—which, at times, is stronger than that of the reflective system—on releasing personal information, as demonstrated in this study. Using the impulsiveness scale customized for the SNS context, practitioners can easily measure the levels of impulsiveness for SNS users and predict how impulsiveness may affect their personal information disclosure behavior. Therefore, we encourage practitioners to take full advantage of this useful instrument to monitor and manage privacy-related behavior on their websites closely and effectively.

Second, contextual factors like immediate rewards and long-term social rewards are revealed to be significant determinants of impulsiveness. Therefore, a balance or parallel strategy is recommended for practitioners. To facilitate SNS users’ release of private information, promoting website activities that can boost immediate rewards would be very effective. For example, an incentive mechanism could be developed as a way to reward those SNS users who offer positive comments and reactions in response to others’ posts (e.g., “like” and “subscribe”). Such features would make these websites more tempting and facilitate more social interactions. To inhibit the impulsive and harmful disclosure of private information, SNS providers should provide long-term social rewards to their users. Website features such as social media image and social network analytics may encourage users to pursue and perceive long-term social rewards, thus inhibiting their impulsiveness but promoting deliberate PID.

Third, we found that the impact of task-focused coping on PID is stronger among users with prior privacy invasion experience. These findings suggest that a deliberate reconfiguration of personal privacy settings becomes more prevalent after the announcement of a privacy-related incident on an SNS. Thus, to assist SNS users in managing their privacy settings, SNS platforms should offer user training for configuring privacy settings or provide “one-click privacy settings” to facilitate coping responses. We encourage practitioners to facilitate task-focused coping, especially if a privacy invasion occurs, by alerting users about the status of their privacy settings and reminding them to properly control their privacy settings.

Fourth, practitioners are also encouraged to exert due diligence to minimize threats arising from the unintentional and hazardous release of private information. Younger users are particularly prone to impulsive and risky activities (NDTV, 2017). Therefore, practitioners should design website features to help members of vulnerable groups avoid making such mistakes by reviewing their posts before posting.

## 6.4 Limitations and Future Research

The first limitation of this study is related to its empirical context, namely, Weibo.com in China. The effects of coping responses and privacy concerns may differ across SNS platforms and cultures. From a practical perspective, including other SNS platforms, such as WeChat (similar to Snapchat) and Instagram, can provide additional implications because different SNS platforms have different privacy settings (Choi et al., 2015). Future research could include other SNS contexts and cultures to examine the factors leading to users’ actual PID.

Second, extending this study longitudinally to further emulate the long-term dynamics of users’ PID on SNS will be another area for future research. Our findings show that prior experience with privacy invasions strengthens the influence of coping responses on PID. Privacy invasion experience accrues over time. Therefore, a longitudinal study could examine the effects of factors under RIM on PID over time.

Third, although we explored the influential contextual and dispositional factors (i.e., immediate rewards, long-term social rewards, and susceptibility to social influence) for impulsiveness based on the extant RIM literature, we did not explore other SNS contextual factors that may lead to impulsiveness, such as trustor safety-related contextual factors (Deng et al., 2022; Wang & Wang, 2019). Future research could explore the relationships between other contextual factors and impulsiveness. It would also be interesting to examine the impacts of certain biases and preferences on users’ rational decision-making. For example, people may have biases when they evaluate privacy threats and efficacy due to their privacy invasion experience (Cho et al., 2010). Whether privacy invasion experience moderates the effects of threats appraisal and efficacy appraisal is worth investigating.

Finally, in this study, we incorporated the audience’s perspective and used peer-rated data to measure PID. Doing so helped us avoid common method threats and biases in self-assessing privacy disclosure. However, it would also be possible to use topic modeling or machine learning methods to assess PID. So far, the accuracy of the machine-learning-based measurement of PID has reached 69.63% (Abadi et al., 2017). Future research could explore more methods and derive added implications from the new measurement.

## 6.5 Conclusions

The privacy paradox is a common phenomenon on SNS. Based on RIM, we reveal that users disclose private information due to the impulsive and reflective systems. Under the impulsive system, the immediate rewards provided by PID and susceptibility to social influence lead to impulsiveness, while long-term social rewards can inhibit it. Impulsiveness, in turn, leads to PID. Under the reflective system, users’ threats and coping appraisals lead to task-focused coping responses on SNS. After the privacy protection mechanism is implemented, an SNS user may become less vigilant and will compensate for their coping responses by engaging in PID; thus, coping responses can lead to PID, especially for users with privacy invasion experience. Social media platforms can examine users’ PID behaviors from a dual-system perspective and adopt measures to reduce PID associated with negative personal and social consequences, thereby promoting the healthy development of social media.

In this study, we conducted a review of theories that have been used to explain PID as well as the privacy paradox. In the main text, we showed that the focus of the privacy literature gradually shifted from rational to irrational factors. Specifically, Categorization 1 consists of the theories that deem human beings as rational animals and will make rational decisions. Her current research focuses on information privacy, information security, and cyberbullying. Her research has been published or forthcoming in academic journals and conferences such as Journal of Association Information Systems and International Conference on Information Systems.

Weiquan Wang is a professor in the Faculty of Business Administration of the Chinese University of Hong Kong (CUHK Business School). He received his PhD in management information systems from the University of British Columbia. His research interests include recommendation agents, human-AI/Algorithm interaction, social media, mobile app diffusion, open innovation management, and online information privacy. His works have been published in Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Management Science, and MIS Quarterly, among other outlets. He serves on the editorial review boards of Information Systems Research and the Journal of the Association for Information Systems. He served as an associate editor for MIS Quarterly from 2012 to 2015.

Lara Khansa is the Sonny Merryman Professor and Associate Dean for Faculty Affairs at the Pamplin College of Business at Virginia Tech. She received her PhD in information systems from the University of Wisconsin–Madison. Her research interests include online user behavior, information privacy and security, and healthcare analytics. Her research has appeared in Journal of Operations Management, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and Decision Sciences. She serves on the board of editors of Journal of Management Information Systems.

Sung S. Kim is the Peter T. Allen Professor in the Department of Operations and Information Management at the Wisconsin School of Business. His research covers broad issues related to online user behavior including habit, addiction, loyalty, switching costs, information privacy and security, gaming, community participation, and socia networking. His research has appeared in Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, Management Science, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and Decision Sciences.
