# Cannibalization and Competition Effects on a Manufacturer’s Retail Channel Strategies: Implications on an Omni-Channel Business Model

Jae-Cheol Kim, Se-Hak Chun

<sup>a</sup>Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST) <sup>b</sup>Seoul National University of Science and Technology

## Abstract

This paper analyzes two effects caused by “channel conflict”, which occurs when firms newly add a direct online channel via the Internet or a mobile device. The first is an “intracannibalization effect” between the firms’ existing retail channel and the new online channel, and the second is the “inter-competition effect” between manufacturers and retailers in the supply chain. In particular, this paper investigates a manufacturer’s retailing channel strategy considering the relative market power between a manufacturer and a retailer in the supply chain, which has been rarely considered in previous studies.

This paper shows the manufacturer’s channel strategies: (i) if customers are very heterogeneous with regard to their receptiveness to online shopping, the manufacturer may use a multi-channel strategy. (ii) if the customer sector becomes homogeneous, the manufacturer will become more willing to adopt an omni-channel strategy. (iii) if customers are neither similar nor very different, the manufacturer uses a brick-and-mortar strategy. This paper also shows results on the issue of channel conflict in terms of market power: (i) the retailer may voluntarily limit its market power and thus, self-created competition in the retail market alleviates the problem of double-markup to some extent. (ii) the manufacturer can use an online channel when inter-competition effect becomes severe.

Key words: Channel Conflict, Channel Management Strategies, Intra-Cannibalization, Inter-Competition, Omni-Channel Model, Pricing Strategies, Supply Chain

## 1. Introduction

The Internet opens a new possibility to firms to increase their market coverage and profits by using their own direct online channel. This channel provides certain advantages including reduced search cost and increased reach. Furthermore, an online channel provides firms a stronger control over pricing and product selection, and enables the manufacturer to target a wider customer group and to actively engage in price discrimination (Ryan et al., 2013). However, adding an online channel also has a number of potential disadvantages, including the introduction of competition, which may lead to lower prices and reduced profits for both channels (Boyaci, 2005). Although there are advantages and disadvantages in using Internet retailing, many companies tend to sell products using both online and offline channels. Recently, various retail channels such as offline stores, Internet websites, and other various mobile devices are integrating into a single channel called an “omni-channel” and the borders between each of the channels are blurring.

Considering recent retailing channel strategies, we can classify companies into four groups according to the method how the company uses its Internet channel: Hybrid channel strategy (or multi-channel strategy), offline concentrated strategy (or passive Internet channel strategy), omni-channel strategy, and online concentrated strategy (pure online retailing strategy).

The first group-namely the “hybrid channel strategy” group includes firms such as IBM, Hewlett-Packard, Pioneer Electronics, Sony, Kodak, Minolta, Panasonic, Cisco, the former Compaq, Mattel, Estee Lauder and Nike. Companies in this group add direct online channels to their existing retail network (Chiang et al., 2003; Hua et al., 2010; Kumar & Ruan, 2006; Tsay and Agrawal, 2004). According to Internet Retailer (2013), manufacturers selling directly to the customer is the fastest growing online retail category, which is becoming a tremendous concern for wholesalers and retailers. In the apparel, publishing and electronic industry, a hybrid channel strategy that uses both offline and online channels is commonly used (Ryan et al., 2013). So, apparel manufacturers such as Polo, DKNY, Liz Claiborne distribute their products through both manufacturer-owned channel (outlet stores and, more recently, online stores) and through major department store chains, such as Macy’s and Kohl’s (Tsay and Agrawal, 2004; Wang and Webster, 2007).

The second group-the “offline concentrated strategy” group-can be classified into two classes according to how the Internet or mobile devices are used. Some companies such as Levi Strauss, the H.E. Butt Grocery Company, Wes & Willy, Colgate-Palmolive, have adopted a passive Internet channel strategy and have used their websites in order to support existing intermediaries and provide information about their brands and products to their customers. Although they are selling their products via their own websites, they are concerned more about selling through traditional offline channels (Lee et al., 2003). Other manufacturers such as JVC, NEC, 3M, Nikon, Cannon, Olympus, Samsung and LG have used the Internet as a mere medium to provide information about their products without selling via their websites (Kumar & Ruan, 2006). Traditionally, auto manufacturers rely on dealer shops for selling their products instead of selling their products on the Internet directly-although there are some exceptions where companies do provide online sales.

The third group of companies–the “omni-channel strategy” group-completely combine various channels into one seamlessly integrated shopping experience (Klosek, 2012; Frazer & Stiehler, 2014; Rigby, 2011). Customers move freely between the offline and online store-PC or mobile, all within a single transaction process in the omni channel (Juaneda-Ayensa et al., 2016). The omni-channel spans over most of the functions involved in the retailers business activities including shipping services, logistics, sales tools, promotional activities and performance measurements, etc. (Bell et al., 2014; Brynjolfsson et al., 2013; Cook,

2014). So, customers able to access services such as “click and collect (placing an order online while collecting the product in the offline store),” “order in-store, deliver home,” “order online, return to store,” “showroom” and other combinations of online and traditional retail activities via the omni channel (Piotrowicz and Cuthbertson, 2014). Examples of this group include Apple, Disney, Virgin Atlantic, Bank of America, Starbucks, REI, Oasis, Chipotle, Argos, Carrefour, Harrods, House of Fraser, Clinique, Lancôme, Made.com, Rebecca Minkoff, Schuh, Waitrose, John Lewis, and so on.

The fourth group of firms that uses an online concentrated strategy includes Dell and other firms in the service industry. These firms, such as in newspapers, media, broadcasting, finance, insurance, tourism, tend to focus on online business by deactivating or reducing their current offline businesses.

However, companies do not always stick to a single constant strategy, but rather maintain flexibility in switching their strategy depending on the market situation or other potentially pertinent factors. For example, Dell was an exemplary firm that adhered to a pure online strategy, but after its profits and market share declined significantly in 2006, it embraced a multi-channel strategy by adding resellers to their channel mix in 2007. In contrast, some companies such as Gateway, Samsung moved from engaging in a multichannel to only selling indirectly to customers (Rodriguez and Aydin, 2015). These examples show that choosing among the different channel strategies can be a complicated issue according to the characteristic of each firm and its respectable industry. What is the root cause under this complexity?

Traditional manufacturers including brick-and-mortar stores still attract loyal customers who are simply reluctant in or having difficulty in purchasing online (Chen et al., 2012). Therefore, companies face a dilemma in which they wish to keep their current offline channels to sell products as well as to create a new online one. This poses a new difficulty in management due to the inevitable competition (hereafter we shall call this intracannibalization effect) between its own products sold through the conventional channel and the electronic channel (Lu and Liu, 2013). Traditional retailers like Barnes and Noble and Toys "R" Us treated online and offline channels as two different business units and did not develop capabilities to create a synergy effect between the two channels (Lightfoot, 2003). Information technology enables these two channels to act as both complements and substitutes to one another. Pauwels et al., (2011) shows that the lower online search costs are especially beneficial for expensive products, suggesting that online searching and offline purchasing can be complementary. In contrast, some customers tend reduce their shopping experiences to offline stores, suggesting their online activities partially substitute for shopping in the physical store. According to Avery et al., (2012) the presence of a retail store has a greater positive impact on the Internet channel than on the catalog channel. Gallino and Moreno (2014) found that online sales decrease while offline sales increase when a buy-online-pickup-in-store (BOPS) project functionality is deployed.

Also, the adoption of a direct online channel can turn a manufacturer (or firms in an downstream supply chain) by giving rise to a negative side effect called channel conflict (or hear after we call this inter-competition effect) (Lee et al., 2003). This may lead the traditional retail partners feel disenfranchised (Yan, 2011; Tsay & Agrawal, 2004). To reduce the inter-competition effect, some manufacturers utilize their direct online channels for solely information-providing and sales-supporting purposes, while leaving the actual sales to the retailer. Some companies such as Levi Strauss & Co., Procter and Gamble (Tide) and Ivory have stopped selling directly to customers (Cohen, 2000; Collett, 1999). In contrast, Procter & Gamble has restarted to sell products directly to US customers online,

## 2.2 The Online Channel Model

Now, suppose that the manufacturer considers selling its products directly online though its vertically-integrated online store. Although both the offline retailer and direct online store sell physically identical products, customers may perceive them differently. Through its online store the manufacturer may provide customers with services that can be digitized, such as product information and consulting, graphic pictures and sounds. However, conventional retailers play a key role in providing diverse transactional and post-sales services that cannot be digitized such as personal inspections, additional expertise, advice, technical support, and easy and prompt replacements or refunds for defective parts. Hence, buying a product from an offline retailer or directly from a manufacturer's online store has its advantages and drawbacks. In other words, buying the product directly from the online store lowers the search and travel cost at the expense of losing the opportunities of a prepurchase evaluation in a showroom of the retail store. Some people feel that online shopping is more convenient than visiting a physical retail store while others do not. For instance, some individuals use the online channel exclusively for gathering information, whereas others both search and purchase online (Mahmood et al., 2004; Waite, 2006).

We will incorporate this difference in attitude towards shopping online by the parameter ??, which measures, in monetary terms, the level of inconvenience customers have to go through when shopping online rather than from the retailer. Hereafter we will call ?? the “online perception parameter”. ?? is relative in the sense that a positive ?? means online shopping is taken as less convenient than buying the product from the retailer. Therefore, ?? can be negative, zero or positive. For example, if someone has full access to an online network, has sufficient knowledge of and experience in online shopping, is not bothered much by waiting for delivery, and derives great utility from getting shopping done with a few key strokes, his ?? will probably be negative. Given two channels for distribution and two types of customers, the manufacturer faces four alternative channel strategies. (i) brick-and-mortar strategy: perform primary businesses offline where customers see, touch and purchase merchandise and only engage in wholesaling by making a contract with an independent retailer for product sales. In our analytical model, a brick-and-mortar strategy is represented by a single retail channel model.

(ii) multi-channel or click-and-mortar strategy: use two or more marketing channels where manufacturers conduct some e-commerce activities and partially utilize their own direct online channels and each channel has a strong and separate strategy. In our analytical model, we analyze two kinds of multi-channel models where the manufacturer uses two pricing strategies for each channel. The first model is a case when customers have strong preference on an offline channel and the other case is when customers have weaker preference on an offline channel and they are not loyal to the offline retailer. (iii) omni-channel strategy: an advanced version of multi-channel in which various channels are integrated so that customers may move freely between the online (PC), mobile devices, brick-and-mortar stores, all within a single transaction process. Although the omni-channel retailing has not been conceptualized we reflect two characteristics in the model in terms of the number of channels and the pricing strategy. In terms of the number of channels, omni-channel is apparently similar to multichannel because two models use multiple channels. However, compared to the multi-channel phase where customers choose an offline or online channel, the omni-channel enables customers to shop online from a desktop or mobile device, via phone, or in a brick-andmortar store. So, the omni-channel tends to involve more channels. However, in the omnichannel, different channels are interchangeably and seamlessly used during the search and purchase process to deliver customers consistent and seamless shopping. The different channels become blurred and the distinctions between physical and online will vanish (Brynjolfsson et al., 2013; Verhoef, et al., 2015). Also, in terms of the pricing strategy, compared to the multi-channel model where each channel is managed separately and has different pricing schemes, the omni-channel model where every channel is interlinked enables customers to buy products at the same price regardless of channel. Considering these differences we analyze a simplified omni-channel model where customers may buy products at the same price regardless of channel. (iv) online channel only strategy: terminate business relations with the retailer and conduct their business activities solely online. In this paper, we did not analyzed this strategy because its implications are discussed in a special case of the omni-channel model.

## 5. Conclusion

As we reviewed previous studies, many researchers have analyzed optimal retailing channel management and pricing strategies. These strategies are very closely intertwined, however, the two research areas have been studied disjointedly. Thus, there is still lack of clarity in the

explanation for optimal channel strategies and pricing strategies when firms add online channels. This is one of the reasons why this paper analyzes retailing channel strategies and pricing strategies while considering the intra-cannibalization effect between existing retail channel and a new direct channel. Also, this paper investigates a retailing channel strategy considering the inter-competition effect between a manufacturer and a retailer in the supply chain, which has rarely been considered in previous studies. Previous studies have focused on an implicit assumption that the offline market size is larger and, thus, they tend to lead to the conclusion that a manufacturer will not use a direct channel as inter-competition effect becomes severe (or as the retailer’s market power increases). This is the second reason why this paper begins by asking why both channels exist at the same time and how a manufacturer (or any retailers) considers two competition effects of intra-cannibalization and inter-competition when it adds a direct online channel. Then, this paper discusses how the inter-competition effect (or the retailer’s market power) and other customer choice factors can be related to the firm’s retailing channel strategies. Last, this study suggests strategic implications on using an omni-channel strategy.

This paper shows that if customers are very heterogeneous with regard to their receptiveness to online shopping (or when there is significant market segmentation between offline and online customers), a manufacturer may use a multi-channel strategy to sell its products. Also, this paper suggests that price differences between both channels should be illuminated by the degree of online convenience; thus, actual online prices include the price itself and reflect the degree of online perception. In general, the higher the market power of the retailer, or the relative market size, the higher the chance of the retailer’s price being greater than the direct online channel. This paper shows that as the degree of intercompetition becomes severe from a manufacturer’s perspective, the manufacturer can use either an online channel or an omni-channel, or the retailer can voluntarily reduce its market power. Also, if all types of customers become homogeneous in their receptiveness to online shopping, the manufacturer will become more willing to adopt an omni-channel strategy. The manufacturer may be free of the risk arising from customer’s behavior factors by integrating its owned stores with a direct online channel.

However, a limitation of this study is that we do not consider the retailer’s choice to cancelling the contract with the manufacturer. For example, the retailer can eliminate the manufacturer's products from its offline showroom depending on the manufacturer's strategy if it perceives the manufacturer's strategy as a threat. Also, the size of the data set for the survey is somewhat limited. Therefore, future study on how inter-channel competition effect and intra-cannibalization effect can differ according to market power and how this affects online retail channel strategies of manufacturers and retailers are necessary.
