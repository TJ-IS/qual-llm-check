# Decision-making in-socio and in-situ: Facilitation in virtual worlds

## a b s t r a c t

Past research suggests that “situatedness”, i.e. the context, as well as the social interaction that occurs within it, play an important role in cognitive processes such as learning and decision-making. Thus far, IT tools have been limited in the level and type of situatedness they facilitate. The advent of virtual worlds has changed this. Virtual worlds provide open, three-dimensional platforms for creating and designing real life-like spaces; they also allow for interaction between users in the form of “avatars” in that space. This research study focuses on virtual worlds as platforms for learning and decision-making. We propose a model that explains how individuals in a group learn and make decisions through a process that is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the two unique characteristics of virtual worlds that enable situatedness: the facilitation for designing real life-like spaces, and the facilitation of rich many-to-many interactions. We draw on theories of situated cognition, social cognition, and <sup>fl</sup>ow to explicate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of these characteristics on the process of learning and decision-making. Data was collected by means of a quasi-experiment in Second Life (SL). Results from this study extend and validate the predictions of situated theories of decision-making within the context of a virtual world environment and suggest guidelines for practitioners who wish to use such environments to support organizational learning and decision-making. Perhaps the most compelling of these is to focus on maximizing the immersion of the individual in the activity by stimulating his or her perceptions of others and activity-related cues in the environment

## 1. introduction

In organizations that rely on information systems to connect and distribute information to employees, the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of supporting individuals’ learning and decision-making with others is exacerbated by the limitations of the technologies used [40,41,44,69]. Technologies differ based on not just the functionalities they offer, but also on interfaces and the level of social presence afforded by them [8,63]. The WWW, while universally accessible, has limited tools for direct user interaction [73]. Similarly, most commercially available group support systems (such as Lotus Notes) too have limited support for direct engagement with others [41]. Those that do, usually have high technological and cost requirements, such as setting up spaces exclusively for synchronous video conferencing, and hence have limited reach and applicability [39]. Technology acceptance research in particular “has not considered group, cultural, or social aspects of decision-making and usage very much” [[5], p. 247]. This shortcoming is a serious one, given that individuals rarely learn or make decisions in isolation in organizations. Rather, it occurs through interaction with others, or in-socio [12,47], and is embedded in the context of the task or activity, i.e. in situ [12,26,47]. There has been a call for a deliberate focus on research on the role of situatedness to better understand how individuals interpret organizational problems, their decisions and solutions, and hence their learning [25]. While situated learning has been studied in face-to-face settings, it has rarely been applied to technologically mediated environments.

Virtual worlds offer a rich, and still evolving, new platform for communication and information dissemination. The application of virtual worlds in collaboration and learning in business, as well as educational contexts, is rapidly increasing [29,45]. Virtual worlds (VWs) are computer-based simulated environments where users, represented “in-world” by avatars, can communicate synchronously over a network [57]. The potential usefulness of VWs to many constituents has not gone unnoticed by practitioners. ICANN's CEO, Paul Twomey, elevated VWs as the future of global commerce [56]. According to the Gartner Research group, “eighty percent of active Internet users will have a ‘second life’ in the virtual world by the end of 2011” [31]. The number of VWs available is growing exponentially [16]. As of September 2009, there were over 200 virtual worlds serving the youth market alone [67]. Club Penguin, for instance, was reported in 2007, just before being acquired by Disney, to have 12 million pintsized members [68]. As these children grow up, immersed in ever more sophisticated virtual environments, they will be as comfortable operating and making decisions in virtual worlds as today's workforce is working in of<sup>fi</sup>ces with email.

Two unique characteristics of virtual worlds set them apart from prior web-based communication technologies: (i) they provide a platform for designing persistent real life-like spaces, and (ii) they allow for informal many-to-many interactions through verbal and non-verbal cues with others who happen to be logged in at the same time in the same virtual space. Given these characteristics of virtual worlds, and a view of decision-making as tied to both the context in which it occurs and the social interactions which enable it, our research question is: “What is the process by which technological characteristics of virtual worlds influence situated learning and decision-making?” By explicitly considering the role of the context and social interaction with others, our study makes a novel contribution in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of information systems where these factors were not generally, or as richly, facilitated by technologies prior to virtual worlds. Our aim is not to differentiate virtual worlds from prior technologies, but rather to arrive at technological characteristics unique to virtual world environments that facilitate situated learning and decision-making.

We use the theories of situated cognition, social cognition, and <sup>fl</sup>ow to explain the process of situated learning and decision-making. Social cognition theory posits that individual cognition is shaped by the interactions between individuals in a group [6,7]; situated cognition theory emphasizes the role of the context in which such interactions occur [11,48,70]; and the theory of <sup>fl</sup>ow describes an individual's mental state when he or she is involved in a cognitively engaging activity [20,21].

Our theoretical contributions include testing these theories, which have been primarily applied in face-to-face settings, in the context of virtual worlds, and exploring two new constructs – social facilitation and context facilitation – to present a research model of decisionmaking. Results from this study contribute to research that ties technological characteristics to the support of organizational processes. In particular, social and context facilitation in virtual worlds combined with an individual's social and context perceptions are studied as factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the process of decision-making. These results are used to prescribe guidelines for practitioners who wish to use virtual world environments for organizational learning and decision-making.

## 2. Theoretical Background

Understanding how individuals learn in organizations is key to making sense of important cognitive processes such as information interpretation and decision-making [25]. Learning and decision-making have been studied through different paradigms. These paradigms encapsulate different philosophies of how learning occurs. A cognitive information processing perspective, for example, assumes that learning occurs only on an individual basis [58], whereas collaborativism believes that learning takes place through sharing knowledge among a group of people [2,3,24,22,43]. Yet another philosophy, objectivism, presumes that learning happens by passing knowledge on from an instructor [49]. Fig. 1 provides a graphical overview of the various philosophies. The <sup>fi</sup>gure also shows that learning philosophies cannot only be distinguished based on their participating or controlling units, but also based on the realism of context they occur in. Cognitive information processing and objectivism, for example, do not assume a real-life environment in that they view learners as individual book readers or as part of a classroom setting. Collaborativism, while accounting for more of the context, still falls short of being “situated,” while socio-culturism accounts for a high degree of contextual realism, but con<sup>fi</sup>nes itself to an individual perspective [49].

This study focuses on what we refer in the following sections to as the “situated” paradigm. The situated paradigm differs from cognitiveinformation processing and objectivism in its focus on the realism of the context [12,49,59]. It differs from the others in that it accounts for the role of others, which constructivism and socio-culturism may not, while still focusing on individual cognition, which collaborativism may not. Most paradigms that assume lower levels of realism have been applied to study decision-making in technologically mediated environments. However, those that assume high levels of contextual realism have thus far not been applicable, and hence not applied to such settings.

The situated paradigm is novel in that it <sup>fi</sup>lls a missing gap in the framework of learning philosophies applied in studying technologically mediated environments. Its fundamental assumption is that an individual's knowledge is strongly tied to the context and emerges from interactions with others. It is in keeping with this epistemology that we draw on the theories of situated cognition, social cognition, and <sup>fl</sup>ow as our underlying theoretical justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for conceptualizing situated learning and decision-making.

Situated cognition theory [11] focuses on cognition as tied to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context where an activity occurs [52,66]. This involves engaging one's mental model based on the interaction with the physical environment, or context, that he is in by drawing on prior mental models to make sense of the environment, and by incorporating new information gained from the environment into existing mental models [23]. Because a VW can simulate attributes of a real world, like a sense of space, it can create a virtual context in which a user is said to be “situated” [55]. This virtual context frames the setting in which a user learns from others as they perform tasks. Users make sense of a virtual context through their experiences in real life. For example, a user, as represented by his or her avatar, would interpret a virtual artifact that looks like a car in a VW as a car, and might decide to obtain one to drive.

Social cognition theory [7] emphasizes the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of social interaction on an individual's cognitive processes. Social interaction exposes individuals to others’ experiences and to alternate points of view, thereby in<sup>fl</sup>uencing their prior mental models [28,53]. The focus of this theory is the social interaction, with the technology serving only as the medium in which the interaction is situated. For example, instead of addressing the research question “does a person make a better decision because of a technology”, a view that accounts for the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of social interaction would address the question of whether the person can make better decisions on the subject matter if working with others when using the technology. Given the property of a VW to be high on sociability through its focus on interaction, social cognition theory can help us to explain how, for example, the perception of others in the VW environment might in<sup>fl</sup>uence a user's learning and decision-making.

The sociability and contextual immersion of virtual worlds are unique in virtual environments and there are examples in practice of these being leveraged for group decision-making. For example, Alpine Executive Center in Second Life provides facilities like expert mediation for group communication as well as recreational activities like virtual skiing and snowboarding to enhance team building. Thus the environment is used as an immersive decision support system (DSS). Theories such as situation and social cognition seem likely to be a better <sup>fi</sup>t for studying such an environment than would theories traditionally used to study technologically mediated environments.

While situated cognition and social cognition theory help us understand factors that shape an individual's cognition, they do not describe the mental state experienced by an individual as he or she is involved in cognitively engaging activities. The theory of <sup>fl</sup>ow [21] describes a state of deep engagement felt by an individual fully involved in such an activity. Factors such as immersion are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as indicative of such a state [1]. Predictions of the theory of <sup>fl</sup>ow have been supported in the use of information systems such as websites [1,13,37,46]. The theory can explain the effects of technologies, including VWs, that are said to be immersive environments and are therefore capable of promoting rich experiences with others and the environment [10,17,72].

While the aforementioned theories of situated and social cognition have been applied in face-to-face settings, to our knowledge no study has used them to study organizational learning and decision-making in technologically mediated environments that provide life-like realism. This is primarily due to the fact that technologically mediated environments have typically caused de-contextualization by removing an individual from real life-like experiences to an environment that is primarily information, rather than experience-driven [27]. Another reason for the limited applicability of such theories is the way in which social interactions are facilitated. Most technologies offer limited support of synchronous many-to-many interactions [33]. Those that do, such as teleconferencing, require pre-arrangement of such interactions. Such limitations pre-empt assumptions of theories, such as situated cognition, which advocates informal interactions with others through happenstance [12]. In a VW, it is through the avatar-to-avatar interaction that users’ experiences are con<sup>fi</sup>gured [10]. Such an interaction is usually synchronous and occurs as avatars chat and share their awareness of others and their movements [38]. In their interaction, users also have at their disposal a wide range of cues in a VW to choose among. These cues are visual, textual, aural, and haptic, and they make a VW different from most other technologies [61]. Along with a 3-D interface, interaction with these cues makes the VW an immersive environment [10].

These unique characteristics of VW are captured by what we term in the following as social and context facilitation. Social facilitation is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as how well a medium supports social interactions. Interaction with others may be through verbal as well as non-verbal cues. In the context of virtual worlds, verbal cues include voice and text communication. Non-verbal cues may include gestures and avatar movements in relation to others. We differentiate social facilitation from social presence of a medium, which is limited to awareness of the presence of another sentient being in the medium [63]. A medium with high social presence may be low on social facilitation, if it does not have tools to facilitate interaction with the others in the medium. Consequently, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne perceived social facilitation as the degree to which individuals are aware of the level of social facilitation of a medium. It is possible that different individuals perceive the same medium, with a certain level of social facilitation, differently.

Context facilitation is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the support that the virtual world environment provides in the form of various cues and instructions in the environment related to the decision-making task. What a person is to do can be a de<sup>fi</sup>nite piece of work that involves others and the elements of the virtual world environment. In a virtual world, instructions can be delivered through multiple formats of media such as text, images, audio, or three-dimensional artifacts. Multiple avatars can access such instructions synchronously. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne perceived context facilitation as the degree to which individuals are aware of the level of context facilitation of a medium.

We study the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of these technological characteristics on an individual's learning and decision-making. Our research model is shown in Fig. 2. We next discuss the relationships between the various constructs in our model.

## 2.1. The Relationship between Technological Facilitation and Individual Perceptions

Per social cognition theory individual cognition is shaped in part by observing others within the context of social interactions [7]. As discussed earlier, observations of others in VWs can occur due to the medium's social facilitation, or the support provided by the virtual world environment for interactions with others based on technological features of the VW. Individuals, via their avatars, can perceive others’ avatars through many cues. Textual channels such as chat and instant messaging, and audio channels such as voice-over-IP, and distance sensitive audio projection,<sup>4</sup> facilitate verbal communication. Cues such as gestures of avatars and “body” language facilitate non-verbal communication. Through social facilitation enabled by virtual worlds, individuals’ can gain an understanding about others, which is a cognitive process that begins with perceiving others actions and behaviors and results in an understanding of others’ in a social sense [9,35]. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne this understanding or comprehension an individual has about others in the same space as social perception. Social perception involves more than a just a perception of others’ presence; it involves understanding others’ in a social sense [9,35]. For example, in a virtual world, an avatar turning and walking away from another avatar signals that he or she is not interested in communicating. Or, seeing an avatar wave and walk towards you could be perceived as a sign for wanting to initiate a dialog. The comprehension that the avatar is not interested in communicating, or wants to initiate a dialog, are examples of social perception. Hence the higher the perceived social facilitation of the technological environment, the better an individual perceives others and their actions, increasing his or her understanding of others. Since this is what we call social perception, we hypothesize that:

Fig. 2. Research Model.

H1. Perceived social facilitation will be positively related to social perception in the virtual world environment.

Technological features that enable interaction with others, besides providing an understanding of others, also facilitate an understanding of where one is. Such an understanding, i.e. an individual's comprehension about where he or she is, is termed as context perception. Having an understanding of one's whereabouts is in part a cognitive process. An individual cognitively processes information that comes from observations to arrive at an understanding of where he or she is. This information could include observations of what others in the environment are doing. Social cognition theory posits that individual cognition is shaped by what is observed of others in the environment [7]. For example, seeing avatars seated in chairs facing an avatar that appears to be addressing the others, may lead an individual to perceive the space to be one used for a lecture or conference. This perception of the space where the avatars are can be made stronger also due to what the avatars are doing, such as if they appear to be writing while being addressed. Similarly, interacting with avatars that are engaged in browsing through virtual products would lead an individual to perceive the space as a virtual store. Since we term such an understanding of where one is as context perception, we hypothesize that:

H2. Perceived social facilitation will be positively related to context perception in the virtual world environment.

According to situated cognition theory, context perception may also be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by virtue of the elements in the environment in which the interaction with others takes place. Based on the theory, individual cognition is shaped by the environmental context within which one is situated [19]. For example, an individual who sees computers, cables, and routers in a room perceives the room as a computer lab. Such perception is also possible in virtual worlds due to their unique characteristic of enabling the design of virtual spaces to emulate real life places [55]. Comprehension of elements in the environment is a cognitive process that is informed by an individual's experiences with objects and activities in the real world that are represented in the virtual world. For example, the person controlling an avatar in a virtual room with virtual computers, cables and routers would perceive it to be a virtual computer lab due to her prior experience with computer labs.

Context facilitation, or the support provided by the virtual world environment to provide information regarding a task, occurs due to technological features that enable different task-related cues to be done in a certain space. In a virtual world, textual, audio, visual, and haptic cues can be placed in the virtual space that provides such cues. For example, a virtual clothes showroom could have posters of clothes available, clothes modeled on virtual mannequins, and “notecards” that provide information about the products. Due to context facilitation, an individual gains an understanding of what is to be done in that space. For example, through various cues that imply that a space is a virtual store, an individual would be led to understand that it is a place where one can shop for the products displayed in the store. Since an understanding of what is to be done in a space is what we call context perception, we hypothesize that:

H3. Context facilitation will be positively related to context perception in the virtual world environment.

Through context facilitation, an individual may have an understanding about what others are doing in that virtual space. Based on situated cognition, the understanding of others, which is a cognitive process, may come from elements in the contextual environment [23]. When it is understood what others are doing and where their actions occur, a more complete awareness of them, in a social sense, can be made. For example, seeing an avatar in a space that looks like a virtual store may imply that the avatar is interested in the products in that store. Or, an avatar in a space with cues that imply there is a virtual class in progress may be a student. Since an understanding of others in the virtual space is what we call social perception, we hypothesize that:

H4. Context facilitation will be positively related to social perception in the virtual world environment.

## 2.2. The Relationship between Individual Perceptions and Focused Immersion

As we noted earlier, social perception has to do with perceptions about others to whom a person relates. Social perception is posited as in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by technological facilitators and can be motivated by a person's desire to understand the causes of others’ behaviors [36,34,50]. With such a motivation, the person becomes concerned about how others relate to him or her, what others are doing in the current environment, and why they do the things that they do. The <sup>fi</sup>rst question focuses on personal factors. The second question focuses on the environment. The last question focuses on understanding each other. As a person sets out to answer these questions, their social perception can lead to a highly cognitive process.

Social cognition theory explains why such a process occurs and what is involved in it [6]. According to the theory, a person assimilates information from their social interactions into their cognitive schemas or mental models [28,53]. It is through this process that a person becomes more mentally absorbed in what he or she is doing [1,20]. We refer to such a state as focused immersion.

Focused immersion is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a state experienced by an individual while doing an activity in which almost all other cognitive demands are ignored. The individual is solely mentally engaged in the activity at hand, also described as front-of-the-mind attention, or cognitive absorption [1,13]. In a state of focused immersion, the individual is said to experience <sup>fl</sup>ow [20,21].

The <sup>fl</sup>ow experience in the state of focused immersion can occur due to social interactions. For example, a group of individuals that meet to solve a problem share what they know regarding the problem through words or actions, engage in behaviors that are related to solving that problem, react to others’ words and actions, and follow social norms while doing so. These activities involve a heightened level of social perception to the extent that people see each other, others’ behaviors, and understand their words and actions. Through these activities, individuals may try to incorporate into what they earlier knew (i.e., their prior mental models), information gained from others. While doing so, they are mentally involved. As this occurs, each person will become more absorbed in what he or she does. This is an instance in which a person experiences <sup>fl</sup>ow [20]. As more and more social perceptions are formed, the person will become more highly immersed in what he or she does through the process of social cognition. This leads us to the following prediction:

H5. Social perception will be positively related to focused immersion in a virtual world environment.

Cognition is also fundamentally situated in the context in which it occurs [19]. This context includes the physical environment [4,54]. By analogy, it should also include environments such as VWs that are in the likeness of the physical environment. That is because the virtual environment is a bounded space in which the users can see and manipulate virtual artifacts [34]. In such a situation, cognition would result from what a person understands about the environment as he or she sets out to know what things are in the virtual environment, and why those things are there. Understanding about the things in a virtual environment is what we describe as context perception. This type of perception is said to allow the external world to “leak into” one's mind [23] thereby in<sup>fl</sup>uencing cognitive processes. Individuals “rely largely upon the persistent physical surroundings themselves to act as a kind of enduring, external data-store: an external ‘memory’ available for sampling as needs dictate” (21, p.68). Data or information from the external environment can act as inputs and stimuli for peoples’ mental models, which are used to represent external reality. In addition to objects that are familiar and map into their prior mental models people may also perceive new information in the environment. The new information is also assimilated into a person's prior mental mode [23]. The processing of old and new information to represent reality causes people to be mentally engaged, or immersed.

This idea is in accord with situated cognition theory [19] as it relates to the notion of cognition as being fundamentally situated in the location it occurs. Thus, situated cognition theory advocates immersion of learners in an environment that closely approximates the context in which the new knowledge will be applied [60]. For example, if a student is in a computer lab inside a VW, she draws on analogous mental models of a computer lab and equipment in a real world lab. Her perception of the computer lab allows her to map similar and new information about the lab with the information she already has in her mental model. The more information there is for her to focus on and perceive, the more cognitively engaged she would be. This state is what we call focused immersion. This leads us to the following prediction:

H6. Context perception will be positively related to focused immersion in the virtual world environment.

## 2.3. The Relationship between Focused Immersion and Learning

Our conceptualization of focused immersion as a cognitively ab sorbing state, in which an individual ignores all other cognitive demands, implies the occurrence of mental processes that cause the individual to be immersed. As discussed earlier, these processes are explained by the theories of social cognition and situated cognition, which result in an individual engaging his mental model related to the subject matter of the activity or task. In doing so, an individual assimilates information from the context and from others engaged in the activity into his existing mental model. The assimilation of new information related to the subject of the activity would cause the individual to re<sup>fi</sup>ne his mental model. When such a change occurs, an individual is said to have learned, as he develops a different understanding of the subject matter. This leads us to the following hypothesis:

H7. Focused immersion will be positively related to learning in a virtual world.

## 2.4. The Relationship between Learning and Decision-Making

Our primary dependent variable is an individual's perceived decisionmaking. By understanding the process by which decision-making can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced through technological facilitation, the goal of this study is to contribute to research and practice on how technological environments can be leveraged for enhancing decision-making support.Theories of decision-making have the mechanisms of information search and learning in common [64]. Hence, learning is key for making sense of important cognitive processes such as decision-making [25].

The object of learning is information that is structured, organized and can be thought as being “meaningful” [62]. The assimilation of new information related to the subject of the activity would cause the individual to refine his or her mental model. Learning outcomes provide evidence that an individual has developed new mental models relating to the subject matter. As per situated cognition theory, the revised mental models incorporate information from the context of the decision-making task. This is predicted to lead to better decision-making [23]. As per social cognition theory, the revised mental models also incorporate information from others involved in the task. Often the process of revision of the mental model, i.e. learning, may cause an individual's mental model to be more similar to that of others. Such shared mental models have also been predicted to be positively related to decision-making [15,24,42]. Individuals use mental models to make sense of problems at hand and make decisions [53]. Assuming that through learning an individual's mental model better represents contextual and social information about the problem, it is possible that the revised mental model will lead to better decision-making. We hence hypothesize that:

H8. Learning will be positively related to decision-making in a virtual world.

## 3. Research Method

The research model was tested through a quasi-experiment conducted within the virtual world Second Life (SL). We chose Second Life because of our decision to focus on non-gaming virtual worlds, which may be used by organizations for employee training and group decision-making activities. Unlike gaming virtual worlds, we were able to control for the setting as well as the task that the subjects were required to do. We chose a lab setting in which subjects had access to the same type of machines, running the same version of SL.

## 3.1. Measurements

Survey items used a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). Measurement items for decisionmaking were adopted from Wixom and Todd [71]. Since our focus was on cognitive aspects, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally those related to mental models, we measured learning through a scale of mental model building adopted from Vandenbosch and Higgins [65]. Focused immersion measures were adopted from Agarwal and Karahanna [1] and social perception measures from Harms and Biocca [35]. Items for context facilitation, context perception as well as social facilitation were developed as part of the pre-studies and mirrored the procedure suggested by Moore and Benbasat [51].

Two pre-studies, including a qualitative and a quantitative pilot, were conducted over an eight-month period prior to actual data collection. The qualitative pre-study comprised open-ended individual and group interviews with eight individuals currently using SL; it was partly conducted in SL and partly in face-to-face settings. Feedback from the qualitative study helped researchers to re<sup>fi</sup>ne themes suggested by the literature and to lay the foundation for a thorough measurement development [51]. The quantitative pre-study was conducted with twenty-eight subjects and consisted of a quasi-experiment, similar in nature to the one chosen for this study, on the topic of computer hardware. The purpose of the quantitative pre-study was twofold: to test and validate the measurement items for each construct and to <sup>fi</sup>ne-tune the experimental procedure. A detailed list of the items can be found in Table 2 in the Appendix.

## 3.2. Sample

The subjects for this study were 148 students who had enrolled in an introductory IS course at a large, public university located in a large city in the south central U.S. Subjects received extra credit for their participation. Their average age was 21 years, almost evenly split between males and females. More than 93% considered themselves very or extremely familiar with computers (μ=6.22); 58% considered themselves familiar or extremely familiar with 3D computer games such as SIMS, World of Warcraft, or PlayStation (μ=4.67); 15% had used SL before the experiment. Data collection was done over a three months period from teams that varied in sizes of 3, 4, and 5 subjects. We obtained a usable sample of 141 data points.

## 3.3. Task

The task chosen for the actual data collection can be characterized as a complex decision-making task, i.e., a task that can have multiple solutions [14]. The task also required group interaction, more specifically, it involved a team working inside a virtual “Telecommunications Lab” to design and build a network typology that conformed to pre-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed rules (see detailed task description in Table 3 of the Appendix). In order to reach one of the possible solutions, subjects had to undergo a series of decisions. The task was chosen so that interactions between participants as well as the context of the task were salient to all involved. In fact, the context was supported, among others, through visual descriptions and objects. Further, the task was designed to match the subject matter expertise of a typical IS undergraduate student.

## 3.4. Procedure

The “Telecommunications Lab” was exclusively built for the purpose of this and related studies on a private island in SL. The lab space comprised of three virtual areas representing: a welcome room, a foyer with virtual exhibits, and a network lab. Also, as part of the laboratory setup, avatars were pre-created to represent subjects.

Once all subjects were logged into SL, all communications were done in-world. Subjects’ avatars were assigned and appeared in the virtual “welcome room.” Subjects were allowed to spend as much time as they needed, typically <sup>fi</sup>fteen to twenty minutes, to interact with others and to get comfortable with in-world behaviors, such as moving around, communicating via chat, and changing their avatars’ appearances. All subjects, without exception, were able to walk and chat within the <sup>fi</sup>rst 10–15 minutes of logging on to SL. Flying was disabled in the telecommunications lab.

After the familiarization phase in the welcome room, subjects’ avatars were led by the administrator's avatar into the foyer of the telecommunications lab. Subjects were then presented with their task in the form of notecards<sup>5</sup> pre-stored in their SL inventory.<sup>6</sup> One notecard contained the actual task description (see Table 3 in Appendix); other notecards contained de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and textual cues regarding different types of network typologies. Additional cues, in the form of text and virtual objects, were in the virtual lab. After the subjects had been allowed a few minutes to interact with one another, to check out the foyer of the lab, and to read the notecards, the administrator's avatar led them to the actual virtual lab. At the center of this virtual room, there was a big virtual table that held the technical components required to solve the task, including <sup>fi</sup>ve virtual computers (one laptop and four desktops), virtual peripherals such as keyboards, and two virtual hub devices that were on the virtual <sup>fl</sup>oor. Adjacent to the table were three “connection switchboards” that allowed subjects, through their avatars, to connect (or disconnect) cables between various devices with the press of a button. This mechanism was implemented to alleviate a user's manual (if virtual) labor. A screenshot of subjects performing the task is shown in Fig. 3.

During task execution, one researcher stayed in the room, in reallife and in-world, but stayed out of view and was therefore not easily seen, if at all, by subjects. The researcher avoided communication with subjects to ensure that he or she had no in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the study. After a group of subjects completed its task, their avatars were led back to the “welcome room” by the researcher's avatar, and they were instructed to quit SL and complete a survey based on their experience with SL. Responses to this survey comprised our dataset for the study.

## 4. Analysis

For analysis purposes, we used structural equation modeling (SEM). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we analyzed our data using PLS Graph, Version 3.0. As with other SEM techniques, PLS differentiates between a measurement and a structural model. While the measurement model analyzes the relationship between latent constructs and their associated indicators in form of internal, convergent and discriminant validity, the structural model, in combination with a sampling technique, estimates the strengths of the relationship among latent constructs in form of path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients and variance explained. We chose PLS over a covariance-based SEM technique due to our exploratory focus of applying traditionally face-to-face theories to a novel context and adding new constructs within this context. PLS has been advocated as a better <sup>fi</sup>t for such exploratory approaches [51], and for research in behavioral and social sciences in general [8], which is the domain of our study.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusion

Despite the increasing reliance on information technologies for communication, there is limited facilitation of factors that have been shown by prior research to be important for learning and decisionmaking – namely social interaction with others, and a consideration of the context. In addressing this problem and opportunity, we study the environment of virtual worlds as platforms for learning and decision-making support. Given the unique characteristics of virtual worlds for facilitating many-to-many interactions and design of immersive real life-like contexts, we proposed and tested a research model that predicts decision-making in the virtual world environment. Our results lead us to draw the following <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

First, the study has shown that technological speci<sup>fi</sup>cities of virtual worlds in form of social and context facilitation play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the formation of decision-making. In de<sup>fi</sup>ning and testing these speci<sup>fi</sup>cities, we emphasize that virtual worlds need to be treated differently than other technological environments. Ontologically, situated theories that take into account social interactions through happenstance, and the role of contextual immersion offer the potential for richer insight than those traditionally used by IS research. By introducing the factors of social facilitation and context facilitation, we contribute to research by providing a way to describe and measure the unique technological affordances of virtual words.

Second, this study shows how situated theories, generally applied to face-to-face settings, predict decision-making in virtual worlds. While these theories do hold in virtual world environments, there is a need to conceptualize and measure new constructs, such as social facilitation and context facilitation, when applying face-to-face theories in a virtual world setting. Similarly, the role of individual social and context perceptions, which may not be as salient in face-to-face settings due to high bandwidth of the medium, is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in this environment. While social perceptions encompass the perceptions an individual has about others to whom she or he relates to, context perception describes an individual's comprehension about where he or she is by virtue of the elements in the environment in which the interaction with others takes place. Social and context perceptions are themselves de<sup>fi</sup>ned through the speci<sup>fi</sup>cities of the medium in which the individual is – in this case virtual worlds.

Third, even though both social and context perceptions demonstrated a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on immersion, the study also found that, in comparison, context perceptions may play a more important role than social perceptions. This is an interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding as it might support the assumption that the strength of virtual worlds lies indeed in stimulating and individual's context perceptions. Applications exist already, such as Facebook, or SkyPe, that have exclusively exploited the socialization aspects. Context, on the other hand, has been explicitly pointed out as a unique affordance of virtual worlds, providing, among other things, the platform for simulations.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings allow us to draw some implications for practice. Organizations that are interested in using virtual world environments for employee training and decision-making activities need to focus on factors identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the model. In particular, such activities should focus on immersion of the employees in the activity. Leveraging their social and contextual perception, by manipulating social and contextual facilitation of the technological environment, can in<sup>fl</sup>uence immersion. Designing spaces in which social interactions are maximized can in<sup>fl</sup>uence social facilitation. Contextual perceptions can be maximized by designing spaces that have task related cues in multiple formats. In doing so, the environment enables a context perception that is in line with the activity that the individual is to be doing in the environment. Spaces that offer low context perception cause an individual to be confused about what he is to be doing, leading to low immersion, and subsequently hampering decision-making.

As with all studies, ours suffers from some limitations. Given the scope of our study, we offer these as avenues for future research. First, the choice of task as a complex decision-making one limits our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other similar tasks. Simple tasks, or those which have a single possible outcome, have not been tested. Second, while the size of the group was not a factor that affected outcomes studied, this model is limited to small group sizes. In organizational settings that may require larger groups to interact for decision-making task, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may not apply. Finally, we restrict our study to focusing on technological characteristics. Other factors, such as individual traits, or task-related factors, may be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictors of individual perceptions.

## Notecard: Task Description

In the next room is a table that has a network connected in a star layout (topology). Your task is to modify the layout based on the following conditions:

1. The modi<sup>fi</sup>ed topology should be fault tolerant, i.e. each node should be able to communication with the other computers even if one connecting cable breaks down.

2. The modi<sup>fi</sup>ed topology should be based on the information provided to you. The new topology can be a combination of two or more of the same or different topologies, i.e., any combinations of star, bus, and ring.

You can modify the existing topology using the buttons on the menu boards to connect or disconnect a cable between two particular nodes. For example, clicking on the button next to “Node 1 – Node 2” will attach a cable between Node 1 and Node 2.

After your group has <sup>fi</sup>nished and agreed upon a new topology that meets condition 1 and 2, please tell {name} what your new topology is as a group.

In networking, the term "physical topology" refers to the layout of connected devices on a network. Components of the network include the nodes (computers), routers (or hub/switch) and cables.

Notecard: Types of Typologies

1. Star: A star network features a central connection point called a hub/switch/ or router. A failure in any star network cable will take down that node's network access. If the hub fails, the entire network fails. There is only one path connecting any two nodes.

2. Ring: In a Ring network, every node has exactly two neighbors for communication purposes. A failure in any cable or device breaks the loop and can take down the entire network. There is only one path connecting any two nodes.

3. Bus: Bus networks use one common cable as a backbone to connect all devices. A failure in the cable brings down the entire network. There is only one path connecting any two nodes.

Norman A. Johnson is an associate professor in the Decision and Information Sciences Department at the Bauer College of Business, University of Houston.
