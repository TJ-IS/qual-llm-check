# Perspectives on challenges facing women in IS: the cognitive gender gap

Margaret F. Reid<sup>1</sup>, Myria W. Allen<sup>2</sup>, Deborah J. Armstrong<sup>3</sup>, and Cynthia K. Riemenschneider<sup>4</sup>

<sup>2</sup>Department of Communication, University of Arkansas, U.S.A.; <sup>3</sup>Management Information Systems Department, Florida State University, U.S.A.; <sup>4</sup>Management Information Systems Department, Hankamer School of Business, Baylor University, U.S.A.

## Abstract

The persistently low number of women in the information systems (IS) field has led to numerous inquiries about barriers women might face to entry and advancement in the profession. Because IS has traditionally been male dominated, masculine values tend to predominate in the profession. In the current study, same sex focus groups of male and female managers discussed challenges women in IS face that their male colleagues might not. By simultaneously analyzing the perceptions of male and female IS managers, we identified areas of overlap and divergence in the concepts as well as in the linkages between the concepts. Although the men and women’s maps shared 10 concepts, none of the linkages between the concepts were the same in the two maps. We thus find that men and women have little cognitive overlap about the challenges that women face. Overall, male participants generally realize that women face a number of challenges associated with female gender role expectations, and more generally challenges they encounter in their workplaces and in the IS field. Their understanding of the challenges, however, appears to be superficial. We call on organizations and the IS profession to devise novel responses and training approaches to promote change in the IS culture.

Keywords: IS; critical theory; women’s challenges in IS; revealed causal mapping

## Introduction

The declining number of women in the information systems (IS) field is well documented (e.g., in the U.S. see Ahuja, 2002; Riemenschneider et al., 2006; Kelan, 2007; for the U.K. and Europe, see Panteli et al., 1999, 2001; Robertson et al., 2001; Goodwin, 2004). U.S. universities report significant drops in female enrollments, leading to a severe shortage of new graduates in the IS workforce (Trauth et al., 2008), a shortage that is shared across the private and public sectors (Allen et al., 2008). In addition, retaining women in the IS workforce presents a significant challenge (Carayon et al., 2001).

Researchers have sought to identify barriers to the retention of female IS employees (e.g., Lemons & Parzinger, 2001; Ahuja, 2002; Riemenschneider et al., 2006; Trauth & Howcroft, 2006). Riemenschneider et al. (2006) studied female IS members and found that managing family responsibilities, stress, job qualities, promotion barriers, discrimination, lack of consistency on the part of management, and work schedule flexibility issues were perceived to be workplace barriers they or a female colleague had faced, which, in turn, influenced their voluntary turnover decisions. They called for further research to validate the relationships between the concepts and to compare men and women’s perceptions of these barriers.

Although space considerations do not allow an explication of their findings here, the current investigation offers an extension of the Riemenschneider et al. (2006) study that provided a review of much of the empirically and interpretively based gender research involving female IS employees. Specifically, this study examines if there are differences in men and women’s perceptions about the challenges women face in the IS workplace. Both the 2006 study and the current research used the same qualitative methodological tool, revealed causal mapping (RCM), to identify respondents’ cognitive structures regarding perceived barriers. In the current study, same sex focus groups of male and female managers discussed challenges women in IS face that their male colleagues might not. By simultaneously analyzing the perceptions of male and female IS managers, we identified areas of overlap and divergence in the concepts as well as in the linkages between the concepts.

Like Panteli et al. (2001), we selected managers since they ‘structure and define occupations and career paths (and how) these might result in gender segregation in computing workplaces’ (p. 6). Managers’ perceptions can consciously and subconsciously shape how they manage their same and opposite sex subordinates and can be associated with the creation or reinforcement of formal power structures that are rooted in what Maier (1997, p. 943) called a ‘myth of meritocracy’, which is the myth of a level playing field for men and women as they seek advancement in their organizations.

But the purpose of this study is not simply to describe; by taking a critical approach, we go beyond description to advocate for change. In our discussion we explore why articulated differences might exist and suggest meaningful changes managers might undertake to address these challenges. According to Bauder & Mauro (2008):

Critical scholarship y [focuses] on the tension between the existing social and material world and the possibility for changing this world. With this focus, critical scholarship realizes its role in society not to blindly reproduce existing social order, but to create the conditions in which progressive change can occur. This focus also means that the separation between scholarship and practice cannot be maintained. Rather, critical scholarship embraces the connection to critical practice. (http://www.praxis-epress.org/ CGR/1-intro.pdf)

Critical approaches encourage researchers not only to discover differences (as interpretivists would do), but also to challenge the profession and practitioners to reflect on the impacts of systematic gender-based treatment discrepancies on the members and the organizations that employ them (Trauth & Howcroft, 2006; Richardson & Robinson, 2007). Given the low and declining numbers of women in IS, investigating these issues is of urgent interest. However, simply increasing the number of women entering the IS field is not likely to resolve these questions if women’s concerns are dismissed, or not taken seriously. Our interest in women in IS is not simply due to an insufficient labor pool, but rather based around the issues of fairness and gender equality (Adam et al., 2004). In the next section we discuss the need for critical research in IS.

Need for critical theory and feminist research in IS Initially, there was some optimism that women in the IS workplace would encounter fewer advancement-related barriers than do women in older occupational fields with more entrenched professional norms or discriminatory practices (Ahuja, 2002). Researchers in the U.S. (e.g., Ahuja, 2002) and in the U.K. (e.g., Panteli et al., 2001), however, have indicated that this initial optimism was unfounded. Perhaps because of IS’s pronounced instrumentalist bent, and the high percentage of men populating the profession since its formative years, a distinctive set of genderbased intellectual as well as social practices have emerged in the profession (Gutek & Bikson, 1985). The same is true for departments or organizational units that are staffed by large numbers of members from the IS profession (Adam & Richardson, 2001). However, given the field’s relative youth, Panteli et al. (2001, p. 4) wrote, ‘this sector allows us to examine the working through of gender issues more clearly y may allow clearer testing of theories about the nature and resilience of gendered divisions, and the construction of gendered organizational cultures’.

In their review of articles published in 10 prominent IS journals over a 10-year period ending in 2002, Adam et al. (2004) found that most of the early gender-focused research was not critical in tone and identified several noteworthy critical and feminist pieces of scholarship. Orlikowski & Baroudi (1991) noted in the early 1990s that the IS field had not sufficiently incorporated multiple research perspectives. They found that 97% of all articles in the three main American IS journals adhered to a positivist epistemology and the remaining ones were interpretive. None were critical studies that expose ‘what are believed to be deep-seated, structural contradictions within social systems’, which could help to ‘transform these alienating and restrictive social conditions’ (p. 6). This led them to suggest ‘that there exist other philosophical assumptions that can inform studies of the relationships between information technology, people, and organizations’ (p. 1).

Reliance on a single dominant approach limits the IS field’s ability to grapple with the complex issues facing the profession, and to adapt to broader societal challenges. Traditional management research grounded in a positivist epistemology tends to reproduce dominant institutions and interests that originate in such an epistemology (Alvesson & Willmott, 2003) whereas ‘(t)he force of feminist theorizing lies in its critical gaze. This gaze can be turned upon the established canon; this is feminism’s critical project’ (Adam & Richardson, 2001, p. 144). Panteli et al. (2001) provide a solid synthesis of some critical arguments raised by liberal and feminist scholars in their discussion of gender-based patterns of employment in IS in the U.K.

The prevalence of critical theory research in IS has increased in the past 10 years (e.g., Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Hirschheim et al., 1995; Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997; Kelcun & Cornford, 2005; Adam et al., 2006; Trauth & Howcroft 2006; Trauth et $a l . _ { \ i }$ , 2008), as has feminist scholarship (see Adam & Richardson, 2001; Adam et $a l . _ { \ l }$ 2004). Indeed, the entire issue of The DATA BASE for Advances in IS in the fall 2001 was devoted to applying critical social theory to enterprise systems. Now almost 20 years after Orlikowski & Baroudi’s (1991) seminal article, although the number is increasing, more studies based on critical theory are still needed.

Interpretivists such as Rousseau (1998, p. 218), in reference to Turner’s work, look at deep structures as ‘ the cognitive schema[s] formed in work settings across roles, over time, and across situations that lead[s] to congruence between self-at work and one’s broader self concept’ (Turner, 1978). Scholars in the critical tradition (e.g., Clegg, 1975) shift the focus by arguing that power arises from structures of domination, which in turn produce the rules of organization. Deep structure rules include shared, unquestioned assumptions that guide our social actions. We hope that by exploring some of the deep structures existing within managers’ cognitions regarding the work-related barriers women face, we can provide an opportunity to question the assumptions on which these structures are based. Many of these deep structures rest on and reinforce notions of patriarchy and gendered role expectations.

## Patriarchy and gendered role expectations

Morgan (2006) in his groundbreaking ‘Images of Organization’ offers the metaphor of psychic prisons to help us understand how ‘organizations are ultimately created and sustained by conscious and unconscious processes, with the notion that people can actually become imprisoned in or confined by the images, ideas y to which these processes give rise’ (p. 207). He goes on to discuss how ‘patriarchy operates as a kind of conceptual prison producing and reproducing organizational structures that give dominance to males and traditional male values’ (p. 218) forcing women to play out their role in ‘male’ terms. We see the psychic prison metaphor played out in formal and informal power structures in IS. Formal organizational power structures often create entry points to informal power networks through which some critical organizational decisions are made and to which women often lack access (Travers & Pemberton, 2000). For example, women’s access to senior management positions, where they might be able to articulate and ultimately address the challenges women face in IS, is still confined to a small cadre of women, thus limiting their ability and perceived legitimacy to make demands for lasting change.

Demands have increased to integrate women as well as other diverse newcomers more effectively into the professional and work culture (e.g., Adam et $a l . ,$ 2006; Trauth & Howcroft, 2006). Diversity brings with it the need to recognize different perspectives and challenges. As the IS professional membership becomes more diverse, ‘homosocial reproduction’ (Kanter, 1977; 1993, p. 63) is not a suitable way of dealing with the management as well as the non-worklife needs of employees (Elliot & Smith, 2004. In the current study we identify some potential tension areas around which the profession and employing organizations should take action.

## Comparing male and female mental models

Elements from both the critical and feminist research traditions informed how we conducted this study in three main ways. First, critical realists see organizations and organizational realities as composed of multiple realities or layers that are not always clearly visible (Archer et $a l . ,$ , 1998). The RCM methodology allows us to identify what would not be clearly visible in standard survey and interview results. Second, feminist frameworks challenge the presence of a ‘unified theory of knowledge’ (Adam & Richardson, 2001, p. 146), arguing that how we know the world or our workplace is conditioned by a multiplicity of experiences and the ‘cultural context and status of knowers’ (Adam & Richardson, 2001, p. 146). To identify and explore the ‘layeredness’ of organizational life and how women and men’s perceptions and experiences differ with regard to these layers, we conducted focus groups with both female and male managers. Third, through the use of focus groups female and male managers were provided with an opportunity to discuss and reflect upon challenges females face that their male colleagues might not. Allen et al. (2006) argued the same sex focus group setting allowed IS employees to discuss issues employees might be reticent to discuss in mixed sex groups. As Bowen & Blackmon (2003, p. 1394) state:

An individual whose demographic attributes, social or personal identity differ from the group norm may not feel able to talk about important aspects of their personal identity. This may initiate an individual spiral of silence, where they feel inhibited in making meaningful contributions to the group and in turn the organization. Small personal silences are thus reinforced and subsequently escalate to inhibit voice on wider organizational issues. y Thus, in this second spiral of silence, employees who initially remain silent about their personal identities may fail to exercise organizational voice even if they have valuable contributions that they could make.

Building on the work of Riemenschneider et al. (2006), we expand the focus to include the perceptions of male IS managers. We seek to identify those challenges men see women facing using all-male focus groups in order to identify how their perceptions might differ from those articulated in all-female focus groups. Also, we explore the complexity of male’s understanding in terms of the concepts they identify and the linkages between the concepts identified. With such issues in mind, we offer the following research question:

RQ Are there differences in men and women’s perceptions about the challenges women face in IS?

## Research design

Whereas in traditional approaches the researcher frames the questions and issues for the research subject, critical researchers assume a different role. Like their interpretivist colleagues, they focus on the sensemaking processes expressed by the informants. However, the critical researcher goes further by examining how societal and organizational values and roles interact or are mirrored in the organizational realities articulated by her informant. Critical approaches explicitly place researchers into the research process, rather than exogenous to it.

Critical research is not wed to one methodology; both qualitative and quantitative approaches can be used to address some of the key questions the researcher wishes to explore (Howcroft & Trauth, 2008). In this study, we examine focus group responses to identify how men and women describe the unique challenges that women face in the IS workplace. We used RCM to create graphic representations of mental models (i.e., mental maps) for each gender. This method is appropriate because this study seeks to understand not only the concepts participants associate with the challenges women face, but also the connections among concepts, which represents the structure and reasoning underlying their mental model.

Informants worked in IS at the headquarters of three companies located in different geographic regions. In each company, the CIO identified the focus group participants and recruited them via email invitation. A convenience sample was deemed acceptable since our goal is depth of understanding around a specific phenomenon and context rather than generalizability (Armstrong, 2005).

Because of their potential importance to employee working lives (i.e., assigning and evaluating job tasks, providing feedback and opportunities, granting absences from work for personal reasons), our informants were 45 IS managers.<sup>1</sup> We conducted six focus groups, one per gender per organization. Eight males and nine females participated from Company A (a transportation company); eight men and five women participated from Company B (a food manufacturer); and eight men and seven women participated from Company C (a beverage distributor). All companies were located in the U.S. (one in a Western state and the other two in the Midwest). Participation was voluntary and participants’ were assured of confidentiality. We chose not to gather additional demographic information due to the potentially sensitive nature of the topics discussed, and in order to increase interviewee trust in the researchers.

Focus group interviews ranged from 45 min to 65 min. Interviews were tape recorded and transcribed verbatim. To reduce gender-based bias or hesitancy on the part of the participants that might occur if the opposite gender were present, male researchers conducted the male focus groups and female researchers conducted the female focus groups. The interview guide was strictly adhered to by each set of researchers (confirmed in the verbatim transcriptions), and all questions were answered. While it is true that no two groups are the same, the method allows us to interview to the point of redundancy and then to aggregate the data for the women and men.

## Data analysis

Mental models are formed in participants’ minds. To understand how the participants think about the challenges women in IS face, we must examine the cognitive representations of the individuals that appear in the statements they make in the focus group sessions. We used RCM, a multi-step process previously described in the literature (e.g., Riemenschneider et al., 2006), to access the mental models and causal reasoning processes of the IS managers. We gathered what participants said were the challenges women face and then cast these concepts into appropriate structural representations.

We systematically examined the interview transcriptions to identify the causal statements. Causal (cause-effect) statements are identified through the use of specific causal keywords (e.g., because, so, if-then) (Narayanan & Fahey, 1990; Armstrong, 2005). For each causal statement, the researchers separated the statement into the ‘effect’ and ‘cause’. For example in a statement such as ‘if you are looking at me differently than you are looking at everybody else, you are not treating me equal’, the phrase ‘if you are looking at me differently than you are looking at everybody else’ would be treated at the cause statement, and ‘you are not treating me equal’ would be considered the effect statement.

Next, the researchers developed a coding scheme by grouping frequently mentioned words together (Narayanan & Fahey, 1990) to create a word or word group to summarize the meaning of the phrase. While the concepts themselves emerged through the phrases captured in the language of the participants, the researchers developed the concept labels (see Appendix). In the example provided, the phrase ‘if I were a women, I’d probably answer differently’, the cause statement was coded as ‘statement of gender’ and the effect statement was coded as ‘behavior modification’. The data were then transformed into causal maps by replacing the phrases with the appropriate concept labels. The researchers initially independently coded the statements and discussed the individual concept labels. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion. This process produced three maps: an aggregate map for the male and female respondents, and two disaggregated maps representing the male and female responses separately.

Once the maps were drawn, measures for the analysis and comparison of the maps were developed. The primary measures used to describe the causal maps are reachability (Knoke & Kuklinski, 1982), centrality, and density (Nadkarni & Narayanan, 2005). The reachability matrix includes both the sum of the direct and indirect effects of one variable on another, and is a measure of the strength of connection between two concepts. For this research we used a 0.3 reachability cutoff because it allowed for a maximum inclusiveness of concepts within the constraint of map readability. Centrality is a measure used for the individual concepts within a map, which measures how central the concept is to the map. It is a ratio of the number of direct linkages involving the concept divided by the total number of linkages in the map (Knoke & Kuklinski, 1982). Density is a map-level measure and captures the interconnectedness of the concepts in the map (Nadkarni & Narayanan, 2005). Density is the ratio of the number of links among concepts to the number of concepts in the map.

## Results

The research question asks: Are there differences in men and women’s perceptions about the challenges women face? First, we briefly discuss the aggregate map structure that provides the reader with a general overview of the dominant themes emerging from the focus group discussions. The second part provides male and female participant maps to highlight the differences in men and women’s cognitions about the challenges they perceive women face in IS. This is followed by a discussion of the findings with regard to the research question, and more broadly with a discussion of the gender blindness existing in many IS workplaces, which has been instrumental in sustaining dominant ideologies often to the detriment of female employees.

## Aggregate map structure

Figure 1 shows the aggregate map that reveals 29 concepts that the participants in the focus groups identified as salient concepts cognitively associated with the challenges women in IS face. Reachability (a measure of the strength of connection between two concepts) is indicated by the number next to the arrow on the map. For example, in Figure 1 the reachability for the relationship between Gender and Work Family Balance is 0.500, which represents the sum of all the paths linking the two concepts.

From Figure 1 we can see that the strongest relationships (reachability all at 0.999) are between Gender and Non-Verbal Feedback; Gender and Cultural Norms; Gender and Gender Expectations; and Actions a Company Can Take and Changes in Norms. The concepts with the highest centrality are Gender (0.216), Work Family Balance (0.162), Family Gender Expectations (0.135), Generic Challenges (0.135), Fear or Emotional Distress (0.108), IS (0.108), and Changes in Norms (0.108). Overlaying the reachability and centrality information, we can see that Gender and Changes in Norms play a key role in the participants mental model of the challenges facing women in IS.

Upon visual inspection of the aggregate map (Figure 1), four clusters of concepts emerge, which we label: Gender and Work Climate, Stress and Burnout, Change, and Work– Family Balance. The Gender and Work Climate cluster contains 10 concepts focused on the connections between gender and the IS working environment. Note that three of the four strongest relationships are contained in this cluster. The Stress and Burnout cluster contains five concepts focused on work-related challenges and stressors. The Change cluster contains four concepts focused on the changing work environment and work culture. The last cluster, Work Family Balance, contains 10 concepts focused on the challenges of managing both a family and career within the IS environment. The next section disaggregates these findings by gender to show how men and women think about the challenges women face in IS.

## Male participant map

Figure 2 is the causal map for the male participants. While the map contains 17 concepts, only seven of these form any type of linkage structure at the 0.3 cutoff level. The remaining links form isolated clusters of two or three concepts. There are 12 linkages in the men’s map, six of which are contained in the seven concept chain. The reachability for all the concepts in the male map was consistent (0.50) except for the linkage between Actions Company Can Take and Changes in Norms (0.999). Illustrating this linkage, one male manager stated,

I think it’s still by and large a male-dominated field and the propensity is to replace like with like. If a male leaves, you think, I want somebody in this position just like the last person. And that probably may carry over to more than just skill set. But, you know, there are advantages now. I mean, we’re all challenged to recruit and promote and hire more women to try to reach that equilibrium. So I think we’re behind here at [Company C] for several reasons, which most of us mentioned. But I think we’re making good efforts in the right direction to try to be more in line with where we should be.

In examining the reachability data for the seven concept chain, we find that men display a general awareness of the challenges women face (e.g., maternity issues, family gender expectations). They recognize that women face work–family balance issues and discrimination related to gender, but do not state specifically how these concepts fit into the larger system or connect to other concepts. The men appear not to ask themselves how or why these challenges exist or what can be done to address them; they just accept that they exist. Many of the issues the men articulated dealt with structural factors. For example, they expressed awareness of gender equity issues as the gender composition of IS positions and workplaces change. ## Female participant map

The female participant map is shown in Figure 3. The contrast to the men’s map is stark. With 25 concepts and 24 linkages the women’s map is much denser. When we examine the reachabilities in the female-only map, Changing Norms link with Performance/Productivity pressures (0.750) and Gender Composition of the Position (0.750); Work Family Balance and experiences of Fear and Emotional Distress (0.753); Emotional Distress and Turnover (0.750); statement of Gender with Nonverbal Communication (0.999); Cultural Norms (0.999); and (generic) Gender Expectations (0.999). The link between Discrimination and a Threats/Hostile Communication Environment was expressed as both a cause and effect (0.500 both directions). This indicates a reciprocal connection in which discrimination creates a hostile environment that causes more discrimination, and so on.

## Analysis

Building on the work of Riemenschneider et al. (2006), in this study we identified 35 challenges, which is significantly more than the six these authors had identified. While many of these challenges have been discussed in other articles (e.g., Adam & Richardson, 2001; Ahuja, 2002), the linkages among the concepts have not. We were especially interested in identifying any differences in how men and women discuss the challenges women face in IS workplaces, which their male counterparts do not. How individuals and groups that are outside the dominant power structure experience and communicate their concerns are important issues to explore (Bowen & Blackmon. 2003).

From the inception of this research project, we assumed that different maps would emerge. We also assumed that our own epistemological and ontological orientations as female scholars would influence what we found, although we attempted to control this by forming a cross-disciplinary research team, utilizing male focus group facilitators, and by talking through coding disagreements. However, we did not involve our focus group participants in evaluating our findings, nor did we add male researchers to our team. While we initially set out to be ‘objective’ researchers studying the barriers women face in IS, once we read the transcripts we realized that as female researchers we had a responsibility to be more critical than descriptive. Previous research (e.g., Ahuja, 2002; Armstrong et al., 2007) exists describing these barriers but few seek to understand some of the more hidden causes or take a critical perspective (for exception see Trauth & Howcroft, 2006). Therefore, as researchers we decided to abandon the hegemonic ideal of ‘objectivity’ in favor of a more critical approach.

Although the men and women’s maps shared 10 concepts, none of the linkages among the concepts were the same in the two maps, which suggested to us that male and female IS managers have very different views of the underlying causes and system of the challenges that women face in IS. Standpoint theory (e.g., Hartsock, 1997; Hallstein, 1999; Adam & Richardson, 2001; Littlejohn & Foss, 2005) indicates that multiple versions of reality exist depending on a group’s location within largely patriarchal and hierarchical social contexts. Overall, our male participants generally realize that women face a number of challenges associated with female gender role expectations (such as mother, wife, or caregiver). They are aware that women also struggle with challenges they encounter in their immediate workplaces and in the IS field in general. However, as would be expected based on standpoint theory, men’s understanding of the challenges appears to be superficial. While they have been socialized in our politically correct society to acknowledge important challenges such as gender equity, or feel the need for behavioral modification, their understanding of such topics is shallow (indicated by the low number of concepts in the male map). Likewise, their understanding of the interconnected nature of the challenges is limited (indicated by the minimal linkages between the mapped concepts).

According to Morgan (2006, p. 218), patriarchy produces and reproduces organizational ‘structures that give dominance to males and traditional males values’. Our current study shows that indeed male managers appear to place a great deal of emphasis on organizational policies (e.g., Actions Company Can Take) and structures (e.g., Gender Composition of the Position, Department, and Organization). Examining the concept maps, about 35% of the concepts the male managers articulated dealt with structural issues at the organizational, departmental, or societal level, compared to about 20% of the female managers’ concepts. While the male managers’ comments are heavily focused around the organization, the female managers’ comments illustrate a working world that is more personal, much less ordered, and less benign. We noted that 40% (10 of 25) of the concepts the female managers mentioned were individual-level concepts, compared to less than 30% (5 of 17) for the men.

## Discussion and conclusions

In their assessment of the gender-focused qualitative research in IS. Adam et al. (2004) concluded that most of the researcher’s recommendations brought about little change and indeed often reinforced both the dichotomy between males and females and negative gender stereotypes. Researchers adopting a critical perspective often recommend interventions to alter problematic conditions (Hotho & Pollard, 2007), such as promoting flexible conceptualizations of career tracks (Quesenberry et al., 2006), aligning HR policies with cultural values (Trauth et al., 2008, p. 18), and encouraging policymakers interested in redressing the gender imbalance in the IS fields to look to the role that work leave, maternity, elder and child care policies might be playing in either enhancing or inhibiting women’s participation (Trauth et al., 2008). In their discussion of the situation facing IS employees in the U.K., Panteli et al. (1999, 2001) contend that careful attention should be given to the development of organizational policies and codes of ethics focused on reducing gender-based barriers given their potential to reify existing patterns of domination. Klawe et al. (2009) remind managers that their own subconscious biases often influence how they differentially interpret the competence and qualifications of men and women and urge them to consider the implications of these biases on their subordinates’ advancement opportunities.

Often where critical studies fall short is by providing only generic recommendations for training. Because mentoring and training center on accepted ways of managing change, these approaches must be changed. If mentoring focuses exclusively on career enhancement, it may not sufficiently deal with the role conflicts that women already experience; in fact it may actually exacerbate them. Therefore, mentors need to be aware of the range and the complexity of the issues females working in IS see as challenges. Likewise, if training focuses on male managers’ ability to better recognize women’s struggles, often that training does not provide them with the tools to understand the origins of these challenges as part of ‘a structural positioning within the masculine power dynamics of the workplace’ (Litwin & Hallstein, 2007, p. 115). Furthermore, the men are not given the necessary tools to resolve conflicts arising from such structures. The outcomes are predictable. Male managers develop a greater sympathy for their colleagues’ plight, but do not feel compelled to understand strategies women must employ to get ahead in a hierarchical work culture (Heim & Murphy, 2001).

An approach to assisting managers and employees in an organization to understand subtle patterns of discrimination and disempowerment might be to hold facilitated discussions in which the participants explore how their cognitions are influenced by and influence the forces (economic, social, and political) that shape the construction of differential power within the IS context. The facilitator for the discussion could be an HR specialist and he/she could share some of the comments that were made in focus groups (similar to the ones conducted here) without revealing any employee identities. As an initial step, the facilitator could encourage an open dialogue between the male and female participants to reveal the different challenges that each group faces. The group could discuss the underlying mechanisms that propagate the challenges and brainstorm on potential avenues to address these mechanisms.

The two maps (male and female) can be used to create scenarios to stimulate discussion about the concept evoked from the focus groups. A facilitator could show the maps to groups of participants and read some supporting text to explain the concepts and linkages. The facilitator would then lead a guided discussion with the participants. In addition, the groups could involve individuals at different levels of the organization. The inclusion of participants at varying levels of an organization can provide insight for the upper-level managers to see situations through a lens of those with less official power. The consideration of the organizational context allows for reinforcement and/or change of the organizational culture. Practitioners may want to take the entire process (from concept elicitation through scenario discussion development) into an organization and develop the maps directly from that specific organizational context.

The research reveals that men and women think very differently about career development issues. Organizations should work with women more effectively by rethinking career development schemes (broadening career opportunities) and building healthier and supportive work environments (Panteli et al., 1999, p. 58). One of the avenues by which IS employees gain power is through increased knowledge (i.e., technical expertise). While not discriminating against men, organizations must realize that women should be provided with learning opportunities (training, education, conferences, and seminars) to increase their expert power (i.e., technical expertise). For example, when a seminar on a new technology is available for a limited number of employees, by ensuring there are equal numbers of men and women attending (if there are enough women in the group to make this possible), women are more likely to ‘have a seat at the table’.

The overarching benefit of this research is that these discussions may allow organizations to shift the focus from how women cope to uncovering the system of gender-based pressures and relations that create the need for women and men to cope (Howcroft & Trauth, 2008). In doing so, IS professionals have an opportunity to rethink the way they organize their work environment and to develop solutions to improve it.

In addition to discussing changes in the workplace, the current study also builds on the increasingly recognized work of critical and feminist scholarship in IS (e.g., Panteli et al.. 2001: Adam et al.. 2004: Trauth & Howcroft 2006). RCM allowed us to identify the complex argumentative chains that emerged from within our focus group participants’ comments and revealed the gaps in cognition between the men and women regarding the challenges women in IS face.

IS, as a profession, appears to be ‘an example of how gender order re-establishes itself in social interactions, even where the institutional frameworks which formerly excluded women are disappearing’ (Kelan, 2007, p. 501). A fundamental question then is why the gender status quo has remained largely unchanged (Trauth & Howcroft, 2006; Kelan, 2007). Surveying men and women’s mental models (concepts and linkages) will not in itself empower female IS workers. but such information assists researchers in ‘trying to understand the nature of relationships between men and women through an examination of the fundamental structures of the way we organize ourselves’ (Adam 2001, p. 255). Eliciting the mental models of men and women will allow researchers to critically analyze and expose the challenges women face as IS professionals and the reasoning behind the challenges. Awareness of these mental models may ultimately serve to identify and break down the ‘structures of oppression that prevent women from equal participation’ (Howcroft & Trauth, 2008, p. 194) and may reveal some previously unconsidered challenges for both academics and practitioners.

Our female participants do not view challenges facing women in IS in terms of structure (e.g., organization, industry) as their male counterparts do, but rather expressed the situation in terms of individual level and communication issues. Perhaps these female managers assume that structurally based protections are in place or perhaps they believe their challenges will remain regardless of structural changes.

Regardless of the reason, this is a problematic viewpoint for female IS managers to hold. Critical theorists have identified various disempowering actions women take in the workplace including ‘strategized subordination’ (Tracy & Trethewazy, 2005), or ‘silencing themselves’ ‘in terms of dissent, creativity, and deviance y in order to preserve relationships’ in unsupportive organizational settings (Chesler, 2001, p. 341). While both actions may be important for the survival of female managers working in male-dominated organizations, these actions do little to change the working lives of their female subordinates.

Given the subtle nature of many of the challenges women in IS face (as illustrated in our findings) and the declining numbers of women in the field, it is unlikely that the status quo will change without more assistance from male feminists who work alongside their female counterparts. Unified action requires communication, and the maps provided in this study suggest that limited communication is taking place with regard to the challenges facing women in IS positions.
