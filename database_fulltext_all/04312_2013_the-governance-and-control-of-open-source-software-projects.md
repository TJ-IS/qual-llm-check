# The Governance and Control of Open Source Software Projects

Dan y Di Tullio and D. Sand y Stap les

Dany Di Tullio is a business intelligence engineer at Amazon’s European Head Office in Luxembourg, where he leads the business intelligence activities of the Kindle tablet and eInk devices division in the European Union. He received his Ph.D. in management information systems at the Queen’s School of Business, Queen’s University, Kingston, Canada. His research has been presented at the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS) and published in Communications of the Association of Information Systems.

D. Sand y St aples is professor and distinguished faculty fellow of management information systems in the School of Business at Queen’s University, Kingston, Canada. His research interests include distributed working practices at both the team and individual levels, knowledge management, and assessing the effectiveness of information systems and IS practices. Dr. Staples has published articles in various journals, including Organization Science, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Small Group Research, Information & Management, International Journal of Management Reviews, and Information Systems Journal.

Abst ract : A comprehensive set of governance mechanisms and dimensions were investigated to identify combinations of mechanisms that are effectively used together in on-going volunteer-based open source software (OSS) projects. Three configurations were identified: Defined Community, Open Community, and Authoritarian Community. Notably, Defined Community governance had the strongest coordination and project climate and had the most extensive use of outcome, behavior, and clan control mechanisms (controller driven). The controls in the Defined Community governance configuration appear to effectively enable open, coordinated contribution and participation from a wide variety of talented developers (one of the virtues of open source development) while managing the development process and outcomes. The results add to our theoretical understanding of control in different types of information systems projects, as the combination of control modes found in OSS projects is different from those found in previous research for internal or outsourced information systems development projects. This could be due to unique features of OSS projects, such as volunteer participation and the controller being part of the development team. The results provide guidance for practitioners about how to combine 19 identified governance mechanisms into effective project governance that stimulates productive participation.

Key words and phras es : configuration theory, control modes and mechanisms, control theory, coordination, development activity, governance, IS development, open source software projects, OSS, project climate.

The governance and cont rol of an informat ion s yst ems d evelopment (ISD) project is an important contributor to the success of a software project [5, 21, 32]. Governance is needed to establish and manage procedures, policies, and decision rights that motivate and allow project stakeholders to work effectively and efficiently to reach the project goals [32]. Governance is implemented through various mechanisms and control modes. IS project control has been defined as activities (i.e., mechanisms) designed to align individuals’ activities to be consistent with organizational goals, objectives, and a project plan [26, 49]. Several studies have examined governance and control in internally developed and outsource-developed projects [25, 48, 49]. Our study builds on and adds to this work by examining governance in a different type of ISD project—open source software (OSS) projects.

OSS projects have become an important method for developing IS [6, 46]. The number of OSS projects has grown dramatically over the past decade, and research on this phenomenon is building [1, 7]. An OSS project refers to a project that releases its application under a licence that allows for the viewing, modification, and redistribution of the source code [1, 7, 42]. Project members are often volunteers who donate their time and abilities (i.e., community-based development), although the participation in OSS projects by firms and paid employees is growing [18, 50]. In studying OSS project governance, we adopt Markus’s definition: OSS governance is “the means of achieving the direction, control, and coordination of wholly or partially autonomous individuals and organizations on behalf of an OSS development project to which they jointly contribute” [29, p. 152].

OSS governance has three purposes: (1) to encourage collaboration among participants (i.e., solve collective action dilemmas), (2) to coordinate the software development process, and (3) to create an effective developer climate that attracts and motivates developers [29, 41]. The importance of these activities may vary depending on the type of the OSS project. The focus of our research is on community-based OSS projects and the governance and control of these organic/autonomous communities [54]. de L aat [11] defined three types/phases of OSS governance and associated project characteristics: spontaneous governance (phase 1), internal governance (phase 2), and governance toward outside parties (phase 3). Spontaneous government fits with small projects that are self-directing with no explicit and formal coordination or control. With perhaps just one or two participants, the need for many governance and control mechanisms is probably low. The third type of governance concerns projects that become increasingly institutionalized. This can happen, for example, when a nonprofit foundation is set up to protect the project’s interests and/or a project works with private companies. Our focus is on projects of the second type—those with internal governance (i.e., internal within the project team). These are projects that have existed for a period of time and have multiple participants; these projects require coordination and control to achieve desired outcomes [32] but may be different from projects with external institutional involvement since external involvement could add governance aspects of hierarchical firms. Volunteer participation, lack of traditional chains of command, geographical dispersion, and lack of face-to-face interaction can make the governance of phase 2 OSS projects very challenging [32].

There is now a significant body of research focused on the growth and expansion of OSS projects. There is less research on the governance rules and structures of these projects, and more is needed to build our understanding of which governance and control mechanisms work together and in what context [7, 12, 38]. As reviewed below, different governance mechanisms have been identified and examined. Most studies so far have examined a select few mechanisms or dimensions of mechanisms. Within a project, a portfolio of mechanisms would be used [25], and these would interact, potentially complementing each other or acting as substitutes [48]. The effects of individual mechanisms are meaningful when considered collectively rather than individually [34]. Recognizing the potential diversity of governance activities in OSS projects, this study contributes to theory and practice by examining a comprehensive set of governance mechanisms to identify viable configurations and uses control theory to predict and explain why configurations may vary in governance effectiveness. A configuration approach, which recognizes equifinality [16] (i.e., in our context, there may be multiple ways to combine governance mechanisms to achieve adequate coordination, climate, and participation), is suitable to explore OSS governance given its complexity [29], and doing so fits with suggestions that the IS field can benefit from a configurational perspective as it complements process and variance theory approaches [16].

Markus’s [29] review of OSS governance concluded that research was needed to examine if and how governance dimensions combine into a limited number of configurations, and to understand the relationships between configurations and the three purposes of OSS governance. Our paper answers this call for research by addressing the following research questions:

RQ1: In phase 2 OSS projects, do the various dimensions of governance mechanisms occur in distinct configurations? If yes, what are these and why do they exist?

RQ2: What are the relationships between the governance configurations and the three purposes of governance? If there are significant differences, why do they exist?

Control theory is used to predict and interpret why specific governance configurations may exist and be more effective than others. Tiwana and Keil [49] investigated control in internal and outsourced ISD projects and found that the impact of control mechanisms varied in these two contexts. Our work adds understanding about a third ISD project type: OSS projects. Open source ISD projects have differences from internal or outsourced projects in terms of Tiwana and Keil’s [49] controller–controllee characteristics. These differences could trigger different theoretical mechanisms, leading to different effects from control modes.

Our results, based on data collected from 184 active phase 2 OSS projects, did find distinct configurations and that controller-directed controls (outcome, behavior, and clan) are used effectively in the configuration with superior coordination and climate. We also find that governance patterns are different in OSS projects and suggest that this is because of the volunteer nature of participation and the different position of the controller. For example, compared to internal and outsourced ISD projects, OSS projects span different boundaries, the controller–controllee relationship is different, and access to knowledge and information is different. The differences allow us to theorize why the most effective governance configuration in our study uses a combination of outcome, behavior, and clan control that appear to be complementary.

Below, we develop two propositions that guide our empirical examination of patterns of governance and relationships with governance performance. The methodology and results for our empirical study are then presented, followed by a discussion of the results.

## Literature Review and Proposition Development

Previous OSS res earch has id ent ified a variet y of governance mechanis ms . For example, some have observed that OSS communities appear to follow a number of different governance mechanisms such as membership management, rules and institutions, monitoring and sanctions, and reputation [45]. Membership is usually open to anyone who is willing to take part in a project, from translators to software developers, although access privileges vary [17]. One of the most common coordination mechanisms found in OSS projects is self-assignment [8]. This coordination mechanism is to be expected in distributed and largely voluntary communities. However, in order to move up in a project and obtain a responsible position, one must go through a process of vetting and quality control [30]. The effect of assigning responsibility for tasks was examined for maintenance tasks. It was found to be beneficial for corrective maintenance tasks but not for enhancive tasks. Enhancive tasks were found to be desirable for enhancing one’s reputation so volunteers did these tasks without being formally assigned responsibility for them [32]. While community members contribute to a project over time, the quality of their work is typically assessed by the core project group and formal membership may be conferred by a consensus vote by this same group of project members [35]. OSS communities may also follow a set of rules and norms and have varying degrees of hierarchal structure [5]. For example, some OSS communities have voting systems that require only a subset of the community to participate in the decision-making process [45]. Monitoring and sanctions may also be present in open source projects. For instance, there can be strong social pressures, in the form of norms, against noncompliance. Collective sanctions such as flaming, shunning, and expulsion from the project also safeguard communication among project members and help control the behaviors of individual members [40]. Furthermore, reputation is a prime motivator for OSS developers, and the desire to maintain reputation levels may compel developers to submit high-quality work and comply with group norms [30]. Sustained participation also relies on opportunities for identity construction and opportunities to learn [17]. Restricted access to the development team can also be considered as an additional governance mechanism and can enhance coordination within projects [40]. Other types of governance mechanisms addressed by open source scholars include open source licenses [43], the amount of open code [5, 10], and membership rules [54]. Researchers have also investigated the role of structure in OSS communities, such as the relative size of the project administrator team, the number of project members, the size of the bug reporter community, and the established software development processes, requirements traceability, and release procedures [36, 51, 54]. O’Mahony [37] defined the features of communitymanaged governance and identified a variety of governance mechanisms: decisionmaking structure and rights, management of conflict, leadership selection processes, membership structures and rights, how authority and participation is structured, and managing new members and socialization processes.

Schweik and English’s [41] comprehensive study of OSS communities used an institution perspective to examine the management and governance systems of OSS projects. Institutions, in this context, are the social norms and formalized rules that create the means for coordination and control. Mechanisms for rule creation, monitoring, maintenance, and enforcement are part of this. A rule can be at an operational level (day-to-day activities), a collective choice level (who can do what and how operational rules are changed), or at a constitutional level (license details, procedures for changing collective rules). Specific rule categories deal with roles and positions, conduct of activities, conflict resolution, authority determination, information flow, rewards and sanctions, and project scope. Their studies identified that a clearly defined vision for the project, leaders setting clear goals and leading by doing (i.e., planning, project management, hard work), and software utility were associated with successful OSS projects (versus abandoned projects). More formalized governance was also found in successful projects (those that released a product and continued to do so and grow).

As reviewed above, many governance mechanisms exist in OSS projects to potentially enhance efficiency and governance effectiveness. Several authors have proposed that the various mechanisms can be categorized into a small number of dimensions, based on the purpose of the mechanism. de L aat [11] identified six categories of mechanisms for internal governance OSS projects: modularization of software, division of roles (who is responsible and allowed to do various activities), delegation of decision making (centralized versus decentralized/shared), training and indoctrination (procedures and requirements for entry into roles), formalization of tools and procedures to coordinate activities and work, and leadership (autocratic versus democratic processes). Similarly, Markus [29] identified six OSS governance categories (i.e., dimensions) of formal and informal structures and rules: ownership of assets (intellectual property), chartering the project (project goals), community management (rules about membership, roles, changing roles), software development processes (structures and rules about important operational tasks of development, such as requirements elicitation, assignment of people to tasks, processes for managing software changes, release control), conflict resolution and rule creation/changing, and use of information and tools (how information flows and how tools and repositories are used).

OSS governance mechanisms and dimensions have been studied mostly in isolation, with notable exception, which is described below [13]. Therefore, we know little about how the dimensions combine into practical governance configurations in OSS projects [13, 29]. Configurational theory suggests that the governance dimensions are likely interdependent and should combine in practice into a relatively small number of groupings. A configuration consists of a number of specific and distinct attributes, and these attributes are meaningful only when considered collectively rather than individually [34]. Configurational theory is based on years of contingency research that has shown how attributes of environments, technologies, and structures interact to limit the variety of viable organizational forms [31]. It is important to study and identify configurations because it is the combined effect of the mechanisms that influences participation and coordination outcomes and a project’s climate [16]; studying specific mechanisms in isolation misses the complexity and dynamics of OSS projects and governance.

Configurational theory predicts that a distinct number of governance patterns will exist in practice and why just a few configurations typically occur: organizational effectiveness stems from superior combinations of mutually supportive elements called “configurations.” The term “configuration” denotes “any multidimensional constellations of conceptually distinctive characteristics that commonly occur together” [31, p. 1175]. Different configurations can overshadow other possibilities because of three underlying reasons [33]. First, from a population ecology perspective, uncompetitive strategies that are not viable are eliminated by the environment, leaving a limited number of options that are viable in any given environment. Second, organizations tend to move toward configurations of elements that are both mutually reinforcing and harmonious [33]. This could take place either as a result of the organization’s own strategic choice or through normative pressures or mimetic actions. Third, organizations implement change either in small incremental steps or in “quantum leaps,” which means that many configurations are often either avoided altogether or left unexplored. Configurational theory recognizes the limited diversity of configurations in practice [16].

Since OSS governance mechanisms influence the effectiveness and sustainability of OSS communities [12], understanding what governance mechanisms effectively combine together is needed to inform practice and theory [11, 29]. To the best of our knowledge, there is currently only one study that has empirically examined OSS governance configurations. De Noni and colleagues [13] examined eight governance mechanisms of 40 active OSS projects. They found that the eight governance mechanisms collapsed into two dimensions: license agreement and leadership structure. And they found four different governance configurations varying on these two dimensions (radical/reciprocal license agreement versus pragmatic/commercial by open /distributed leadership structure versus closed/concentrated). De Noni et al.’s research confirming that OSS governance is not uniform (i.e., it is multidimensional and is not monolithic) is valuable in that it answers Markus’s [29] call for research in this area; however, there are more than eight mechanisms and two dimensions possible for OSS governance.

Based on examination of their two dimensions, De Noni et al. [13] found empirical evidence suggesting that all four possible governance configurations existed. Broader research, incorporating more governance mechanisms and dimensions, is needed to examine which of the many possible OSS governance configurations exists to effectively coordinate projects and encourage participation. Our study attempts to do this. With more dimensions, the number of possible combinations grows quickly, and configurational theory suggest that organizational and/or external forces would cause the number of configurations that exist in active phase 2 (i.e., internally governed) OSS projects to be limited to a relatively small number. Theory and empirical evidence leads us to propose:

Proposition 1: In active phase 2 OSS projects, OSS governance dimensions will combine into a relatively small number of governance configurations.

While configurational theory predicts that a distinct number of governance patterns will exist, it does not help us predict what configurations would be expected in an OSS project context and why. Control theory, a widely used control framework [22], is adopted in this study to enable prediction. An overview of control theory and its use in ISD and OSS project research is next.

Organizations and teams are made up of individuals who may only partially share the same goals and objectives. Control mechanisms can be put in place by the controller (the principal) to attempt to align the behavior of the controllee (the agent) with the controller’s interests and reduce the opportunistic behavior (undesirable behavior) of a controllee. Control theory suggests that control mechanisms have informational and social requirements that affect how effective the mechanism will be. Researchers have conceptualized four categories of control mechanisms, classified into two types, formal controls and informal controls:

1. Outcome controls (formal control): involve defining specific interim or final outputs and assessing the performance of the controllee with respect to meeting the targets (e.g., target interim or completion dates, budgets).

2. Behavior controls (formal control): involve defining the procedures to accomplish project objectives and performance is assessed according to the controllees following of the prescribed procedures (e.g., development methodologies, rules, and procedures). Outcome controls specify outcome targets, whereas behavior controls focus on the process used to meet the targets.

3. Clan control (informal control): involves mechanisms that create common goals, values, beliefs, and expectations about acceptable behavior (e.g., socialization, rituals and ceremonies, shared ideologies, hiring and training practices).

4. Self-control (informal control): involves the controllee determining his or her own behavior (e.g., self-monitoring and management, empowerment and autonomy, self-set goals, and rewards).

In contrast to the other three modes of controls that are driven by the controller, self-control involves controllee-driven control mechanisms. However, the controller can help establish a work environment to facilitate this mode of control [25, 49].

Consistent with the idea of configurations of mechanisms, research has found that organizations implement a combination or portfolio of the four modes of control mechanisms. The use and effectiveness of the mechanisms varies depending on the project characteristics, the ability to measure outcomes and observe behavior, the knowledge of the controller, and role expectations [39, 49]. For example, Kirsch et al. [26] found that clients of ISD projects used outcome controls when outcome measurability (i.e., project outcomes) was high. If the client had knowledge and understanding of the development process and behaviors were observable, behavior controls were used. However, if the client had a low understanding of the process (and behaviors were observable), clients used clan control. This allowed them to build their knowledge about the processes and understand team activities. If behaviors were not observable (and outcomes not measurable), the clients were left to rely on self-control.

Tiwana and Keil [49] examined control in internal ISD projects and outsourced ISD projects. They found that control in internal projects did enhance performance and that more self-control was utilized in internal projects. More outcome, behavioral and clan control was used in outsourced projects (controller driven). However, these control attempts in outsourced projects did not enhance project performance. In an outsource setting, opportunism is higher so formal controls are perceived to be desirable, but Tiwana and Keil’s results suggest that the necessary informational and social requirements needed to make the controls effective in these types of projects are not easily obtained. In an internal setting, agency hazards are lower as the boundary crossed is simpler (departmental), shared corporate values potentially exist, and observability of behaviors is potentially higher. These conditions appear to make the control mechanisms more effective.

Tiwana [48] examined the effect of clan control on the effectiveness of outcome controls and behavior controls in outsourced IS projects. He found that clan control operates as a substitute for outcome controls and is a complement to behavior control. Clan control appears to provide reliable information that fits with the evaluation information needs of behavior controls, so these two types of controls are mutually beneficial. While evaluative information is also required for outcome controls (to move from attempted to realized control), clan control does not help provide this objective information and can potentially send conflicting signals to the vendor (i.e., outcome controls suggest a lack of trust, whereas clan controls rely on socialization that can build trust), leading to dysfunctional behavior.

Control has also been specifically examined in OSS projects to a limited extent. For example, Basnet and Lane’s [3] survey of core developers found that clan control and self-control were both positively associated with team effectiveness, with clan control having somewhat stronger association. Neither outcome nor behavioral controls were assessed in their study. Gallivan’s [19] analysis of nine published case studies found that the majority of the examples of control mentioned in these cases dealt with informal controls (clan control and self-control). For instance, rules and norms were stated in frequently asked questions and controlling membership privileges, via member voting, appeared to be an effective sanction to enforce norms. Xu et al. [55] found that measures of outcome control, clan control, and self-control all positively predicted task completion performance in their sample of 93 community-based OSS teams. Xu et al. argue that behavioral controls are not present or feasible in OSS projects, but they did not empirically examine this. In contrast, Ikonen et al.’s [24] in-depth case study found that behavior control (e.g., guides and policies) was the strongest control mode in the project they studied, with guides and policies in place to direct behavior. Collaborative development tools used by the project members make behavior control feasible by increasing observability. The other three control modes were also found in their study, with outcome controls being the least used. Stewart and Gosain [47] examined ideology (a mechanism of clan control) and found that it affected effectiveness. Sagers [40] suggested that OSS communities use network governance structures that are based on informal controls and social interactions.

In summary, while some researchers suggest that the volunteer contributions and distributed nature of OSS projects make behavioral controls infeasible [55] and that clan and self-control dominate OSS governance [52], research has found a variety of all four modes of control. Understanding which portfolios of controls may be effective in an OSS context requires consideration about the features of OSS projects. Tiwana and Keil [49] directly compared the use of control in internal and outsourced ISD projects, and defined the characteristics of these projects relevant to control. Table 1 describes these major control characteristics with the addition of OSS project characteristics (the right-most column).

OSS projects are different from internal or outsourced ISD projects in several key aspects. The controller is part of the project team (i.e., the founder and/or project administrator), which means that he or she has high technical knowledge and high ability to know what the desired outcomes of the project are and appropriate methods and behaviors to achieve these. Although typically physically separate from team members, the tools used by OSS projects do provide visibility on behaviors and results [24]. These characteristics make the use of behavioral controls feasible. The volunteer nature of the project members (i.e., the controllees) creates a number of differences compared to the outsourced and internal projects. The boundary spanned to reach, recruit, and retain team members is hard to define and is at the individual level. The project founder has to attract the attention and interest of individual project members in order to obtain resources (volunteer time). The controller has no formal authority over the controllees; controllees can easily withdraw their services (creating resourcing problems for the controller), implying that agency hazards can be characterized as high. Therefore, while the controller has the knowledge required to define outputs and measure if they are achieved, the limited authority over the controllees makes it likely that outcome controls will be sparingly used. Trying to impose outcome controls could result in discouraging participation.

Clan control should be feasible in OSS projects since the parties share common beliefs in the value of open source projects and have freely chosen to become involved in the project (implying the controllees agree with the purpose of the project). Consistent with previous findings that suggest that clan control complements behavior control [48], specific mechanisms that create common goals and shared beliefs about the project would also reinforce the use of behavioral controls since it would help develop understanding about what was needed in the project and ways to achieve the goals.

Self-control in internal projects enhances performance but lowers performance in outsourced projects [49]. Tiwana and Keil [49] suggested that this is because internal projects are more likely to have the social requirements needed to make self-control effective. Providing autonomy to the controllee (IT department) in an internal project can also be effective since it allows the IT department to use their technical expertise to determine how best to complete the project while still having common organizational norms to guide behavior. However, using self-control in outsourced projects creates the potential for undetected opportunism by the vendor. Self-control, where controllees determines their own behavior, fits well with the volunteer nature of OSS participation. In phase 1 OSS projects, self-control may be common since there are likely few participants, so there would be less need for coordination and control within the project. However, for phase 2 (internally governed) OSS projects (the focus of the current study), where more formalized governance has been found [41], increased coordination is necessary due to the growth of the project and membership. ## Methodology

This allowed us to statistically examine the governance dimensions, test for configurations, and test the relationships with governance outcomes.

## Construct Measurement and Development

Governance is hypothesized to serve three purposes: (1) to solve collective action dilemmas, (2) to solve coordination problems, and (3) to create a climate for contributors [29]. The last two constructs were measured via established scales: coordination effectiveness (five items [56]) and climate for project excellence (seven items [15]). A measure of development activity (Version Control System [VCS] Reads/Writes) from each project’s Web site was used as a proxy for assessing collective action since it represents a major outcome of collective activities performed in OSS projects (i.e., project contributions).

Our own review of the literature and exploratory case studies [14] found Markus’s [29] six OSS governance dimensions to be comprehensive and appropriate for the community-based OSS context. Accordingly, as others have done (e.g., [20]), we adopted them as the basis for this study. The Ownership of Assets governance dimension was measured by examining the project license information on a project’s Web site. The other five governance dimensions were measured via structured questions to project administrators or project members (depending on the focus of the question). Since measures for the other five governance dimensions did not exist in the literature, standard procedures for developing reliable and valid measures were followed [27]. Card-sorting techniques, questionnaire pretests, and pilot tests were used to establish and enhance initial validity and reliability. Item pools for each construct were generated from literature reviews and from our understanding of OSS projects, based on case study investigations [14]. Two rounds of card-sorting exercises were conducted to establish initial construct validity. A pretest of the questionnaire was done with a combination of faculty, doctoral students, and OSS practitioners to establish face validity and questionnaire flow. A pilot test with OSS participants (n = 150) was done to determine initial reliability and preliminary factor structure. Modifications were made throughout these procedures to improve the psychometric properties of the questionnaire used in the final study.

For many of the dimensions, several constructs were needed to accurately capture the meaning (see [14] for more details). Chartering the Project dimension had three constructs: goal visibility, goal clarity, and goal origin. Community Management had four constructs: decision making, rule clarity, roles, and role changes. Software Development Processes had nine constructs: software requirements visibility, software requirements clarity, software requirements origin, task assignment, VCS roles, VCS clarity, release manager role, partitioning, and timetables. Conflict Resolution had two constructs: noncompliance norms and collective sanctions. Use of Information and Tools had four constructs: use of information rule clarity, use of information rule enforcement, use of tools rule clarity, and use of tools rule enforcement. Initial reliability analysis of the multi-item constructs found that all but one of the constructs had Cronbach’s alpha values above 0.70, and the one construct falling below the 0.7 target, Information Rule Enforcement, was close enough to proceed (0.65).

## Sample Characteristics

A key informant approach was used for each project, combined with the collection of publicly available project data from the SourceForge Web site (http://sourceforge. net). Some variables, such as project performance and decision-making, could be best assessed by project administrators, whereas others, such as goal clarity and software requirements visibility, were best assessed by project members (to provide unbiased answers), so responses were collected from both project administrators and project members via online questionnaires.

A list of all the projects and contributing individuals was extracted from SourceForge using the University of Notre Dame’s SourceForge research data. We then selected projects that met the following criteria: the project was active<sup>1</sup> (i.e., the projects had at least one mailing list post within the past three months and had not been abandoned); the project had at least six project members (required given the focus on team processes and dynamics), and members included at least one project administrator and one project member. We examined each project Web site to determine if the listed individuals were project members or project administrators. Once this final list was compiled (8,107 project administrators and 26,257 project members working on 2,678 projects), individuals were invited via e-mail to participate in the research. Valid responses were obtained from 627 project administrators and 1083 project members. Since we needed responses from the team perspectives in order to have complete measures for a given project, the project administrators were matched with the project members, resulting in a usable sample of 184 projects (average project size = 27 contributors [ranging from 6 to 423] and average project age of 6.9 years [ranging from 1 to 12 years]).

## Analytical Techniques

Confirmatory factor analysis was performed for each governance dimension to assess how the proposed model of constructs fit the data (using AMOS version 18). This was done to ensure validity of the dimension structure before proceeding to investigate the configurations. This analysis did result in some minor changes: the visibility and clarity constructs in both the Chartering the Project dimension and the Software Development Process were collapsed into one construct (Visibility); the roles and role changes constructs were merged into one construct in the Community Management dimension (Member Roles); the Use of Information and Tools dimension was split into two dimensions—Use of Information and Use of Tools, with one construct for each. See the Appendix for final model fit indices, item loadings, and item wording.

Two constructs, Coordination Effectiveness and Climate for Project Excellence, were measured with data from both the project administrator and project member of each team (the other constructs were measured by responses from only one project member). Since a team-level measure was needed, the $r _ { w g }$ procedure was used to assess the validity of aggregating the values. The $r _ { w g ( j ) }$ values were acceptable (averages of 0.83 and 0.76, respectively), and these construct values were aggregated and used in the analysis.

To determine if the data indicated in the governance dimensions combined into distinct configurations, and if so, how many, the fit as gestalt approach was adopted using cluster analysis. Gestalts define the degree of internal coherence among a set of theoretical attributes. Cluster analysis is a statistical technique for the grouping of entities (in this study, open source projects) into groups such that the variation within groups is similar relative to the variation across groups [2]. Researchers have used cluster analysis techniques to study various phenomena in IS [4, 28], including OSS development [23]. We followed Bensaou and Venkatraman’s [4] recommendations for cluster analysis: (1) scores were standardized, (2) square Euclidean distances were used as a similarity measure, and (3) Ward’s minimum variance method was used for cluster formation. When using Ward’s method, cluster membership is assessed by calculating the total sum of squared deviations from the cluster mean. The fusion criterion is to produce the smallest possible increase in the error sum of squares. Once we knew if clusters emerged and had defined them, analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were used to examine the relationships between the clusters and the outcome constructs.

To test Proposition 2, we classified the 19 constructs used to assess the governance dimensions into the four control modes: outcome (formal), behavior (formal), clan (informal), and self (informal). Two people independently coded the mechanisms into control modes. One coder was an author and the second coder was knowledgeable about control theory but blind to the details of this study. Classifying the governance mechanisms into control types was relatively straightforward (i.e., initial interrater agreement was 79 percent, and discussion about coding differences quickly resolved the initial four differences).

## Results

## Analysis of the Configurations

To d et ermine t he number of clust ers , we inspected the dendogram produced by Ward’s method using Euclidean distances and the amalgamation coefficient (numerical value at which various cases merge to form a cluster) [2]. The accuracy of cluster analysis is affected by sample size, so Ward’s hierarchical method as well as the K-means algorithm was adopted to achieve higher accuracy with small sample sizes.

Analysis of the agglomeration coefficients indicates that a three-cluster solution is optimal as subsequent clustering adds less to distinguishing between cases. We also used the K-means algorithm to generate four-, and five-cluster solutions to test the robustness of the three-factor solution. K-means clustering partitions the observations into a number of predefined clusters. This allowed us to ensure that the constructs discriminatory power was greatest for the three-factor solution that initially emerged. The results from the three-factor solution are provided in Table 2.

The means for each governance construct were categorized as high, medium, or low to help understand the relative meaning of the three clusters (see Table 3). Two different categorization schemes were used to try to discriminate between the three clusters, and we found that half a standard deviation was more effective in discriminating between clusters than one standard deviation. We interpreted the clusters and gave them relevant names.

Analysis indicates that projects that are defined as an Open Community (n = 98) have bottom-up community goals that are mostly suggested by project members. Decision making is also largely in the hands of project members, as opposed to project administrators. In addition, software development processes are undefined, with explicit software requirements and vague processes for managing software changes, among other things. In the event of conflict, there are few norms with weak compli ance mechanisms, and when there are norms, they are rarely enforced. However, how project information and technical tools should be used and managed is clear, and these rules are enforced within the project.

A Defined Community (n = 28) project is characterized by a somewhat more managed overall process. While these projects still largely have bottom-up goals (Community Goals) and decentralized management, their software development process is managed, instead of undefined, meaning that software releases are supervised by release managers. In addition, conflict is addressed and attended to when required. The use of information in addition to the use of technical tools is clear and enforced.

An Authoritarian Community (n = 58) is characterized by project goals that are generally proposed by both project members and project administrators. This is the only type of project with centralized management, suggesting that decision making and determining roles are carried out more by project administrators than project members (hence, the more authoritarian role of the administrators). The software development process is somewhat defined but conflict within the project is unmanaged. The rules regarding the use of information and technical tools were unclear and not enforced.

## Limitations

When considering the results, limitations regarding the type of project studied should be kept in mind. We deliberately restricted our sample to projects hosted on SourceForge that had at least six members and that were active within the past several months (i.e., active, phase 2 projects). This possibly limits the ability to generalize our results to small projects (i.e., one or two members), inactive projects (e.g., projects that were abandoned or could be considered experiments or failures), or OSS projects that are being done jointly with firm sponsorship or even entirely within a firm [50]; such as projects where a volunteer community works with firms and their employees could require different governance variations to handle the firm–community relationship [9, 44]. Small projects, of which there are many on SourceForge, may have fewer coordination issues and may even have a monolithic governance pattern (little variation) [41].

## Discussion of the Findings

This st ud y is t he first , t o our k nowled ge, that has examined a comprehensive set of governance activities and dimensions. We did so studying active OSS projects that fit de L aat’s [11] phase 2 definition. A limited number of patterns (three) in the variety of the dimensions were found in practice (supporting Proposition 1), indicating that there are different ways to structure governance in these projects. While all the projects studied were successful in terms of being active (i.e., not abandoned) and having substantial development activity, they did vary on the levels of coordination and the project climate.

The Defined Community configuration had the most positive climate and most effective coordination, and the Authoritarian Community was the lowest on these variables. Examining the control mechanisms, we found that the Defined Community had high scores on two of the three outcome controls, five out of six behavior controls, four out of four clan controls, and a lower score (than the other two configurations) in the one self-control mechanism that had statistically significant differences. In the majority of cases, the configuration with the lowest scores was the Authoritarian Community configuration, except for self-control. The Open Community configuration was typically between the other two. These patterns suggest a positive relationship between outcome, behavior, and clan control and governance outcomes. While our research approach does not allow us to determine causality, the results suggest that OSS projects with controls that provide clarity on requirements and the processes to reach the requirements, which help establish and maintain norms and also allow participation in setting goals and making decisions, create a good climate and effective coordination. Why this may be is discussed next by reviewing control mechanisms in each of the four control modes and how these could affect behavior in projects for each configuration.

Outcome controls, such as having well-defined, visible project requirements and defined project timing (although timing was relatively loosely defined for all the configurations) would potentially contribute to better coordination and fewer duplicated activities. We see this pattern in the Defined Community (i.e., significantly higher than the other two configurations on both of these constructs). Goal visibility, which is part of the Chartering the Project dimension, also provides potential outcomes to measure against but, for IS projects, these would be at a more abstract level than specific software requirements. This could be contributing to the lower coordination effectiveness of the Open Community configuration. Outcome controls cannot be effective unless valid outcomes can be defined, measured, and assessed. Having poorly defined goals and software requirements would weaken any attempted outcome controls, which could have contributed to the poorer coordination observed in the Authoritarian Community.

Behavior controls provide guidance on how things should be done (e.g., expectations, procedures, and processes). The Defined Community appears to do this well. It had the highest level of clarity in the version control system and partitioning procedures, in the precision about the use of information and use of technical tools, and the defined use of release managers. The Open Community configuration also had fairly strong behavioral controls with respect to rules about the use of information and tools, and clear rules about project membership. Although the Authoritarian Community configuration tended to have a person(s) in the role of release manager more so than the Open Community configuration, the procedures for using information and tools and for project membership were considerably less well defined. These patterns mirror those found in the governance effectiveness indicators, indicating a positive effect.

The use of several clan control mechanisms differed across configurations. Both the Open Community configuration and the Defined Community configuration had more member participation in setting goals and making decisions than the Authoritarian Community. This could create wider knowledge of and commitment to goals and the project decisions, potentially leading to shared goals and values. For controls to be effective, there must be ways to enforce them, such as through formal authority, sanctions, or other ways to reward compliance. Only the Defined Community exhibited the existence of group norms and sanctions to enforce them. This, combined with the higher participation in goal setting and decisions, likely contributed to the superior coordination and climate observed in this configuration. Effective clan control can also help by reinforcing behavior controls, as found in previous research [48].

Of the two self-control mechanisms that were assessed, there was a significant difference only in how tasks were assigned. In the Open Community and the Authoritarian Community configurations, members had more ability to work on the tasks they chose, as compared to the Defined Community configuration. Therefore, while self-control mechanisms were evident in all three configurations, which is logical considering the volunteer nature of the project teams, it was weakest in the Defined Community. This is possibly because of the higher levels of the formal and clan controls in the Defined Communities. Members in these types of project have more guidance and direction so they do not have to rely on their own self-control as much. In the Defined Community, the clan and formal controls may also help by creating an environment that makes the self-controls and contributions effective [39].

Tiwana and Keil [49] directly compared the use of control in two types of ISD projects: internal projects and outsource projects. We contribute to this conversation by adding a third type of ISD project: OSS projects. Tiwana and Keil [49] found that both internal and outsourced projects used a wide variety of control modes; we also found this in our OSS projects, consistent with previous OSS control research [24, 55]. Controllers in internal projects are typically from business units so their specific ISD knowledge is low, making it hard to specify appropriate behavioral and outcome controls. The controllers’ knowledge in OSS projects is very high as they may be the founder or key contributors to the project. This implies that controllers have strong abilities to specify appropriate behavior controls and outcome controls (formal controls). Consistent with this logic, we observed high use of behavior controls in the most effective OSS configurations, with some use of outcome controls. We theorize that the use of outcome controls in OSS projects is not limited by controller knowledge but is limited by the volunteer nature of the project team. Formal authority is low and controllees’ ability to leave the team is high (an agency hazard), so controllers cannot impose timelines or requirements. However, timelines, requirements, and goals that have been agreed to by team members can be used as outcome controls since participation by team members in setting these creates awareness and commitment.

Clan control was used in both the internal and outsourced projects that Tiwana and Keil [49] examined; however, clan control did not influence performance in either context. They suggest that this is because it is difficult to develop the social requirements needed to make this type of control effective. Social requirements consist of having shared norms, values, beliefs, and preferences as well as the ability of the controller to both detect noncompliance and impose sanctions. The social conditions necessary for effective clan control would exist in OSS projects. The controllers (project administrators) would be active participants in project meetings and be involved in regular interactions with controllees (contributing to shared norms and expectations). Since the controllees choose to become involved with the project and can readily withdraw, the purpose and approach of the project that they are contributing to is likely to be consistent with their own values and needs. Shared beliefs about open source ideology would also help establish clan control [47]. Given their high levels of knowledge and involvement, OSS controllers do have the potential ability to both detect and deal with noncompliance. We did find evidence of noncompliance mechanisms (i.e., sanctions) for norms and practices, and the Defined Community configuration used these more than the other configurations. We suggest that these features of OSS projects make it relatively easy to supplement formal controls with clan controls and that these controls can be effective.

Self-control was observed in all the OSS configurations, which is logical since members have autonomy because of the volunteer nature of membership (in the type of OSS project we studied). To encourage participation in a given project, the controller needs to provide some freedom of choice in terms of roles and assignments. Self-control was slightly higher in the Authoritarian Community. It could be that more autonomy is necessary to encourage participation in these communities, and providing this balances the stronger top-down goal setting and decision making with looser community management. It is possible that combining more controlleedriven controls with the existing ones in the Authoritarian Community could create a configuration that would lead to low participation and project abandonment (i.e., an infeasible configuration for active projects); future research is needed to examine this and other configuration possibilities.

Overall, it appears that unlike internal and outsourced ISD projects, the unique features of OSS project make it possible to have effective behavior, clan, and self-control mechanisms. Outcome controls are more limited to a focus on project scope and fairly loose timing goals (financial outcome not being relevant). The use of a portfolio of controls in OSS also appears to differ from how different types of controls interact in other contexts. In outsourced ISD projects, Tiwana [48] found that clan controls complemented behavior control (helped effectiveness) and acted as a substitute for outcome control (hurt effectiveness). In our OSS communities, higher levels of clan, behavior, and outcome controls were found in the most coordinated configuration, implying that all are complementary. Again, this could be because of the unique characteristics of OSS. There are no contracts in place or financial incentives to provide objective outcome measures. Developing outcome measures around requirements and timing is challenging. If project members do not know what the target outcomes are or agree with them, the controls are unlikely to be useful, and could result in participant withdrawal. In OSS, clan controls that build awareness and agreement with goals, decisions, and requirements not only develop shared values and beliefs but also develop information that enable the outcome controls. Given the voluntary nature of participation in OSS and the relatively weak outcome controls (e.g., agreed-on outcomes versus outcomes set by the controller), clan controls used to generate shared beliefs, values, and trust are unlikely to result in sending mixed signals to controllees (reducing the potential for clan controls to hinder outcome controls).

Our results suggest that combining informal with formal controls in OSS projects creates effective configurations; however, future research is needed to verify this by empirically testing interaction effects among different controls. While our results allow us to theorize about how OSS controls contrast to those found in other types of ISD projects, future research is needed to directly compare control in these types of projects using comparable measures.

Future research is also needed to examine governance configurations and control in different types of OSS projects. As described in our Limitations section, the ability to generalize our results to smaller OSS projects (i.e., phase 1 projects), firm-sponsored OSS projects (i.e., phase 3 projects), or less successful projects (e.g., abandoned projects) is unknown. We would not expect the governance configurations found in our study to be appropriate for all other types of projects [41, 53]. Exploring what governance configurations fit with different project types (both successful and unsuccessful) would be useful for additional theorizing about the use of controls in OSS projects. How governance and control evolves through the life of an OSS project would also be worthwhile research for longitudinal case studies. The need to balance controls and dynamically adjust configurations (control type, degree, and style) has recently been identified in an offshore context [22]. Applying these concepts to the study of OSS project governance has high potential.

We found control theory to be a powerful lens to explain the OSS governance configurations. Operationalizing the various dimensions of OSS governance in a comprehensive framework builds on previous research [13, 29, 38, 41] and provides a foundation for future OSS research. Establishing that the dimensions occur in a distinct number of configurations in active OSS projects is a useful contribution to the OSS governance body of knowledge since it provides knowledge about which mechanisms fit well together and that there is more than one way to govern active OSS projects [29]. Valuable future research could also theorize and empirically examine the influence of individual dimensions on governance and project performance.

Our findings can also inform practice. Our results suggest that there are three ways to combine governance mechanisms that result in solid participation; however, open source projects that use a Defined Community approach to governance will have a better climate for project excellence and have more effective coordination. The combination of a relatively tightly managed software development process with a decentralized community management structure creates a balance between freedom and control that allows projects to benefit from the open contribution and participation of a wide variety of talented developers while avoiding the pitfalls of an uncontrolled and scattered development process. Project controllers do need to have rules and procedures in place to guide contributors. They need to do this while involving project members in decision making and determining the project goals and requirements. Using appropriate controls can help achieve this balance. The list of control mechanisms used in our study should provide guidance regarding things project administrators and team members should consider implementing. Understanding how to effectively govern OSS projects may also inform other types of open source activities that also have to balance coordination and control with volunteer participation. The use of open source approaches to create communities for innovation is growing rapidly beyond software projects [13].

## Conclusion

In t his st ud y, t hree governance configurat ions were id ent ified and were found to have varying degrees of effectiveness in terms of coordination and project climate. The most effective configuration, which we labeled the Defined Community, appears to be successful due to a balance between freedom and control, as evidenced by the presence of participative decision making and the existence of a well laid-out and specific software development process. Different types of controls can be used to create this balance, and it appears in OSS environments that controller-directed controls are complementary. The results of this study add to what we know about governance and control in ISD projects, suggest several avenues for future research, and potentially inform other innovation practices that use open source approaches.
