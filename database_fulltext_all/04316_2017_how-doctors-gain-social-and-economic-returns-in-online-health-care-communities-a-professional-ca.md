# How Doctors Gain Social and Economic Returns in Online Health-Care Communities: A Professional Capital Perspective

SHANSHAN GUO, XITONG GUO, YULIN FANG, AND DOUG VOGEL

ABSTRACT: An online health-care community (OHC) is a novel channel through which doctors share medical or health-care knowledge with patients. While the sustainable development of an OHC relies on doctors’ participation, we have limited information on how doctors can gain benefits from OHCs. In attempting to close this knowledge gap, we examine the determinants of social and economic returns of doctors at OHCs by extending the social exchange theory into the professional domain. The notion of professional capital, as a set of renewable resources for social professionals developed by good education within a territory of social practice, is introduced to understand the unique resources available to doctors for social exchange. Specifically, we examine the effects of status capital and decisional capital (two dimensions of professional capital) on doctors’ social and economic returns. Moreover, we explore how such effects differ across different doctor groups. The results show that, in addition to the widespread pursuit of celebrity doctors (who can be recognized by their high status capital) offline, the doctor’s decisional capital is also an important professional component in maintaining exchange returns at OHCs. This study provides empirical evidence of the relationship between professional capital and the exchange returns, and extends prior research on OHCs through a professional capital perspective with implications for theory and practice.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: online communities, online health-care community, professional capital, social exchange theory.

Increasing medical demands arising from a dearth of health resources have become a growing global issue, with information and communication technologies (ICTs) regarded as important solutions for this problem. An online health-care community (OHC) as an Internet-based virtual community platform enabling health-care-related stakeholders (e.g., doctors, medical staff, or patients) to communicate and share medical information is increasingly affecting people’s lives through the accessible provision of information and communication channels (for details, see Online Appendix A). Several studies have confirmed the significant potential of the Internet generally, and in OHCs particularly, for improving people’s lives [2, 28, 35, 58, 75]; however, most studies have been conducted from the perspective of patients. While it is imperative to understand how patients benefit from OHCs, an OHC will be viable only when participating doctors also gain returns from OHCs. Unfortunately, research focusing on explaining the returns of doctors who willingly participate in OHCs is limited. Our research attempts to fill this gap.

The two notable returns of participating doctors in OHCs are social returns and economic returns. For doctors, OHCs enable a more efficient system of fame and reputation than traditional offline medical channels [26], and include public thankyou letters, online votes from patients (Likes), and virtual gifts. Patients can offer these incentives to doctors conveniently and easily in OHCs, and online reputations can be broadly recognized by patients through the Internet [40]. Therefore, an OHC offers a platform for doctors to improve their expressive benefits (e.g., esteem, being valued by another) and address their social needs, and hence their reference as social returns [23]. Social returns are especially important to social professionals such as doctors because: (1) social returns represent the recognition of their social work, which in turn projects the message that the doctors are treated with dignity; and (2) social returns as a form of visualized reputation (e.g., virtual gifts) contribute to attracting more patients by projecting a message about the doctor’s competence [15]. In particular, regarding professional services (medical treatment) related to life, the reputations of doctors have significant effects on patients’ trust [76, 79].

Besides social returns, in China, OHCs also provide patients’ economic returns for doctors online. These online economic benefits mainly include not only doctors virtual gifts and bonuses but also the benefits obtained from providing telephone consultations. Economic returns enhance doctors’ financial needs, which are treated as the basic and direct returns of practical transactions and exchange activities. Considering the practical importance of social and economic returns for doctors in OHCs,<sup>1</sup> we aim to address this research question: What factors determine the social and economic returns of participating doctors in an OHC?

Although it is possible to approach this question from a purely economic exchange perspective by understanding the doctor–patient interaction as a business service delivery process, prior research posits that the doctor–patient interaction is not a simple business process, but an archetypal professional service [74] delivered through a special social exchange involving health-care professionals [48]. In the context of health-care services, patients lack knowledge and expertise in both diagnostic skills and treatment options, whereas the health-care provider is the specialist who possesses these relevant technical skills and knowledge, and these are treated as valuable exchange resources in doctor–patient interactions. Given the asymmetry of health-care knowledge between the two parties, health-care systems tend to require a series of interactions that generate feelings of personal obligation, gratitude, and trust [7] that extend beyond mere economic exchange.

However, while ample research has focused on understanding doctor–patient interactions from the perspectives of communication efficiency [1], service delivery [76], and information asymmetry [61, 63], research on the interaction process from a social exchange perspective remains lacking. Thus, this study aims at addressing our research question by drawing on the social exchange theory and extending it to the domain of professional service.

More specifically, we conceptualize the process of gaining social and economic returns as a social exchange process by drawing on the theory of social exchange. The theory suggests that human actions in relationships are motivated by the exchange of resources embedded in the interaction process [7, 14]. Based on this theory, we explain the social and economic returns of doctors in OHCs by identifying the social exchange resources of the doctors through a distinctive and understudied theoretical lens: that of professional capital. Professional capital refers to a set of renewable resources developed by good education within a territory of social practice, and belongs to social professionals (e.g., doctors, teachers, and lawyers, etc.) [9, 30, 56].

The concept of professional capital provides a critical point of departure for our exploration on the social exchange resources of this specific profession. Created by inequalities in power (unbalanced dependencies give doctors an advantage in their relationships with patients), we posit that status capital, as one dimension of professional capital, can attract social and economic returns from patients for further interactions. Meanwhile, based on a viewpoint from a series of interactions, decisional capital, as the other dimension of professional capital, reflects not only the capability of doctors to make sound judgments but also their commitment (e.g., how enthusiastic and responsible they are) to social professionals.

In extending our model even further, we investigate how social and economic returns are differentially obtained by doctors with different levels of professional capital, based on prior strong evidence supporting the existence of heterogeneity in the social exchange process [15, 22]. For instance, doctors with different statuses (e.g., chief doctor vs. resident doctor) may have different social exchange trajectories from the influence of initial structural advantages.

## Theoretical Background

## Social Exchanges Between Doctors and Patients

We assert that doctors’ participation in OHCs is social exchange behavior dominated by the social exchange theory [15, 20] rather than being purely an economic exchange. The social exchange theory advocates the analysis of noneconomic social behavior using an economic approach [47], and has been widely applied in understanding the dynamic exchange processes in social relationships [46]. Based on a systematic review of the literature on the social exchange theory [15, 20, 47], two important characteristics of a social exchange have been identified: (1) structural power (i.e., the power of dependent relationships between actors in a social exchange); and (2) dynamic interactions (i.e., rounds of exchange actions) [11].

Molm [47] purported that a structural power of exchange networks is differentiated from social exchanges of other types of exchanges such as bargaining transactions. Structural power is the potential power created by relationships of dependency among actors in exchange networks [47]. The dependence of A on B indicates the priority of one party to control exchange relationships and to access the values of those alternative relationships. In particular, the imbalance of dependent relationships creates structural power that facilitates social exchange behaviors [50]. Regarding medical treatment, doctors who are relatively fewer compared with the large number of patients in need, and regarded as experts (having authority) are highly depended on and respected by patients, and sometimes even regarded as possessing life-critical skills. For this reason, doctors have significant structural power in the exchange network between doctors and patients.

In addition, with regard to the social exchange theory, dynamic interaction is usually seen as interdependent and contingent on the actions of another person [7]. Cropanzano and Mitchell [15] emphasized that a series of interactions that evolve over time into perceived trust and obligation are the agreed basics of social exchange theories. Similarly, medical treatment behaviors between doctors and patients regularly occur through a series of interactions, including, for example, testing, interrogation, and diagnosis. The OHCs we intend to investigate provide users with various functions for interactions between doctors and patients, such as the sharing of medical articles, online and telephone consultations to support the continuous doctor–patient communications on disease prevention, diagnosis, recurrence, and maintenance. During the interactions, patients can have the autonomy to decide whether to initiate, continue, or stop communicating with their doctors. On their part, doctors can also independently make their decisions on patients. In other words, the behaviors of doctors and patients in OHCs are a series of two-way, dynamic interactions.

Generally, the doctor’s participating behavior in an OHC is a form of social exchange with patients that features structural power and dynamic interactions. Furthermore, a social exchange includes a bidirectional transaction, which translates into “something has to be given and something returned” [15, 48]. As mentioned previously, patients can provide social and economic returns for doctors in OHCs in the form of exchange returns. However, whatever resources doctors do use in social exchanges with patients need further identification. We introduce the notion of professional capital to capture the unique resources that doctors possess for social exchanges.

## Elaborating on Professional Capital and Developing a Professional Capital Exchange Model

As previously defined, professional capital is a special kind of rare, long-lasting, and valuable capital affiliated with social professionals, as it relates to power advantage (in a structural society, doctors are “born” to be relied on) and professional commitment (as a main distinction between normal experts and social professionals, displaying, e.g., enthusiasm and dedication) [3, 24, 52]. In a social structure (dependent relations between a doctor and a patient and the hierarchical system of doctors), resources are considered as a form of potential status power or as the use of resources through decisional behaviors [8]. Accordingly, professional capital can be classified as status capital (in the form of individual and social advantages) and decisional capital (deemed the decision behavior empowered by the ability and willingness to make sound judgments). Decisional capital indicates “what action” in the context of a social exchange, while status capital indicates “who and where.” Professional capital is important to doctors and valuable to patients in the social exchanges between both parties. Under the concepts of social exchanges described by structural power and dynamic interactions, we elaborate the reasons for the two dimensions of professional capital (status capital and decisional capital) as follows.

First, social professionals such as doctors, lawyers, and teachers are involved in “birth, survival, law-based social order, finance, and credit information” [21, p. 135]. The uncertainties in risk societies create many problems such as the crisis of trust in modern life [3, 12]. To eliminate these uncertainties, professionals need to embody structural, occupational, and institutional arrangements where structural power arises [4, 21].<sup>2</sup> Structural power endows the party with more power (such as doctors) with individual and social advantages during social exchange processes (compared to the dependents, e.g., patients).<sup>3</sup> Official certifications (e.g., education, professional titles, related organizations and institutions) assist other actors in evaluating the individual and social advantages of social professionals and in making decisions on social exchanges. Patients may assess doctors by their qualifications such as clinical titles (practical certifications), academic titles (educational certifications), and hospitals of employment (organizational certifications). Accordingly, individual advantages are the personal qualities and attributes that indicate the social exchange actor’s structural power in the network, such as the doctor’s clinical and academic titles. Similarly, a social advantage requires that the social exchange actor be powerful in achieving better social resources through colleagues and organizations. For instance, a doctor working at a hospital of a higher level is deemed to work with better partners and more technically advanced equipment than those who do not do so. Therefore, status catpital, as one dimension of professional capital, is defined as the individual and social advantage of the structural power of social professionals. The acquiring of status capital is a long-term ambition that changes little over time.

Second, as we discussed previously, social exchanges between doctors and patients are embodied in a series of dynamic interactions. In OHCs, patients can assess the value of a doctor by the frequency and distribution (how and how many medical articles are shared, online and telephone consultations) of their interactions, thus reflecting the ability of the doctors and their willingness to work [60]. Ability, which is the doctor’s capability to express competence, judgement, insight, and inspiration in different and complex situations [31], indicates a doctor’s rich, clinical experience in making accurate judgments of their patients’ conditions (How good are you? Can you do the job?) [30]. On the other hand, the doctor’s willingness, reflects his or her attitude to interact with patients in OHCs under unexpected exchange returns, and indicates the doctor’s commitment to social professionals<sup>4</sup> (How enthusiastic, how responsible, and how driven by a moral purpose are you as a professional?) [30]. Thus, decisional capital, as the other dimension of professional capital, is defined as the capability to make sound judgments and the commitment to social professions through a series of interactions [29, 30]. However, the decisional capital of a doctor may not have been directly observed by patients, but through a massive quantity of evidence from the decision results (treatment behaviors in dynamic interactions) made through their capability and commitment. In other words, decisional capital is manifested through dynamic interactions (i.e., behavior reflected in consultations in the case of doctors participating in OHCs) of social professionals, as their interaction behavior is ensured by their capability to act independently and is secured by a code of commitment [3].

In summary, in a social exchange between doctors and patients, the exchange resources of doctors are their professional capital that reflects the doctors’ status in a social structure and also in decision behaviors in a series of dynamic interactions. Accordingly, we propose that a doctor’s participation in an OHC is a social exchange process of professional capital for social and economic returns. ## Hypotheses Development

## The Effects of Professional Capital on Social and Economic Returns

Status capital represents the individual and social advantages in a social structure. Doctors are especially sensitive to their status capital and, importantly, this is recognized by patients, given that over time they may interact with a number of doctors,<sup>5</sup> and make comparisons based on the doctors’ titles and affiliations. Status capital is a kind of structural power that is officially certified [8, 15, 47] and can be exchanged directly without one literally contributing resources [47, 49, 68]. In an institutionalized social structure, doctors with higher status capital are expected to be more reliable and valuable. For instance, a higher clinical title and/or academic title signals to patients that the doctor is officially identified as better (e.g., more expert, more knowledgeable, etc.) and patients may regard doctors’ status (e.g., titles) as the proxy of the qualities of their diagnosis or consultations and are thus willing to pay back more.

Moreover, actors who have a powerful social advantage in a social structure should have more and/or better alternatives that they can use to obtain greater resources. In this regard, actors in a better organization tend to have better control of resources. For instance, an outstanding hospital may be linked to better-qualified doctors, equipment, and even other hospitals. More specifically, studies show that well-developed cities control greater health-care knowledge and resources [27]. In other words, the organization (i.e., hospital) and geographic location (i.e., city) reflect the social advantages of doctors in accessing better resources. For this reason, patients may render positive benefits (social and economic returns) to doctors who offer better social advantages such as higher hospital and city levels.

Accordingly, we purport that status capital, which comprises a doctor’s individual and social advantages of structural power, is expected to result in better economic and social returns.

Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1a: The social and economic returns of doctors in OHCs are positively associated with their status capital.

In contrast to status capital, a doctor’s decisional capital cannot be recognized by patients without the mobilization of dynamic interactions. In other words, compared with the doctor’s capabilities and commitments, what patients would see as a valuable resource is the doctor’s decisional behavior, as evidenced in a series of interactions in an OHC. In fact, decisional capital has been transferred into a series of exchange actions (e.g., sharing of medical articles, online and telephone consultations). The exchange behaviors are used to link doctors’ decisional capital and patients’ established reward systems. For instance, with a greater number of interactions that transpire (e.g., online chatting for consultations), patients will recognize the better capabilities (e.g., in making accurate judgments in complex conditions) of the doctor, and then more exchange returns will be accorded. Similarly, more frequent interactions indicate that the doctor is more enthusiastic and dedicated and hence, more social returns from patients will ensue. Moreover, we assume that there is a positive relationship between social and economic returns and decisional capital through a series of dynamic interactions, which is another proposition in our professional capital exchange model.

Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1b: The social and economic returns of doctors in OHCs are positively associated with their decisional capital.

We propose that status capital and decisional capital complement each other [8, 54, 72]. In other words, the effects of status capital/decisional capital on social/economic returns are positively and mutually moderated. When status capital is exchanged directly without mobilization, the social returns are likely to be limited due to the relationship distance (in relation to patients) because patients cannot establish intimacy with a doctor through a series of interactions [16]. The social exchange with the mobilizing of decisional capital, however, is conducive to a good doctor– patient relationship [1] compared with the involvement of only status capital, because doctor–patient interactions enable closer relationships. Therefore, decisional capital strengthens the effects of status capital on exchange returns. By contrast, with better use of status capital, the effects of decisional capital on social exchange returns are strengthened because a doctor’s certified status capital eliminates the distrust of patients and increases the approval of their judgment behaviors resulting from their use of decisional capital [26]. For example, higher clinical titles reinforce the positive effects of treatment interactions from returns by ensuring the quality of treatment. Hence, we propose the inclusion of an interactive effect between status capital and decisional capital through exchange processes in exchange returns.

Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1c: The social and economic returns of doctors in OHCs are positively associated with the interaction between their status capital and decisional capital.

## Exchange Heterogeneity in the Professional Capital Exchange Process

Flap [22] proposed that exchange processes associated with goal attainment (e.g., gaining social and economic returns) vary among individuals, and thus the social exchange trajectory may not reflect a homogeneous pattern between different professionals [67]. According to the social exchange theory, the concept of individual differences has been proposed to investigate exchange heterogeneity [15, 37, 65]. Individual differences represent the differentiation related to individual actors and/or caused by personalities, for example, initiatives, motivations, objectives, and so on [51, 65]. In our current study, we purport the concept of individual differences to interpret doctors’ heterogeneous patterns in gaining social and economic returns in OHCs.

The two categories of returns (social and economic returns) may be interpreted differentially among doctors. In general, the value of returns is not assessed equally for a doctor who may prefer one form of returns to another during the professional capital exchange process. For instance, motivations and objectives of participation, as a type of a doctor’s individual differences, vary among doctors [41]. A doctor who targets outcomes of more wealth and places less value on reputation will be motivated to exchange specific forms of professional capital, such as a series of interactions through fee-based telephone consultations. Besides differentiating between the categories of returns, the doctors may also factor in the level of returns based on the different levels of their exchange ideology [77]. A doctor with a greater sense of exchange ideology is more calculating, and thus the doctor–patient exchange with more returns and/or less professional capital costs will be regarded as a priority. Based on the research of Deighton and Grayson [16] regarding the social and economic values of exchange, two combinations of exchange returns, such as high economic returns and low social returns versus high social returns and low economic returns, are exchanged in two distinct procesess. Moreover, significant exchange heterogeneity, based on different combinations (by categories and levels) of social and economic returns, exist in doctors’ professional capital exchange processes.

Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2: The professional capital exchange processes of gaining different combinations of social and economic returns exhibit significant exchange heterogeneity.

Furthermore, we extend H2 by developing the following more specific hypotheses correspoinding to each dimension of professional capital and their interactions, according to H1a–c; in other words, we hypothesize that the doctor’s professional capital and the interactions between the two dimensions (status capital and decisional capital) affect doctors’ social and economic returns differentially. Specifically, when exchange heterogeneity exists in the professional capital exchange process, we argue that the three proposed relations between exchange resources and returns are heterogeneous among the distinct groups of doctors who obtain different combinations of social and economic returns. The heterogeneity of relations between exchange resources and returns can be explained by doctors’ different appreciations (or assessments) of social and economic returns, and/or the available options of alternative (or comparative) returns [16]. For instance, doctors with the same high status capital may acquire different combinations of social and economic returns because patients are willing to pay for more economic returns to a doctor who appreciates wealth, while more social returns will be granted to a doctor who appreciates reputation. Moreover, considering the different levels in the appreciation of returns, doctors who begin with a significant level of status capital may have the advantage of better alternative options, and thus obtain exchange returns at a faster rate, compared to those with a lower level of status capital. In addition, a highly proactive doctor is likely to have more decisional capital (with more interactions), which may strengthen the relationship between the doctor’s status capital and exchange returns [37]. Thus, to extend the effects of exchange heterogeneity in our research model, we classify H2 as three subhypotheses, corresponding to each assumed relationship.

## Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2a: The associations between social/economic returns and status capital are heterogeneous among different groups of doctors.

Hypothesis 2b: The associations between social/economic returns and decisional capital are heterogeneous among different groups of doctors.

Hypothesis 2c: The moderating effects of status capital and decisional capital on social/economic returns are heterogeneous among different groups of doctors.

## Dependent Variables

The dependent variables in this study are the professional capital exchange returns of doctors. Lin [42] defined and measured social and economic returns based on reputation and wealth (earnings and assets), respectively. In our target OHC, the professional capital exchange returns were generated from consultation fees, virtual gifts, thank-you letters, and votes.

Virtual gifts can be presented not only as gifts in gratitude and as praise, but also for bestowing recognition on doctors (e.g., plaques, pennants, etc.). Several researchers have acknowledged the significant role of virtual gifts in enabling doctors to gain a reputation; thus, the number of virtual gifts can be seen as a measurement of the social returns of doctors [6]. With respect to the design of our target OHC data, virtual gifts were bought on our OHC, and 90 percent of the value was given to the doctors; hence, virtual gifts were also seen as economic returns for doctors. We propose that virtual gifts can be considered as both social and economic returns for the following reasons. First, to a patient seeking a doctor for a consultation, virtual gifts can be symbols of reputation functioning as social returns. Since some patients prefer to provide economic returns for doctors, sending virtual gifts is worthy of consideration. Second, the identification of whether a virtual gift is a social or economic return is determined by the factors that doctors most value. Finally, we applied a factor analysis on dependent variables for confirmation, and the results indicated that virtual gifts straddle social and economic returns.

In summary, the dependent variable of this study consists of two dimensions: economic returns (ER) measured by the total income from telephone consultations and virtual gifts; and social returns (SR) measured by the total number of thank-you letters, virtual gifts, and votes a doctor has received.

## Independent Variables

According to the hypotheses, our independent variables comprise two main professional capital exchange resources, namely, status capital (SC) and decisional capital (DC), and the interactions between them.

## Operationalization of Status Capital

As mentioned previously, status capital is defined as doctors’ individual and social advantages of structural power [52]. The certified attributes of individuals (e.g., professional qualifications, educational levels, etc.) in a hierarchical structure are a form of status, and may be used to characterize and measure one’s individual advantages in a structural position [25, 42, 73]. In China’s medical hierarchy, the doctor has a unified evaluation system, specifically based on the titles of doctors. Regarding the titles, the clinical title is assessed in terms of the level of industrialization and technology,<sup>6</sup> whereas the academic title refers to the level of academic proficiency.<sup>7</sup> Moreover, the working environment to which doctors belong also plays an important role in determining their status capital. Some of the hospitals to which they belong and the cities in which they work can be advantageous within the medical system. A better hospital and city location indicates that colleagues or the surroundings hold or contain good resources that enable professionals to obtain better social advantages.

Thus, the status capital (SC) of doctors is assessed by individual and social advantages, namely, clinic title (clinicTitle), academic title (acdTitle), hospital level (hspLevel), and city level (cityLevel). In summary, status capital indicates who and where the doctor is. A more detailed description is presented in Table 2 (later in the article).

## Operationalization of Decisional Capital

Decisional capital (DC) is perceived as the doctor’s exchange behaviors in a series of dynamic interactions with patients. Molm [47] purported that social exchange resources can be the behaviors that one actor performs to produce consequences of value for another actor, namely, the interaction between actors. According to Cropanzano and Mitchell [15], exchange behaviors can be demonstrated by the frequency and distribution of dynamic interactions.

The frequency of online interactions reflects a doctor’s capability to deal with more work under limited time constraints. At the same time, it also reflects the doctor’s enthusiasm and dedication to treating patients in an OHC. However, exchange distribution refers to the quantity of doctors’ decisional capital exchange behavior following different interaction strategies, based on three functions provided by our target OHC: sharing of medical articles, online consultations, and telephone consultations.

When guided by a doctor’s capability, medical articles are explicitly organized (referring to knowledge that is well-organized and formed succinctly into words) and posted voluntarily, while online and telephone consultations are implicit (referring to doctors’ consultations based on patients’ individual symptoms) [3, 17]. In the case of treatment behaviors related to doctors’ commitment [69], the online consultation is a free and asynchronous treatment service. Patients write down their problems, and when doctors log in, they choose whether or not to answer the patients’ questions, without obvious benefits. This kind of interaction is normally repeated for detailed treatment. Thus, online consultation represents a doctor’s voluntary exchange, although volunteers are often considered to have considerable responsibility and obligation. In our target OHC, medical articles and online consultations are voluntary (undertaken without promised economic returns), whereas telephone consultations are deemed as monetary, since the amount of the consultation fee has been dealt with before an interaction.

Combining distribution and frequency of exchange interactions, we measured decisional capital by the doctors’ dynamic interactions with patients in the OHC as follows: quantity of medical articles (Qart), quantity of online consultations (Qonl), quantity of telephone consultations (Qtel), and frequency of exchange behaviors (Freq).

## Computation of Interactions

The interaction between SC and DC is measured as the multiplier of SC and DC, which can be calculated by their resulting product. According to the basic assumptions of the least squares method [13] and the experience of data analyses, the sample size should have more than 30-fold of independent variables. Given the limit on the number of interactions between two-level and three-level subfactors (112 additional interaction variables are present), we measured the interactions in our full regression model with the constructed SC ( <sup>\~</sup> ) and DC ( <sup>\~</sup> ), calculated separately for linear combinations of subfactor standard deviations. To ensure the viability of constructed variables, we calculated the correlation and dissimilarity matrix among the subfactors (shown in Online Appendix D). The results indicate that the methodology is acceptable.

## Control Variables

We controlled for other factors, such as the doctors’ types of specialty in online experiences, that could affect their personal performance [57]. We used the type of care offered by doctors (departments to which the doctors belong) as a dummy variable (departments). The categories of the departments were captured from our pilot OHC, and we found a total number of 28 departments. Longevity, which can be treated as an early mover advantage and enables the description of the duration of doctors working in OHCs [64], was likewise regarded as a control variable to eliminate cumulative effects.

## Empirical Model

Before testing our hypotheses, we reduced the data noise according to our research objective in such a way that only doctors who are willing to use the OHC are considered. Specifically, we removed the data for doctors who never used OHCs or who were only active in the first month since their registration.<sup>8</sup> A total of 29,337 samples were deemed acceptable after the data were processed.

According to our hypotheses on the exchange heterogeneity of social exchanges, doctors obtaining various returns needed to be classified into groups. First, according to the scholarly commonsense of the coauthors, the samples were grouped by the mean ER and SR values. As the samples did not completely conform to the standard normal distributions but were nevertheless supported [53], the mean value was used to represent the entire data set. Following this classification, the point of origin in the two-dimensional quadrant of doctors should be O $\begin{array} { r } { ( \overline { { E R } } , \overline { { \mathrm { \tiny ~ S R } } } ) = \mathrm { \tiny ~ O } \left( \frac { E R _ { 1 } + E R _ { 2 } + . . . . . . + E R _ { N } } { N } , \frac { S R _ { 1 } + S R _ { 2 } + . . . . . . + S R _ { N } } { N } \right) } \end{array}$ 2 where N is the data capacity (number of doctors).

Furthermore, a K-means cluster was applied to categorize the doctors. The final number of clusters was found to be four, and the clustering result substantially matched the size of each group with mean-value taxonomy. Finally, the paired samples test was applied to examine the differences between the two taxonomies. The results $( p = 0 . 0 0 0 , t = 1 1 . 6 9 2 )$ imply that the null hypothesis is not supported, indicating that no significant difference exists between the two categories (for details, see Online Appendix C).

The two-dimensional (2D) quadrant of doctors is thus derived by drawing on the principle of the mean value (these two dimensions thus signify the four different groups of doctors in OHCs, as seen in Table 2). For instance, if doctor n has $E R _ { n } { > } \overline { { E R } }$ and $S R _ { n } { > } \overline { { \mathbf { S } \mathbf { R } } } .$ then doctor n should be located in the first quadrant of a 2D coordinate system. In parallel, $E R _ { n } { \mathrm { < E R } }$ and $S R _ { n } { > } \overline { { \mathbf { S } \mathbf { R } } }$ belong to the second quadrant; $E R _ { n } { \mathrm { < } } { \overline { { \operatorname { S R } } } }$ and $S R _ { n } { < } \overline { { \mathbf { S } \mathbf { R } } }$ belong to the third quadrant; and $E R _ { n } { > } \overline { { \operatorname { S R } } }$ and $S R _ { n } { < } \overline { { \mathbf { S } \mathbf { R } } }$ belong to the fourth quadrant. Least squares regression was used to estimate our model. First, to explore the different behaviors among the four groups of doctors, multiple-group regressions were run separately on different categories of doctors. Similar to the principle of quantile regression [36], the grouped regression approach based on dependent variables provides a more comprehensive picture of the effects of the independent variables on the dependent variables. However, by estimating the model with average effects on the total number of samples with heterogeneous conditional distributions, the dependent-variable-grouped regression approach produced different effects concerning the distribution of the dependent variables. Specifically, in this study, quadrant distribution was applied. In the first model, the detailed variables of the main effects of the SC and DC were included independently.

Next, to compare the interaction effects of the different groups, we performed a regression following the methodology of Jaccard and Turrisi [33], in which the groups of doctors were constructed into dummy variables and incorporated into the same regression model. Recoding the dummy variables that represented the other groups enabled us to obtain the coefficients of the other groups. The multiple regressions comprised three levels of interaction, which are as follows:

Our results indicate that our constructed model possesses decent explanatory power. In terms of exchange distribution (quantity), consultation behaviors (online and telephone consultations) have a stronger association with ER and SR than with medical articles. There are several possible explanations. From the implicit or explicit knowledge perspective [17], one possibility is that the medical articles refer to explicit knowledge that is well-organized, formed into words, and constant [38], despite the different categories of patients. However, minor differences in personal illnesses and symptoms may require different forms of consultations. Therefore, together with personal characteristics, implicit knowledge shared through consultations is difficult to acquire [17] and should be more beneficial than explicit knowledge. From the perspective of online platforms, Paul [55] proposed that the teleconsultation has a positive impact on delivering implicit knowledge, which tends to have little impact on the transfer of explicit knowledge.

Another explanation from the perspective of behavior in OHCs is that patients are more inclined toward consultations offering implicit knowledge and pay less attention to explicit knowledge, which is more easily acquired from other forums. Results related to the sharing of implicit knowledge indicate that behavior with voluntary and monetary knowledge sharing is positively related to returns, although the sharing of monetary and implicit knowledge is nonsignificant for Group 2.

Our study also examines the interaction effects between SC and DC. A partially positive interaction effect between SC and DC on the benefits of doctors suggests that H1c is partially supported. The proposed explanation for the unsupported H1c is that a significant interactive effect exists between SC and DC, which, however, is not a common relationship for the observation of doctors, indicating that additional variance is required. To examine the predicted explanation of the interaction effects between SC and DC, the results of an additional model were used to provide further support. Detailed results indicate that the relationship between SC and voluntary DC becomes supplementary only when doctors have low SC, that is, as the voluntary exchange behavior of low-SC doctors increases, their SC will have a stronger relationship with their ER. However, for high-SC doctors, regardless of the quantity of voluntary knowledge-sharing behavior, no additional efforts will supplement the relationship between their SC and ER. With regard to SR, the supplementary impact is effective only for doctors who conduct numerous voluntary exchange behaviors. However, increased monetary exchange will reduce the relationship between SC and SR for doctors.

Subsequently, with ER, the interaction between SC and voluntary DC is significant only when SC is low. In the case of SR, the interaction between SC and voluntary DC is significant only when the voluntary DC is high; but regarding SR, the interaction between monetary DC and SC is negative.

## Theoretical Implications

Our research explores how OHCs benefit social professionals through social exchanges with their professional capital. By providing a new perspective on professional capital research, we examine the OHC exchange process of a specific profession. Hence, this study renders several important theoretical implications.

First, the research provides a new theoretical understanding of the returns of doctors who are willing to participate in OHCs by reconceptualizing professional capital from a social exchange perspective. Previous studies [10, 71] have investigated social capital and knowledge sharing in online or virtual communities, and remarkable contributions have been made in interpreting the motivations for knowledge sharing. However, social professionals, as a special group of online community users who have valuable knowledge and skills, dedicated and moral social attributes, and significant positions in the social structure, will benefit from a new framework to understand their online returns. Thus, we conceptualize the concept of professional capital, which is a special kind of rare, long-lasting, and valuable capital affiliated with social professionals, to conduct an insightful investigation of doctors social exchange processes in OHCs. Although most previous studies of professional capital have focused on the importance of professional capital and how to obtain it, our study is extended to include the social exchange perspective. We explore the process of exchange behavior by identifying it as a social exchange that contrasts strongly with transactions focusing more on economic returns in business processes. Logically, the idea of the social exchange process can also match the characteristics of social professionals.

In particular, this study also contributes to the professional capital literature by examining the various dimensions and interrelationships among social professionals based on empirical data. Hargreaves and Fullan [30] proposed three separate dimensions of professional capital, namely, human, social, and decisional capital. However, assessing human capital in terms of talent is difficult. At the same time, with decisional capital, judgment ability is not the result only of experience, since commitment is considered a very important aspect enabling the differentiation of social professionals from other workers. Neither capability nor commitment is easy to measure directly. Therefore, from the perspective of the social exchange theory, professional capital is a kind of social resource that can be understood in terms of structural power and dynamic interaction. Accordingly, we propose a method to measure professional capital by using observed data.

Second, our specific research context allows us to comparatively investigate the returns of the doctor’s professional capital, in the face of both voluntary and monetary options, in a visible and same context [77]. Doctors in OHCs consider telephone consultations as a form of social exchange tied to predictable economic returns, while online consultations are seen as an uncertain benefit exchange with, at best, no fee required as an OHC mechanism. In a voluntary exchange, no explicit fee is charged, and exchange returns are contingent on not knowing whether, when, or to what extent the other will reciprocate [48]. Moreover, voluntary exchange is postulated to produce better relationships than does a monetary exchange and allows for individuals to be more trusting. The relevant results support the prior research finding that voluntary exchange relates to social returns [32], while the returns of a monetary exchange tend to be more explicit and quid pro quo than one of a voluntary exchange. Furthermore, monetary exchanges are often treated as part of economic transactions [15, 48, 50]. Our results confirm the relation between voluntary knowledge sharing and social returns. Nevertheless, studies of different groups indicate that economic returns are not consistent with the advantages of monetary exchange. On the contrary, our deeper analysis demonstrates the complementary relationship between voluntary exchanges and doctors’ status capital on economic returns for low-SC doctors. Thus, in conjunction with the opportunities supported by an online community, this study extends empirical research in the social exchange literature through a comparative study of voluntary and monetary exchanges and their influences on exchange returns.

Moreover, our study applies observational data in assessing exchange returns, thereby making an empirical contribution to the extant literature that largely relies on perceptual, subjective data that would create measurement errors due to the gap between the psychological expectations of individuals and the real returns they are likely to receive [78].

## Practical Implications

We have several suggestions based on our findings for doctors, executives of OHCs, patients, and even governments. The results indicate how an OHC supports different groups of doctors to obtain social exchange benefits.

The practical implications of this study for doctors would help them better understand and tailor their investment on returns. In other words, based on their returns, they desire to know what an OHC offers and how it will support them. We argue that there are individual differences among users and thereby the social exchange trajectory may not reflect a homogeneous pattern between different professionals. Doctors in Group 1, who have obtained both SR and ER from OHCs (in addition to their high SC), find that high interactions also result in considerable direct and indirect benefits through moderating the effects of SC. Group 2 doctors, who have obtained more recognition than wealth brought on by OHCs, regard SR and ER as being especially related to their decisional capital. In particular, voluntary exchange as a kind of decisional capital exchange strategy supplements low-status capital on ER, and is also applicable to Group 3. Therefore, voluntary decisional capital exchange is encouraged for doctors with low SC to achieve better returns. Group 4 doctors who have acquired more ER than SR, perceive the monetary decisional capital exchange strategy as significantly related to high ER. However, our findings also show a significant negative interaction effect of monetary DC on the relationship between SC and SR.

Based on the above implications for doctors, incentive mechanisms for sustainable use can be created by executives of OHCs for doctors who are dissatisfied with the status quo and/or expect better returns. With the implicit assumption that the social exchange theory is widely used to explain the motives of people in different areas, this study could be interpreted to gain an insightful understanding of how to encourage the sustainability of doctors in OHCs [44, 59, 71]. By surveying doctors motivations for using OHCs, specific suggestions for creating expected returns through social exchange can be customized for each category of doctor. For instance, doctors in Group 2 who want to gain ER can benefit from a suggestion that more monetary decisional capital exchange should be incorporated by OHCs. However, for doctors in Group 3, the executives of OHCs might encourage more voluntary decisional capital exchange first.

Moreover, our findings indicate that patients prefer to accord ER to doctors who have significantly higher initial levels of SC. This phenomenon is noted in the current study as the effects of celebrity doctors who can be recognized by high status capital (i.e., clinic title, academic title, city level, and hospital level). Whenever possible, people tend to choose treatment from professionals of high status, whereas, in reality, these professionals rarely have sufficient time to respond, given their limited energy and unlimited work. This demonstrates that the evaluation of the quality of diagnoses and consultations is not transparent [70] and is difficult to estimate prior to consummation [19, 34]. Fortunately, it also shows that OHC supported doctors with low SC and high DC can benefit from their voluntary decisional capital exchange to gain returns. Furthermore, our research stresses that the effects of celebrity doctors substantially reduce SR in OHCs (the coefficients of SC factors are nonsignificant or have a minimal effect on SR). Therefore, this perspective suggests an important alternative practical lens in the contemporary doctor–patient context and we propose that commitment is an important component of professional capital and can be deemed an important factor in evaluating professionals [5].

This research highlights the effects of an OHC on improving societal health care for governmental organizations. Our charting of the medical levels of cities indicates an insignificant relationship with ER and SR (six of eight coefficients are not significant). Hence, OHCs eliminate the impact of doctors’ geographical location on returns [27]. To some extent, OHCs assist in allocating limited medical resources equitably and widely. However, social network analysis from a macro perspective should be conducted for further verification.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

This study has several limitations that future research may address. First, given the dynamic nature of doctor–patient interactions in OHCs, cross-sectional data may limit the explanation for a dynamic interaction process. We propose that crosssectional data are more suitable in this study for exploring the effects of accumulated exchange interactions on total returns (on which the exchange returns of voluntary behavior are contingent); however, in future, researchers could address this limitation by extending panel data to accurately simulate the interactions over time. For example, research on the costs and efforts of doctors in OHCs could contribute practical implications for doctors’ better usage of OHCs for better returns, and for incentive mechanisms of OHCs on sustaining use. A longitudinal study on doctor– patient interaction histories tracking specific patients may help in exploring the mixed-strategy scenario in the black box of decisional capital exchange. Moreover, our statistical results on certain diseases (Department) may indicate some heterogeneities (in service preferences) from an OHC to traditional health care (for details, see Online Appendix G); however, more empirical studies with online versus offline data are encouraged to investigate the differences (e.g., preferences and heterogeneity) between OHCs and traditional health care. Additionally, in this study, we collected data on social exchange investment and returns simultaneously. Although this type of data gathering strategy is fairly typical for drawing conclusions on correlations, efforts are encouraged to account for causality, including endogeneity. For instance, exploring doctors’ energy balance with the online community and offline traditional treatment through a difference in differences study [18] would contribute to causality. Finally, although our results effectively explain the extrinsic returns of doctors in OHCs, in-depth interviews or surveys of doctors could further confirm our results. Furthermore, future research can examine intrinsic returns as a plausible perspective to understand what doctors gain from their participation in OHCs.

## Conclusion

The important role of OHCs in improving health-care services has been widely acknowledged by both practitioners and researchers; however, significant voids remain in our understanding of the returns of doctors who willingly participate in OHCs. This study has examined the social and economic returns of doctors in a Chinese OHC, a topic that, for the most part, has not been studied from the perspective of doctors. We used a fresh theoretical lens—professional capital. Doctors as social professionals are a special group of online community users who have structural advantages and valuable knowledge and skills. As other health-care stakeholders (i.e., patients, OHC executives) endeavor to encourage doctors to participate in OHCs, it is important to study the returns of doctors’ online social exchange. To that end, this study contributes theoretical and practical insights by extending the lens of professional capital and the social exchange theory to understand doctors’ returns in OHCs.

This study adds two important contributions to extant literature. First, we import the concept of professional capital to offer insights into the relative benefits (social and economic returns) that are likely to drive doctors to participate in OHCs. Our professional capital exchange model is significantly interpretable on the returns of doctors in OHCs. The three exchange elements—exchange resources, exchange processes, and exchange returns—generate a deeper understanding of the black box of doctor’s online social exchanges. This model could also be used to improve the explanatory potential of future research, especially on social professionals online participation. Moreover, this study possibly represents one of the first efforts to assess professional capital and quantify the interrelationships among its dimensions, based on a unique data set. Second, our specific research context enables us to apply the empirical method on unique observation data, thereby overcoming a major limitation of traditional measurements. It contributes to existing literature by comparatively investigating doctors’ professional capital investment to obtain exchange returns, recognizing both voluntary and monetary options. Given the gap between the expectations and reality of returns, we contribute new findings by assessing exchange returns through observational data, thus alleviating common method bias.

Our findings offer implications for doctors to gain a better understanding of how an OHC supports them in obtaining social exchange returns. Different incentive mechanisms for doctors’ participation can be suggested by OHC executives, which are customized to suit each group of doctors. Moreover, we propose that the doctor’s decisional capital is an important professional component in maintaining exchange returns. This study also demonstrates the potential of using OHCs to weaken the effects of celebrity doctors and to optimize the allocation of medical resources (i.e., doctors). We hope that this study stimulates additional research to further enrich our understanding of OHC dynamics.
