# Applying network analysis to investigate interpersonal in<sup>fl</sup>uence of information security behaviours in the workplace

Duy Dang-Pham\*, Siddhi Pittayachawan, Vince Bruno

School of Business IT and Logistics, RMIT University, Australia

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Security compliance Security behaviour Security management Interpersonal in<sup>fl</sup>uence Social network analysis Exponential random graph modelling

## 1. Introduction

## A B S T R A C T

As organisations are developing people-centric security workplaces, where proactive security behaviours are fostered, it is important to understand more about the sources of security in<sup>fl</sup>uence. This research applied social network analysis methods to investigate security in<sup>fl</sup>uence within a large interior contractor in Vietnam. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings revealed that security in<sup>fl</sup>uence occurs between employees in the same department, particularly those in senior positions, have longer tenure or younger age. Engagement in daily work and security-related activities can also increase the likelihood of in<sup>fl</sup>uencing security behaviours. Moreover, the security in<sup>fl</sup>uence network is transitive and has a hierarchical structure.

In the most recent Risk Management Summit [1], security practitioners were discussing the concepts of people-centric workplaces where security controls are relaxed, whereas top management focuses on reinforcing employees’ sense of security duties and accountability. This people-centric security workplace puts emphasis on trust and collaboration between the top management and the employees, who are empowered by the training and security communities’ culture to make their own informed risk decisions [1]. Even though the security culture of trust and personal accountability was mentioned by Williams [2], Gartner [1] emphasised the importance of these concepts by arguing that preventive and restrictive security controls will be less feasible in the modern digital businesses that demand frequent access to large volume of data. In addition, Kirlappos et al. [3] conducted 118 interviews with employees and managers in a large multinational organisation and found ‘shadow security practices frequently occurred, particularly at a team level. According to these researchers, ‘shadow security’ practices are security workarounds invented and propagated among the employees within the department, and these practices emerged because of the lack of collaboration and high security burden in daily operations.

The above discussions suggest high dependency of one employee’s security behaviours on another. In fact, security practices were argued to involve collective information practices and perceptions of risk that are shared and codeveloped by the communities [4]. Examples of dependency of security behaviours were also documented in research studies, such as the end user’s delegation of security responsibility to other individual, technological controls or the organisation [5]. Moreover, Dang-Pham et al. [6] found that a large number of researches have been predominantly testing for the effects of individualistic cognition on the end user’s security performance while overlooking the social interactions between them. Therefore, it was suggested that the adoption of social network analysis techniques needs to be explored further.

This study has two key motivations. One is the need to gain more knowledge about security interactions and dependency among the employees. The second motivation is the lack of social network analysis in behavioural security domain. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we examined the formation of security in<sup>fl</sup>uence ties or the likelihood of an employee in<sup>fl</sup>uences another’s security behaviours by performing network analysis in a large organisation in South East Asia. The exploratory visual analysis on the network structure and use of exponential random graph modelling (ERGM) technique sheds light on the factors that lead to such formation of security in<sup>fl</sup>uence. To our knowledge, our study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to conduct empirical network analysis in a behavioural security domain. Thus, this study contributes both practical and theoretical implications to the current body of knowledge, particularly by proposing a set of criteria to identify the sources of security in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the workplace and a demonstration of network analysis in this domain.

## 2. Literature review

Furnell and Rajendran [7] discussed that security behaviours can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by three workplace-related factors, among which we focus on the factor workplace interactions. They further suggested that security interactions consist of supervisor/management leadership and colleague behaviours, in which the latter was deemed to have more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence. However, they warned that such weighting based on the focus group could be subjective and vary depending on different cases. Leach [8] also argued that the employees’ security behaviours can be affected by senior management and colleagues, and by another interaction between the employees and their employer conceptualised as a psychological contract. These studies provided us the directions to review relevant literatures on security in<sup>fl</sup>uences that come from supervisor and colleagues’ practices.

There are various explanations for why supervisor and colleagues’ behaviours could in<sup>fl</sup>uence security behaviours. For example, researches on security climate have argued that the presence of such practices in the workplace gives rise to the employees’ perceptions of information security being treated seriously and prioritised that subsequently motivate their compliance [9–12]. Another way to study the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the work communities on one’s security behaviours is through subjective norms, which was found as one of the best predictors of security compliance by the systematic literature review of Sommestad et al. [13]. However, both subjective norms and security climate inform little about the speci<sup>fi</sup>c mechanisms that explain why and how an employee interact with and follow their immediate environment’s practices.

By integrating different theories, recent empirical researches elaborated the group’s mechanisms that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the employees’ security behaviours, especially through social learning and bonding. For instance, Warkentin et al. [14] adapted Social Learning Theory’s perspective and found that situational support, verbal persuasion and vicarious experience have positive impacts on the employees’ self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy that motivates their intention to perform security practices. Furthermore, they suggested that organisations may increase security feedback and implement a mentoring system to encourage social learning. In another study, I<sup>fi</sup>nedo [15] tested Social Bonding Theory’s hypotheses and found that increased attachment and involvement lead to higher compliance through positive attitude and subjective norms towards security policies. Most recently, Safa and Von Solms [16] found that subjective norms drove intention to share information security knowledge that arguably mitigated information security risks. With the empirical evidences suggesting the mechanisms of in<sup>fl</sup>uence in organisational security context, we proceed to consult the theoretical perspectives for additional insights and establish our hypotheses in the next section.

## 3. Hypothesis development

In network studies, social learning and bonding can be described in the forms of interactions and relations. Furthermore, network interactions and relations, or termed ties in general, can be categorised as instrumental (e.g., seek/give work advice) and expressive ties (e.g., be friend with, social support) [17] according to the network theory. Instrumental ties involve the exchange of resources that enable an employee to complete their work-related tasks, whereas expressive ties refer to interpersonal affect and in<sup>fl</sup>uence [17,18]. Instrumental and expressive ties hold important roles in the workplace, as Saint-Charles and Mongeau [18] found that employees tend to seek the experts in their instrumental network when in uncertain situations (i.e. do not know how to do), whereas trusted friends are more commonly sought in ambiguous situations (i.e. do not know what to do). These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent with Ashforth [19] theory about the formation of organisational climate, which also involves the process of informational and normative in<sup>fl</sup>uences that clarify uncertainty and ambiguity and allow the employees to reach a consensus of the meanings of their workplace.

## 3.1. Instrumental ties and information security influence

An important instrumental network that has been commonly studied is the exchange of work-related advice that contributes to problem-solving and enhancing work processes [17,18]. Besides the ‘work advice’ network, we consider another instrumental network that facilitates the sharing of organisational updates, such as new procedures and policies. This network is included because information security matter is commonly introduced to the workplace as a component embedded in work procedures and regulations [20].

Previous studies have overlooked the effects of instrumental networks on information security in<sup>fl</sup>uence, despite their crucial roles in facilitating and maintaining the organisation’s operations. When information security priorities are communicated to the employees, some would encounter uncertainty because of their unfamiliarity with the technical requirements and security practices [20]. As reducing workplace uncertainty is one of the employees’ basic needs [21], they are expected to clarify their confusion about information security with the colleagues who often collaborate with them in completing daily work tasks [18]. Similarly, seeking advice from sources that often update them with the latest policies or procedures would be useful for reducing such uncertainty as well. Because it demands both specialised (about information security) and job procedural knowledge to perform security behaviours while ful<sup>fi</sup>lling primary work duties, the information sources in the ‘work advice’ and ‘organisational updates’ networks could provide relevant advice.

To explain the links between these instrumental networks and information security in<sup>fl</sup>uence, we re<sup>fl</sup>ect on French and Raven [22] theory about the <sup>fi</sup>ve bases of social power. In particular, this theory posits that a person is perceived as in<sup>fl</sup>uential when they can make themselves appear as knowledgeable and demonstrate expert power to others. Consequently, the employees in the mentioned instrumental networks, who are sought by others for work-related advice and organisational updates that clarify their uncertainty about security, would hold in<sup>fl</sup>uential power over the others’ security behaviours as well. Thus, we proposed the following hypotheses.

H1a: Employees who give work advice tend to in<sup>fl</sup>uence others security behaviours.

H1b: Employees who are sought for organisational updates tend to in<sup>fl</sup>uence others’ security behaviours.

In the behavioural security context, sharing advice about information security [14,16] and providing security troubleshooting [5] can be considered as instrumental ties, because these interactions help the employees to resolve information security issues in their work. It is worth mentioning here that while providing security advice and troubleshooting may appear as two similar behaviours, we will treat them separately in our subsequent analysis. This is because while sharing information security advice aims at educating the employees or changing their knowledge of security issues, this purpose may not be automatically achieved by simply receiving security troubleshooting.

For instance, previous research found that employees with low perceived self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy transfer their information security duties to their ‘technical friends’, whose role is to solely troubleshoot security issues for them [5]. Furthermore, it has been discussed that employees have a common tendency to attribute information security responsibility to the IT department or other employees that they perceive as being more responsible for the organisation security [23,24]. Even though the IT department and expert colleagues troubleshoot security issues for anyone in the organisation when requested, as a part of their job, it does not guarantee that all assistance seekers would learn more about information security.

Similar to the above hypotheses, employees who are frequently sought for information security advice and troubleshooting would demonstrate expert power [22]. Therefore, these security experts would be able to in<sup>fl</sup>uence others’ information security behaviours.

H1c: Employees who are sought for security advice tend to in<sup>fl</sup>uence others security behaviours.

H1d: Employees who are sought for security troubleshooting tend to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the others security behaviours.

## 3.2. Expressive ties and information security influence

In addition to the instrumental networks hypothesised above (H1a–H1d), we examine the effects of expressive network on information security in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Friendship and interpersonal trust are commonly examined as expressive networks by the previous studies [17,18]. These expressive networks are important in the workplace because they facilitate the socialisation that reduces workplace ambiguity [21]. In contrast to uncertainty that refers to the lack of information, employees feel ambiguous when they are overloaded by information and <sup>fi</sup>nd it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to select the best course of action [18].

In the information security context, some employees would prefer discussing security practices with their trusted colleagues to understand the meaning of security compliance, or how they should perform security practices in alignment with other work priorities. In fact, there can be multiple interpretations of information security risks in the workplace [4] that can result in ambiguity. Informal security discussions between colleagues were also found to create and propagate security workarounds within the organisation [3]. Furthermore, employees tend to form matching identity with the referent groups or individuals that they feel af<sup>fi</sup>liated and admire, and these identities subsequently make the employees align beliefs and behaviours with their referents [25]. Such an attachment to the work groups of trusted colleagues was found to affect individuals’ information security behaviours as well [2,26]. Similarly, French and Raven’s [22] social power theory also postulates the referent power that makes a person appear in<sup>fl</sup>uential to others. Provided the ample evidences that suggest the link between trust network and information security in<sup>fl</sup>uence, we hypothesise as follows.

H1e: Employees who are trusted tend to in<sup>fl</sup>uence others security behaviours.

## 3.3. Effects of influential statuses on security influence

By engaging in the organisation’s networks, the employees hold strategic network positions that subsequently affect interpersonal in<sup>fl</sup>uences. Network positions can be determined by physical proximity or social centrality [17]. These researchers explained that one can achieve in<sup>fl</sup>uential statuses in the organisation by being located in socially central positions, which grant access and control to information and other resources.

Highly central individuals can then enjoy the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of their social position, and they can make use of their privileges and acquired resources to in<sup>fl</sup>uence other network actors. By considering a directed network of giving security advice, an employee who is nominated to give security advice to many people is regarded as in<sup>fl</sup>uential in giving security advice. These central employees would have the network advantages that give them a higher chance to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the others’ security behaviours.

It is therefore interesting to examine more in depth the information security in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the prominent employees, who hold central positions in the networks discussed thus far, on others’ information security behaviours. While the aforementioned hypotheses H1a–H1e focus on the work interactions that result in information security in<sup>fl</sup>uence between two random employees, the hypotheses about in<sup>fl</sup>uential statuses investigate the extreme cases that possess signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amounts of network connections and their abilities to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the others’ security behaviours.

With the theoretical backgrounds for each of the networks effects on security in<sup>fl</sup>uence explained in the previous section, we proposed the following hypotheses.

H2a: Employees who are in<sup>fl</sup>uential in work advice network tend to in<sup>fl</sup>uence others security behaviours more.

H2b: Employees who are in<sup>fl</sup>uential in organisational updates network tend to in<sup>fl</sup>uence others security behaviours more.

H2c: Employees who are in<sup>fl</sup>uential in security advice network tend to in<sup>fl</sup>uence others security behaviours more.

H2d: Employees who are in<sup>fl</sup>uential in security troubleshoot network tend to in<sup>fl</sup>uence others security behaviours more.

H2e: Employees who are in<sup>fl</sup>uential in trust network tend to in<sup>fl</sup>uence others security behaviours more.

## 3.4. Effects of personal attributes on security influence

Besides the in<sup>fl</sup>uential effects of being socially embedded in the organisational networks, individuals’ perceptions can be developed through direct interactions that are facilitated by their physical location and af<sup>fi</sup>liation [17]. This argument has been tested by empirical network researches as they con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the effects of homophily, which is described in layman’s term as ‘birds of a feather <sup>fl</sup>ock together’. For example, homophily effects can be observed when individuals, who have the same gender, work position or shared department membership, tend to have ties to each other [27]. In the security context, the effects of homophily were evident in the qualitative study of Kirlappos et al. [3] which showed that security workarounds were shared and adopted by members in the same department. After considering a directed security in<sup>fl</sup>uence network, we can determine a homophily effect by identifying an employee who nominates another in the same department or of the same gender as capable of in<sup>fl</sup>uencing his or her security behaviours. In addition to homophily effects, we expected that employees having higher seniority, tenure and age would have higher chance to in<sup>fl</sup>uence security behaviours as well. This hypothesis follows French and Raven [22] theory, which postulates that employees who possess high formal ranks and statuses can establish legitimacy power and are perceived as more in<sup>fl</sup>uential. Moreover, it aligns with the argument that new employees would know less about the organisation’s practices than those having a longer service record [28,29]. Consequently, employees with higher tenure would be perceived as more in<sup>fl</sup>uential.

H3a: Employees who have the same gender tend to in<sup>fl</sup>uence each other’s security behaviours.

H3b: Employees who work in the same department tend to in<sup>fl</sup>uence each other’s security behaviours.

H3c: Employees who have higher seniority have a higher chance to in<sup>fl</sup>uence security behaviours.

H3d: Employees who have longer tenure have a higher chance to in<sup>fl</sup>uence security behaviours.

H3e: Employees who are older in age have a higher chance to in<sup>fl</sup>uence security behaviours.

## 4. Methods

## 4.1. Research context

Our study was conducted in a large interior contractor in Vietnam as a developing country in South East Asia. This organisation, named XYZ to safeguard their identity, hosts a work force of more than 300 employees at three of<sup>fi</sup>ces and 1000 workers at two factories in multiple locations in Vietnam. XYZ has been operating in Vietnam since 1994 and its businesses focus on delivering architectural design and turn-key construction projects for local and international clients in private and public sectors. In addition, XYZ also manufactures high-quality furniture and ships its products worldwide. Presently, XYZ is establishing its branch of<sup>fi</sup>ce in Myanmar to expand its business to other South East Asian countries.

The pressures of globalisation and XYZ’s continuous efforts to remain as the leading interior contractor in Vietnam resulted in one of its strategic goals to improve its current information security management systems. The top management at XYZ believed that such improvement would provide a better and more secure solution to manage their growing volume of data, and they can enhance their reputation and gain more trust from international prospects. In early 2015, XYZ began the implementation of the ISO 27001 security standard and enlisted us as academic advisors. We took this opportunity to conduct this network research at XYZ, and XYZ agreed to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from our research <sup>fi</sup>ndings as long as they found them relevant and useful for the implementation. As our primary motivation is to test theoretically informed hypotheses and we are not contracted by XYZ to deliver tried-and-trusted best practices, this research is free from con<sup>fl</sup>ict of interests.

## 4.2. Measures and data collection

To capture network data of instrumental and expressive ties between XYZ’s employees, we conducted a web-based survey that consists of eight questions (see Table 1). Each employee was asked to nominate maximum seven people at XYZ per question. We set the limit of seven nominations based on Merluzzi and Burt [30] recommendation, which suggests that segmented networks in Asian-Paci<sup>fi</sup>c organisations would require <sup>fi</sup>ve to six names per node for detecting clear network effects. The questionnaires were sent to the of<sup>fi</sup>ce staff that frequently use computers and handle con<sup>fi</sup>dential documents at work, and the response rate was 71% (264/373). Personal attributes such as age, gender, department membership, seniority and tenure were extracted from XYZ’s human resource database.

To test hypotheses H2a–H2d, we calculated degree centrality measures for each employee to describe their in<sup>fl</sup>uential status in a particular network. In-degree centrality measures were computed based on the sum of in-coming ties of an employee, which is equivalent to their total nominations received from the others. For example, if John was nominated by six of his colleagues for sending out security advice, then John’s in-degree or in<sup>fl</sup>uential status in the ‘seek security advice’ network is 6. In contrast, the employee’s in<sup>fl</sup>uential status in the ‘work advice’ network was described by their out-degree. In other words, an employee is in<sup>fl</sup>uential in the ‘work advice’ network if she/he can send out work advice to many people. Because an employee can nominate maximum seven people, the highest in<sup>fl</sup>uential score one can possibly get is 7.

As compared with traditional survey research, ERGM is similar to the use of structural equation modelling (SEM) method that both approaches aim to infer the relationships between variables. The only difference between the two approaches is that ERGM is suitable for analysing relational data such as network ties, which violates the independence-of-observations assumption of SEM and therefore prohibits its adoption [27]. An alternative method for network regression analysis is called multiple regression quadratic assignment procedure (MRQAP) [27], which was employed in another recent behavioural security network research by Dang-Pham et al. [20]. However, ERGM is superior to MRQAP particularly in our study, because ERGM can estimate the structural effects of the network on the occurrence of ties, which MRQAP cannot do. These effects offer more information about the structural features of the information security in<sup>fl</sup>uence network, and help to answer our research question as stated at the beginning.

ERGMs are constructed by adding ‘terms’ that describe speci<sup>fi</sup>c network con<sup>fi</sup>gurations. In our research, we used the R package for an ERGM called statnet [31], and thus, the naming convention of our included terms follows the package’s syntax. For example, the most fundamental term in many baseline ERGMs is ‘edges’, which describes the occurrence of a tie between two actors. For a full list of available terms in the statnet package, we refer the readers to the article of Morris et al. [32].

The logic of adding terms to ERGMs can be explained as follows. Network researchers collect empirical network data that present an observed network, which is regarded as one particular pattern out of many possible network patterns [33]. Because we do not know the stochastic process that forms the network pattern as such, we assume that the network ties come into existence depending on other features and processes in the network. We then construct an ERGM by adding terms that re<sup>fl</sup>ect those features and processes, which also re<sup>fl</sup>ect our dependence assumptions or theoretical hypotheses. Furthermore, the constructed ERGM needs to accurately replicate the observed network through simulation. Finally, by observing signi<sup>fi</sup>cant results of the estimated parameters and appropriate goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t of the ERGM, we can infer plausible explanations for the process that leads to the formation of our observed network. The terms used in our model, which correspond to our hypotheses, are illustrated in Table 2.

We developed our random graph model that contains the terms in Table 2 by following the <sup>fi</sup>ve-step approach outlined by Robins et al. [33]. Overall, there are three types of ERGMs that are characterised by their dependence assumptions. They include the Bernoulli model, dyadic models and Markov random graph models. Bernoulli model is the most basic type which assumes that the formation of ties is independent from other processes. Dyadic models are more complicated and realistic than Bernoulli model because they consider dyadic con<sup>fi</sup>gurations and node attributes when explaining the formation of ties.

However, both were argued to contain unrealistic assumptions in empirical and theoretical circumstances because they do not take the shared partners between actors into account [33]. Consequently, we added terms that describe Markov dependence. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, these terms re<sup>fl</sup>ect the assumption that two network ties are conditionally dependent on a shared partner, and they depict a more realistic process of forming ties. Furthermore, incorporating these assumption’s con<sup>fi</sup>gurations in our ERGMs allows us to interpret the high-order network patterns such as triad effects. This is useful for revealing the structural features of the security in<sup>fl</sup>uence network, particularly when no previous studies have examined the patterns of security in<sup>fl</sup>uence before.

## 4.4. ERGM estimation

We estimated our random graph model by using the Monte Carlo Markov Chain (MCMC) estimation approach with interval and burn-in rates at 5000 and 50,000. The model converged at the second iteration out of 20, which suggested that sensible terms were included. Goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t for the model was calculated by setting both burn-in rate and interval relatively large at 100,000 to ensure accurate results [34].

Evaluating network transitivity involves the analysis of triads, or the con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of every three nodes in a network [27,36]. Networks that have many triad con<sup>fi</sup>gurations that facilitate such balanced connections are subsequently transitive as well.

To compare the networks’ hierarchical features, we calculated the measure of out-degree centralisation, or the variation in the number of outgoing ties among all the nodes. ## 5.2. Effects of influential statuses

An employee is well embedded in a network and achieves an in<sup>fl</sup>uential status as they receive many nominations from the others for doing something (e.g., sending security advice, being trusted). Consequently, these employees would hold certain advantages that can result in more power and in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Hypotheses H2a–e focused on the in<sup>fl</sup>uential effects of the employees on the propensity of forming security in<sup>fl</sup>uence ties.

The results indicated that central employees in the ‘work advice’ network would have higher chance to be nominated by colleagues as in<sup>fl</sup>uential in security. These employees have high out-degree centrality in the work advice network, which means that they send out work advice to many others in daily operations. Similarly, employees who are sought by many others for organisational updates were also more likely to be nominated as in<sup>fl</sup>uential in security. In contrast, we did not detect any statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant patterns regarding the effects of being in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by other networks on the likelihood of in<sup>fl</sup>uencing security behaviours. Thus, only hypotheses H2a and H2b were supported.

## 5.3. Effects of network ties

The network ties’ effects hold a critical role in our research since they specify the likelihood of in<sup>fl</sup>uencing security behaviours because of active engagement in daily work activities, which are more visible and can be intervened by the management. Fig. 5 shows a clear explanation of the ties’ effect, which sometimes termed entrainment effect by network studies [35]. The entrainment effects in our research refer to the term ‘edgecov’ in our random graph model that were included to test hypotheses H1a– H1e. This effect describes the relationship between two types of network ties, in which the occurrence of one type is conditional on the other type. For example, we used the term ‘edgecov’ in our model to examine the entrainment effect of the occurrence of an ‘information security in<sup>fl</sup>uence’ tie, which was hypothesised to be conditional on the occurrence of a ‘give work advice’ tie (H1a).

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggested that an employee’s active interactions in most of the networks (except ‘giving work advice’ network) increase the likelihood that they will in<sup>fl</sup>uence the others’ security behaviours. However, the previously mentioned estimate of being in<sup>fl</sup>uential in the ‘give work advice’ network indicated that employees with high-out degree would have a higher chance to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the others’ security behaviours. Interpreting these results together suggested that while giving work advice and in<sup>fl</sup>uencing security behaviours would not likely to co-occur, giving work advice to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of other employees and become in<sup>fl</sup>uential in the work advice network can increase such likelihood. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight the opportunities and increase the con<sup>fi</sup>dence for security managers to appoint local security champions within each department and maximise the diffusion of security in<sup>fl</sup>uence. The positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant results of these network effects well supported our hypothesis H1a–e.

## 5.4. Effects of structural patterns

Besides testing the hypotheses about the personal attributes and network effects on security in<sup>fl</sup>uence, our model also revealed the structural patterns of the security in<sup>fl</sup>uence network by examining the estimates of the terms gwesp and gwdsp. Both these terms describe the relationships between every two nodes i and j and their shared partners k. On the one hand, gwesp captures the situation, where i in<sup>fl</sup>uences j’s security behaviours directly and indirectly via k. On the other hand, gwdsp captures the situation, where i can only in<sup>fl</sup>uence j’s security behaviours indirectly via k. Fig. 6 provides a clearer illustration of these two situations.

A positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant estimate of the gwesp term contains much information about the pattern of security in<sup>fl</sup>uence. First, it informs the transitivity of security in<sup>fl</sup>uence, which can travel from one employee to another both directly and indirectly through many common partners. Second, it denotes the hierarchical structure in the security in<sup>fl</sup>uence network, where employees such as i in Fig. 6 are at the controlling position and can freely spread their in<sup>fl</sup>uences. In contrast, employees at node j’s position can only receive the in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Moreover, a positive estimate of gwesp also tells that the propensity of node i can in<sup>fl</sup>uence j’s security behaviours is proportional to the number of shared partners k they have in between. In other words, if i can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the security behaviours of multiple k and those k can in<sup>fl</sup>uence j, then there is a high chance that i can in<sup>fl</sup>uence j’s security behaviours directly as well.

A non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant estimate of the gwdsp term further supports those patterns re<sup>fl</sup>ected by gwesp. By itself, gwdsp also holds interesting meanings regarding the structural patterns of a network. A positive estimate of gwdsp describes situations that involve the movement of a physical good from one location to the <sup>fi</sup>nal destination through multiple intermediaries. Such a situation implies that there are no direct paths between the starting point and the destination. However, such a pattern would be rather uncommon in the context of interpersonal in<sup>fl</sup>uences because in<sup>fl</sup>uences can travel more freely. The only plausible explanation for the lack of direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence between i and j but only through k is that j does not know the existence of i in the organisation or their skills/knowledge. As the estimate of the gwdsp term in Table 3 is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, the likelihood to observe this pattern in our security in<sup>fl</sup>uence network is rare.

## 6. Discussion and implications

The outcomes of the tested hypotheses are summarised in Table 4, which shows that most of them were supported. In this section, we compared our <sup>fi</sup>ndings with previous studies and discussed the practical and theoretical implications.

## 6.1. Identifying the sources of security influence based on personal attributes

By evaluating the effects of the employees’ personal attributes and in<sup>fl</sup>uential statuses on security in<sup>fl</sup>uence, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings are most useful for identifying the important sources of security in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the workplace. Throughout our analysis, we learned that employees who work in the same department were found to in<sup>fl</sup>uence each other more, and these <sup>fi</sup>ndings mirror Borgatti and Cross [28] regarding information seeking and learning in the workplace. Physical proximity allows the employees to have better access and knowing of the others’ skills and knowledge, and thus supports more effective information seeking [28]. It is also plausible that employees working in the same department could in<sup>fl</sup>uence each other more easily because of the same reason. Furthermore, prior behavioural security studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed local norms and workgroup sanctions as important factors that consistently impact behavioural intention or actual compliance [13,37]. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings reinforce such an effect by looking at another angle i.e., the physical proximity of the employees.

Employees holding senior positions and longer service time were found to have higher chance of in<sup>fl</sup>uencing security behaviours. The prior <sup>fi</sup>nding re<sup>fl</sup>ects the in<sup>fl</sup>uential effect of formal rank and status in organisation [17], as well as the notion that newer members tend to know less [29] are thus arguably less in<sup>fl</sup>uential. Moreover, it is consistent with Kirlappos et al. [3] study about the development of ‘shadow security’ groups at team level, particularly through local security induction delivered by direct supervisors. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings explain why shadow security in<sup>fl</sup>uence comes primarily from the direct supervisors. Interestingly, young employees also have a higher chance to send out security in<sup>fl</sup>uence. We could not <sup>fi</sup>nd any existing studies that explain why young employees can be more in<sup>fl</sup>uential in security. However, we believe that the younger employees could be seen as more technological savvy [38], and thus can understand and appear more trustworthy in persuading security behaviours more easily.

Our literature review shows that the amount of extant studies on selecting security champions is scarce. Security practitioners can use the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed attributes as the basic guideline to select security champions or identify potential malicious perpetrators. Besides the personality traits of good security champions such as imagination and altruism [39], which can be determined by having the candidates take a personality test, we add that practitioners can quickly <sup>fi</sup>lter out potential candidates by considering their age, seniority and department membership. More importantly, we recommend appointing local security champions rather than assigning external champions to the departments, to leverage the effects of physical proximity and sharing department membership. Similarly, the same criteria can be considered to identify the employees who send out false security directives or develop shadow security.

## 6.2. Developing strategies to maximise security champions’ influence

In contrast to our initial expectation that employees sending out or receiving more ties would be more likely to establish security in<sup>fl</sup>uence tie, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings did not detect many of such patterns. Only the employees holding central positions in work advice and organisational updates networks were found to increase the likelihood of in<sup>fl</sup>uencing security behaviours. The abilities to provide security advice and security troubleshoot increase tremendously an employee’s ability to convince the others’ security behaviours. It is interesting to observe from the <sup>fi</sup>ndings that employees nominated by others as trustworthy and capable of providing security supports (i.e. advice and troubleshooting) can in<sup>fl</sup>uence security behaviours, yet receiving too many nominations (i.e., in<sup>fl</sup>uential in that network) diminishes their in<sup>fl</sup>uence. One plausible explanation is that the employees tend to have their security behaviours in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by another person who engages in a one-on-one, direct and exclusive relationship with them. An in<sup>fl</sup>uential employee in any networks (e.g., ‘trust’, ‘give security advice’ or ‘give security troubleshooting’) who receives nominations from many colleagues is not necessarily nominated by the in<sup>fl</sup>uenced agent in the ‘security in<sup>fl</sup>uence’ network. In other words, employee A can receive many nominations in the ‘trust’ network by B, C, D, etc., but such popularity of A in the ‘trust’ network does not make A in<sup>fl</sup>uence their colleague E’s security behaviours if E does not nominate A in the ‘security in<sup>fl</sup>uence’ network. Furthermore, a person who is too popular in a social network may not be desirable because their attention and commitment are distributed to many others.

It is also worth highlighting that giving organisational updates (e.g., changes in policies or procedures) ranked the highest in terms of their contributing effect towards likelihood of security in<sup>fl</sup>uence, and being entrusted ranked the second. In addition, central employees in the work advice network, who frequently give work advice to many people, have a higher chance to in<sup>fl</sup>uence security behaviours. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support the results about the contributing effect of centrality on interpersonal in<sup>fl</sup>uence in network theory-based studies [17]. They also re<sup>fl</sup>ect the nature of organisational security that concerns with business processes and policies. When top management integrates security priorities into daily operations, individuals who actively give work advice or announce organisational updates would be more frequently sought by peers for information about the security directives. Consequently, these individuals can in<sup>fl</sup>uence their peers’ security behaviours.

More interestingly, giving out security troubleshoot has larger impact than security advice in leading to security in<sup>fl</sup>uence, which suggests the higher importance of actual supports as compared with mere advice. From our analysis, a security champion (or shadow security staff) who can do both these tasks, coupled with their engagement in daily work activities such as giving work advice or being trusted by peers, can increase the likelihood of in<sup>fl</sup>uencing others’ security behaviours to 80%. Besides the security awareness training that equips security champions with technical skills and knowledge, it is imperative that practitioners should select those who are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by local peers as trustworthy and supportive in daily operations.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also support Saint-Charles and Mongeau [18] study about how employees cope with ambiguity and uncertainty in the workplace. Considering that information security may be a complex and technically advanced topic to many employees, they would face uncertainty and ambiguity in understanding how and which security practices should be performed [10]. In these situations, Saint-Charles and Mongeau [18] found that the employees tend to rely on trusted friends to resolve ambiguity (what should be done), whereas experts are sought to deal with uncertainty (how to do). The analysed results show that giving security troubleshoot is more effective than security advice, and both are superior than other resources such as work advice and trust. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that security in<sup>fl</sup>uence was perceived to help resolve uncertainty more than ambiguity.

By identifying these relationships among daily work activities and security in<sup>fl</sup>uence, we made theoretical and practical contributions to the current body of knowledge. Future studies may advance our <sup>fi</sup>ndings by elaborating in greater depth the mediating factors and mechanisms that link those relationships. For instance, potential research questions can aim at explaining why employees who actively send out work advice and are entrusted by their colleagues could lead to greater security in<sup>fl</sup>uence. In fact, we suspected that varied organisational and contextual factors could also result in <sup>fi</sup>ndings different to ours.

## 6.3. The structural patterns of security influence network in the workplace

By analysing the network interactions with social network analysis techniques, we detect that security in<sup>fl</sup>uence tends to have a transitive pattern and hierarchical structure. In other words, security in<sup>fl</sup>uence travels in a one-way direction from a central employee to another both directly and indirectly. It was also found that establishing a security in<sup>fl</sup>uence tie between two employees is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, as shown in the negative estimates in our ERGMs and the visualisation of security in<sup>fl</sup>uence network that is sparse and thin.

Investigating these network patterns reveals that XYZ, our industry partner in this research, may have a ‘command and control’ security management model. Moreover, the visualisation in Fig. 3 supports the presence of this model by pointing out that only a few IT staff in XYZ were nominated as in<sup>fl</sup>uential in security. The visualisation also suggests the existence of shadow security groups, where security in<sup>fl</sup>uence is locally dense and inaccessible from the outside. From the practitioner’s perspectives, network research methods provide a clear view of an organisation’s security workplace. This can assist top management to make strategic network alterations and interventions.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings revealed that security in<sup>fl</sup>uence come from a few dominant sources, and practitioners can locate these sources to either make them security champions or deter any misleading information that they may send out. Moreover, such a transitive and hierarchical structure of security in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the workplace advocates the use of security champion or ‘train the trainer approach, as compared with the ‘big bang’ approach that delivers mass training to all employees. On the basis of our results, we argue that informal security groups and practices exist regardless of the methods of training, which are either known or unknown to the management. Consequently, practitioners would inevitably have to identify and deal with the in<sup>fl</sup>uential sources of security practices in the workplace.

Throughout our analysis, we have demonstrated the adoption social network research techniques, especially ERGMs, in behavioural security domain to test theoretically informed hypotheses. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the distinctive characteristics of our industry partner and their unique cultural traits. Future studies may replicate our study in a different context, perhaps in other industries or <sup>fl</sup>atted structure organisations, and compare results regarding security in<sup>fl</sup>uence patterns or mechanisms. ## 7. Conclusion

This research aims to improve theoretical understanding of security in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the workplace and provide recommendations to security managers, especially in terms of selecting security champions and devising strategies to diffuse security practices within their organisations. The visualisation of the security in<sup>fl</sup>uence network could serve as a potential tool for the top management to examine their security environment, whereas the ERGMs provide reliable statistical inferences for the network effects. Because our study is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst researches in the behavioural security <sup>fi</sup>eld to employ network analysis techniques to investigate the security interactions and relations between the employees rather than their individualistic cognition, we encourage future studies to consider these network techniques and validate or extend our empirical results.

We have found empirical results that explained why some employees would be more in<sup>fl</sup>uential in security than others. The contributing factors of one’s ability to in<sup>fl</sup>uence security behaviours consist of inherent personal traits such as high seniority, longer service time, department membership and age. Other important factors include the employee’s central position in daily operations and security-related networks, which can be achieved by actively engaging in activities such as giving work advice, being trusted by colleagues or performing security troubleshoot for the others. Furthermore, we found that security in<sup>fl</sup>uence network tends to have a transitive and hierarchical pattern, with only a few network actors holding dominant positions.

Similar to other whole-network studies, our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings are drawn from a particular setting (or <sup>fi</sup>rm in our case) that has unique cultural and contextual features. As XYZ is a Vietnamese organisation, the workplace interactions that occur between the employees there would be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the Vietnamese cultural values to a certain extent. Two notable traits of Vietnamese culture are strong collectivism and high power distance, which make Vietnamese employees nurture the relationships with their groups and respect formal authority [40]. Consequently, these cultural traits may have resulted in our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, which showed that employees of XYZ tend to have their security behaviours in<sup>fl</sup>uenced more by formal IT staff rather than by others. Moreover, the high power distance is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the detected effect of being a director that signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increases the likelihood to in<sup>fl</sup>uence others’ security behaviours. The positive effect of ‘trust’ ties on information security in<sup>fl</sup>uence ties may be explained by the Vietnamese cultural value that nurtures group relationships as well. Nevertheless, these conjectures require more in-depth qualitative methods such as case study or ethnography to elaborate and verify.

With regard to directions for future research, behavioural security researchers interested in network analysis methods may consider replicating our study in a Western context. Given the different cultural traits, we anticipate interesting results that would contradict ours. For example, the homophily effect caused by working in the same department could be strong in XYZ, but network researchers may <sup>fi</sup>nd a weaker effect when examining the security in<sup>fl</sup>uence network of a <sup>fl</sup>atter organisation where joint projects and communications across departments are more common. Likewise, the homophily effect of genders, which was not found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in our study, may be particularly strong in contexts where culture restricts mixed-genders work collaborations. Evaluating the strength of information security in<sup>fl</sup>uence ties is another research venue that is de<sup>fi</sup>nitely worth exploring. In this research, we only captured binary network data that informs whether an employee can in<sup>fl</sup>uence another’s security behaviours. By investigating the magnitude of in<sup>fl</sup>uence while also considering other valued networks, researchers can further determine which networks can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly result in more information security in<sup>fl</sup>uence.

We believe that the network analysis approach deserves more attention in the behavioural security <sup>fi</sup>eld, particularly when identifying and taking advantages of the sources of security in<sup>fl</sup>uence become more important for developing people-centric security workplaces and preventing the development of shadow security groups. We hope that our study could serve as a foundation to stimulate novel and practical research ideas in this <sup>fi</sup>eld.
