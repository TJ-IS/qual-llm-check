# The mechanism through which members with reconstructed identities become satisfied with a social network community: A contingency model

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Vanity Disinhibition Moderation Virtual identity Identity reconstruction Social network community

## A B S T R A C T

This study investigates members’ satisfaction with interest-based social network communities from an identityreconstruction perspective. Drawing upon the self-discrepancy theory and the regulatory focus theory, we theoretically modeled the factors (relating to identity reconstruction) that influence members’ satisfaction with social network communities and further investigated whether these influences on satisfaction are contingent upon how much the virtual identity is reconstructed. Data (n = 1063) were collected from six interest-based social network communities. Results support most main efects and further support two moderating efects. The efect of bridging social capital on satisfaction and the efect of privacy concern on satisfaction are contingent upon identity reconstruction.

## 1. Introduction

With the evolution of information technology, people are able to interact with others through a variety of new avenues. Specifically, social network platforms<sup>1</sup> have deeply permeated people’s daily lives and developed rapidly in the recent decade. For instance, Facebook had 1.71 billion monthly active users worldwide by the second quarter of 2016 [1], followed by QQ (operated in China) with 899 million monthly active users [2]. On social network platforms, individuals can not only maintain existing relationships but also build new connections with strangers [3]. According to the social capital theory [4], strangers are very valuable because they can provide new resources that the current friends do not have. An efficient way to meet and interact with a large number of strangers is to join a social network community [5]. For example, car lovers can join car communities to meet new friends and share ideas with each other. Interest-based social network com munity is one of the typical online social groups consisting of individuals who share common interests but are not connected with prior personal relationships [6]. In an interest-based social network community, people are connected beyond traditional bonds (such as personal relationships and geographical regions). Members could make new friends in the community and extend their social circle [6] and also could share/acquire information and knowledge from one another. Such a social network community is a combination of two Internetbased innovations: social networks and traditional online communities (i.e., online forums). On the one hand, derived from social network platforms, social network communities have the functions of social networks. On the other hand, social network communities have the functions of traditional online communities, i.e., sharing information/ knowledge among a large number of people who are likely strangers.

By using social network software, a community member can quickly build a social tie with a stranger in the social network community. An individual’s online profile is the first channel for other members to get some knowledge about him or her. Therefore, such an online profile is very important for a member, thus acting as his/her virtual identity in the social network community. As Selwyn [7] noted, most people might mispresent themselves online such as providing fake personal information to others. To present a better image or hide certain personal information, an individual may manipulate the information in the profile to build an online identity that is partly or even completely diferent from his or her real-world identity, which is referred to as online identity reconstruction [8,9]. Given that the profile is created by a member himself or herself, it is dificult to assess the validity of the personal information in the online profile [10]. Thus, an individual can reconstruct an online identity as s/he wishes. Then the member can use the reconstructed virtual identity to interact with other members in the social network community. By using a reconstructed virtual identity, some members may become more satisfied with participating in the social network community [9].

An interesting question then arises: what is the mechanism through which a member with a reconstructed virtual identity becomes satisfied with participating in the social network community? In particular, whether members’ satisfaction with participating in social network communities is contingent upon how much they reconstruct their virtual identity? Addressing these questions, the current study aims to contribute to the literature in two ways: (i) theoretically modeling the factors (relating to online identity reconstruction) that influence a member’s satisfaction in a social network community and (ii) investigating whether the influences on satisfaction are contingent upon how much the virtual identity is reconstructed in the social network community.

Relative to the extant literature, the current paper is distinguished because it investigates social network communities from a diferent perspective (i.e., identity). Although prior studies have investigated social network communities, they were conducted from a variety of perspectives rather than the perspective of identities. For example, some studies investigated social network communities from an educational perspective [11–14]. Other perspectives include the sense of community [15] and the social interaction between social network communities [6].

The current research is the first quantitative study examining user satisfaction in social network communities from the perspective of identities. In the physical world, traditionally, a person’s social circle cannot go beyond this person’s real identity (including occupation, wealth, social status, derived power, etc.) which determines the relative social position of this person within the society [16]. By contrast, as mentioned above, in online social network communities built based on interests, participants are from diferent places of the world and are typically strangers. The nature of such social network communities further provides a facilitating condition for those strangers to reconstruct their identity. Using a reconstructed virtual identity, an individual can get benefits that s/he cannot get in the physical world with his/her real identity [9], thereby becoming satisfied with participating in the social network communities. Thus, the key point here is identity. That is. the maior difference between these two environments (the physical world vs. social network communities) lies in the diference of the identities in the two environments. Therefore, the identity perspective is a good angle to explain the advantages ofered by social network communities and why participants are satisfied with partici pating in social network communities.

Further, members’ satisfaction in a social network community is important to the sustention and development of communities in particular and of the social network platform in general [17,18]. If members are satisfied with participating in a social network community, they will become active and may introduce more people to join, thereby making the community prosperous and also contributing to the prosperity of the social network platform as a whole. If members are not satisfied, conversely, the community cannot sustain.

This paper is structured as follows. First, the theoretical foundation used to frame this research is introduced. Then, a research model is proposed, and detailed hypotheses are presented. Subsequently, the research methodology is described. The results of data analysis are then reported, followed by the discussion of results, contributions, and limitations. The paper ends with a brief conclusion.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

Why do some participants reconstruct their virtual identity in social network communities? They may be unsatisfied with their real-world identity and thus want to make their virtual identity more ideal.

Alternatively, they want to escape from the regulations, responsibilities, or evaluations in the physical world [9]. This can be explained using the self-discrepancy theory [19].

## 2.1. Self-discrepancy theory

The self-discrepancy theory postulates three domains of the self: the actual self, the ideal self, and the ought self [19]. The actual self represents an individual’s self-concept. It is the identity that people actually possess. The ideal self reflects hopes, wishes, and aspirations, whereas the ought self reflects duties, responsibilities, and obligations. Both the ideal self and the ought self constitute significant standards for self-evaluation [20] and thereby are adopted as self-guides [21].

In the physical world, owing to various constraints, it is almost impossible for an individual to fully match his/her identity with the ought self and the ideal self in the self-guide. The part of identity that matches neither the ought-self guide nor the ideal-self guide is the selfdiscrepancy. Specifically, the gap between the actual self and the oughtself guide is referred to as the actual-ought discrepancy, whereas the gap between the actual self and the ideal-self guide is the actual-ideal discrepancy [19,22]. According to the self-discrepancy theory, a greater discrepancy will induce greater psychological discomforts [19]. In this case, to alleviate psychological discomfort, individuals will try their best to align their self-concept (i.e., actual self) with the self-guide (i.e., the ideal self and/or the ought self) [22]. Therefore, if individuals can achieve less self-discrepancy through reconstructing a virtual identity in a social network community, they will be more motivated and satisfied when participating in the community [9].

An individual’s self-discrepancy decreases when the actual-ideal and/or the actual-ought discrepancy decreases [19]. In social network communities, a member can reduce the actual-ideal discrepancy by reconstructing a virtual identity s/he dreams of. For example, an unemployed person can pretend to be a working professional. With a superior virtual identity, people can build up high self-esteem and perceive a high level of social value in the social network community [23,24], thereby becoming more satisfied with participating in the social network community. In addition, when a member reconstructs a virtual identity, certain real personal information is hidden. Then the member with a reconstructed virtual identity may feel less restrained by legislation, social norms, and responsibilities (i.e., the ought self) [7] because others may not know who he or she exactly is. Consequently, the ought-self guide diminishes in the social network community. Then the actual-ought discrepancy is likely to decrease because of the diminished ought-self guide [19,22]. On the basis of the self-discrepancy theory [19,22], both the reduced actual-ideal and actual-ought discrepancies will lead to better psychological status, which makes an individual more likely feel satisfied with participating in the social network community.

The process through which people align their self-concept (i.e., the actual self) with appropriate goals and standards (i.e., the ideal self and the ought self) is referred to as self-regulation [25]. Hence, online identity reconstruction in social network communities can be further explained using the regulatory focus theory [26,27], which proposes self-regulation processes for individuals. The regulatory focus theory is closely related to the self-discrepancy theory, given that both theories were proposed by the same scholar and based on the same concepts (i.e., the self-concept and self-guides) [19,22,26,27]. While the selfdiscrepancy theory provides an in-depth explanation of online identity reconstruction in social network communities, the regulatory focus theory provides a framework for all the factors (positive and negative outcomes) investigated in this study. The regulatory focus theory is explained in more detail below.

## 2.2. Regulatory focus theory

When people try to follow their ideal-self guide, they are actually approaching pleasure (e.g., make their dreams come true), and when people try to follow their ought-self guide, they are actually avoiding pain (such as avoid being punished by laws or criticized by others) [26,27]. But there are diferences in the processes when people try to approach pleasure and avoid pain. Therefore, the regulatory focus theory proposed two distinct but coexisting systems to frame individuals’ self-regulation processes: promotion focused and prevention focused [26–28].

Individuals with a promotion focus endeavor to pursue the presence of a positive outcome [25,28,29]. For example, using a reconstructed virtual identity to hide shortcomings and manipulate merits, some members can glorify themselves in a social network community to satisfy their vanity; they may pretend to be a successful person that is more likely to be admired by others. With a reconstructed virtual identity diferent from their physical identity, individuals can make new friends that they could not usually meet in real life, thereby increasing their bridging social capital. Individuals with a prevention focus endeavor to pursue the absence of a negative outcome [25,29]. For instance, in social network communities, some members may re construct their virtual identity to liberate themselves from social constraints and release inhibition (thereby they can express themselves freely). With a reconstructed virtual identity, people can also avoid privacy violations and risks resulting from identity theft.

As Arazy and Gellatly ([28], p. 92) noted, such promotion and prevention focuses “are related yet distinct motivational states” [30]. Thus, it is possible for a person “to experience high levels in one focus, simultaneously in both focuses, or in neither focus” ([28], p. 92 [26]).

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

As noted above, individuals are motivated to bring themselves in line with goals and standards (the ideal-self guide or ought-self guide) [19,22]. When people are engaged in promotion-focused regulation, their growth and development needs motivate them to endeavor to bring themselves into alignment with their ideal selves [25]; in so doing, they experience the pleasure of gain (such as the above-mentioned bridging social capital and vanity). By contrast, prevention-focused regulation is concerning satisfying security needs [28,29] such as the earlier-noted disinhibition, risk, and privacy concerns. When ob taining positive outcomes and being isolated from negative outcomes, people generate a feeling of satisfaction [27]. By incorporating these positive and negative outcomes, a research model (See Fig. 1) is proposed to investigate how a member with a reconstructed virtual identity becomes satisfied with participating in social network communities.

## 3.1. Vanity

Some individuals have needs to fulfill their vanity. Vanity has been defined as “an excessive concern for, and/or a positive (and perhaps inflated) view of one’s physical appearance and/or personal achievements” ([31], p. 612). People with vanity need are promotion focused with regard to this. They strategically present themselves and manip ulate their public image for positive feedback [32]. For example, to build a positive image, an individual selectively posts photos that display his/her physical attractiveness and social status [33,34]. Bareket-Bojmel et al. [35] also found that some individuals strategically disclose favorable personal information in their profiles and share positive life events. Individuals with a high level of vanity strongly desire to attract attention from others. They want their appearance and/or achievements be recognized not only by themselves but also by others [36,37].

As such, one condition for fulfilling vanity is that others can witness an individual’s display of his/her achievement or wealth. If a context can facilitate an individual to fulfill his/her vanity, this individual will be more likely to be satisfied with participating in this context. Established within social network platforms, social network communities are ideal places for people to display vanity, given that people have full control over their self-presentation and they have access to a large number of audiences [38]. In the social network community, a member can easily show his/her achievements related to the topics commonly concerning community members; in so doing, s/he feels his/ her achievement would be admired by others. Thereby, his/her vanity is fulfilled. Prior research has also suggested that vanity is a strong predictor of social network site use [39]. In a social network community, the social admiration and attention gained from other members may make a vain person more motivated and satisfied with participating in the community. The positive evaluation from others makes this vain member experience the pleasure of gain [26–28]. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1a. Vanity is positively associated with a member’s satisfaction with his/ her participation in a social network community

Online identity reconstruction may provide a facilitating condition for a vain person to be satisfied with participating in the social network community, given that other people’s impressions of a person are mostly based on what the person discloses in online communications [40]. With a reconstructed virtual identity, a person can hide unsatisfying characteristics, magnify his/her achievements, and present the ones s/he dreams of. To improve one's physical appearance, for example, a weak man can pretend to be strong by sharing pictures of a muscular man and telling others that is he. He can also act as a successful businessman, a powerful leader, etc. When an individual can reconstruct his/her identity (to magnify his/her achievements) in the social network community, this community will be a more ideal place for this individual to fulfill his/her needs for vanity and therefore be come satisfied with participating in the social network community. After reconstructing more ideal self in one's virtual identity, a greater proportion of the ideal self in the self-guide will be fulfilled, and the actual-ideal discrepancy (reflecting the gap between the actual self and the ideal-self guide) will decrease, thereby improving the individual’s psychological comfort [22]. Therefore, when an individual reconstructs his/her virtual identity, it is more likely that the person (who has the need for vanity) is satisfied with participating in the social network community. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1b. The efect of vanity on satisfaction is moderated by online identity reconstruction in such a way that the efect will be stronger among people who have reconstructed their virtual identity.

## 3.2. Bridging social capital

Bridging social capital is a set of resources that are embedded in external ties, whereas external ties refer to the connections out of a collectivity [41]. In essence, bridging social capital is embedded within a relationship established between two individuals who belong to two groups; these two groups are diferent (e.g., having diferent backgrounds or belonging to diferent fields) [42]. In social network communities, members come from diferent places and have diferent backgrounds. Therefore, it is possible that members in the social network community can make friends that are diferent from their friends in the real world, thereby increasing their external ties and bridging social capital. For example, prior studies have found that many people join the social network platforms to make new friends, although they can also maintain existing relationships there (e.g., [43,44]). Viewing the online profiles to search for the person they are interested in [45], people can easily make new friends in social network communities, thereby creating bridging social capital [46]. Bridging social capital is associated with many benefits, such as novel information and new perspectives and access to external resources [42,47]. Thus, people who want to obtain bridging social capital are promotion focused in this vein, given that the presence of positive outcomes will make them feel pleasure [26,27]. Accordingly, Chang and Zhu [48] have suggested that perceived bridging social capital can influence satisfaction. Therefore, it is very likely that individuals who gain bridging social capital in social network communities would feel satisfied with participating in the social network community. Then, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2a. Bridging social capital is positively associated with a member’s satisfaction with his/her participation in a social network community.

Online identity reconstruction may facilitate the efect of bridging social capital on an individual’s satisfaction with his/her participation in a social network community because the individual can make more friends from diferent areas by reconstructing an attractive identity with ideal aspects distinct from his/her real identity, thereby becoming more satisfied with participating in the social network community. In a social network community, for example, a cashier could construct a totally new identity in the other area (e.g., a lawyer) through which s/ he makes new friends in the new field. When a person can add more ideal-self and more distinct aspects to the reconstructed virtual identity, it is more likely that this person (with a more ideal and diferent identity) can make more friends with people of diferent backgrounds and thereby become satisfied with participating in the social network community [48]. Hence, the following moderation hypothesis is proposed:

H2b. The efect of bridging social capital on satisfaction is moderated by online identity reconstruction in such a way that the efect will be stronger among people who have reconstructed their virtual identity.

## 3.3. Disinhibition

In the physical world, people are restrained by social norms and regulations. In virtual settings (such as social network communities), by contrast, the increased disconnectedness from real-life contexts and lower levels of surveillance make people feel less inhibited by social norms and less responsible for their online actions [7]. They can say and do things that they would not usually say or do. For instance, prior studies have noted that people can express their true self on the Internet, thus disclosing what they really think and want [49] and sharing personal experiences and confessions [50,51]. The state in which people feel less restrained, loosen up, and express themselves more openly in the online setting is referred to as online disinhibition [40]. As a result, the ought-self guide (which reflects obligation, duty, or responsibility) in the virtual environment diminishes. Given the reduced ought-self guide, the actual-ought discrepancy (reflecting the gap between the actual self and the ought-self guide) is likely to decrease. According to the self-discrepancy theory, when the actual-ought discrepancy decreases, the disinhibited individual will be more comfortable psychologically [22] and thus become more motivated and satisfied [19,22]. For example, previous research has found that disinhibition encourages students, especially shy students, to participate in sensitive subjects more freely in online discussions [52]. It is also found that disinhibition and self-disclosure on emotions and experiences have a significant efect on reducing stress and depression [53]. The disinhibited online environment could be especially beneficial for people with low self-esteem in face-to-face communications, like introverted people or disabled persons [54–57]. When people take advantage of the efects of online disinhibition in social network communities, they avoid the potential negative outcomes of inhibition and are prevention focused. They enjoy themselves in such a less inhibited environment and thus may become more satisfied with participating in the social network community. Hence, the following hypothesis is proposed:

## H3a. Disinhibition is positively associated with a member’s satisfaction with his/her participation in a social network community.

The above-mentioned positive efect of disinhibition on satisfaction can be facilitated by online identity reconstruction in the social network community. In essence, when the individual reconstructs his/her identity (by hiding or fabricating personal information), it is more likely that this individual (who wants to be disinhibited) can escape from social evaluations and regulations [58] and thereby become satisfied with participating in the social network community. Being less inhibited by social evaluations and regulations due to the reconstructed virtual identity, it is easier for an individual to meet the ought-self guide and reduce the actual-ought discrepancy. The decreased actual ought discrepancy will lead to greater psychological comfort [22], which makes an individual more likely to be satisfied with participating in the social network community. Hence, identity reconstruction provides a facilitating condition for the efect of disinhibition on an individual’s satisfaction with participating in the social network community. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H3b. The efect of disinhibition on satisfaction is moderated by online identity reconstruction in such a way that the efect will be stronger among people who have reconstructed their virtual identity.

## 3.4. Privacy concerns

Privacy concern is an individual’s concern for “controlling the ac quisition and subsequent use of information” about him or her [59], thus reflecting an individual’s awareness and assessment of the potential risks of privacy violations [60]. When an individual participates in a social network community, s/he gives other community members access to his/her profile, which contains plenty of personal information. In the process of communicating with others in the community, an individual may also disclose some private information. Privacy concerns are more likely to arise when one’s personal information is used without his or her permission, or the personal information is not used properly [61]. Prior studies found that members of social network platforms are concerned about their privacy [62]. People who are concerned about privacy are prevention focused with regard to this [29] and would try hard to prevent negative outcomes (e.g., privacy violations) from happening. As revealed in prior research, for instance, they would take diferent measures to protect it, such as changing privacy settings to restrict the access of their profiles [63,64], controlling information disclosure in interactions [64], and deleting or blocking unfavorable friends [65]. Prior research has found that if people perceive that their privacy is not well protected when using the services of social network platforms, they will feel reluctant to use them [62,66]. Similarly, in a social network community, if a member is afraid of the misuse of his/her private information by others, the potential negative outcome will lead him/her to experience worry and anxiety [26,27], thereby afecting his/her satisfaction with participating in the social network community. Hence, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H4a. Privacy concerns are negatively associated with a member’s satisfaction with his/her participation in a social network community.

The negative efect of privacy concerns on an individual’s satisfac tion with his/her participation in a social network community may be contingent upon whether this individual reconstructs his/her virtual identity. It is found that providing inaccurate information (such as a fake name or fake age) and hiding sensitive information (such as contact information) are also used as privacy protection strategies [65,67]. If an individual reconstructs a brand-new identity by hiding or fabricating all personal information, then s/he is almost anonymous in the community; in this case, privacy concerns will be less likely to decrease his/her satisfaction with participating in a social network community. Thus the following hypothesis is proposed:

H4b. The efect of privacy concerns on satisfaction is moderated by online identity reconstruction in such a way that the efect will be weaker among people who have reconstructed their virtual identity and stronger among people who do not reconstruct their virtual identity.

## 3.5. Risk aversion

Hofstede and Bond [68] defined risk aversion as “the extent to which people feel threatened by ambiguous situations” (p. 419). People who are highly risk-averse usually feel vulnerable in uncertain situations and tend to avoid risk and ambiguity [69]. Risk-averse people are less likely to invest in risky assets and less likely to be self-employed [70]. They make conservative decisions to maintain low risk exposure [71]. They try hard to prevent risk and uncertainty, and thus, they are prevention focused with regard to this [29]. In social network com munities, highly risk-averse members may consider the interactions with strangers to be risky, given that it is uncertain whether the strangers are malicious. It has been found that perceived risk is negatively related to overall satisfaction with online services [72]. Thus, it is likely that risk aversion also negatively afects an individual’s sa tisfaction with participating in a social network community. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H5a. Risk aversion is negatively associated with a member’s satisfaction with his/her participation in a social network community.

When people perceive a high level of risk, they tend to manage the risk through risk reduction [73]. By reconstructing a virtual identity that is diferent from his/her real identity, a highly risk-averse member can discuss and share his/her ideas without personal disclosure [74] and reduce potential risks in interactions [24]. If the individual hides or fabricates all of his/her personal information, s/he is totally anonymous in the community; then the possibility for him/her to face risks is very low. In this case, even if this individual is highly risk-averse, risk aversion will not afect his/her satisfaction with participating in the social network community because there is no chance for him/her to face risks, given the fake identity. Hence, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H5b. The efect of risk aversion on satisfaction is moderated by online identity reconstruction in such a way that the efect will be weaker among people who have reconstructed their virtual identity.

Risk aversion reflects an individual’s perception about the uncertainty of outcomes [75]. In the context of social network communities, the privacy risk is salient to risk-averse members. Disclosing personal information increases the risk of potential loss. Individuals can be hoaxed, and malicious criminals might use their identities for fraudulent purposes [76]. The loss of control over private information induces uncertainty, and that is what risk-averse people try to avoid. Therefore, members with a high level of risk aversion may be more concerned about their privacy. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H6. Risk aversion is positively associated with privacy concerns.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Research site

The research site for this study was QQ social network communities. As a social network platform launched in China by Tencent Company in 1999, QQ has been developing rapidly. By the second quarter of 2016, QQ had approximately 899 million monthly active users worldwide [2]. Within QQ, individuals can create or join QQ communities based on their interests. Members can post messages in QQ communities, and the posted messages are received by all the community members. They can also view other members’ profiles through the member list. Any two members can start a private conversation and add each other as a friend when necessary.

A member’s profile within the QQ community is created by the member himself/herself; however, it is dificult for other members to check the validity of the information in the profile. By providing fake personal information, a member can construct a virtual identity in the QQ community as s/he wishes. Fig. 2 shows an example of QQ communities. There are 1856 members in the community, and 1059 members are online. As shown in the chatting area, for example, a member uses a penguin as his/her profile photo, and the other mem ber’s profile photo is a picture of cat. A community member’s name may comprise letters, numbers, and/or symbols. In this example community, no one uses his/her real name or real photo.

## 4.2. Measures

The survey measures for this study were derived from previously published studies and adapted for the context of social network com munities. The scales for vanity were adapted from Netemeyer et al. [31]. The disinhibition items were developed based on Denollet [77] Stunkard and Messick [78], and Bond et al. [79]. The items measuring bridging social capital were adapted from Williams [80]. Privacy concerns were assessed with items from Son and Kim [81], and risk aver sion was measured with items adapted from Bao et al. [82]. Finally, the satisfaction items were adapted from Wixom and Todd [83] and McKinney et al. [84]. The degree of agreement or disagreement with the survey items was normalized with a seven-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 4 = neutral, 7 = strongly agree). Identity reconstruction was measured using two items: one was adapted from Qian and Scott [85]. The other item was developed to reflect the per centage of a member’s virtual identity in the QQ community being the same as his/her real identity in the physical world (See Appendix A for details).

The target sample of this study is the members in QQ communities, who are Chinese. Given this, the survey items were carefully translated from English to Chinese and back-translated into English. After comparing the two versions of items several times, the discrepancies were resolved. Backward translation ensures consistency between items [86,87]. To refine the initial version of survey items, a pilot study was conducted, and it involved 121 respondents. The internal consistency and discriminant validity of items were assessed. On the basis of the pretest in the pilot study, the wording of survey items was refined, and the item with low factor loading was removed.

## 4.3. Respondents

This study used a web-based survey to recruit participants. To elicit respondents, online survey invitations were sent to six QQ communities. The selected communities were interest-based communities such as communities of online gamers (where players can discuss the know hows of the games with each other) and communities of traveler (where travelers can share their experience about travelling). These QQ communities are open and everyone can join. The topics discussed in these communities do not focus on any particular travel agency or game brand. In essence, nobody can force members to focus on a specific company or brand, given the openness of the communities. These communities were selected because of the big amount of community members (each has more than 1500 members) and high active parti cipation level (each exceeding 1000 messages in 5 min during peak times). The survey invitations were posted as public messages during of-peak times (accessible to all the members) in the target communities for a period of 1 month. Because the number of members in the six communities floated during the period of data collection (old members left and new members joined), it is not possible to calculate the total amount of person who have access to the survey. Thus, the response rate cannot be calculated. Finally, 1063 questionnaires were collected from the six communities. Among the 1063 respondents, 511 (accounting for 48.1%) were male, and 552 (accounting for 51.9%) were female. Their ages ranged from under 20 years old to above 50 years old. In addition, they had diferent educational backgrounds, varying from high school (or below) to a doctorate degree (or above) (See Appendix B for detailed demographic information).

To test potential nonresponse bias, we compared early and late responses, as suggested by Armstrong and Overton [88]. As noted above, data were collected from six interest-based social network communities. Because it took time to convince and get approval from the moderator(s) of the social network community, online survey invitations were not posted at the same time in the six communities. Hence, potential nonresponse bias was tested in each community, respectively (with the six communities numbered from 1 to 6). Specifi cally, t-tests were conducted to examine diferences in demographic data and key constructs between early (first quarter) and late (last quarter) responses in each community. For the demographic information (age, education, work experience, and tenure in the community), the diferences were not salient. Only age and education diferences were found in one community (community no.4). No significant differences were found in other demographic information.

For the key constructs (i.e., vanity, bridging social capital, disinhibition, privacy concerns, risk aversion, satisfaction, and online identity reconstruction), the diferences were not salient, either. Except the significant diferences found in satisfaction and privacy concerns in only one community (community no. 2), no significant diferences were found in the other five communities (except for few diferences in four single items). A possible reason for the few diferences found between the early and late responses might be that the time participants saw the online survey invitation were not the same, given that participants have their own daily work and family obligations in the physical world [89]. Therefore, nonresponse bias is not a concern in this study.

## 5. Results

We used partial least squares (PLS) to test the model; the specific software program used was SmartPLS 2.0 [90]. PLS is widely used for data analysis in the field of Information Systems (IS) because theoretical knowledge in the discipline is "still in the formative stages where theoretical models and measures are often simultaneously developed " and tested ([91], p. xii). PLS is accepted as a method for testing a theory newly adapted in a field. Additionally, PLS is capable of testing complex models with multiple direct efects and multiple moderation efects together [92–95], which suits the requirement of the current study. Further, compared with multiple regressions, PLS can assess the reliability and validity of construct measures and test the relationships among the constructs simultaneously [96]. Thus, we chose PLS to analyze the data.

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

This study has investigated how the factors relating to online identity reconstruction influence members’ satisfaction with participating in social network communities on the basis of two theories: selfdiscrepancy theory and regulatory focus theory. While the self-discrepancy theory provides an in-depth explanation of identity reconstruction in social network communities, the regulatory focus theory provides a framework for all the factors (positive and negative outcomes) investigated in this study.

First, the self-discrepancy theory gives a theoretical explanation for why the context of social network communities provides a facilitating environment for people to reduce their self-discrepancy, thereby alle viating their psychological discomfort (and eventually they become more satisfied with participating in the social network community).

Specifically, in social network communities, when one manipulates or hides one’s personal information in the online profile (i.e., reconstruct one’s virtual identity), such a reconstructed virtual identity can help reduce either one’s actual-ideal discrepancy or actual-ought discrepancy. For example, an individual may reconstruct a virtual identity s/he dreams of to fulfill his/her vanity [31], thereby reducing actual-ideal discrepancy. On the other hand, an individual may re construct a virtual identity by hiding certain real personal information, then others do not know who he or she exactly is, and consequently, this member feels less restrained by legislation, social norms, and responsibilities (i.e., ought self) [7]. In so doing, this member reduces the actual-ought discrepancy. When actual-ideal discrepancy or actualought discrepancy decreases, the individual’s psychological discomfort decreases, and thereby he or she becomes satisfied with participating in the social network community [19]. In essence, it is such a context (i.e., social network communities) that provides an ideal environment for people to reconstruct their virtual identity to reduce self-discrepancy and thus become more satisfied [22].

The regulatory focus theory proposed two self-regulation processes: promotion focused (pursuing positive outcomes) and prevention fo cused (avoiding negative outcomes) [26–28], thereby providing a fra mework to categorize the positive and negative outcomes investigated in this study: promotion focused (vanity and bridging social capital) and prevention focused (disinhibition, privacy concerns, and risk aversion).

## 6.1. Findings

The results of the data analyses validated the hypotheses that were proposed based on the two theories. According to the results, the relationship between vanity and a member’s satisfaction with his/her participation in the social network community is positive, thus sug gesting that a member tends to be satisfied when his/her need for vanity is fulfilled. By showing of achievements in a social network community, a member can gain positive outcomes such as attention and admiration from other members, thereby making him/her feel satisfied. This finding is in line with previous research, which suggested that vain people are more gratified when they are able to fulfill the needs of gaining respects [101]. Additionally, the results suggest that bridging social capital plays a vital role in building members’ satisfaction in social network communities. With the increased bridging capital, individuals can gain various positive outcomes (such as access to new information and new resources), thus making them become satisfied with participating in the social network community. This finding proves the importance of bridging social capital [48]. These findings (i.e., the positive relationship between the two factors and satisfaction) are in line with the above reasoning based on the self-discrepancy theory and the regulatory focus theory.

Moreover, the results indicate that disinhibition is an important predictor of members’ satisfaction in social network communities. When people are able to express themselves and behave without restraint in social network communities, they tend to feel satisfied. This is consistent with a prior finding that the more a person is self-disclosed in Facebook, the more s/he is satisfied with Facebook [18]. In addition, the results of this study demonstrate that the relationship between privacy concerns and a member’s satisfaction with participating in social network communities is negative. This finding is also consistent with a previous study that suggested privacy concerns are negatively associated with users’ continuance intentions regarding social network sites [62]. These two findings (about disinhibition and privacy concerns) are in line with the regulatory focus theory, which proposed that individuals with a certain prevention focus seek for the absence of negative outcome about that ([26,27]).

The results of the study also reveal that people with a high level of risk aversion tend to be concerned about their privacy. To achieve the goal of protecting their privacy, highly risk-averse individuals try to reduce potential risks by engaging in prevention-focused behaviors to avoid the pain of privacy loss [26,27], such as withholding personal information when communicating with others in the social network community. Not as expected, the association between risk aversion and satisfaction is not significant. The first plausible explanation is that risk aversion is a personal characteristic. People with the characteristic of risk aversion may try to avoid uncertainty in all situations, not specifically in social network communities. The second plausible explanation is that user satisfaction may be a mixed bag because individuals who participate in the interest-based social network community may want to make friends and also seek knowledge. In other words, both “making friends” and “seeking knowledge” may contribute to a participant’s “satisfaction with the social network community.” Risk aversion may have a negative efect on “making friends” because highly risk-averse members may consider the interactions with strangers to be risky (for instance. it is uncertain whether the strangers are malicious). whereas risk aversion may have a positive efect on knowledge seeking due to a desire to avoid looking silly.<sup>2</sup> Eventually, a combination of a negative efect (e.g., the efect of risk aversion on making friends) and a positive effect (e.g.. the effect of risk aversion on knowledge seeking) might lead to the insignificant efect of risk aversion on satisfaction.

Furthermore, this study investigated not only the direct efects of these factors on satisfaction (proposed on the basis of the self-discrepancy theory and the regulatory focus theory) but also the moderation efects of online identity reconstruction on them (i.e., the direct efects of these factors on satisfaction). The results indicate that the positive relationship between bridging social capital and satisfaction with social network communities is contingent upon online identity reconstruction; this positive relationship is stronger among members who have reconstructed their virtual identity to greater extent because a reconstructed identity can help a member make friends with people who have a diferent background. In addition, the negative relationship between privacy concerns and satisfaction is also contingent on online identity reconstruction in social network communities. Among members who have reconstructed their virtual identity to greater extent, the efect of privacy concerns on satisfaction is weaker. In line with the selfdiscrepancy theory, the findings regarding the contingency efect of identity reconstruct have extended the existing literature related to online identity reconstruction [102–104], thus highlighting the mod erating role of online identity reconstruction.

## 6.2. Contributions to the literature

The current study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, this paper investigates user satisfaction in social network communities from the identity-reconstruction perspective, which difers from the perspectives used in prior studies on user satisfaction in social network communities. Specifically, this study has proposed a research model to investigate how the factors relating to online identity reconstruction influence members’ satisfaction with participating in social network communities. Second, this paper uses the self-discrepancy theory and the regulatory focus theory as the theoretical foundation. A combination of the two theories in explaining online identity reconstruction and user satisfaction is new in the literature. The findings of this study are in line with the two theories (i.e., self-discrepancy theory and regulatory focus theory). Moreover, this study has introduced certain new constructs (such as vanity and risk aversion) to research on online identity reconstruction in social network communities.

More importantly, this study has investigated whether the above influences on satisfaction with participating in social network communities are contingent upon how much a virtual identity is reconstructed. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that has investigated such contingency efects. As a contingency study, this work provides a nuanced understanding of the mechanism through which a member with a reconstructed online identity becomes satisfied with participating in a community. For example, this study has found that the positive relationship between bridging social capital and satisfaction with social network communities is contingent on online identity reconstruction, whereas the negative relationship between privacy concerns and satisfaction is also contingent upon online identity reconstruction. This study has advanced our understanding of online identity reconstruction in social network communities in particular and identity reconstruction in online environments in general.

## 6.3. Implications for practice

The findings of this study have important implications for practitioners about how to better satisfy participants in social network communities. On the basis of the positive relationship between vanity and satisfaction, practitioners could develop features that enable community members to show of themselves, such as leaderboards, which display members’ achievements through rankings. The efect of bridging social capital also indicates possible solutions to improve members’ satisfaction with social network communities, such as the devel opment of features that help members to make new friends (e.g., friend recommendation).

By understanding that disinhibition is positively associated with social network community satisfaction, service providers could employ functions that free members from social pressures. For example, service providers could enable social network community members to post anonymous messages every day so that the members can freely express themselves. At the same time, the operator of the social network community should monitor and remove ofensive messages, given the concern that disinhibition may lead to flaming and cyberbullying in the community [105,106]. For example, a famous social network “Secret” removes any messages that are defamatory or insensitive about other people [107]. Improving privacy protection mechanisms (e.g., highlighting privacy settings) may also help to make community members satisfied.

Considering the moderating efects of identity reconstruction, we suggest service providers give community members freedom to reconstruct their virtual identity. Then, it would be easier for members to gain bridging social capital and protect their privacy, thereby becoming more satisfied with participating in the social network community.

When members are satisfied, they will stay and induce more people to join the community, and thereby, the social network community becomes prosperous and successful. As a result, the social networking company can maximize its profits.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

It should be acknowledged that this study has some limitations. First, like most studies in IS field, the current research is a cross-sectional study, and thus has similar limitations as other IS studies do. Future longitudinal studies could be conducted to investigate social network communities. For instance, a longitudinal study can help to examine the long-term change in the relationships between social network community members. In addition, given the cross-sectional nature of the data, this study examined the moderation efects of identity reconstruction only at the individual level (i.e., investigating the diferences among individuals). Hence, future research can implement a longitudinal study to investigate the moderation efects of online identity reconstruction at the collective level: at an early stage, a social network community requires members to input real personal information in their online profile, and then several months (or years) later, this social network community allows members to freely manipulate personal information as they wish. Then, this longitudinal study (with data collected in two stages) can test the moderation efect of identity construction at the collective level, i.e., test the diferences of the community as a whole between the two stages. Without such a longitudinal study, the current research could investigate only the diferences among individuals.

We also acknowledge that identities are socially created. Although the virtual identity can be reconstructed (by manipulating personal information) in the online social network community, the person behind the reconstructed online identity is still the same person who is also living in the physical world. Thus, social norms in the physical world can still have an influence on this person and thereby the online identity. An interesting direction for future research would be to investigate how the nature of the online social network community and the social norms of the physical world interact to afect identity reconstruction. Moreover, we acknowledge that the social network community is a complex environment, and user satisfaction with par ticipating in the social network community is also a complex phenomenon. The current study provided only a perspective and thus cannot draw a complete picture. Many other factors such as speed of the Web server, ease of use, and rich functionalities of the social network software can also afect users’ satisfaction in the social network community. Particularly, the ease of use (part of Technology Acceptance Model) would have a large influence on users’ satisfaction [108,109]. However, the current study is still valuable because it provides a new and diferent perspective, which can complement other perspectives to draw a more complete picture of the complex phenomenon. In future research, it would be an interesting direction to investigate the relationship between online identity reconstruction and the general use of the social network platform.

Given that this is the first quantitative study measuring online identity reconstruction, there is no well-established construct in the literature. Thus, we developed this construct by (1) adopting an item from Qian and Scott [85] and (2) developing the other item, which reflects the percentage of a member’s virtual identity in the social network community being the same as his/her real identity in the physical world. As shown in Appendix A, these two items measured online identity reconstruction in a reversed way for the following two reasons. First, given that the first item adopted from Qian and Scott [85] was measured in a reversed way, it is better to measure the second item in the same way. Second, the way that the second item was arranged is consistent with the way of people’s thinking: 1 (10% the same), 2 (20% the same), 3 (30% the same), …, 10 (100% the same). In other words, the number is consistent with the percentage. $\mathrm { I f } \ ^ { \mathrm { ~ \textit ~ { ~  ~ } ~ } }$ indicates 90% the same (while “9” indicates 10% the same), however, respondents would have to reverse their thinking; this makes it more dificult for respondents to answer the question. Of course, we can use “1” to indicate 10% diferent, “2” to indicate 20% diferent, and “9” to indicate 90% diferent, but it is easier for people to imagine how much is the same (than to imagine how much is diferent) in their mind.<sup>3</sup>

To ensure its reliability and validity, we conducted a pilot study (with 121 participants) to validate it together with other constructs. Then, this construct was further validated using the data collected from the field in the formal study. Although two-item constructs are not perfect, they are acceptable and used in IS studies published in top journals such as MIS Quarterly (e.g., [110,111]). Given the foundation provided by the current study, future research could further validate and enrich this construct (e.g., measuring the extent of online identity reconstruction using a seven-point Likert scale) in other contexts.

At the concept level, additionally, future research is suggested to investigate the other two relevant but diferent concepts: self reconceptualization [112] and identity falsification [113]. Previous re search found that people may reconceptualize themselves after a brain injury or disability, thus resulting in identity reconstruction in the real world [112,114]. But the real-world identity reconstruction is restricted by the physical characteristics and the corporal body [104]. However, for online identity reconstruction (examined in the current study), the person does not need to reconceptualize himself/herself, but rather, s/ he only needs to change the information in the online profile. In addition, some researchers proposed the concept of “identity falsification,” which refers to creating a fake identity for deceptive purposes [113]. Compared to identity falsification, which highlights the negative side, online identity reconstruction has a relatively neutral connotation. In essence, it has both a dark side (e.g., deceive others, catfishing) and a positive side (such as free-expression, mitigate privacy concerns, etc.).

This study is based on field data with a large sample size, which enhances the relevance of the findings [115,116]. As a field study, however, this research cannot manipulate the predictor variable; this is also a limitation of this study. Future research could use experiments to manipulate the degree of identity reconstruction for the same person and examine its efects. The members in QQ communities are mostly Chinese, and thus, the findings of this study have reflected the Chinese culture in social network communities. The findings can help social networking companies understand the Chinese culture to serve the Chinese market and maximize their profits. However, we acknowledge that the specific culture may limit the generality of the findings to social network communities in other cultures. Future research could be con ducted in other cultures to replicate this study and validate its results.

## 6.5. Conclusion

This study has provided a nuanced analysis of the mechanism through which a member with a reconstructed identity becomes satisfied with participating in a social network community. Drawing upon the self-discrepancy theory [19,22] and the regulatory focus theory [26,27], this study has revealed how the factors relating to online identity reconstruction (such as vanity, disinhibition, bridging social capital, and privacy concerns) influence a member’s satisfaction with participating in a social network community. Furthermore, this study has shown that the efect of bridging social capital on satisfaction is contingent upon the extent that the virtual identity is reconstructed in such a way that the efect will be stronger among people who have reconstructed their virtual identity. The efect of privacy concerns on satisfaction is also contingent upon online identity reconstruction in such a way that the efect will be weaker among people who have reconstructed their virtual identity. Such a nuanced understanding is useful for informing the operators of social networking platforms as well as the coordinators of social network communities about how they could satisfy participants in social network communities.
