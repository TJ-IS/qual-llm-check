## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Coopetition Inter-organizational IT Knowledge networking Network social Capital

## A B S T R A C T

By enabling more e<sup>fi</sup>cient information and knowledge sharing and reinforcing the shared goals for participating members, inter-organizational information technology (IT) becomes instrumental to both accomplishing coopetitive outcomes and avoiding the pitfalls of partner opportunism. We posit that as network social capital is accumulated in technology-enabled coopetition networks, collaboration will enhance the strategic value of outcomes and positively a<sup>f</sup>ect goal convergence among network users. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings illustrate that the role of technology in coopetition extends beyond facilitating knowledge exchange and may play a vital role in the strategic development of <sup>fi</sup>rms pursuing coopetitive options.

## 1. Introduction

There is a growing understanding of the extensive role of information technology (IT) in facilitating knowledge sharing at the individual and group levels [1,2]. Particularly, as teams increase the use of IT, they can more e<sup>f</sup>ectively combine the knowledge of members to create project-level knowledge that is better suited to contribute to team and organizational goals [2]. Although extant research emphasizes the role of IT in facilitating individual and team-level knowledge sharing, less is understood regarding its role in managing knowledge across <sup>fi</sup>rm boundaries.

Collaboration among competitors (i.e. coopetition) is a widely uti lized strategic option that provides <sup>fi</sup>rms unique avenues of accessing resources that would otherwise be too onerous to obtain [3,4]. While historically a tactic reserved for relatively resource-abundant <sup>fi</sup>rms, coopetition has become increasingly accessible to a broader population of <sup>fi</sup>rms, as advances in inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm communication and social networking technologies (e.g [5].) have decreased barriers to forming coopetitive relationships. This trend coincides with the rise of open applications of inter-organizational IT (e.g. [6]). These platforms largely eschew strict formalized avenues of knowledge sharing to leverage the social capital of members in order to facilitate socialization and collaboration by providing e<sup>fi</sup>cient communication channels and protections against the improper access and use of shared knowledge [7].

Recent <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this domain are promising, as evidenced by scholarly interest in open innovation networks that draw on the knowledge capital of teams – whose membership span <sup>fi</sup>rm boundaries – to actively discover and transfer knowledge with external partners via collaborative innovation and communication systems [8]. Further, related research emphasizes the coordination and control mechanisms enabled by IT to manage risk in knowledge sharing across organizational boundaries [7,9]. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings illustrate the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role of technology in facilitating contemporary coopetition networks in protecting against threats of knowledge hoarding and opportunism (e.g. [10]), while simultaneously promoting innovation and strategic agility among collaborating partners.

We extend these <sup>fi</sup>ndings and posit that the assumptions underlying the threats of coopetition are not inherent to these agreements in general, but instead are an artifact of bounded reliability in partner selection and the resulting information asymmetries therein. Hence, the threats to coopetition may be partially mitigated by properly designed mechanisms for managing information and knowledge exchange (see for example; [9]). Further, while the resource dependence of innovation-minded resource-de<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fi</sup>rms makes them likely to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the exchange provided through coopetition ([11]; Lechner et al., 2014), these constraints also make <sup>fi</sup>rms less capable of managing partner risks. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, as dependence limits the extent to which resourcede<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fi</sup>rms (such as non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs)) are able to e<sup>f</sup>ectively exert power and minimize risk within coopetitive arrangements via e<sup>f</sup>ective contracting [3].

Recent <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest this disparaging relationship may be ameliorated with suitable technological infrastructure [12,13]. Con temporary IT applications can reduce the resource burden necessary to facilitate reliable and transparent information exchange [5]. IT can accomplish this by reducing information asymmetries and promoting transparency allowing for more e<sup>f</sup>ective partner monitoring. Along these lines, we posit that technology-enabled knowledge sharing in a coopetition network can at least partially address the risks of traditional coopetition relationships by reducing information asymmetries and threats of knowledge hoarding (e.g [14].).

While there is a growing body of research surrounding inter-organizational IT – predominantly focusing on supply-chain partnership (e.g. [15,16]) – there is little, if any, research of IT applications in coopetition networks, a gap the current research seeks to <sup>fi</sup>ll. In doing this, we empirically investigate a technology-enabled coopetition net work of non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t SMEs. The characteristic resource constraints and willingness to adopt novel technologies make SMEs a key population for studying contemporary technology-enabled coopetitive trends [17]. SMEs in a technology-enabled coopetition network can voluntarily exchange knowledge coordinated entirely through a transparent knowledge-sharing platform. We explore technology-enabled knowledge sharing and goal convergence in a network of non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t SMEs to address the following research question: Does technology-enabled coopeti tion networking facilitate more efective knowledge sharing and promote goal convergence of participating firms?

In addressing this question, our study contributes to the IT strategy literature in three ways. First, we empirically investigate the value of inter-organizational IT in accumulating network social capital (as re-<sup>fl</sup>ected in network legitimacy, trust, and resilience). Second, we examine the role of inter-organizational IT in facilitating knowledge exchange in the context of coopetition networks. Third, this study contributes to the inter-organizational IT research by expanding the boundary from the predominately emphasized large resource-laden <sup>fi</sup>rms to less explored resource-de<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fi</sup>rms.

## 2. Theoretical framework

Exploring the processes through which technology-enabled coopetition may impact processes and outcomes can best be done using social capital theory. Organizational social capital theory [18] is used to explain interactions among coopetition network members and how participating organizations’ contributions collectively bene<sup>fi</sup>t networks. Social capital is generally analyzed via three dimensions: cognitive, relational, and structural [19]. The cognitive dimension refers to resources that provide participating actors with shared representations, interpretations, and systems of meaning. These shared meanings, values, and norms are conducive to actors eschewing the pursuit of immediate self-interested outcomes in order to prioritize mutually bene <sup>fi</sup>cial collaboration. The relational dimension refers to relationships that develop within a network through social interactions and en compasses the extent to which trust, obligation, and reciprocity exist between actors. The structural dimension refers to social capital derived via the structural con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the network and concerns the structure for creating opportunities to generate collective bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

In the view of developing social capital in a coopetition network, it is essential to consider how technology-enabled networking may alter the accumulation of cognitive, relational, and structural social capital. Particularly, contemporary research recognizes that collaborative technologies shift the nature of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration from centrally controlled contractual activities to de-centralized social phenomena surrounding the engagement of individual <sup>fi</sup>rms [20]. Recent research has illustrated the potential of this approach by forgoing the traditional focus on speci<sup>fi</sup>c transactions and cause and e<sup>f</sup>ect outcomes (for review see [10]), in preference for studying competitive dynamics within speci<sup>fi</sup>c ecosystems (for notable examples, see [21,22]). While the structure of these systems remains of primary interest, the social capital emergent among network users remains an important, though under researched, component necessary for motivating knowledge sharing among users [20,23] and the sustained relevance of technology-enabled knowledge-sharing networks [24].

As such, we direct our inquiry toward understanding coopetitive network social capital as perceived by the individual members engaged in active collaboration. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, network legitimacy, trust, and resilience are viewed as critical network characteristics that re<sup>fl</sup>ect various aspects of social capital, which we posit informs the network’s processes and outcomes. We therefore focus on network social capital as a higher order metaconstruct re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the network legitimacy, trust, and resilience as perceived by individual members in a technology-enabled coopetitive network.

## 2.1. Network legitimacy

Network legitimacy refers to network actors’ perceptions of the coopetition network to be desirable, proper, or appropriate, with regard to the instrumentality of shared norms, values, and beliefs in achieving desired outcomes [25]. Perceptions of network legitimacy are fundamental to successful inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaborations [26]. This is due to the fact that in order to motivate the commitment of resources necessary for successful coopetition, individual network members must view the network, and by proxy their fellow members, as legitimate partners able and willing to contribute to collective outcomes [26]. However, network legitimacy is not only an antecedent to engaging in a coopetitive network, but it also has a recursive e<sup>f</sup>ect in that perceived network legitimacy is enhanced as a result of successful coopetitive out comes. This is evidenced by the fact that perceived legitimacy is not only necessary for the initiation of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration in general, but is also a key indicator of its continuation [19]. Extending these arguments, we posit that perceived network legitimacy re<sup>fl</sup>ects the cognitive social capital of a coopetitive network by informing members of the proper standards of exchange and normative behaviors. Standards and norms are a primary indicator that individual members possess shared representations, interpretations, and systems of meaning regarding the collective goals, rights, and utility of the network as a whole.

As technology-enabled knowledge sharing is increasingly predicated on standards of transparency and regulation, members view social networks as increasingly legitimate [27]. This suggests that technology enabled coopetition can facilitate and promote e<sup>fi</sup>cient engagement among network actors necessary for enhancing shared understanding of values and norms. Thus, open knowledge-sharing networks, such as the coopetition network studied in the present research, can become in creasingly long term and assist the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm relationships to be more permanent [7,28]. As a result, perceived network legitimacy indicates not only the potential for collaborative outcomes but also the alignment of individual <sup>fi</sup>rm strategies with the collective behaviors of the net work. Additionally, over time, there is an accumulation of cognitive social capital of individual network members.

## 2.2. Network trust

Network trust refers to the risk willingness and vulnerability of member organizations when dealing with the coopetition network (e.g. [29]), by trusting the information provided by other organizations and their positive intentions for its use. Network trust has been widely recognized as a critical factor that a<sup>f</sup>ects actor’s strategies in a coopetition network. Trust is required before organizations commit valuable resources to the network and provides actor’s con<sup>fi</sup>dence that partners will curb opportunistic behaviors (e.g [30].). Research extending the construct of partner trust to the network level (e.g. social trust [31];) suggests that member perceptions of network trust simultaneously consider two aspects: the trust perceptions of individual network members, and trust in the e<sup>fi</sup>cacy of the network itself. When partner trust at the network level exists, it aids in establishing social norms and standards that encourage transparency and collaboration which can serve as a check on how knowledge is accessed and used by network members, thus limiting the risk posed by opportunistic partners [31]. This suggests that network trust re<sup>fl</sup>ects the relational social capital generated through engagement in a coopetitive network.

Relational social capital emphasizes the relationships between actors developed through a history of interactions, $\mathrm { i . e . , }$ the extent to which trust, obligation, and reciprocity exist [19,32]. In the context of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration, network trust is vital for its ability to facilitate collaborations among members as it encapsulates the extent to which knowledge exchanges among network members are reciprocally perceived truthful and valuable. Prior research suggests that relational exchanges between partners can indirectly build trust through enhancing familiarity and communication, even when network members are only indirectly tied [15,26]. This suggests that network trust is particularly informative of the relational standards within a network. Hence, we posit that network trust serves as a primary re<sup>fl</sup>ection of the rela tional social capital of coopetition network members.

## 2.3. Network resilience

Network resilience refers to the network actors’ perceptions of the sustained relevance and adaptability of the coopetition network structure for ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the individual and collective goals of its members (Christo<sup>f</sup>erson, 2012; [33]). In the case of technology-enabled coopetition, it refers to the perceptions of individual members that the social network and underlying technology are suitable for facilitating e<sup>fi</sup>cient and reliable knowledge exchange among members. Additionally, the network and underlying technologies must create opportunities for engagement in order to generate collective bene<sup>fi</sup>ts both now and in the future.

Network resilience is concerned with collaborative outcomes, rather than with network actors. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, network resilience is the ability of the network technology, protocols, and control mechanisms to provide a platform that satis<sup>fi</sup>es the needs of each individual network member that is conducive to bene<sup>fi</sup>cial individual and collective out comes. Network resilience perceptions are reinforced by both the current relevance and adaptability of the network, the eagerness of members wanting to engage in the network, and the expectation that network protocols and norms are relevant to achieving outcomes (Christo<sup>f</sup>erson, 2012; [34]). We therefore posit that network resilience re<sup>fl</sup>ects the structural dimension of social capital in three ways. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is by informing member perceptions of their con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the structure, adaptability, and stability of the network. The second is by aligning network members with network protocols and norms. The third is de monstrating the capacity for the network itself to create and manage opportunities for collective bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

## 3. Hypothesis development

Building from our foundation of social capital theory, we argue that network legitimacy, trust, and resilience re<sup>fl</sup>ect the cognitive, relational, and structural dimensions of network social capital in tech nology-enabled coopetitive networks. Extending arguments from extant work recognizing the multi-dimensionality of the social capital meta construct in contemporary research [32,35,36]; we posit that individual network members’ perceptions of the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order factors of network legitimacy, trust, and resilience re<sup>fl</sup>ect in a second-order network social capital. Further, the second-order network social capital construct will inform various network outcomes. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, as network social capital is enhanced, members will perceive a greater strategic utility from coopetitive resource coordination and thus become more willing to commit resources to the collective goals of that network. We argue that actors perceiving greater social capital gains from their network association will utilize the resources provided by the network to evolve their strategy in a way that complements their collective capabilities and prioritizes the goals of the network as a whole.

We also contend that both shared goals and normative knowledge sharing are primary outcomes of network social capital. When actors perceive networks as legitimate, trustworthy, and resilient, they are more likely to maintain a proactive reliance on the network resources, even in the absence of immediate and commensurate returns. Much like the intention to adopt, a new technology requires the adopter to perceive the technology as valuable in terms of both objective utility and expected future utility via aligned long-term needs and goals [37,38]; members must perceive network social capital within a coopetitive network in order to commit resources to it

Just as network social capital increases, so too will the extent to which actors exchange knowledge resources and rely on the knowledge they derive from the network in shaping strategic goals. While extant coopetition research suggests this relationship may be hindered by partner opportunism and knowledge hoarding [39], technology-enabled network social capital provides an adequate infrastructure and assurances to transparency that directs partner action with less threat of adverse selection, partner shirking, or dishonesty [40].

However, in the context of coopetition networks, SMEs are less likely to commit resources to inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration as they, and their partners, often face immediate survival threats, forcing SMEs to eschew high potential collaborative options [41]. While survival threats remain a concern, the enhancement of network social capital through perceptions of network legitimacy, trust, and resilience will at least partially ameliorate these concerns for network actors. Thus, network actors will be motivated to engage in the coopetitive network as a more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant component in their strategy and will shape their strategic goals accordingly.

Thus, as wider swaths of <sup>fi</sup>rms increasingly engage in coopetition, we expect their overall strategic goals to evolve to greater align with a coopetition approach (e.g. [86]). In other words, network social capital – re<sup>fl</sup>ected by network legitimacy, trust, and resilience – promotes the emergence of normative strategic co-evolution among network actors and is thus indicative of goal convergence among coopetitive network members.

Hypothesis 1. Network social capital, as composed of network legitimacy, trust, and resilience, is positively related to goal convergence in coopetition networks.

Goal convergence requires that members coordinate their activities to complement the strategic resources provided by the network. Hence, while network social capital is necessary, it is the commitment of knowledge resources to the network and the leveraging of those knowledge resources within the organization that facilitates strategic co-evolution and goal convergence. The extent a <sup>fi</sup>rm is inclined to share knowledge within a network is dependent on both social norms within the network and the actors’ beliefs regarding the perceived value of future resources from partners [42,43]. When resource commitment in the network is expected to enhance competitive positioning [43], or diminish resource de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies [42], we expect enhanced knowledge sharing among network actors, even within networks lacking prosharing norms (e.g [44].).

This view echoes the tenants of social capital theory, suggesting that the shared norms and values within the coopetition network as a whole represent, primarily, the cognitive social capital desired by network actors. Applying this logic to coopetition networks, when actors perceive greater network social capital, they are motivated to commit greater knowledge resources toward the collective goals of the network.

Notably, prior theorizing suggests that shared goals foster knowledge transfer [35] and empirical research suggests that the presence of common goals increases the intention to share knowledge [15,31]. Hence, our hypothesis that knowledge sharing results in goal convergence is an ostensible reversal of the commonly studied direction of this relationship. This discrepancy is owed to the idiosyncratic context of coopetition wherein competing <sup>fi</sup>rms have di<sup>f</sup>erent, often incompatible goals. Indeed, Inkpen and Tsang [35], in outlining the boundary conditions for when shared goals foster knowledge transfer, state that shared goals of employees facilitate intra-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge transfer but as <sup>fi</sup>rms have competing goals it often hinders knowledge transfer in inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm relationships. Our contention here is that the unique bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of technology-enabled networking reduces information asymmetries and enhances knowledge transparency (e.g [9].), thus al lowing for more informed goal setting.

Knowledge-sharing activities are largely catalyzed by cognitive perceptions of strategic value, and network social capital also serves to reinforce this relationship, particularly within technology-enabled coopetition networks. Namely, the importance of network social capital emerges through both perceptions of individual exchange partners and the overarching network (e.g. social trust; [31]). Hence, network social capital provides a higher-order assurance that the technological infrastructure itself contains adequate protections from opportunistic use of organizational knowledge resources. In the same vein, network actors will consider their previous experience with the network and must perceive the technology as adaptable and capable of facilitating longterm collaborations for <sup>fi</sup>rms to be willing to dedicate valuable knowledge resources to pursue long-term coopetitive arrangements.

Taken together, we posit that network social capital, composed of network legitimacy, trust, and resilience, as perceived by network members is positively related to knowledge sharing in coopetition networks.

Hypothesis 2. Network social capital, as composed of network legitimacy, trust, and resilience, is positively related to knowledge sharing in coopetition networks.

Historically, coopetition research emphasizes that long-term outcomes are marred by partner opportunism which we contend are, at least partially, ameliorated by the e<sup>f</sup>ective application of technologyenabled coopetition networking. Hence, the ability of technology-enabled networking to impact the strategic impetus and outcomes of coopetition is reliant on the extent to which members of a coopetitive group commit to using the network to share valuable information. Further, the security, convenience, and protections of emergent technologies are not readily substituted by available modes of contractual or social enforcement that are traditionally considered in extant coo petition research and that are less accessible to resource-de<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fi</sup>rm [17].

Over time this network perspective creates a unique strategic con text for <sup>fi</sup>rms developing strategy around on-going resource commitment in a coopetition network. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, as members collaborate and become increasingly committed to normative knowledge-sharing behaviors, they are likely to become embedded within the operating and collaborative logics of that network [45]. As a result of this em beddedness, actors may alter their operating logic in ways that exhibit strategic alignment with their fellow members, and evolve their strategy toward one that prioritizes leveraging shared knowledge alongside internally controlled resources [46–48]. Incorporating these phenomena into the study of knowledge sharing in coopetition networks, as network actors perceive greater social capital within the network – predicated on network trust, legitimacy, and resilience – their desired strategic outcomes will become more congruous with their fellow network actors. We argue this shift is grounded in the strategic schema of actors prioritizing collaborative logics within their coopeti tion networks resulting in the coordinated co-evolution of network strategy as evidenced by goal convergence.

We posit that the technology-enabled coopetition network catalyzes the informal social alignment of actors, and therefore promotes their strategic alignment while simultaneously providing protection against partner opportunism. In other words, as network social capital in creases, we expect the risks associated with coopetition (e.g. [49,50]) to diminish and the strategic value of resources exchanged to be enhanced. Particularly in the case of networks of non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t SMEs whose characteristic resource de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies restrict capacity for diversi-<sup>fi</sup>ed strategic portfolios, we expect knowledge sharing to be a necessary function underlying the process through which network social capital enhances network goal convergence. When coopetition becomes a more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant aspect of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s overall strategy, we expect coopetition outcomes to play a more pivotal role for overall organizational success. In other words, as technology-enabled knowledge transfer makes inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm knowledge exchange more available, relevant, and reliable, <sup>fi</sup>rms are likely to simultaneously commit greater resources to a coopetition network as well as increase their strategic reliance on its outcomes (e.g [7,50,51].). Hypothesis 3. Knowledge sharing mediates the relationship between network social capital and goal convergence in coopetition networks.

While coopetition networks provide members with access to resources and knowledge capital held by their competitors that can lead to mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial outcomes, these e<sup>f</sup>ects require members to remain actively engaged in the network. Network cohesion represents the normative engagement and propinquity of network actors and is an important guard against partner opportunism for partners with power di<sup>f</sup>erentials [52]. Network cohesion may also indicate the extent to which long-term goals of the members of the network are congruent [53]. Hence, we argue that the co-evolution and normative knowledge exchange, while emergent in a variety of coopetitive contexts (e.g [6,9].), are likely emphasized in long-standing and established coopetitive relationships.

Further, by increasing engagement, network members are provided the opportunity to more closely observe knowledge sharing by fellow network actors and are thus given the opportunity to actively signal knowledge-sharing norms and best practices ([54] [2];). As a result, greater cohesion among network members would augment the e<sup>f</sup>ect of knowledge sharing on goal convergence. Contrarily, coopetitors that perceive networks as useful and share knowledge but are not embedded in the network (i.e. infrequent actors) are not expected to co-evolve their goals alongside their coopetitors as they rely less on collective knowledge resources when evaluating strategic options. This suggests that network cohesion moderates the relationship between knowledge sharing and goal convergence.

Hypothesis 4. Network cohesion moderates the relationship between knowledge sharing and goal convergence in such a way that strengthens the positive relationship.

## 3.1. Methodology

In order to test the hypotheses, we collected data from a survey distributed to 1250 members of 15 non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations belonging to a multi-organizational coopetitive association housed at a large U.S. University. Association <sup>fi</sup>rms have a large dynamic membership base of both student volunteers and employees and primarily manage fundraising and diverse volunteer activities targeting University students with goals of university, community, and social welfare. Those surveyed are full-time members of organizations and are given a large degree of authority and autonomy to attract and coordinate the e<sup>f</sup>orts of individual volunteers and temporary employee teams.

Although the operational motive of non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations may di<sup>f</sup>er from for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations, both organizational types do face similar pressures and collaboration issues with their respective competitor organizations. The density of non-pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, diminishing federal and state-funding sources, surging competition for grant funding, and climbing failure rates has increased the intensity of competition in the modern non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t sector [55,56]. These competitive pressures have led to the emergence of a variety of inter-organizational collaborative structures and services targeting non-pro<sup>fi</sup>ts (c.f [57,58].), including a trend for university sponsorship and housing of non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t associations [59,60]. Together these characteristics make non-pro<sup>fi</sup>ts a population ripe for contemporary studies of inter-organizational collaboration and coopetition, particularly as they are resource-de<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fi</sup>rms [61].

The multi-organizational coopetitive association provides a number of resources for the collective use of member <sup>fi</sup>rms. One such resource is an open knowledge-sharing network curated by a council of elected members of each <sup>fi</sup>rm to facilitate formal, albeit voluntary, socialization and collaboration of individual managers within a closed coopetition network. These knowledge networking technologies, which merge social networking and knowledge-sharing behaviors, are increasingly common and used in a variety of organizational contexts [62,63]. The knowledge networking technologies also provide a foundation for moderate and transparent information exchange among members of the coopetition network.

Participants were informed of their anonymity and encouraged to voluntarily participate. In order to ensure the robustness of the data collection, surveys were designed in order to methodologically and cognitively separate questions relating to their speci<sup>fi</sup>c organization from those referring to the informational network as a whole (ala [64]). Surveys were returned from 783 participants resulting in a raw response of 62.6 %. Incomplete surveys, those with incorrect answers to quality control questions, or surveys with inconsistent responses regarding collaborative activity, were removed to ensure the internal validity of the responses. The <sup>fi</sup>nal sample includes 597 usable cases, resulting in a <sup>fi</sup>nal response rate of 47.76 %. A priori power estimations [65] suggest this sample size is suitable to e<sup>f</sup>ectively test hypothesized models and conduct post-hoc robustness tests. Before the primary analysis was performed, t-tests compared responses and response rates across the 15 organizations and results indicated that response bias does not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact our analysis. Latent constructs are measured using modi<sup>fi</sup>ed versions of previously established scales; internal reliability and construct validity robustness tests were incorporated to ensure their appropriateness in the current sample.

Network social capital is speci<sup>fi</sup>ed as a second-order construct that is composed of three dimensions: network legitimacy, trust, and resilience. Conceptually, the three dimensions are the results of network social capital; the changes in the level of network social capital would lead to changes in the three dimensions in a cohesive way. Network legitimacy, trust, and resilience are viewed as critical network characteristics that represent various aspects of social capital and should be inter-correlated. For example, network legitimacy would inform network trust and resilience and vice versa. Each dimension is measured using established and previously validated scales modi<sup>fi</sup>ed to the appropriate referent level. Network legitimacy is measured using a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed stakeholder legitimacy scale [66] and emphasizes the perceived legitimacy of the coopetitive strategies of fellow network actors. Network trust is measured using a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed version of the ally trust scale [67] and emphasizes perceived normative trust among network actors and the knowledge they share. Network resilience is measured using a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed version of the social resilience scale [34] and emphasizes perceived sustainability of the network structure as a whole.

Knowledge sharing is measured using a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed version of the norm of shared knowledge scale [42]. Knowledge sharing is used to measure the ability and willingness of organizational members to commit to the ongoing sharing of valuable knowledge within their coopetition network and their perceptions of fellow network organizations to do the same. Network cohesion is measured using a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed version of the perceived solidarity scale [68] and is used to measure the perceived normative engagement and propinquity of coopetitive network actors. Goal convergence is measured using a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed version of the goal commitment scale [69], which incorporates member perceptions of goal alignment at both the individual and group levels. Multi-level survey questions are necessary in the present research because the impetus of coopetitive success is predicated on collective, not solely organizational, goal commitment.

Several control variables were partialled out of the measurement model to ensure robustness of our measures. Organizational tenure, rank, previous involvement, and organizational performance were controlled for with no changes in the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance levels in the measurement model. However, due to limited sample size at the organizational level, organizational performance was not included in primary structural analyses. Each construct used seven-point Likert scales, with 1 indicating “strongly disagree” and 7 as “strongly agree.” Table 1 provides scale items, principal component analysis loadings, and reliability analysis results.

Since data regarding both network characteristics and outcomes were collected using the same instrument, common method variance (CMV) is a concern. The data collection incorporated procedural controls in an attempt to limit this concern. Controls suggested by Podsako<sup>f</sup> et al. [64] were implemented in the survey design in order to methodologically, psychologically, and temporally separate instruments when measuring organizational and network components sepa rately (p. 888). These methodological separation strategies were in corporated in an attempt to minimize the e<sup>f</sup>ect of CMV [70]. Statistical tests of the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of these procedural controls (ala [64]), including the Harman’s single-factor test, suggest CMV is not a major issue in the present research. While we are unable to avoid the threat of CMV entirely, the approach used in collecting data incorporates both statistical and methodological separation strategies and is considered the “ideal” approach to combat CMV bias ([70]; pp. 581).

## 4. Procedures and results

Survey items were assessed to ensure their integrity and appro priateness for testing developed hypotheses. Table 2 provides the aggregate construct intercorrelations, average variance extracted (AVE), and composite reliability scores for our constructs. Data are analyzed using structural equation modeling (Mplus; [71]) to assess the <sup>fi</sup>t of our measurement and structural models. The measurement model was evaluated using CFA procedures, which indicated that the data <sup>fi</sup>t the model adequately $( \chi ^ { 2 } 1 4 5 . 5 5 ;$ CFI 0.977; TLI .973; RMSEA .043). This initial model presents minor threats to the convergent and discriminant validity of network legitimacy, trust, and resilience, the items of which tended to cross load. While VIF and AVE factors are in acceptable ranges, suggesting these concerns do not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly threaten the validity of our measurement model, these issues are reconciled in our <sup>fi</sup>nal measurement model that considers network social capital as a single metaconstruct.

We specify network social capital as a second-order construct composed of network legitimacy, trust, and resilience. Since our model includes constructs referring to perceptions and dynamics of both the organizational and network level, there is a threat of multicollinearity in our measurement model. As such, our hypotheses are tested using a multilevel measurement model from network actors in each of the 15 organizations in our sample that controls for cross-level e<sup>f</sup>ects (c.f. [72]). This second-order construct is validated using a <sup>fi</sup>ve-step multi level con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (MCFA [73];). This procedure allows for the simultaneous modeling of latent constructs with both organi zational (i.e. legitimacy and trust) and network (i.e. resilience) referents as indicators that compose network social capital as a second-order construct. As shown in Fig. 1, the MCFA procedures resulted in a model with adequate <sup>fi</sup>t $( \chi ^ { 2 } 1 8 2 . 4 9 ;$ CFI .926; TLI .897; RMSEA .053), with network legitimacy $( . 4 6 6 ; p < . 0 1 )$ , network trust $( . 2 7 0 ; p < . 0 1 )$ ), and network resilience $( . 8 4 1 ; p < . 0 1 )$ each signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lending support to the notion that they re<sup>fl</sup>ect independent dimensions of the network social capital construct.

## 5. Hypothesis testing

A model testing the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of network social capital on goal convergence registers adequate <sup>fi</sup>t $( \chi ^ { 2 } ~ 1 8 4 . 5 0 ;$ CFI .962; TLI .951; RMSEA .044) and is statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( . 3 9 9 ; p < . 0 5 )$ , thus supporting H1. This result suggests that actors viewing a coopetition network as legitimate, trustworthy, and resilient are more likely to develop convergent and complementary strategic goals aligned with their fellow network actors.

H2 and H3 are tested with a four-step mediation technique (e.g. [74,75]). The mediation model demonstrates consistent <sup>fi</sup>t when compared to our direct e<sup>f</sup>ects model $( \chi ^ { 2 }$ 323.38; CFI .954; TLI .940; RMSEA .043). Our results suggest that network social capital is strongly related to knowledge sharing (.654; $p < . 0 1 )$ , which, in turn, mediates the relationship between network social capital and goal convergence approaching signi<sup>fi</sup>cance (.118; $p < . 1 )$ , providing strong support for H2 and marginal support for H3. In this mediated model the direct paths between the network social capital and goal convergence become nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant, suggesting full mediation.

To test H4, a <sup>fi</sup>nal structural model was developed with a two-stage moderation test of latent variable interaction in moderated mediation models using orthogonalized indicators [76–79]. In our <sup>fi</sup>nal structural model, shown in Fig. 2, network cohesion moderates the relationship between knowledge sharing and goal convergence. This <sup>fi</sup>nal model registers superior <sup>fi</sup>t $( \chi 2$ 434.04; CFI .955; TLI .945; RMSEA .037) with network cohesion signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and positively moderating the relationship between knowledge sharing and goal convergence $( . 1 3 0 ; p < . 0 5 )$ and greatly increasing the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of this relationship when compared to the purely mediated model. Together these <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the co-evolution of network strategy is not a generalized artifact of the network as a whole, but a phenomenon restricted to the most active actors of the network, providing strong evidence in support of H4.

## 6. Post-hoc robustness analysis

Built on network social capital theory and <sup>fi</sup>ndings of prior studies, our hypotheses suggest that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of network social capital on goal convergence is mediated by an organization’s propensity of knowledge sharing. Nevertheless, a mediation model like ours is possibly subject to the issue of reverse causality, particularly when considering extant work illustrating reverse e<sup>f</sup>ects between goal convergence and knowledge sharing (e.g. [15,31]). While our cross-sectional data limit us to establish temporal causality, indirect e<sup>f</sup>ect analyses show no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant issues in our <sup>fi</sup>nal model. However, we cannot fully rule out this issue. In an attempt to determine the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of these threats, secondary organizational performance data were used to validate our <sup>fi</sup>ndings at the organizational level; the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of which generally complement those in our main analyses. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide general supplemental support for our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings and suggest that the convergence of goals among coopetitive network actors results in sig ni<sup>fi</sup>cant collective performance bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, which we assume are the result of convergent strategic goals among cohesive network members. Unfortunately, this performance data are only available at the organi zational level and thus cannot robustly be applied to our primary analysis. Hence, while these post-hoc tests suggest our <sup>fi</sup>ndings do not su<sup>f</sup>er from signi<sup>fi</sup>cant endogeneity issues with regard to the omitted variable bias in our model, we are not able to wholly reconcile these threats, which remain a limitation of this study.

## 7. Discussion and implications

This study has several important implications for the on-going study of inter-organizational IT in coopetition networks, organizational social capital, and inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration in general. In developing the metaconstruct of network social capital, we recognize and apply a multi dimensional understanding of the social mechanisms underlying tech nology-enabled coopetition networks. While the presence of a technological platform that facilitates coopetition is in and of itself necessary for our study, we emphasize emergent avenues for accumulating social capital provided by technology-enabled coopetition networking, which informs our distinctive <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our study results suggest the role of inter-organizational IT in accumulating network social capital and how it improves processes and outcomes of a coopetition network. Our measure of social capital is representative of the current state of the literature and incorporates common elements discussed in inter-organizational social capital research. Thus, the suggested network social capital construct can be applied to explain the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of inter-organizational IT. Although commonly studied examples of technology-enabled coopetition may note industryspeci<sup>fi</sup>c idiosyncrasies that alter the nature of network social capital, our development of network social capital o<sup>f</sup>ers a foundation for the general study of knowledge exchange and goal convergence in broad

coopetitive networks.

Our initial framing of the network social capital construct provides a foundation for research exploring the role of both technology in contemporary studies of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration and of the distinct social mechanisms and outcomes in such groupings. However, as coopetition becomes an increasingly available tactic for wide swaths of business populations, primarily as a result of decreased barriers for accessing knowledge-sharing and social networking technologies [10], we encourage exploration into additional avenues for the ongoing development of this construct. To aid in this, we present implications of our study that can inform future research regarding the evolving nature of coopetition at the intersection of IT and <sup>fi</sup>rm strategy as well as practical implications for managers.

## 7.1. Research contributions

While the above broad implications re<sup>fl</sup>ect the motivation for the present research, our speci<sup>fi</sup>c research <sup>fi</sup>ndings present three primary contributions to current understanding of coopetition networks, particularly their strategic utility to small resource-de<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fi</sup>rms.

First, with regard to on-going discussions of inter-organizational IT, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the utility of technology-enabled knowledge sharing within a coopetition network can be captured by the accumulation of cognitive, relational, and structural social capital and promotes strategic alignment among embedded network actors. While inter-organizational IT has been known for promoting transparency and e<sup>fi</sup>cient communication, we take a step further to exhibit that interorganizational IT can be applied to the accumulation of network social capital. With perceived network legitimacy, trust, and resilience enabled by the inter-organizational IT, the members are more willing to share knowledge and achieve goal convergence. This is driven by the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of technology-enabled networking as a tool for knowledge sharing and thus resource acquisition. Owing to the coopetitive actions of network members, this leads to a ground-up decentralized method of co-evolution and value creation. This is a distinct <sup>fi</sup>nding in this lit erature, which has historically emphasized the limitations of long-term coopetition. We believe the present research demonstrates that the constraints of long-term coopetition are at least partially ameliorated by the presence of a technology-enabled coopetition network that creates social capital for network actors.

Second, this study extends the current inter-organizational IT re search, from predominantly supply chain partners who have a cohesive goal (e.g. [32,70]) to <sup>fi</sup>rms in coopetition networks that need to develop convergent goals. While research in network partners with a shared goal focuses on performance as the outcome of the IT adoption, our study emphasizes goal convergence as the result of inter-organizational IT. It therefore underlines the strategic value of IT, for both altering the resource investment of <sup>fi</sup>rms and the dynamics of the participating <sup>fi</sup>rms. For example, the knowledge-sharing and social networking platform in our study exempli<sup>fi</sup>es that it can address the members concerns of monitoring mechanism and knowledge hoarding by offering an open communication channel. While prior research con<sup>fi</sup>rms the value of inter-organizational IT in collaborative contexts, our study results underscore the value of such IT among members that potentially have con<sup>fl</sup>icts of interests. Technology-enabled networking underlies coopetition and provides not only the vehicle, but the impetus for strategic co-evolution and co-value creation among coopetitive <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Third, this study expands the boundary of inter-organizational IT research, from the predominately emphasized large resource-laden <sup>fi</sup>rms to less explored resource-de<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fi</sup>rms. Technological investment is often considered burdensome for small, resource-de<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fi</sup>rms such as non-pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and SMEs. Thus, the research in strategic applications often focuses on how large <sup>fi</sup>rms achieve and maintain competitive advantages (e.g. [80]). In our study, the observed value of technology-enabled coopetitive networking is particularly critical for the strategic management of small resource-de<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fi</sup>rms, given the limited resources and the need to leverage knowledge from other network members. Overall, the present research seeks to answer calls for ex panding relevant populations of technology-enabled strategy research to smaller <sup>fi</sup>rms (e.g [81,82].) and provides greater understanding of the strategic idiosyncrasies of such <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Fourth, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that knowledge sharing can emerge as a norm shared by the network members. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we provide empirical evidence of bilateral normative knowledge exchange facilitated by the technology-enabled network across <sup>fi</sup>rm boundaries. This knowledge sharing is particularly relevant in the study of coopetition since the knowledge being shared is generally more competitively relevant than in other collaborative structures. The results therefore suggest the increased technological sophistication of coopetition networks may, overtime, blur the line of propriety knowledge resources and promote collaborative behaviors among competitors. This <sup>fi</sup>nding contributes to the increased attention to the role of knowledge resources in the strategic development of contemporary sophisticated <sup>fi</sup>rms, which spans multiple research domains (e.g. [7,9,83]).

## 7.2. Practical implications

In addition to the research implications detailed above, our <sup>fi</sup>nding also present three important practical implications. First, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that resource-de<sup>fi</sup>cient organizations, such as SMEs and nonpro<sup>fi</sup>ts, can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from an IT facilitated coopetition strategy. This supports recent empirical evidence espousing the strategic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of inter-organizational collaboration and knowledge networking [9,87]. The majority of extant research in the role of IT in inter-organizational collaboration suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms tend to under-invest when outcomes cannot be entirely internalized or provide bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that are simultaneously captured by other organizations [84,85]. However, our study results suggest a di<sup>f</sup>erent strategic possibility for resource-de<sup>fi</sup>cient organizations. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings here provide evidence that the generalized multi-organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of technological investment in coopetition networks not only lack the disincentive for investment illustrated above, but may actually be a preferred option, given the reduced resource commitment compared to pure internal investment. This technological investment reduces barriers to knowledge acquisition and promotes collective growth bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of entire eco-systems of <sup>fi</sup>rms. As a result, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings – in alignment with recent <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding open-innovation [87] and knowledge-sharing coopetition [9] – provide evidence that managers in resource-constrained <sup>fi</sup>rms may gain a competitive advantage from collaborating with similarly situated <sup>fi</sup>rms when doing so allows them to hone their distinctive strategic competencies. While we do not recommend removing intellectual property protections, resource-de<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fi</sup>rms are able to extend and amplify their competitiveness – particularly against large resourceladen counterparts – by capitalizing on collaboration with similarly situated partners to coordinate non-proprietary support activities (e.g. demand forecasting [9];).

Second, centered around the concept of network social capital, our study <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest some feasible and practical directions for <sup>fi</sup>rms to work toward in order to succeed in a coopetition network. For example, <sup>fi</sup>rms in the coopetition network should strive to establish shared norms and systems of meaning, to facilitate frequent interactions among members, and to provide assurance of the sustainability of the network. In doing so, perceived network legitimacy, trust, and resilience can be elevated, the level of knowledge sharing can be promoted, and ultimately goal convergence can be reached. Firms in a coopetition net work are also advised to enhance the embeddedness of contributing members.

Third, our study results underscore the central, enabling role of knowledge sharing in leading to goal convergence of a coopetitive network and encourage <sup>fi</sup>rms to engage in technology-enabled knowl edge sharing in a coopetition network. In particular, our results show that knowledge sharing fully mediates the e<sup>f</sup>ect of network social ca pital on goal convergence. This result implies that knowledge sharing among members of the coopetition network is essential for reaching goal convergence, regardless of the level of network social capital. Technology-enabled knowledge sharing in a coopetition network can reduce information asymmetries and threats of knowledge hoarding for both resource-abundant and resource-de<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fi</sup>rms. Nevertheless, given the resource constraints for partner monitoring mechanisms and vulnerability to coopetition con<sup>fl</sup>icts of SMEs and non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations, we expect to see a more profound impact of technology-enabled knowledge sharing in a coopetition network

## 7.3. Limitations

While we strive to ensure the validity of our hypothesized model and <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we recognize minor limitations in our study. First, data were collected from a sample of non-pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Although they are representative of resource-de<sup>fi</sup>cient organizations and <sup>fi</sup>t the target as speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in the research question, we acknowledge the di<sup>f</sup>erence between non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t and for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations. Since no performance or competitive data were used in the direct analyses – organizational performance data were used in the robustness tests and <sup>fi</sup>ndings are aligned with our hypotheses – we are con<sup>fi</sup>dent that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are generalizable to a variety of coopetitive settings. The generalizability should extend particularly well to for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t resource-de<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fi</sup>rms, namely SMEs, which share a variety of competitive realities to similarly situated non-pro<sup>fi</sup>ts [61]. However, we recognize that the extent of knowledge sharing and normative behaviors of non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t coopetition networks may present a distinct context, and our <sup>fi</sup>ndings should be interpreted in light of these distinctions.

Additionally, our lack of longitudinal data restricts our ability to speak toward the strategic co-evolution process of network members and instead focuses entirely on theoretically recognizing and empiri cally predicting the outcomes of these processes. While our approach is theoretically justi<sup>fi</sup>ed and the robustness of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings empirically validates our position, future research with data that are more sensitive to the processual components of strategic co-evolution may more wholly explain the variance in our network embeddedness <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 8. Conclusion

The present research suggests that as coopetitive partners perceive legitimacy, trust, and resilience of a network, they are likely to share more valuable competitive knowledge. This knowledge sharing consequently alters <sup>fi</sup>rms’ perceptions of the competitive landscape and promotes goal convergence among embedded network actors. In exploring these phenomena, we present evidence regarding the role of technology in enhancing the strategic relevance of coopetitive options and providing the impetus for strategic development in which interorganizational collaboration is more bene<sup>fi</sup>cial than without such technology.
