## A B S T R A C T

The existing information system (IS) literature has acknowledged computer self-efficacy (CSE) as an important factor contributing to enhancements in computer-related task performance. However, the empirical results of CSE on performance have not always been consistent, and increasing an individual’s CSE is often a cumbersome process. Thus, we introduce the theoretical concept of self-prophecy (SP) and examine how this social influence strategy can be used to improve computer-related task performance. Two experiments are conducted to examine the influence of SP on task performance. Results show that SP and CSE interact to influence performance. Implications are then discussed in terms of organizations ability to increase performance.

## 1. Introduction

In the field of information systems (ISs), there has been an enduring interest in efforts to predict and enhance individuals performance on computer-related tasks. This is evident in models such as the task technology fit [17], among others, which articulates the relationships between task, technology, and individual characteristics and their impacts on task performance. A large body of work within the IS field has shown that computer self-efficacy or CSE [11] significantly influences individuals performance on technology-related tasks. Further, it has also been demonstrated that improving performance on a technologyrelated task requires extensive training to enhance an individual’s CSE. Despite the large volume of work on this subject, the ability of CSE to predict performance has not yet been fully explained. Moreover, studies conducted earlier have shown that CSE may occasionally exhibit contradictory results [12–14,24].

These contradictory results highlight the need for further research on CSE. One such study has argued that the construct of CSE is multifaceted, consisting of internal and external components, and the reasons for contradictory findings surrounding CSE in the past could have been owing to a lack of understanding of these multifaceted components [42]. Thatcher et al. [42] further noted that additional research will be required to explicate how each of the facets interacts to influence task performance. Despite these findings, studies employing the multifaceted model still have reported inconsistent results, suggesting that additional models or moderating variables may be necessary to fully understand the nature of CSE and its influence on performance (see Table 1 for a summary of a sample of studies that help highlight the equivocal nature of CSE). To summarize, the ability of CSE to predict performance is still slightly ambiguous suggesting that it might be beneficial to examine if there are other relevant constructs in similar domains that can individually or in conjunction with CSE help explain performance.

An examination of the consumer psychology literature reveals one such relevant construct and highlights that the IS field is not alone in its desire to predict and influence individual performance and behavior. This particular construct represents a phenomenon whereby behavior is changed by merely asking people to make an a priori prediction about their performance [29]. This phenomenon is often referred to as self-prophecy (or SP)<sup>1</sup>; its effect has been shown to influence a variety of different behaviors, including recycling [36,38], commitment to health and fitness [36,40], cheating [34], choosing healthy snacks [39], and gender stereotyping [33]. All of these studies have indicated that the SP effect influences behavior in the direction of social norms [41].

While previous studies have strongly supported the effect of self-prediction on the performance of various behaviors, to date, no study has tested the effectiveness of SP within the context of a technology-related task, and on performance of that task. SP is particularly interesting for IS researchers because it suggests that the performance on a technology-based task might be enhanced by merely having a person or group predict their future performance. If this holds true, then enhancing the performance of technologybased tasks would not necessitate the additional (and often expensive) training required by mechanisms based on CSE; instead, changes in behavior could be achieved by asking a simple question.

Thus, the objective of this paper is to examine the effect of SP in technology-related behaviors. Specifically, the study seeks to address a threefold research agenda: (1) Does SP significantly influence individuals’ future performance in the context of a technology-related task? (2) How does SP interact with CSE and does this interaction influence task performance within a technological task context? (3) Do restrictions imposed in the task environment moderate the influence of CSE and/or SP?

The remainder of this article is structured as follows. Next, the CSE and SP concepts have been detailed based on a literature review. This review leads to further explication of the research objectives and hypotheses. Next, a program of research is established and the findings from the two experiments are reported. Finally, the article concludes with a discussion of the results and limitations of this research.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Computer self-efficacy

The concept of self-efficacy, the expectation of one’s own ability to perform a behavior, is derived from the social cognitive theory. The social cognitive theory, introduced by Bandura [3], postulates that a reciprocal relationship exists among environmental factors, personal factors, and human behavior. This reciprocal relationship takes a triadic form where human behavior influences environmental and personal factors, personal factors influence environmental factors and human behavior, and finally environmental factors influence human behavior and personal factors.

The theory argues that none of these forces exist in isolation; rather, they all exert an influence upon each other until equilibrium is achieved. Building upon this theory further, Bandura next identified two sets of expectations as the major cognitive forces guiding the behavior. The first set of expectations, outcome expectations, has been well articulated in the field through research in technology acceptance [15,44,45], and TTF [17]. However, the second set of expectations, those related to self-efficacy, had received little attention in the IS field until Compeau and Higgins [11] proposed their concept of computer self-efficacy. In their 1995 article, Compeau and Higgins adapted the theory of selfefficacy to the context of technology and subsequently introduced the construct of CSE into the IS field. They define CSE as ‘‘. . . a judgment of one’s capability to use a computer’’ ([11], p. 192). Compeau and Higgins [11] state that CSE focuses on how confident people are about what could be done in the future and more specifically about one’s capabilities to perform broader computerrelated tasks.

Compeau and Higgins [11] further articulate the theory by providing three distinct dimensions to CSE: magnitude, strength, and generalizability. First, the magnitude of CSE refers to one’s expectation about his/her level of computer-related capabilities (i.e., how confident one feels about his/her computer-related capabilities). Second, the strength of CSE refers to how confident one feels about his/her ability to apply those capabilities to specific tasks. Finally, the third dimension deals with the generalizability of CSE, which refers to whether one’s computer-related capabilities are limited to specific domains or can be applied to different hardware and software configurations.

Compeau and Higgins [11] developed a 10-item measurement of CSE, widely used in CSE research, to validate and test the CSE concept. Thatcher et al. [42] further expanded upon the CSE instrument by identifying two sub-dimensions within the construct namely external and internal CSE. External CSE is defined as the belief that one has the ability to perform a computer-related task using one’s own computer-related skills and with the availability of an external support. However, internal CSE is defined as the belief that one has the ability to perform a computer-related task using one’s own skills without the use of external support.

Since the introduction of the CSE concept, many researchers have studied the effect of CSE on individual task performance; however, research results in this area are often equivocal or contradictory [24]. For instance, Compeau and Higgins [12] show in one of their studies that CSE significantly affects task performance in some groups but not in others. Further, although CSE is shown to significantly affect task performance, the question then becomes one of how to increase the CSE. Previous studies seeking to answer this question have shown several determinants that can influence CSE including external support, training, and verbal persuasion [24]; however, studies have also shown that these determinants have not always provided the anticipated results. For example, Thatcher et al. [42] argue that contrary to the initial expectations, organizational support was found to be negatively associated with CSE [11]. We summarize some of the other studies presenting equivocal results on the role of CSE on performance in Table 1.

Even if one disregards the equivocal results surrounding the effect of the determinants, in practice it is often cumbersome and expensive to rely on training or other methods for improving CSE and thereby performance [2]. To address these shortcomings of CSE, and in an effort to find additional variables that might help enhance performance, and or CSE, in this study, we focus on the concept of SP. SP is a theory from the marketing literature that has been robustly supported as an effective method to predict and enhance human behavior. Introduction of this concept into the IS field will provide a well-supported model and an alternate perspective for enhancing task performance.

## 2.2. Self-prophecy

The SP effect was first introduced in an article by Sherman (1980; then referred to as the self-erasing nature of errors of prediction), who highlighted that people usually mispredict the degree to which they will perform a socially desirable behavior, but that such prediction errors tend to be self-erasing when the behavior is later performed. In essence, this means that if a person predicts that he/she will perform a behavior, there will be a greater propensity for him/her to actually perform the behavior in the future than if he/she had not made such a prediction.

Sherman [29] conducted three experiments to test this basic hypothesis involving the behavior of writing a counter-attitudinal essay, singing the ‘‘Star Spangled Banner’’ over the telephone and volunteering 3 h to collect money for the American Cancer Society. In all three experiments, there was a discrepancy between the percentages of people in the prediction-only group who predicted they would perform a behavior and the percentages of people in the request-only group who actually performed the behavior. The experiments revealed that once people had made a self-prediction, then performance of the behavior was biased in a socially normative direction (e.g., people making a prediction were less likely to sing over the phone and more likely to volunteer to collect money).

Since then, researchers have empirically demonstrated this latter effect on the performance of other behaviors. For example, Greenwald et al. [18] found that people were more likely to register to vote and actually vote, if they were asked to predict their behaviors a priori. Spangenberg and Obermiller [34] also demonstrated that self-prediction could reduce cheating behavior in a college classroom by asking students to predict if they would cheat on an upcoming assignment.

More recently, researchers in the field of marketing have shown that the effect of SP manifests itself consistently and robustly to influence a range of consumer behaviors [38,41]. For example, Spangenberg [32] examined SP in the context of an actual behavior and found that answering an anonymous prediction question led to an increase in the likelihood of actually attending a health club. This research provides the earliest demonstration of how the effect of SP can be used in a real-world setting. Since then, a significant amount of research has been conducted showing that SP has an effect on various socially desirable consumer behaviors including recycling [36,38], choice of low-fat snacks [39], having a health assessment [40], and attending a fitness center [36].

Traditionally, the SP effect has been studied based on the idea that people will self-erase their prediction error and act consistently [29,8]; however, contemporary research has sought to expand on the robustness of the SP effect by proposing additional theoretical explanations of why such an effect exerts itself. For instance, Spangenberg et al. [37] propose the ‘‘ideomotor theory’’ and argue that asking a question will activate ‘‘a perceptual image or idea of an action being asked about, with the activated image guiding future performance of the behavior’’ (p. 104). In other words, upon being asked to make a prediction, certain perceptions that relate to the prediction will be formed in a person’s mind, which later leads to actions that are coherent with such perceptions, and this will be the case especially when no contradictory ideas are present (James, 1950 c.f. [37]). This explanation is also consistent with Fitzsimons and Moore’s [16] argument that asking questions trigger the creation of behavior scripts or implementation intentions for people to follow.

## 3. Research agenda and hypotheses development

Previous studies have shown that when people predict that they will perform a task well, they will be motivated to act in a way consistent with their prediction [29,1,27]. Based on this evidence, it is expected that individuals who predict that they will perform well in a technology-related task will subsequently perform better than those subjects who do not make a prediction or predict that they will perform the task poorly. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1. People who predict themselves to perform well in a technology-related task will have a better performance later in the task than people who do not make such predictions beforehand.

Consistent with previous research on computer self-efficacy, we also expect that people with a higher CSE will perform better in a technology-related task [11,12]. Hereby, we propose our second hypothesis:

H2. People with higher CSE will perform better in a technologyrelated task than people with lower CSE.

This research also seeks to explore the nature of the relationship between SP and CSE pertaining to task performance. In other words, one of our objectives is also to understand whether SP and CSE interact to influence technology-related task performance, and if so, the nature of this interaction. First, we would like to emphasize that SP and CSE differ in their nature and their foci, and thus there is a potential that they might complement each other in influencing behavior and performance. For instance, SP is by its nature an ‘‘action’’ concept and thus the focus is on intervention, implying that by presenting a question to the subject, the effect exerts itself through the subject’s action of answering the question and making a prediction [29]. Conversely, CSE is by nature a ‘‘perception’’ concept, and the focus is on understanding how people perceive their information technology (IT)-related capabilities. Such perception could increase through interventions such as training and external support [11]. Therefore, when combining SP and CSE, it is reasonable to speculate that the action concept and the perception concept will interact with each other when it comes to technology-related task performance, as perceptions can be triggered by actions.

Indeed, SP-related research has identified possible contingency conditions for the effect. For instance, Spangenberg and Sprott [35] find that SP has stronger effects for people who are lower in selfmonitoring, vis-a-vis those who are higher in self-monitoring. These findings can be explained by the theory of ideomotor which suggests that the effects of SP can be traced to the creation of scripts or perceptions that later guide people’s behavior and performance [37,16]. Hence, people having higher self-monitoring capabilities already enjoy a higher level of psychological guidance and therefore do not benefit much from the creation of another script.

Similarly, we can argue that people having a higher degree of CSE will be influenced less by an SP prediction than those having a modest amount of CSE in relation to technology-related task performance. People with a higher CSE have a greater perception of their IT capabilities and therefore making a prediction of their performance on a technology-related task will have a lesser influence on their performance as they already have a certain degree of psychological assurance. However, people with lower

CSE do not perceive themselves as competent in terms of technologies and hence could benefit from having a perception of performing well that is created by making such predictions. In other words, SP as an intervention influences to a lesser extent the task performance for people with a higher CSE than for those with a lower CSE.

Thus, it is reasonable to speculate that CSE and SP will interact such that the intervention of asking a prediction question will influence the relationship between CSE and task performance. CSE is defined as individuals’ perceptions of their own capabilities to complete a computer-related task [3,11]. When individuals are prompted with a prediction question regarding their task performance, which triggers the development of the perception that they would do well on the task, the behavior guidance resulting from the intervention might dampen the importance of CSE to task performance. Hence, we argue that the relationship between CSE and technology-related task performance is weaker when a positive prediction of task performance is made. Thus:

H3. SP will moderate the effect of CSE on technology-related task performance.

Finally, it has been shown in prior literature that organizational climate may have a significant influence on individuals’ motivation to complete a task and that individuals will perform differently when they are not restricted to just one task ([6,23], pp. 81–82). Such an influence due to organizational climate may be brought about because individuals feel more comfortable asking for help with tasks that they have little working experience with in an unstructured environment. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H4. Restrictions utilized in the working environment will influ ence both SP and/or CSE.

## 3.1. Study 1

## 3.1.1. Research method

In order to explore the effectiveness of the SP construct within the technological context and to test the first three hypotheses, we designed and conducted an experiment using student subjects. While students may not always be the ideal subject pool because of their limited knowledge base [28], for this study the use of student subjects was determined to be appropriate given that we needed a population that did not have prior exposure to the types of applications used in the study [21].

3.1.1.1. Subjects. For this study, 152 subjects were recruited from an introductory IS course within the College of Business at a large northwestern university in the U.S. Subjects varied in age from 18 to 38, with a mean age of 20.5. Approximately 64% of the subjects were male, with the remaining 36% being female. Subjects were given course credit for their participation in this research with no other incentives provided.

3.1.1.2. Experiment procedure. This study was based around a hypothetical summer internship to make it more realistic for the student subjects. The subjects were asked to imagine that they had been hired as a summer intern for an architecture firm, and that their job would be to determine the best architecture software application for the company to purchase when the company’s current architecture software license expired later in the summer. An excerpt from the script has been provided below.

You have been recently hired as a summer intern for an architecture firm that specializes in designing and building custom single-family homes. The company is currently contemplating acquiring a software application to help them with drawing designs, as well as managing the home building process. Your job will be to evaluate various different software packages that the company is thinking about purchasing, and as your first project, you will evaluate the technical specifications of two different architecture software packages that the company is considering acquiring.

For empirical evaluation of the proposed research hypotheses, we designed three experimental scenarios. In the first group, subjects were exposed to the SP instrument before the CSE construct, while in the second group this was reversed with the CSE instrument being introduced before the SP instrument. This split in the dataset was determined to be necessary because previous research on SP has suggested that exposure to the SP instrument could prime the subjects, thus introducing confounding influences into the study [38], and it was suspected that this might be the case with the CSE instrument as well. In addition, by alternating the order of exposure to the instrument, the researchers were able to control for any ordering effects. Finally, data were also collected from a control group that had not been exposed to either the CSE or the SP instruments so that a baseline could be determined.

The first two groups were asked to predict if they would make a good decision and to fill out a survey about their degree of CSE, based on the CSE instrument used by Compeau and Higgins [11]. The sole difference between these groups was the order in which these instruments were provided. Finally, the control group was given neither of the instruments and was just asked to complete the task.

For the remainder of the experiment, the subjects were placed in a situation where they needed to determine the software applications that met the technical requirements for the company. They were provided a list of the company’s computers, access to the websites for the software, and a conversion chart (see Appendix for more details on these instruments) to accomplish this task. After reviewing the relevant information for the company’s hardware and the technical requirements for each of the architectural software packages, the subjects were asked to provide their recommendation as to which software would be appropriate for the company given the company’s current hardware configuration.

Controls were used in this experiment to ensure that the subjects worked exclusively on this study for the duration of the experiment. These restrictions were enforced using the SynchronEyes software running on all the lab computers and through direct observation of the subjects during the experiment. SynchronEyes allowed the researchers to limit the lab computers to only being able to access the survey software and the relevant websites used in the study. The researchers also observed the participants to prevent collaboration or interaction between individuals during the experiment.

## 3.1.2. Measurements

3.1.2.1. Dependent variable. There are three basic ways to measure individual task performance: task correctness, amount of time needed to perform a task, and after-task recollection [22]. In this study, task correctness was used as the dependent variable and was measured by asking the subjects to choose merely one of the two competing architectural software applications. Based on the materials presented to the research participants, only one of the software applications met the technical requirements for the hypothetical architecture company.

3.1.2.2. Independent variables. Previous literature manipulated SP by simply asking subjects to make a self-prediction regarding their behavior [18]. Following this approach, the SP manipulation was presented as a dichotomous question asking subjects to predict whether they believed that they would perform the task well in this study (see Appendix). This decision was then coded as ‘‘1’’ for those who believed they would perform the task well and ‘‘0’’ for those who believed that they would not do well.

In an effort to understand the interactive effect between SP and CSE, we went beyond the mere observation of the sign of the effect (i.e., positive vs. negative; e.g. [26]). Specifically, we split the data into two groups using the SP scores. For this analysis, logistic regression was again used to analyze performance in relation to CSE for subjects who indicated they would (SP = 1) or would not (SP = 0) perform the task well. The results, presented in Table 5, show that CSE has a significant effect on task performance when SP equals ‘‘0’’ $( p = 0 . 0 2 6 )$ . However, for the other group, where SP equals ‘‘1,’’ CSE does not have a significant effect on task performance. Fig. 1, in line with the suggestions provided by Rosnow and Rosenthal [26], further illustrates the moderation effect of SP on the relationship between CSE and task performance.

## 3.1.4. Study 1 discussion

Study 1 shows that CSE has a significant effect on task performance and further shows the significant effects of interaction between SP and CSE. This interaction, when more closely examined, shows support for the supposition that SP moderates the influence of CSE on task performance. As shown, CSE does not show any significant effect on task performance for those individuals who predicted that they would perform the task well. However, CSE has a strong effect on task performance when the subject predicted that they would not perform the task well.

In study 1, students were performing in a structured environment, wherein they were only allowed to work on the specified task. However, this is probably not the case in the realworld working environment, where employees will not be restricted to only one task. Previous literature on organizational climate defines structure as ‘‘. . .the feelings that employees have about constraints in the group, how many rules, regulations, procedures there are. . .’’ ([23], pp. 81–82). In addition to being one of the key dimensions that define the organizational climate, it has also been found that organizational climate has an impact on individuals’ motivation to pursue a positive job outcome and hence affects the job performance [6]. Therefore, it may be beneficial to also study the effects of CSE and SP in an unstructured working environment.

## 3.2. Study 2

## 3.2.1. Research method

3.2.1.1. Subjects. As with the previous experiment, 257 students were recruited from an introductory IS course at a large northwestern university. These subjects were recruited during a different semester to minimize the overlap between studies. Subjects varied in age from 18 to 50, with a mean age of 20.3. Approximately 65% of the subjects were male, with the remaining 35% being female. Subjects were given course credit for their participation in this research study with no other incentives provided.

3.2.1.2. Experiment procedure. For this study, subjects were given the same scenario and asked to perform the same task as the subjects in study 1. However, the restrictions enforced in Study 1 to ensure that subjects only working on this task were removed. Instead, for this study, we handed out the instruction sheets to the subjects and then let them work on the task on their own; in a completely unrestricted classroom environment without the use of content monitoring and internet blocking we had employed in study 1. Further, students were allowed (but not encouraged) to interact with each other during this task. These conditions were deemed to be the closest to the actual world that we could achieve with student subjects in a classroom environment.

## 3.2.2. Measurements

All the variables were measured similar to Study 1. The reliability of the measurement instruments for CSE, DC, and ITK was all within or near the suggested threshold of 0.80 [10]. Therefore, the Cronbach’s alpha scores for CSE (0.897), DC (0.801), and ITK (0.802) are all shown as being highly reliable as expected from previously validated instruments [11,5,4]. Tables 6 and 7 show, respectively, the descriptive of the variables and the correlation between the variables.

## 3.2.3. Data analysis

As with the previous study, an ANOVA was conducted on the performance data from the two treatment groups and the control group. Results from this ANOVA revealed no significant difference (F = 0.236, p = 0.32) between the three groups. Based on these findings, the data from the treatment groups were once again collapsed to just one group for the remainder of the analysis.

## 3.2.4. Study 2 discussion

The results from this data collection and analysis have shown that the SP effect does exist in a technology-related task. As shown from the analysis, SP had a significant influence on the outcome variable while controlling for the effect of CSE. CSE was simultaneously shown in this study as not having a significant effect; however, given the inconsistent findings in the prior literature, these results were not surprising, as the CSE instrument has been shown to occasionally exhibit equivocal findings.

This study provides an empirical support for the SP effect within a technology-related task context by showing that subjects who are allowed to work at their own pace in an unstructured environment are more likely to adjust their performance in keeping with their earlier prediction. These findings indicate that the SP effect may be able to enhance overall task performance when subjects are prompted to complete a task, but not subsequently monitored for the duration of the task. This context would be similar to an actual working environment where the employee is assigned a task, and then allowed to complete the task on their own using their own expertise or by seeking assistance from other sources.

## 4. Discussion and potential contribution

The results of study 1 found no main effects for SP or CSE on task performance associated with selecting a software application. While these results stand somewhat in contrast to prior research, the study did demonstrate a significant interaction between these two constructs on task performance. This interaction supports the supposition that SP has a moderating influence on the effects of CSE. In particular, while there is a strong effect of CSE on task performance when subjects predicted that they would not perform the task well, the effect of CSE disappeared when individuals predicted that they would perform the task well. Thus, an affirmative self-prediction eliminated any effects of CSE. Based on this analysis, H1 and H2 were partially supported and H3 was fully supported.

In study 2, the nature of the work environment was adjusted so that the subjects were not forced to solely focus on the task at hand. This was done to study the effect in an environment that more closely resembles a traditional work environment. The results of the second study highlight that in this type of environment there was no significant effect of CSE. However, on the contrary, SP showed a clear significant effect in this unstructured environment. These findings support H4.

These results suggest that both CSE and SP may be beneficial for predicting task performance and thus provide the opportunity to more effectively target training efforts to enhance task performance. Specifically, in a structured task environment, this program of research would suggest that using CSE in conjunction with SP is likely to be most beneficial. While in an unstructured environment, it may be most advantageous to solely rely on SP for enhancing task performance.

In terms of SP research, the findings of this study are rather unique. The majority of prior SP research has only examined the effects of responding to a self-prediction and not the nature of that response. By contrast, the current research is one of the few studies that have shown that the nature of the self-prediction itself (i.e., whether the person said they would do well or not do well on the task) can influence future performance. Future research could usefully build upon these findings by more fully exploring how the nature of a prediction (not simply making a prediction) influences behavior.

This research also contributes to the field of ISs by demonstrating that when using SP in combination with CSE, it is possible to highlight those subjects who would benefit the most from additional training. Traditionally, a company could rate the CSE of an employee and then try to increase that CSE level through training. Based on the current work, using self-prediction in combination with CSE, companies can now see that increasing CSE may only be relevant for those employees who believe that they will perform the task well. For those individuals who believe that they will perform well, valuable training resources may not need to be spent. However, this recommendation should be considered with caution due to the limitations inherent in this study, which we discuss in the next section.

As suggested, using these two concepts in combination has the potential to yield significant cost savings for industry through providing a new opportunity for the prediction and enhancement of task performance, while minimizing the amount of additional training required for technical tasks.

## 5. Limitations

The primary limitation of this study is that it used a crosssectional design at a specific point in time. While this provided us with the ability to detect whether an SP effect exists, within the context of a technology-related task, it does not provide a detailed understanding of the effects or the opportunity to see how performance changes over time based on the introduction of the SP effect. Both these implications are important to build a cumulative body of knowledge surrounding the effect of SP on task performance. Future studies should endeavor to study the effect of SP over time using a phase-wise approach.

The use of a binary outcome variable (i.e., picking the correct software choice) is another limitation. This provided a greater opportunity for subjects to guess the correct answer, which undoubtedly introduced a degree of noise into the analysis of the dataset. Most previous studies on CSE have used a continuous outcome variable to alleviate the potential confounding influence of guessing. However, this study was intentionally limited to a binary outcome measure because the SP construct had never been studied within a technological context, and the construct has traditionally employed a binary outcome variable. The use of a binary outcome measure also allowed the researchers to obtain a larger sample size for the overall study by limiting the duration required to complete the study and therefore increased the likelihood of detecting an effect. At present, an initial support for using SP in the IS realm has been established; hence, future research should endeavor to further the findings by first expanding upon the dependent variable to correct for the limitations of the binary measure. Ideally, this research should incorporate paired samples in order to further support the robustness of the SP effect. This could be done via a repeated measures test where the intervention, that is, the SP effect, was provided in between tests for one subset of the tested population.

Finally, the use of student subjects also limits the generaliz ability of the overall study as the student subjects are a homogenous sample with limited industry experience. However, in this exploratory study the use of student subjects was appropriate because if the effect was apparent within this limited sample set, then it is likely that other sample sets will also conform to the SP effect.

## 6. Conclusion

As with any other study, the results of this work are not all encompassing. The results indicate that using SP in conjunction with CSE may be beneficial for improving task performance. This study further suggests using both these models, the industry may be able to realize costs savings through a decreased need for training. For academia, our study makes an important contribution by introducing SP as an important construct within the technology domain. Further, the results from this study show that future research into CSE could benefit by incorporating the SP effect into the research agenda. Using self-predictions in conjunction with CSE for research endeavors is likely to provide more robust results that have greater degrees of explanatory power. Regarding industry, the results of this research have shown that using SP in conjunction with CSE may allow companies to target training to those specific employees who will be most benefitted from this.
