# Understanding information systems continuance: The case of Internet-based learning technologies

Moez Limayem <sup>a,</sup>\*, Christy M.K. Cheung <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Information Systems Department, Sam M. Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, AR, USA

<sup>b</sup> Department of Finance and Decision Sciences, Hong Kong Baptist University, Hong Kong

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 6 July 2005 Received in revised form 11 November 2006 Accepted 17 February 2008 Available online 16 April 2008

Keywords: Information systems continuance Satisfaction Prior behavior Habit Internet-based learning technology e-Learning

## A B S T R A C T

Our research model expanded the Bhattacherjee’s IS continuance model by adding a moderating effect (IS habit) to IS continuance intention and IS continued usage, postulating direct links between satisfaction and IS continued usage, as well as between prior behavior and IS continued usage. The model was tested with an Internet-based learning technology in a longitudinal setting. Data collected from 505 students were examined using partial least squares analysis. The results presented strong support for the theoretical links of IS continuance model, and for the new moderating effect. Both satisfaction and prior behavior were found to have significant impact on IS continuance. In addition, our findings confirmed the theoretical argument that the strength of intention to predict continuance was weakened by a high level of IS habit.

## 1. Introduction

Bhattacherjee [4] discussed the importance of IS continuance and proposed an IS continuance model. Building on expectation confirmation theory, the model sought to explain users’ intention to continue using an IS. The model basically assumed that continued use was primarily determined by continuance intention. However, this may not fully explain the phenomenon: in particular, frequently performed behaviors have been shown to become habitual and thus automatic over time [17,21]. As argued by Thorngate [25], people’s baseline response to many situations related to continued usage may not be predominantly determined by intention, but rather by habit. The purpose of our study was therefore to extend Bhattacherjee’s IS continuance model by adding habit as the moderator between IS continued intention and IS continued use. We also added two direct causal links from users’ prior behavior and satisfaction to IS continued use. The extended model was then empirically tested in the context of Internet-based learning technologies (IBLT).

Research on e-learning and the use of Internet-based learning technologies has received considerable attention [15,22,28].

We focused on the continuance usage of IBLT because this type of advanced Internet-based technology enhances the teaching and learning experience. Students can access and download teaching materials, including both text and video, directly. They can also make use of an online chat room or video conferencing to communicate with their instructors and fellow students. Understanding students’ adoption of the Internet-based learning technologies (IBLT) is important, as teachers are reluctant to invest time and effort in a new medium if they are not confident that students will find it acceptable. However, an IBLT implementation can only be considered a success when a significant number of students have moved beyond initial adoption and use the technology on a continued basis. Thus, there is a need to identify the factors that encourage students to continue using the Internet-based learning technologies.

## 2. Theoretical background and research model

Fig. 1 depicts our extended model of IS continuance.

## 2.1. IS continuance model

Building on expectation confirmation theory, Bhattacherjee proposed the IS continuance model to explain user post-adoption of information technologies. IS continuance intention was predominantly determined by satisfaction and perceived usefulness. The model reflected current thinking that perceived usefulness was the only construct consistently influencing user intention in both adoption and post-adoption phases. In addition, the model related satisfaction and perceived usefulness to the degree with which user’s expectations about the use of an IS were confirmed. Expectation was thus the baseline level against which users determined their satisfaction. We believed that satisfaction with the use of IBLT reinforced students’ intention to continue using it. The confirmation of students’ evaluations of their use of IBLT would also affect their perception about its usefulness, as well as their satisfaction with it and this, in turn, would influence their satisfaction and intention to continue using the technology.

## 2.2. The role of satisfaction

Satisfaction is one of the most important concepts in marketing, and has attracted much of research interest. Bolton and Lemon [5] empirically demonstrated that customers with higher levels of satisfaction (at a time t) will have higher usage (later, at t + 1) than customers with lower levels of satisfaction. Danaher and Rust [9] also found empirical evidence that a customer who is satisfied with a service will have higher subsequent use. This is consistent with adaptive expectations theory [20] where customers predict future preferences based on their current preferences and do not incorporate future taste changes. We build upon this line of argument and suggested that a user who was more satisfied with their experience would have a higher level of continued use.

## 2.3. The role of prior behavior

Prior behavior has been one of the core topics of many studies in the psychology and marketing domain (e.g. [3,8,19]). Ajzen [2] in his theory of planned behavior argued that prior behavior could be used to test the sufficiency of any model designed to predict future behavior. In marketing, Bolten and Lemon suggested that a customer’s recent usage experience could be used to predict future usage. In IS, Davis and Venkatesh [11] found that users’ hands-on experience was the key driver of sustained usage. They empirically showed that inclusion of prior usage behavior as an antecedent of IS continuance behavior rendered all other determinants insignificant. Cheung and Limayem [6] also empiri cally showed that user’s prior usage experience had a direct impact on continued use. We therefore expected that prior usage behavior of the Internet-based learning technologies would have a positive impact on IBLT continued use.

## 2.4. The moderating impact of habit

Habits are commonly understood as ‘‘learned sequences of acts that become automatic responses to specific situations which may be functional in obtaining certain goals or end states’’ [26]. Once a habit has been established, the performance requires little (if any) conscious attention and only minimal mental effort [27]. Supporting this, Aarts et al. [1] found that habit strength attenuated the amount of information acquired and utilized before a decision was made. So when a behavior was habit-driven, the person did not think about it [18]. Some behaviors may no longer require a conscious effort by the individual; e.g. checking email every morning. We therefore argued that habit reduced the predictive power of intentions.

We used a moderation perspective to illustrate that if individuals are habitually performing a particular behavior, the predictive power of intention is weakened; applied to the context of IIBLT, we expected habit to have a moderating impact on the relationship between continuance intention and its continued usage.

## 3. Method

We used the Blackboard Learning System (www.blackboard.- com). This is a Web-based server software platform that offers course management, an open architecture, and a scalable design for integration with student IS and authentication protocols. Blackboard was adopted as a teaching platform for several courses provided by a local university. Students login to the system to download lecture notes, share documents with their project teammates, and communicate with fellow students and course instructors. The use of this system was voluntary and students could use other ways to download and upload materials and communicate with others. Students were not penalized if they did not wish to use the system. Furthermore, instructors did not make use of the statistics generated by the system to monitor usage.

## 3.1. Data collection procedure

An email invitation was sent to all first year students of the faculty of business in the local university. Students take the same courses in their first year. Thus all students are under exactly the same conditions. In order to encourage participation in this survey, an incentive was given to participants in the form of bookstore cash coupons.

Our participants were asked to complete an online questionnaire about their experience with Internet-based learning technologies. Data was collected at two different point of time. As shown in Fig. 2, at time 1 (after their initial use of IBLT in a 4-week time period), participants answered a questionnaire to determine measures of perceived usefulness, confirmation, satisfaction, IS continuance intention, habit, and prior usage. At time 2 (4 weeks after the first round), participants were asked to complete another questionnaire with measures about their use of the IBLT. A total of 505 students responded in the first round, and 313 completed the second round. The last four digits of the participants’ mobile phone numbers were used to match their answers across the data collection. The demographic profiles of respondents are given in Table 1.

## 3.3. Data analysis

The analysis of the data was performed holistically using partia least squares (PLS): the measurement and structural models were run simultaneously using PLS Graph Version 3.00.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

Our model builds upon the IS continuance model by adding habit as a moderator, and the direct impacts of both prior behavior and satisfaction on IS continued usage. Our findings presented strong support to the existing theoretical links of the model as well as to the new parts hypothesized in our study.

## 5.1. Implications for theory and research

Our research model expands upon Bhattacherjee’s IS continuance model by adding the moderating effect (IS habit) on IS continuance intention and IS continued usage, postulating direct links between satisfaction and IS continued usage, as well as between prior behavior and IS continued usage. Our findings confirmed the argument that the strength of intention to predict continuance is weakened by a high level of IS habit. Precisely, they implied that intentions cannot be regarded as the only predictor of behavior.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

The findings lead to several important recommendations to instructors, academic institutions and Internet-based learning technology designers. We found that usage level as well as user satisfaction should be managed and enhanced for sustainable IS usage. Prior behavior was an important factor in determining the level of IBLT continuance. This suggested that once students adopted these technologies, they had a greater tendency to continue to use them. Therefore, university administrators and instructors should design an implementation plan that encourages IBLT usage at an early educational stage: a ‘‘bad start’’ in initial usage could significantly hinder continuous use. Second, satisfaction became the most important factor determining the level of IBLT continuance. The IS success model [12] asserted that user satisfaction can be maintained by managing information, system, and service quality. Thus, IBLT designers should keep quality in mind when designing and developing IBLT. Finally, when students gain experience with the system, there is a shift from a consciously driven to a habitual behavior. Instructors should try to get students into the habit of using IBLT by making use of discussion forums that facilitate in- and out-of-class discussion, and making lecture notes and other material available for downloading. Once the habit of using the IBLT increases, usage behavior becomes more automatic, leading to a sustainable continuance.

## 5.3. Limitations

We should note some limitations. First, generalizing these results to other types of IS (e.g. CRM, ERP, etc.) should be handled carefully; they have different characteristics. Second, prior research has demonstrated the difference between self-reported measures and computer-recorded measures. Students may have over-reported their usage behavior to give a more politically correct impression, which is a common phenomenon [24]. Finally, our model only explains about 23% of the variance. We therefore believe that other significant factors (such as sociocultural and political impacts) may affect students’ decisions to continue using the IBLT. In Hong Kong, as in other Chinese communities, social factors have significant impact on usage behavior.
