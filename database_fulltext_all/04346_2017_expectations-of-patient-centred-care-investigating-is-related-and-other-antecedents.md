## Abstract

Patient-centered care (PCC) can improve healthcare quality and efficiency. However, it is unclear what constitutes PCC and which individual (including information system (IS) related) factors influence people’s PCC expectations. This study identifies the dimensions of PCC and draws on empowerment theory to model the antecedents of PCC expectations. The test results of the model, based on surveying 171 individuals, indicate that internal health locus of control, information technology (IT) self-efficacy, medical outcome expectancy, and trust in healthcare providers affect people’s expectations of PCC. In addition, the current state of PCC does not meet people’s expectations along all dimensions. This study contributes to research and practice on PCC and healthcare IS.

Keywords: Patient-centered care, internal health locus of control, IT self-efficacy, medical outcome expectancy, trust

## 1 Introduction

Traditionally, the roles of healthcare providers and patients have been clearly defined and distinct. As receivers of care, the sole responsibility of patients has been to provide their personal medical history and describe the symptoms of their illness, which is then used by healthcare providers to diagnose and prescribe suitable treatment. The term “biomedical approach” that focuses purely on biological factors for the identification and treatment of diseases (Mead and Bower 2000), is the approach characterized by healthcare providers having little understanding of the meaning of the illness to their patients and their patients’ treatment preferences. With the aim to improve healthcare quality through patient participation, healthcare organizations are starting to embrace a new paradigm of patient-centered care (PCC), which refers to care that is respectful and responsive to individual patient preferences and needs, and ensures that patients’ values guide clinical decisions (Breen et al. 2009). Unlike the passive role patients play in traditional healthcare provision, here patients take on a pivotal role in the healthcare process. While recognizing its importance, researchers and practitioners have not arrived at a consensus about the defining dimensions of PCC (Kitson et al. 2013). In the absence of a coherent set of PCC dimensions, further research and understanding in this area are hindered.

The interest in PCC is being driven by several suggested benefits including increased patient satisfaction (Rathert et al. 2012), improved healthcare outcomes (Weiner et al. 2013), and higher efficiency of care (Stewart 1995). Nevertheless, the PCC approach is not without its naysayers. For instance, it has also been reported that increased consideration of patients’ needs, values, and preferences may entail more (rather than less) time and healthcare resources required for patient care, and possibly translate into increased costs for healthcare providers (Buerhaus et al. 2005). In view of the time and monetary investments that providers must make for achieving PCC, there is a need to understand whether people are receptive to PCC and what influences their expectations of it. Moreover, it is unclear to what extent people’s expectations of PCC are being met, considering that healthcare providers must transform themselves to address the expectations. Thus, it also becomes important to examine the expectation–perception gap for PCC.

PCC is about empowering patients by incorporating their opinions, needs, and values in their healthcare (Cambria et al. 2012; Holmström and Röing 2010). Accordingly, we consider empowerment theory, which seeks to understand the process and consequences of people’s efforts to exert control and influence over their lives (Zimmerman 2000) to be a suitable overarching lens to understand the determinants of PCC expectations. Yet, although empowerment is a central concept in the PCC literature, there is little empirical study of how empowerment-related constructs influence PCC perceptions (Baird 2014). Overall, researchers have called for more theoretically guided, empirical research on PCC and the role of information technology (IT) in facilitating this approach (Wilson et al. 2014).

#

Indeed, information systems (IS) are being suggested as a key enabler for the transformation toward PCC (Johansson et al. 2010; Klecun 2011). Examples include electronic medical records for the provision of integrated patient information, Internet healthcare websites as a source of health-related information, and e-consultation or e-appointment to provide ubiquitous access to care (Klecun 2011; Krist and Woolf 2011). IS could also reduce the costs of access to care in PCC, though this would depend on individual’s use of the technology. However, although IS are proposed to facilitate the transformation toward PCC, there is a lack of understanding of how people perceive the use of IT in this regard, especially in the formation of their expectations of PCC. Particularly, the majority of previous studies in this area are from physicians’ perspective (Davis et al. 2009; Margalit et al. 2006), and there is a paucity of theoretically grounded research evaluating the influence of IS for PCC from a patient’s perspective (Baird 2014). The development of healthcare IS for the public cannot be successful without considering user’s views (Archer and Cocosila 2014). Thus, it is important to gain insights into how healthcare IS perceptions determine people’s PCC expectations.

Thus, this study aims to address the following research questions: (1) What are the dimensions that define PCC? (2) Are there gaps between people’s expectations and their current perceptions of PCC along the various dimensions? (3) What are the individual (including IS related) factors influencing people’s expectations of PCC? The lack of consensus on the dimensions of PCC motivates our first research question. In response, we reviewed the existing literature to derive a coherent set of six PCC dimensions. On the basis of this set of PCC dimensions, we further examined the expectation–perception gap for PCC using the gap analysis technique from the SERVQUAL literature (Jiang et al. 2002) to answer the second research question. Subsequently, we drew on empowerment theory to identify and model the antecedents of people’s PCC

#

expectations along the various dimensions to address the third research question. The model was tested through a survey of 171 working adults and found to be largely supported. Our results are intended to help healthcare organizations and policy makers assess people’s expectations of PCC; understand the gaps between people’s current perceptions and their expectations of PCC; and identify the individual (included IS related) factors influencing these expectations.

## 2 Conceptual Background

We first describe PCC and derive its dimensions by synthesizing its conceptualizations from previous studies. Subsequently, we describe empowerment theory and identify the antecedents of PCC expectations based on the theory. Finally, we review the PCC literature to map our proposed antecedents to the IS-related factors, PCC motivations, and barriers from this literature. The remaining factors from the literature review are incorporated as control variables (alternative explanations) in our research model.

## 2.1 Patient-centered Care

Since the inception of this term, different conceptualizations of PCC have surfaced in research and practice domains (Kitson et al. 2013). For example, the Institute of Medicine in the US defines PCC as “health care that establishes a partnership among practitioners, patients, and their families to ensure that decisions respect patients’ wants, needs, and preferences and that patients have the education and support they need to make decisions and participate in their own care” (Institute of Medicine 2001). The Picker Institute, a worldwide nonprofit healthcare organization, identifies coordination of care and access to care as two important aspects of PCC in addition to the definition provided by the Institute of Medicine (Picker Institute 2011). In general, previous studies have used definitions of PCC that focus on its different aspects, with a lack of consensus on its dimensions (Kitson et al. 2013).

#

To gain a clearer understanding of PCC and derive a coherent set of dimensions for it, we conducted a comprehensive review of the related literature<sup>1</sup>. Synthesizing the conceptualizations from these papers, we extracted six dimensions of PCC for which the definitions and corresponding concepts from the literature are shown in Table 1.

In response to the first research question, we propose these six aspects, i.e., shared decisionmaking, psychosocial support, access to information, access to care, coordination of care, and self-management, as the defining dimensions of PCC, which can be used to assess PCC. Here, we use them to measure the current state and the expectations of PCC. The concept of PCC expectations (i.e., belief of how good PCC should be) is analogous to a similar concept in the SERVQUAL literature, as applied to healthcare services. As a new healthcare paradigm, PCC aims to transform the delivery of healthcare services. Indeed, the perceived quality of healthcare services is closely related to patient satisfaction (Andaleeb 2001). However, the perceived service quality depends on the gap between the perceived service and the expected service, along the various dimensions of service quality (Jiang et al. 2002). Similarly, here, we assess the gap between people’s current perception and their expectations of PCC along its six dimensions to address our second research question. Furthermore, to better understand how the gap can be bridged, we also examine the factors influencing individual’s PCC expectations along its six dimensions. This is analogous to studies that examine the antecedents of individuals’ expected service in the SERVQUAL literature, e.g., Zeithaml et al. (1993). We consider empowerment theory as a suitable lens to understand the antecedents of PCC expectations as explained below.

## 2.2 Empowerment Theory

The rationale for PCC is based on the assumption that people desire to be involved in their healthcare – or else they would be satisfied with leaving their care responsibility fully to healthcare professionals, i.e., the traditional biomedical approach. Indeed, the previous literature states that PCC is about empowering patients by incorporating their opinions, needs, and values in their healthcare (Cambria et al. 2012; Holmström and Röing 2010). Accordingly, the empowerment theory, which seeks to understand the process and consequences of people’s efforts to exert control and influence over their lives (Zimmerman 2000), explains this rationale. The empowerment theory has previously been used in the healthcare domain, mainly through the concept of patient empowerment (Anderson and Funnell 2010; Bodenheimer et al. 2002) that is related to people taking greater care of their own health (Windrum and Garcia-Goni 2008). Yet, although empowerment is a central concept in the PCC literature, there are limited empirical studies of how empowerment-related factors influence PCC perceptions (Baird 2014). As our study focuses on individual’s PCC expectations, we make use of the empowerment concept at the individual level, i.e., psychological empowerment, and in the nonwork context (Zimmerman 2000; Zimmerman and Rappaport 1988). Empowerment theory proposes that psychologically empowered individuals tend to create opportunities to gain control over their actions, have an impact on the decisions that affect their lives, and develop skills to achieve their goals (Deng et al. 2013). Moreover, previous literature on the outcomes of psychological empowerment indicates that psychological empowerment will lead to a proactive and participatory orientation (Chiang and Hsieh 2012; Spreitzer 1995). In this sense, an empowered person will take greater care of their own health (Windrum and Garcia-Goni 2008), and seek or create opportunities to actively participate in their healthcare process. In the context of moving from traditional healthcare to the PCC approach, this implies that people with higher

#

psychological empowerment will possess higher desires and expectations of PCC. Thus, we propose that individual factors corresponding to the components of psychological empowerment will influence people’s expectations toward PCC.

Previous research conceptualized psychological empowerment as represented by a combination of three components, i.e., personality, cognitive, and motivational, with the belief that one can influence one’s own outcomes (Zimmerman 1995; Zimmerman and Rappaport 1988). First, Zimmerman (2000) conceptualized the personality component as individual’s locus of control (LOC) that refers to a person’s belief about the relationship between the successes and failures in his/her life and his/her own actions. Although external LOC relates to the actions of social referents or external chance, which are out of people’s control (Kuwahara et al. 2004), we focus on internal health LOC in our model as this relates to individuals’ control on their own outcomes. For instance, individuals with higher internal health LOC tend to demonstrate more health-promoting behavior than those with external LOC (Koelen and Lindström 2005). Second, Zimmerman (2000) posited that the cognitive component of psychological empowerment is reflected by self-efficacy, which is an individual’s judgment or confidence of his/her ability to reach the desired goals. In our study, we proposed two forms of self-efficacy as relevant to our model, i.e., IT self-efficacy and medical knowledge efficacy. IT self-efficacy in our study refers to the degree of confidence that the person has in the ability to use IT for healthrelated purpose (Malliari et al. 2012), whereas medical knowledge efficacy indicates the confidence the person has in applying their medical knowledge for health-related purposes (Van Beuningen et al. 2009). Although individual’s medical knowledge efficacy is important in making healthcare decisions, IT self-efficacy is also important as the public now gain most of the medical knowledge from IT-enabled sources (Wilson et al. 2014). Third, the motivational

#

component of psychological empowerment relates to people’s desire to achieve control to obtain certain outcomes (Zimmerman 2000). In the context of our study, we conceptualize this concept, as the desire to achieve PCC due to its expected positive medical outcomes i.e., medical outcome expectancy, and include it in our model.

In addition to these constructs (internal health LOC, the two forms of self-efficacy, and medical outcome expectancy), as mentioned above, we also consider trust as an antecedent of PCC expectations in our model. This is because empowerment in the healthcare context is related to trust in the healthcare provider (Johns 1996). Trust refers to the belief that the trustee will act cooperatively to fulfill the trustor’s expectations without exploiting the trustor’s vulnerabilities (Pavlou and Fygenson 2006). Patients’ trust in healthcare providers depends on their perception of the competence, benevolence, and integrity of the healthcare provider (Leisen and Hyman 2001; McKnight et al. 2002). While empowering people through better knowledge is suggested to positively influence their trust in healthcare providers (Tiu Wright et al. 2006), at the same time, trust of people in their healthcare providers is regarded as a crucial factor influencing the switch from the traditional healthcare model to PCC (Holmström and Röing 2010; Johns 1996). Thus, trust may serve as an important mediator for the linkage between cognitive (knowledge related) components of psychological empowerment and people’s expectations of PCC. Overall, the empowerment theory provides us an overarching foundation to identify and interrelate the antecedents of PCC expectations in our model.

## 2.3 IS, Motivations, and Barriers in PCC

With the advances in IT, healthcare organizations are implementing new IS with the aim to improve efficiency and quality as well as to enable the transformation toward PCC (Klecun 2011). To explicate this phenomenon, we conducted a review of the PCC literature to identify examples of key IS that support the different dimensions of PCC. From the review, we also identified individuals’ motivations and barriers toward PCC, which are related to their expectations of PCC. Of these motivations and barriers, a number of them map to the antecedents of PCC expectations derived using the empowerment theory in the previous section. The remaining factors are included as control variables in our model. The review results are described below.

As a result of the first part of the review, Table 2 provides examples of relevant IS for each PCC dimension and their contributions to that dimension. Our analysis indicated that except for psychosocial support, which may be better enabled in face-to-face sessions, different types of healthcare IS can facilitate the remaining PCC dimensions. It is also noted that some healthcare IS such as electronic health records<sup>2</sup> (EHRs) can contribute to more than one dimension of PCC. For example, information from EHRs can serve as a basis for discussions for the best form of treatment between the health-care provider and patient (i.e., shared decision-making); allow people to be familiar with their health condition (i.e., access to information); and promote seamless collaboration of care teams (i.e., coordination of care).

Subsequently, we identified people’s motivations and barriers toward PCC through the literature review. The motivations toward PCC could be grouped as: better control over personal health, convenience, and better medical outcomes (see Table 3), while the barriers were summarized as: people’s lack of technical skills, medical knowledge, and trust in the provider (see Table 4).

#

As indicated in Tables 3 and 4, a number of the motivations and barriers of PCC identified from the previous literature, i.e., better control over personal health; better medical outcomes; lack of technical skills or literacy to find information; lack of background knowledge regarding illness and treatment; and lack of trust in healthcare providers, correspond to the model predictors identified based on the empowerment theory in the previous section. This supports our premise that the empowerment theory is a relevant theoretical lens to examine the individual factors influencing PCC expectations. With regard to the remaining motivation and barriers identified from the previous literature, i.e., convenience, concerns regarding credibility of medical information online, and concerns regarding information security and privacy, these are included as control variables in our model in addition to demographic variables.

## 3 Research Model and Hypotheses

As discussed above, we propose the following theoretical model to explain people’s expectations of PCC (see Figure 1). The dependent variables are the six dimensions of PCC, which we derived earlier, i.e., shared decision-making, psychosocial support, access to information, access to care, coordination of care, and self-management. The independent variables as discussed above are internal health LOC, IT self-efficacy, and medical outcome expectancy. Furthermore, medical knowledge efficacy is proposed to serve as a mediator between IT self-efficacy and trust, and trust as a mediator between medical knowledge efficacy and various PCC dimensions because they can explicate the mechanism underlying the relationships between IT self-efficacy and these PCC dimensions. We identified gender, age, education level, income, occupation, years of work experience, type, and severity of illness as demographic controls (Roberts 1999). We also added convenience (Klecun 2011), perceived source credibility (Murray et al. 2003),

#

and information privacy concern (Johansson et al. 2010) as controls for this model that correspond to alternative explanations to be accounted for (see Tables 3 and 4).

## 3.1 Internal Health LOC

Internal health LOC should affect those expectations of PCC that people can effectively take control of and believe that they have an effect on their health. Shared decision-making, access to information, access to care, and self-management are the dimensions that possess these properties. However, PCC dimensions, which are mainly the responsibility of the healthcare providers and do not require participation from patients, i.e., providing psychosocial support and the coordination of care among healthcare providers, are not expected to be influenced by internal health LOC.

Shared decision-making requires active patient involvement as patients are required to express their needs and preferences, make choices of treatments, and actively participate in the healthcare process (Gravel et al. 2006). For people with high internal health LOC, they will be likely to perceive that they should participate in these activities to take proper care of their health. Therefore, they may have higher expectations of discussing their treatments with their healthcare providers. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1a: Internal health LOC is positively related to the expectation of shared decision-making As suggested by Cross et al. (2006), people with higher health internal LOC are more likely to seek medical information. Various sources can provide people with information regarding the hospitals and healthcare providers, illnesses, available treatments, and individual’s own medical history. For people with higher LOC, as they believe in controlling their health through their own actions, they are more likely to actively seek this information and expect such access through PCC. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1b: Internal health LOC is positively related to the expectation of access to information People who believe that they should take control of their health are likely to demonstrate behavior to support their belief. For instance, they will tend to initiate more communication with their healthcare providers (Kay et al. 2008). Therefore, people with higher internal health LOC are more likely seek access to their healthcare providers and communicate frequently with them through one channel or another. Their expectations of access to care through PCC would be correspondingly higher. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1c: Internal health LOC is positively related to the expectation of access to care Self-management is another dimension of PCC that requires patient involvement, in which people need to actively take action to manage their own health (Hook 2006). For people with high internal health LOC, if they perceive that they are in control of their health, they may be more likely to take these responsibilities. This should increase their expectation of selfmanagement through PCC. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1d: Internal health LOC is positively related to the expectation of self-management

## 3.2 IT Self-Efficacy

IT self-efficacy, which reflects people’s confidence in their ability to use IT for health-related purposes (Malliari et al. 2012), should influence PCC expectations on those dimensions that involve people’s participation in their healthcare through IT, i.e., shared decision-making, access to information, access to care, and self-management. However, as shared decision-making can be achieved more easily in offline than in online contexts, IT self-efficacy by itself may not be sufficient to explain people’s expectation for shared decision-making through PCC. Therefore, we hypothesize the influence of IT self-efficacy on expectations of access to information, access to care, and self-management.

#

As mentioned previously, a variety of IS are available for people to use to enable PCC. However, people can only make use of IT effectively for this purpose if they possess confidence in their knowledge and skills to perform these activities, i.e., they have high IT self-efficacy (Klein 1997). For example, online healthcare websites or EHR systems can provide people with healthrelated information that they may need, but only if they have the capability to access the information. When people have confidence in their ability to use these systems, they are likely to be more receptive to the information access needed for PCC. Therefore, people with higher IT self-efficacy are more likely to seek access to information and expect it from PCC. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2a: IT self-efficacy is positively related to the expectation of access to information

Getting access to care requires people to be able to use multiple communication channels to connect with their healthcare providers (Gulliford et al. 2002). For example, telemedicine has the potential to support more equitable access to primary health care and other services for rural and remote patients. As another instance, people can arrange for appointments online through the healthcare organization’s web portal, or receive E-consultation on the platform provided by the healthcare organizations. As familiarity with IT is a prerequisite for the use of these different communication channels, people who have more confidence in performing these activities should be more likely to actively seek care and connections with their healthcare providers and expect it from PCC. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2b: IT self-efficacy is positively related to the expectation of access to care

People can manage their own health and gain advice required for managing their own health through various online sites and tools (Hawn 2009) and mobile health apps. Indeed, these IS have become the main source of health information for the public (Wilson and Strong 2014). If people have confidence in their ability to make use of these online sites and m-health apps, they are more likely to seek the responsibility of performing self-management with the help of these IT and expect this from PCC. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2c: IT self-efficacy is positively related to the expectation of self-management

With the development of healthcare-related IT, such as online healthcare websites and EHRs, the public now gains most of its medical knowledge from IT-enabled sources (Wilson et al. 2014). Compared to those with lower IT self-efficacy, people who are confident to make full use of these tools will have better ability to find and manage relevant medical information through them. Hence, they are more likely to be able to acquire relevant medical knowledge and have the confidence to use it with the facilitation of such IT. Therefore, people with higher self-efficacy in using IT for health purposes can be expected to build up their confidence in using and managing their medical knowledge. Thus, we hypothesize:

H3: IT self-efficacy is positively related to medical knowledge efficacy

## 3.3 Mediators

When people have higher confidence in finding and using relevant medical knowledge, it allows them to validate the recommendations of their healthcare providers. In this sense, when people have higher medical knowledge efficacy, they are able to judge the trustworthiness (competence, benevolence, and honesty) of healthcare providers and hence can choose more trustworthy healthcare providers to build up relationships with them (Johns 1996). Moreover, higher medical knowledge efficacy helps people to have more meaningful interactions with their healthcare providers, which is valuable for the development of trusting relationships. Therefore, people who have higher medical knowledge efficacy are likely to choose and build trusting relationships with their healthcare providers. Thus, we hypothesize:

#

H4: Medical knowledge efficacy is positively related to the trust in healthcare providers Participation in shared decision-making requires people to disclose their preferences and needs, express their opinions, and form a close partnership with their healthcare providers. This information sharing is a prerequisite for making shared decisions, which can be facilitated by people’s trust in their healthcare providers (Charles et al. 1997; Gravel et al. 2006). Previous research found that most people express a positive relationship between trust and their desire to be involved in medical decision-making (Kraetschmer et al. 2004), while only a few people who have blind trust in healthcare professionals show a negative effect of trust on desire for shared decision-making, i.e., these people totally depend on the decisions of their physicians. As medical information is readily available nowadays, people rarely have blind trust in healthcare professionals in developed countries (Rowe and Calnan 2006). This means that the positive relationship between trust and desire for shared decision-making may be more common. Therefore, we expect that people who trust their healthcare providers are likely to have higher expectations of shared decision-making for PCC. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5a: Trust in the healthcare providers is positively related to the expectation of shared decisionmaking

Expectation of access to information here refers to people’s expectation to acquire medical history, illness information, treatment information, and medication information. The acquisition of this information partly depends on healthcare providers’ competence, benevolence, and integrity, whereby they are willing to share the information with the patient (Thiede 2005). When people trust their healthcare providers more, they are more likely to build up communications with their healthcare providers, and hence expect more in terms of access to information through PCC. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5b: Trust in the healthcare providers is positively related to the expectation of access to information

Furthermore, a person who has trust in the healthcare providers and system is more likely to seek help to receive appropriate treatment (Rowe and Calnan 2006). Thus, people who trust their healthcare providers may seek access to healthcare and have expectations of access in PCC.

H5c: Trust in the healthcare providers is positively related to the expectation of access to care At the same time, building a relationship with the healthcare provider is also needed for effective psychosocial care provision. Building a relationship and trusting the healthcare provider enable the individual to be open to receiving psychosocial support (Botti et al. 2006) Thus, trust in the healthcare provider is likely to increase the expectations of psychosocial support.

H5d: Trust in the healthcare providers is positively related to the expectation of psychosocial support

However, we do not hypothesize a relationship between trust in the healthcare provider and coordination of care. This is because trust in individual’s main healthcare provider does not imply that the person is aware of the communication and coordination process among different healthcare providers. Often, these activities are performed at the back end (if needed) and the trust is developed with the primary healthcare provider that the individual mainly interacts with.

## 3.4 Medical Outcome Expectancy

People seek healthcare services with the salient purpose of receiving treatment for their medical conditions. Although there has not been a change in this goal over time, people’s needs have increased because of the shifts in control and power from healthcare providers to patients (Berwick 2009). This includes achieving better management of their health (Lee and Lin 2010),

#

expecting more coordinated care (Bates and Bitton 2010), and obtaining psychosocial support from healthcare providers (Breen et al. 2009), as part of the new paradigm of PCC. Conversely, people’s needs of access to information, access to care, and shared decision-making may not change much during the shift toward the new paradigm of PCC. First, access to information and access to care were part of people’s initial expectations of healthcare even before PCC. So, we do not expect that medical outcome expectancy from PCC will increase these expectations. Furthermore, shared decision-making is a new aspect as a part of PCC that people may still be wary of and not expect as a key outcome (Wilson and Strong 2014). Thus, the remaining three dimensions, i.e., self-management, psychosocial support, and coordination of care, are likely to be desired outcomes and mainly realizable through PCC. Hence, we hypothesize:

H6a: Medical outcome expectancy is positively related to the expectation of self-management H6b: Medical outcome expectancy is positively related to the expectation of psychosocial support

H6c: Medical outcome expectancy is positively related to the expectation of coordination of care 4 Research Methodology

To test our hypotheses, we adopted the survey methodology because of the possibility of deriving more generalizable results (Creswell 2009). The survey items were generated by adapting measures that had been validated in previous studies to fit our study context, and creating additional items where necessary (see Appendix A for a full list of items). For the six dimensions of PCC, we measured both people’s expectations and current perceptions of PCC to respond to our second research question about the gap between the two. All subjective measures were rated on seven-point Likert scales anchored from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7).

#

These include the dependent variables, i.e., shared decision-making, psychosocial support, access to information, access to care, self-management, and coordination to care; the independent variables, i.e., internal health LOC, IT self-efficacy, medical knowledge efficacy, trust in healthcare provider, and medical outcome expectancy; and the three nondemographic control variables, i.e., convenience, perceived source credibility, and information privacy concern. The remaining control variables, i.e., gender, age, education level, income, occupation, years of work experience, type, and severity of illness, were measured as single-item objective measures.

## 4.1 Data Collection

For our survey, we collected data from financially independent adults pursing a Master’s degree in a large public university. Working, financially independent, adults are considered a suitable sample for this study because they control their own healthcare choices and expenses once working. As a token of appreciation, each participant was given 10 dollars after completion of the survey. A total of 171 valid responses were received (see Table 5). Most of the respondents were men (77.2%). But this may not be a salient issue, because gender is not regarded as an important variable in the context of PCC (Bertakis and Azari 2011; Mead and Bower 2000). In addition, most respondents were in the age group of 21–30 (87.1%). Again, this may not be a very salient issue, because PCC is a relatively new paradigm and needs patients to put in some effort and use IT tools to manage their personal health. As a result, compared with elderly people, young people may be more aware of PCC (Arora and McHorney 2000). In addition, PCC is not only about curative healthcare but also about preventive healthcare (such as fitness) that is relevant to youngsters. Moreover, old people often may not be directly involved in their own care. Instead, their younger caregivers, such as their adult children, can assist in PCC for them.

Many respondents worked as IT professionals (52.9%) and most of them had 2–5 years of work experience (58.9%). Most of them mentioned Internet healthcare websites as their primary source to obtain medical knowledge (77.2%). The survey did not require each participant to mention their exact medical conditions to avoid privacy concerns. Instead, we requested them to indicate the type of illness (broad category) and their perception of its severity (on a Likert scale) that they based their answers to the survey on. The severity of illness variable is slightly skewed to the left, but nevertheless, the differences among the first five levels of severity are relatively minimal, i.e., each level of illness severity is adequately represented.

## 5.2 Expected and Current State of PCC Gap Analysis

In response to the second research question, we conducted a gap analysis between people’s expectations of PCC and their current perception of PCC. The gap score (G) is the difference between the current state (C) and the individual’s expected state of PCC (E) for each dependent variable (G=E−C). The significance of the gap is determined by the chi-square test, which provides a statistical test on the null hypothesis, similar to the SERVQUAL gap analysis (Jiang et al. 2002). We first tested the gap for each dimension of PCC. For both the current and the expected states, the average for each construct was taken and the gap was computed as the difference between the average of expected state and the average of current state. The descriptive statistics of the gaps are shown in Table 8.

The descriptive statistics show that access to information is the dimension with the largest gap between the expected and current state, as well as the largest variance, whereas the gap for selfmanagement is the least. Measures of skewness and kurtosis were also analyzed to examine normality of the PCC dimensions among the respondents. Skewness and kurtosis values between −2 and +2 are indicative of no problematic deviations, as shown in our results. We next evaluated the significance of the gap for each dimension using chi-square analysis (see Table 9). The chi-square test results demonstrate that the gaps between the current and expected states for all dimensions of PCC are statistically significant. We further examined if each item showed differences in the expectation–current state gap from the rest of the items in the same dimension, and found that each gap was significant, implying that our results are consistent even at the item level.

To answer the third research question, we conducted data analysis for testing the model hypotheses. Partial least square analysis was performed with SmartPLS 2.0, which was used to

test the hypotheses as it places minimal restriction on measurements and sample size, and can assess measurement model parameters and structural path coefficients simultaneously. The results of hypothesis testing are shown in Table 10. A total of 12 of 15 hypotheses in our model were supported. The model explained 15.8% of the variance in shared decision-making, 29.7% of access to information, 21.3% of access to care, 28.6% of self-management, 20.3% of psychosocial support, and 29.1% of coordination of care. The independent variables explained 2.8% of shared decision-making, 14.6% of access to information, 6.5% for access to care, 12.0% for self-management, 7.1% of psychosocial support, and 1.1% for coordination of care.

Internal health LOC was found to positively influence people’s expectations of access to information, access to care, and self-management, i.e., H1b, H1c, and H1d were supported. However, internal health LOC was not related to shared decision-making expectation, i.e., H1a was not supported. With regard to IT self-efficacy, it positively influenced people’s expectations of access to information and self-management, i.e., H2a and H2c were supported. However, IT self-efficacy was not related to the access to care expectation, i.e., H2b was not supported. The results also showed that IT self-efficacy had a positive effect on medical knowledge efficacy, while medical knowledge efficacy in turn positively influenced trust in healthcare providers, i.e., H3 and H4 were supported. At the same time, trust positively influenced the expectations of shared decision-making, access to information, and psychosocial support, i.e., H5a, H5b, and H5d were supported, but not access to care, i.e., H5c was not supported. Last, medical outcome expectancy positively influenced self-management, psychosocial support, and coordination of care expectations, i.e., H6a, H6b, and H6c were supported.

## 6 Discussion and Implications

On the basis of our findings, internal health LOC positively influences people’s expectations of access to information, access to care, and self-management. However, the hypothesized positive relationship between internal health LOC and shared decision-making was not significant. A possible explanation is that internal health LOC may influence whether people depend on their physicians for most of their problems, rather than affecting their interest to achieve shared decision-making. For example, when people have high LOC and believe that they can handle many health issues by themselves, they may be less likely to go to their physicians and may not pay attention to the need of shared decision-making. In contrast, when they have low LOC and seek help from their physicians, their interest to participate in the medical decision-making process may still be influenced by other factors such as their knowledge related to the illness. So, either way LOC may not influence expectations of shared decision-making.

Our results show that IT self-efficacy influences people’s expectations of access to information and self-management as well as medical knowledge efficacy. In addition, we found that the effect of medical knowledge efficacy on PCC expectation is fully mediated by trust, and trust influences the expectations of shared decision-making of PCC, access to information, and psychosocial support. However, IT self-efficacy was not related to people’s access to care expectations. This may be due to people’s perceptions that having access to care has lower requirements on their IT skills. For example, previous research has suggested that patients perceive e-consultation as easy to use and not requiring special skills (Mair and Whitten 2000). In addition, the technologies typically used to communicate with healthcare providers such as email and chat are now being widely used in individuals’ daily lives.

The last hypothesis, which was not supported, is the relationship between trust and access to care expectation. The insignificant result for this relationship may be because trust does not play an important role in people’s desire to access healthcare. Access to care refers to the expectation that people can access healthcare conveniently as and when they need, whereas trust refers to the relationship with their main healthcare providers. Regardless of the extent to which people trust their individual healthcare provider, they may still seek for convenient access to healthcare in general. Indeed, healthcare is crucial for people’s life and hence access to care may be a basic requirement of healthcare for the public (Wilson et al. 2014).

Finally, as expected, we found that medical outcome expectancy had significant positive effects on individuals’ expectations of PCC as expected, such as self-management, psychosocial support, and coordination of care.

## 6.1 Research Contributions

Through this study, we contribute to the literature on the empowerment theory in the domain of PCC. Three components of psychological empowerment, i.e., personality, cognition, and motivation (Zimmerman 2000; Zimmerman and Rappaport 1988), are conceptualized as internal LOC, IT self-efficacy, medical knowledge efficacy, and medical outcome expectancy, with the

#

additional factor of trust provider in this context. Through investigating the effects of the factors corresponding to the components of psychological empowerment on PCC expectations, our study provides insights on the influence of psychological empowerment, which is lacking in the previous literature applying the concept of empowerment in the context of PCC (Breen et al. 2009; Wilson et al. 2014). In addition, by conceptualizing the six dimensions of PCC expectations as the outcomes of psychological empowerment, we further extend the applicability of empowerment theory and enrich the nomological network of psychological empowerment in the healthcare field.

Moreover, this study also contributes to healthcare IS research, specifically in the area of PCC in several ways. First, despite the increasing interest in PCC in the healthcare sector, there is a paucity of research identifying the dimensions of PCC from the patient perspective (Robinson et al. 2008). We emphasize that the focus on patients in the study of PCC is essential as this approach entails people playing an active role in their healthcare process. On the basis of an extensive review of the PCC literature where there is a lack of consensus on the dimensions (Kitson et al. 2013), we synthesized the different conceptualizations of PCC into six dimensions. From these dimensions, we identified two dimensions, i.e., psychosocial support and coordination of care, which are mainly the onus of the healthcare providers to deliver, while the rest of the dimensions would require patient inputs and participation. We further identified relevant IS that can facilitate achieving the six dimensions of PCC, noting also that psychosocial support is still mainly delivered through face-to-face interaction without the use of IS. This contributes to a deeper understanding of the role of IS in the new paradigm of PCC. Second, we contribute to research on PCC by holistically identifying antecedents influencing people expectations of PCC by using the lens of empowerment as theoretical grounding.

Extending from the previous research on the components of psychological empowerment and situated in the PCC context, we identified salient influencing factors for different PCC dimensions. By proposing and empirically validating the antecedents of people’s expectations of PCC, we achieve a better understanding about the enabling factors that can potentially enhance individuals’ participation in PCC. Our findings show that internal health LOC, IT self-efficacy, medical knowledge efficacy, medical outcome expectancy, and trust in the PCC context can indirectly or directly affect people’s PCC expectations.

Third, the IT self-efficacy construct in our model plays an important role in forming PCC expectations, both directly and indirectly. Our results show that IT self-efficacy has direct effects on the two dimensions of PCC, i.e., access to information and self-management. In addition, IT self-efficacy indirectly (through medical knowledge self-efficacy and trust) affects the three dimensions of PCC, i.e., access to information, shared decision-making, and psychosocial support. Of these, expectation of access to information is influenced both directly and indirectly by IT self-efficacy. Thus, IT self-efficacy directly or indirectly influences four of the six dimensions of PCC, i.e., people’s expectations of access to health information, managing (e.g., record/track) their own health, shared decision-making, and getting psychosocial support from their providers. Moreover, the “access to information” dimension of PCC in our model is an important construct from the IS viewpoint, as IT self-efficacy has both direct and indirect effects on it.

Finally, we add to the previous literature by extending SERVQUAL gap analysis to the context of PCC. SERVQUAL gap analysis was originally used to measure customer service quality, and has since been adapted to several other areas such as patient satisfaction (Pakdil and Harwood 2005) and e-learning experience (Udo et al. 2011). In our study, we used SERVQUAL to

#

examine the gap between PCC expectations and perceptions across all six dimensions of PCC. In this manner, our study contributes to a better understanding of the current state of PCC and what is expected from it. This is especially valuable as the literature often explicitly or implicitly assumes that people strongly desire all the dimensions of PCC. Furthermore, these results can be used to identify areas where the largest gaps exist and prioritize efforts to reduce the gaps.

## 6.2 Implications for Practice

The findings from this study suggest that to encourage higher expectations of PCC and increase demand for PCC from the public, policy makers and healthcare can take actions along five ways, i.e., empower people by enhancing their (1) internal health LOC, (2) IT self-efficacy, (3) medical knowledge efficacy, (4) trust in healthcare providers, and (5) medical outcome expectancy. For example, healthcare organizations can enhance people’s internal health LOC by providing faceto-face education to patients regarding their illness and treatments available as well as distributing specific and relevant information through online sites, mobile apps, and/or visual aids (Hashimoto and Fukuhara 2004). These healthcare education programs can also help in boosting medical knowledge efficacy.

Moreover, as IT self-efficacy plays an important role in forming PCC expectations, both directly and indirectly, public agencies or policy makers can facilitate PCC by promoting people’s IT self-efficacy for health purposes. There are various healthcare IT tools through which people can gain healthcare information and participate in PCC. For example, in our study sample, 132 subjects used healthcare websites most commonly, 13 subjects used EHRs, 17 subjects used health-related mobile apps, and 9 subjects used other tools. It appears that the use of healthcare websites is common, followed by health-related mobile apps. Public agencies and healthcare

providers can not only introduce training sessions to improve the use of existing tools but also suggest the use of new tools to improve people’ IT self-efficacy.

Last, healthcare organizations can enhance people’s trust in their healthcare providers to promote their expectation of shared decision-making and psychosocial support. Previous research suggests that healthcare provider’s conveyance of competence, integrity, and caring can largely boost people’s trust (Hillen et al. 2014; Klostermann et al. 2005). As a result, healthcare providers are advised to build their patients’ trust during their consultations, such as mentioning their relevant successful past experiences, expressing the limitations of the provided treatments, and actively addressing their patients’ concerns. Healthcare providers can also embark on initiatives to highlight the potential benefits of PCC to their patients, and what it takes to understand these benefits. Because PCC requires active participation by patients, these expectations should be communicated to people to allow them to recognize their role in PCC. However, we also note that patients may not necessarily expect and desire shared decisionmaking even though they are empowered to do so. This may stem from the respect of the medical expertise that the healthcare provider possesses, such that although patients have an understanding of their medical conditions, the decision for their treatment is left to the healthcare provider. Therefore, healthcare providers will need to gauge the patient’s willingness to participate in PCC and customize their care accordingly.

As our results show that people do not experience the level of PCC that they expect from their healthcare providers, policy makers and healthcare providers would like to take necessary steps to meet or manage (when not feasible) these demands and expectations within the constraints they operate under. In view of the disparity between the current state and expectations of the public, policy makers may consider providing incentives to encourage implementation of PCC

#

by healthcare institutions. Specifically, incentives could be provided to implement IS and promote their use for PCC. Overall, IS can be an effective tool to fill the gap between people’s expectations of PCC and the current state. With a full gap analysis of their current situation, healthcare organizations can choose the dimension most urgently in need of improvement and implement the corresponding IS. Change management is needed during the IS implementation process to ensure a smooth transition toward PCC as this constitutes a paradigm change in health care. After the IS is implemented, education programs and training sessions, as mentioned above, are required to increase people’s internal health LOC and IT self-efficacy, enabling them to familiarize with the new IS, take full advantage of it, and actively participate in PCC.

## 7 Limitations and Future Research

There are a few limitations of this study. First, our data set was mainly collected from financially independent working adults, and hence our results need to be applied with caution. Future collection of data from hospital patients and senior citizens could increase the generalizability of our findings. Second, as our respondents were mainly men and young people, future research may use a more gender-balanced and age-balanced sample to verify the results from this study. Third, our study considered healthcare IT for PCC in aggregate. Future research could further explore the roles of different types of IT in PCC. Fourth, our data were obtained from primary sources by using a survey questionnaire. Surveys can be complemented by other methodologies such as interviews to shed further light on the antecedents driving PCC expectations. Lastly, our study focuses on people’s expectations of PCC and their current PCC perceptions. Therefore, actual participation in PCC has only been investigated in part. Future research can focus on examining the relationship between expectations and actual behavior.

## 8 Conclusion

#

As PCC emerges as a new approach for healthcare provision, changes in how healthcare is delivered necessitate changes from both providers and the public. This study first identifies the different dimensions of PCC to gain clarity about the concept, with a lack of consensus in the previous research. The use of healthcare IS can facilitate the realization of PCC benefits and alleviate several concerns regarding PCC such as increased time and human resources required for health care. This paper offers examples of healthcare IS that can facilitate PCC, realize their benefits, and overcome the barriers of use. With a focus on people’s expectations of PCC, this study developed and tested a model on the antecedents of people’s expectations. Internal health LOC, IT self-efficacy, trust in healthcare providers, and medical outcome expectancy were found to influence PCC expectations. The gap in people’s current perceptions of PCC and their expectations was also computed. The current states of people’s perception of all the dimensions of PCC are below their expectations, indicating considerable scope for enhancement. This study contributes by shedding light on this under-researched phenomenon.
