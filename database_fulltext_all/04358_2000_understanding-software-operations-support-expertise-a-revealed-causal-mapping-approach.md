## Abstract

This paper utilizes a qualitative methodology, revealed causal mapping (RCM), to investigate the phenomenon of software operations support expertise. Software operations support is a large portion of the IS work performed in organizations. While we as researchers have access to generalized theories and frameworks of expertise, very little is known about expertise in this critical area. To understand software operations support expertise, a mid-range theory is evoked from interviews with experts and the construction of RCMs from those interviews. The results of this study indicate that software operation support expertise is comprised of five major constructs: personal competencies, environmental factors, support personnel motivation, IS policies, and support personnel outcomes. Additionally, this study revealed that these constructs interact differently in contexts where software support is the main activity versus contexts where the focus is development. This study demonstrates that the use of the RCM methodology yields constructs of software operations support expertise that are not suggested by generalized theory. In addition, the use of RCM as an evocative, qualitative methodology reveals the interaction and linkages between these constructs. This paper also provides a history of and tutorial to the RCM methodology for use by the research community.

Keywords: Cognitive mapping, IS personnel, IS maintenance, IS policy

## Introduction

In the information age, the expertise of information systems (IS) personnel is critical to the successful development, deployment, and support of information technology (Applegate 1995a, 1995b). In recent years, software operations support has become a critical element of the information systems function, with as much as 90% of the software life cycle effort and cost in many organizations being spent in the operations support phase after the application has been developed and installed (Bennett 1991; Gorla 1991). We use the term “software operations support” rather than “software maintenance” in this paper (Banker et al. 1987, 1991). Software operations support includes the activities included in the IEEE (1990) definition of software maintenance, but also encompasses activities such as evaluating user business processes for opportunities, assisting users with new or modified requirements, and ongoing platform or technology changes. Although many IS support specialists refer to themselves as software maintainers, the authors believe that the time has come to describe this activity in a way that more completely and meaningfully reflects the actual range of activities performed.

A survey of critical IS activities ranked supporting the existing portfolio of applications as the most important current IS activity (Trauth et al. 1993). There is also a growing recognition that expert software operations support personnel can contribute to faster and better quality software operations and organizational change initiatives (Gorla 1991; Leitheiser 1992; Ramanujan and Cooper 1994). Expert software operations personnel are becoming a scarce resource at the same time that they are being recognized as key contributors to organizational success (Rebello 1996). A Society for Information Management member survey recently ranked recruiting and developing IS human resources as a top 10 key issue, emphasizing current and future personnel shortages (Brancheau et al. 1996). Under these conditions, organizations will have to do more to develop and retain experts. This will require insight into the nature of IS expertise in general and software operations support expertise in particular.

A systematic identification of major constructs for software operations support as a specific job category has yet to receive significant attention. In spite of calls for a broadened view of expertise (Scarbrough 1993), the domain of software operations support remains relatively unexplored (Batra and Davis 1992). Many frameworks and definitions of general expertise currently exist (Bedard 1991; Marchant 1990; Shanteau 1992b; Shanteau and Stewart 1992; Slatter 1990). Information systems personnel expertise has also been studied (Batra and Davis 1992; Detienne 1991; Koubek et al. 1989; Patel and Groen 1991). These IS expertise studies have primarily studied novice-expert differences or have focused on general technical expertise. Between the generalized studies of expertise and the IS skill specific studies of expertise, there is a gap in the expertise literature. This gap is present because we know very little about the holistic conceptualization of IS expertise and we know even less about expertise in specific domains such as software operation support.

Filling these gaps in our knowledge requires the development of mid-range theories; theories where general frameworks are available but lack domain specific operationalized constructs. These theories can be evoked through qualitative research methods using experts in a particular domain as respondents and are interpreted through the knowledge found in generalized theories, in this case, theories of expertise.

One qualitative methodology that lends itself well to the development of mid-range theories is revealed causal mapping (RCM) (Narayanan and Fahey 1990). Revealed causal mapping is a subcategory of cognitive mapping where respondents, in this case software operations support experts, reveal their set of causal assertions about a domain specific phenomenon through the interview process. This interview process is guided by very generalized theories of the phenomenon, but is designed to evoke new constructs and linkages that are specific to the domain of study. While the revealed causal mapping process is inductive in evoking constructs and linkages, it contributes to building mid-range theory in that the result of the process is a series of revealed causal maps that reflect the frequency that causal linkages appear in the respondent interviews. In this way, domain specific constructs are identified and a picture of how these constructs are linked also emerges. The revealed causal maps provide the basis for further quantitative investigation of the phenomenon. By providing a great deal of insight into a particular domain, RCMs provide mid-range theory that goes beyond high level theories and frameworks.

This paper makes two principal contributions. First, we use the revealed causal mapping methodology to evoke mid-range theory in a domain where general frameworks are available, but specific operationalizations are yet to be constructed. This paper also presents a history and tutorial on revealed causal mapping, which will serve as a reference for future researchers.

Second, this paper contributes to the understanding and description of software operations support expertise. This study indicates that expertise in software operations support is a complex, multi-dimensional, and multi-layered phenomenon. Five constructs of software operations support expertise were evoked in this study: personal competencies, environment, motivation, IS group outcomes, and IS policy. These constructs are interrelated and have a number of interrelated concepts underlying them. We also found that the relationships among the constructs and concepts differed in two different support contexts: a mature support-oriented context and an evolving development-oriented context.

## Toward a Theory of Software Operations Support Expertise

## General Theoretical Frameworks of Expertise

Expertise has been defined in several ways. Bedard (1991) defines expertise as the possession of a large body of knowledge and procedural skill. Other studies have defined expertise as a combination of knowledge and ability, and the capability to achieve results with this knowledge. Marchant (1990) defines expertise as a process by which individuals develop the ability to achieve task-specific superior performance. Similarly, Frensch and Sternberg (1989) define expertise as an ability, acquired by practice, to perform qualitatively well in a particular domain. In perhaps the most encompassing framework developed to represent expertise, Shanteau and Stewart (1992) define an expert as someone displaying special skill or knowledge in a specific domain based on training or experience.

All of the above definitions recognize the domaindependence of expertise. The knowledge engineering literature has also identified domain knowledge as a critical component of expertise (Slatter 1990). Additional studies have shown that domain knowledge is critical in acquiring expertise in domains such as auditing (Shanteau 1992a), software design (Adelson and Soloway 1985) and political science (Voss et al. 1983).

Psychological traits have been shown to be a component of expertise in many frameworks (Shanteau and Stewart 1992). In addition to domain knowledge, psychologists have also argued that experts often display a common set of psychological traits that reflect “self presentation” (Goffman 1959), the creation and maintenance of a public image. These traits include strong selfconfidence, excellent communication skills, the ability to adapt to new situations, and a clear sense of responsibility (Shanteau 1984, 1988).

Prior literature on expertise has also described various cognitive skills commonly possessed by experts. Although these skills were previously included under psychological traits, some researchers have argued that they should be considered separately (Shanteau 1992b). Some of these skills include highly developed attention abilities, a sense of what is relevant, the ability to identify exceptions to rules, and the capability to work effectively under stress. Although prior studies have identified some categories of cognitive skills and psychological traits commonly possessed by experts, their relative importance differs from domain to domain. For example, “chess experts do not appear to be better general thinkers for their genius in chess” (Anderson 1990). These findings point to the need for developing mid-range, domain specific theories of expertise from the generalized frameworks that currently exist.

Another characteristic of expertise is decision strategies used by experts (Shanteau and Stewart 1992). Experts need to have the ability to apply their knowledge and skills effectively in executing their tasks. Research has shown that experts use different decision strategies and many of these strategies are unique to given domains. For instance, novices have been found to reason backward from the unknowns to the givens in solving physics problems. Expert physicists, in contrast, reason forward using stored “functiona units” from the givens to the goal (Larkin 1979). Once again, the literature has shown that many strategies are unique to given domains, demonstrating the need for mid-range theories in specific disciplines.

Task characteristics are also a part of expertise (Shanteau 1992a). Task characteristics determine whether it is possible for experts to behave competently or not. Even with the appropriate knowledge, traits, skills and strategies, the competence observed in an expert depends on the task. The importance of understanding task characteristics has been emphasized repeatedly in judgment and decision research (Hammond and Arkes 1986).

## IS Expertise

Prior research in the IS discipline has investigated knowledge, skills, and abilities in specific domains. Studies of expertise in programming (Detienne 1991; Koubek et al. 1989), database design (Batra and Davis 1992), and systems analysis (Vitalari 1985) have identified specific skills and knowledge categories associated with experts in those areas. Studies in different domains of IS provide certain categories of generic knowledge required by IS experts (Patel and Groen 1991). However, these studies have not identified categories of knowledge specific to the domain of software operations support. Moreover, we do not yet know which categories of the generic IS knowledge are most relevant to support expertise.

Studies in IS often address support as an artifact of software development rather than as a distinct part of the total system life cycle (Dekleva 1992;

Perry 1992; Swanson and Beath 1990). Further, although software operations support has traditionally been addressed from three perspectives—the technical perspective, the application development perspective, and the maintainer perspective—the vast majority of the literature primarily addresses the technica perspective (Banker et al. 1987, 1991; Baxter 1992; Gibson and Senn 1989; Kafura and Reddy 1987; Peercy 1981; Rombach and Ulery 1989). Kim and Weston (1988) are among the few who present the individual support person’s view of software operations support, although technical characteristics of support rather than the expertise of the support personnel are the focus of their study.

## Toward Software Operations Support Expertise

In summary, two types of knowledge are available about software operations support expertise: Generalized frameworks of expertise, such as that presented by Shanteau and Stewart, and concrete specific knowledge about technical expertise in the IS support and development domains. Very little research has been conducted on expertise in the software operations support domain.

If expertise is domain dependent, this raises an interesting methodological problem for researchers studying expertise in the software operations support area. Do we begin by importing generalized models of expertise to the software operations support domain? At the other extreme, should we completely abandon existing theories and develop a grounded theory of expertise in this area? To more fully understand the phenomenon of software operations support expertise, we need knowledge of the mid-range. What is lacking is a methodological approach to building a representation of software operations support expertise that falls between grand abstract theory and domain specific detail.

As an IS field, we know that many in-house IS organizations do mostly support while others do mostly development, and that the expertise required in these two contexts is very different (Krogstie 1995). How are we going reveal a level of depth about the specific concepts and constructs that represent software operations support in these differing contexts? Revealed causa mapping offers one useful approach (Narayanan and Fahey 1990). By eliciting the hidden knowledge of experts, we can use existing theory to guide us to an understanding of software operations support expertise. Through this use of expert respondents, this methodology can evoke both concepts and constructs of software operations support expertise, as well as the causa linkages between constructs.

## Software Operations Support Contexts

Software operations support is often studied as an afterthought of software development (Dekleva 1992; Perry 1992; Swanson and Beath 1990). Many organizations that do not produce software as their primary product or service are now purchasing and customizing applications rather than building them from scratch (Soh et al. 2000). Thus most of the software activity in the organization is software operations support rather than new development. Additionally, these organizations are supporting older legacy systems that have yet to be replaced. For the purpose of this study, these organizations are defined as having a mature support context, in that the majority of IS work being performed is support related.

In contrast, there are organizations where software is a key business competency and the choice has been made to develop specialized applications in-house. These organizations constantly redevelop and dispose of old systems as business needs change. Less than 20% of the IS work is in software operations support. The focus is on development. In this study, these environments are called the evolving software operation support context. Consistent with prior literature on development and maintenance (Guimaraes 1985; Krogstie 1995), we believe that the investigation of mature and evolving support expertise contexts will reveal some differences in how support expertise is conceptualized. Table 1 summarizes differences in the two support contexts.

## The Mature Software Operations Support Context

Mature software operations support organizations can be extremely complex. The technological environment is a combination of legacy systems and newer, purchased technologies. Software operations support is the primary IS function in these organizations, with as much as 90% of the IS budget spent on this activity (Bennett 1991; Gorla 1991). The software operations support function is mature, stabilized, and has grown over a long period. Activities performed include supporting legacy systems, migrating these systems to a client server and ERP environment, as well as providing support services to the user departments. Most of the support groups act as internal consultants to the user organizations. The software operations support function is often decentralized in these organizations, with each group functionally specialized and having a high degree of autonomy. Support is often viewed as an “art” in this environment. One respondent from the mature environment in this study describes the environment as highly creative:

I know from my own experience that the imposition of structured methods causes a rub. It takes away part of their creative juices. Now they're getting something thrust upon them. Well, I used to do this all the time my way and it's no good anymore? “Not invented here” syndrome starts to phase in and they start thinking of it as taking away their creative thinking that's involved in their job. (Support Expert, Manufacturing Organization, User Interface Group, 7/96)

As the above quote suggests, the work culture within each group is informal and relatively unstructured. However, at the organizational level, there is a top-down rather than a team type of work design. A high degree of coordination is required between the large number of software operations support groups to execute functions across the organization.

Almost any system change is going to be multi-person and is going to touch many programs that are integrated together. In the same way that you have programs integrated together, you need to have your group that is doing the changes to them integrated together so at the end they have met the business requirement. (Support Expert, Manufacturing Organization, Corporate IS Group, 7/96)

## The Evolving Software Operations Support Context

Software operations support is a minor area of the IS function in the evolving context. Support personnel play a dual role in these organizations, performing both support and development. The support to development ratio is approximately 20:80 and the legacy versus new technology ratio is also 20:80. The Vice President of IS of an evolving organization in this study describes this lower ratio of support activity in this way:

When we find out that we are spending more than 20% of our money on software operations support, we look for ways to replace systems with new technologies that will give us a competitive advantage. (VP IS, Retail Organization, 10/96)

The software operations support environment is less diverse and complex in evolving organizations compared to the mature manufacturing organization. The IS function is more centralized and the support groups are specialized based on the type of applications they support. Support personnel do multiple tasks within a team. The culture within the groups is relatively structured, but the emphasis is on team effort. One respondent, from the evolving environment, describes the difference between mature and evolving environments as:

From the standpoint of someone who is on the point of moving into distributed processing and a PC environment, hardware skills are becoming more important. It used to be as a programmer you had to know your JCL and your COBOL. We didn't have to know the operating system.…We didn't have to know a lot about the hardware. Now, with the move into distributed processing in the PC environment, it's becoming more and more important. You've got to understand more about a PC operating system. Whether it's Windows, Windows 3.1 or Win 95, whether it's how to add hardware and software to it and how they interact, and you do need to know more. (Support Expert, University Organization, Distributed Systems Group, 8/96)

The support personnel in these organizations do a variety of functions in a structured environment of relatively new technologies. Examples in this study include C++ applications, a PeopleSoft ERP and Oracle database customized installation, and Visual Basic and Access applications written inhouse.

## The Causal Mapping Approach

## History

Causal mapping is a relatively new qualitative methodology that has been used in studies of managerial cognition and strategy. Cognitive maps were first used by Tolman (1948), as a representation of mental models. Cognitive maps represent cognitive structures in human memory (Shavelson 1972). Both cognitive mapping and causal mapping are a form of content analysis that isolates the key assertions within a document that deal with causality, existence, or categorization (Eden 1992; Huff 1990).

Social and organizational scientists began using causal mapping in the 1970s. In a study of the belief systems of political leaders, the earliest study using causal maps, Axelrod (1976, p. 72) defined causal mapping as:

a specific way of representing a person's assertions about some limited domain such as a policy problem. It is designed to capture the structure of the person's causal assertions and to generate the consequences that follow from the assertions.

The major impetus for using cognitive and causal maps in areas of strategy and management came from a special conference on managerial cognition held in 1987. Selected papers from this conference were included in a special issue of Journal of Management Studies, including Fahey and Narayanan's (1989) longitudinal study of evolution of causal maps. Concurrently, another attempt to further the use of cognitive mapping in the area of strategic management was made by Huff (1990), who presented a wide array of methods for mapping strategic thought including causal mapping (Narayanan and Fahey 1990; Reger 1990). More recent research has been inspired by the insights provided by these studies. A special issue of Organization Science on managerial cognition presented different methods of mapping cognition including causal mapping (Laukkanen 1996; Meindl et al. 1996; Priem 1996).

## Revealed Causal Maps

In an attempt to clarify the meaning of causal maps, we introduce the concept of revealed causal maps as representations of true causal maps. True causal maps of individuals cannot actually be captured since they reside inside people's minds. Causal maps are revealed to the world by decision makers during the process of public discussions and exposure (Narayanan and Fahey 1990). A revealed causal map (RCM) can be defined as the network of causal relations embedded in an individual's explicit statements. RCMs are assertions of causality that individuals choose to reveal to the world around them. The correlation between “true” and “revealed causal maps” is never perfect and is shaped by how the maps are revealed and the context in which it happens. There is also a distinction between articulated theory or logic and theory or logic “in use,” which can result in differences between true and revealed maps (Argyris 1982). Strong assumptions regarding awareness, exhaustiveness, and accuracy constrain any approach in accessing causal maps. Such assumptions are partly circumvented in the case of RCMs. This methodology allows researchers to make and validate linkages while gaining an understanding of the phenomenon one step lower in the ladder of inference than the one represented in existing theories (Fahey and Narayanan 1989). Although the causal mapping technique has most frequently been used to understand specific decisions made in a policy or strategic context, as a methodological approach its applicability is much broader, a topic we will discuss in the following section.

## Applicability of Revealed Causal Mapping

Revealed causal maps represent evoked, domain specific theories that link revealed observations with constructs and then link constructs into a network of causal relations, such as those found in a wide variety of theoretical fields. We emphasize domain specific, since the maps are best visualized not as general cognitive assumptions or theories, but as causal representations of a specific phenomenon. In this sense, causal maps represent an entity's (individual, group, or organization) inductively derived theories (Dutton et al. 1983). Although all theories are inductively derived (Holland et. al. 1986), revealed causal maps provide the opportunity to build mid-range theories where generalized frameworks and theories exist but no domain specific constructs and linkages have previously been revealed.

At least six distinct causal mapping approaches are available in the literature. First, Eden (1992) has developed a causal mapping method to clarify a decision problem and help policy makers come to a decision resolution. Second, Boland et al. (1994) illustrate an intensive IT augmented approach to causal mapping to facilitate a hermeneutic process of inquiry. Third, Zmud et al. (1993) demonstrated the use of mental imagery in requirements analysis. Fourth, Bougon et al. (1977) utilized an etiograph to study manageria cognition in a group setting. Fifth, Ford and Hegarty (1984) elicited causal linkages from respondents among predetermined concepts in a hypothesis-testing context. Finally, textual analysis or causal mapping of texts (derived from interviews or archival data such as annual reports) has been used in theory construction and testing (Narayanan and Fahey 1990).

The six approaches to causal mapping demonstrate the use of the methodology for a variety of classes of problems. When the methodological intention is to facilitate intervention in a specific organizational decision, the methods developed by Eden, Boland et al., or Zmud et al. are especially appropriate. Boland et al. and Zmud et al. are also appropriate in grounded theory approaches, where the intent is to generate theoretical insights or to develop a new theory. Finally, where a confirmatory methodology is needed to operationalize a theory or test a hypothesis, then less intrusive methods such as Ford and Hegarty or textual analysis are appropriate.

The examples above show that causal mapping has been widely applied, from general domains with undeveloped theories to specific domains with clearly formulated theories. Between these domains remain a class of theoretical problems which, for our purpose, we will label as “evocative” studies. Evocative studies deal with specific domains where general theoretical frameworks may be available, but operationalization of concepts and specification of linkages among the concepts are still unknown. In evocative studies, experts who practice in a specific domain are available, but studies are needed to unearth their knowledge and examine it through available general theoretical frameworks to construct domain specific theories. In these studies, we need approaches and techniques that fill the gaps between qualitative identification and quantitative verification, and that evoke the constructs and linkages particular to the specific domain. As discussed earlier in this paper, software operations support expertise is one of the specific domains where evocative studies are needed.

## Methodology

## Evaluation of the Revealed Causal Mapping Methodology

As we will demonstrate in this study of software operations support expertise, revealed causa mapping can be employed in evocative studies. To distinguish this type of study from confirmatory studies with which people may be more familiar, we compare the research challenges in both types of studies using six methodological dimensions.

1. Choice of source. Where do we get information to advance our theoretical interests? In confirmatory studies, where theories are clearly defined, there is a wide array of available sources such as observation, archival sources, and more personal sources such as interviews. In evocative studies, the task is to access relevant experts, people that can educate us, and to assist them in articulating their mostly tacit knowledge (Laukkanen 1996; Narayanan and Fahey 1990).

2. Sampling criteria. In confirmatory studies, the sampling criteria are likely to be determined by the demands of the inference procedure. In evocative studies, statistical inference is secondary to the task of arriving at a representation of the domain specific theory. Rather than control for differences, we seek heterogeneity to build generalizable theories.

3. Categories and linkages among categories. In confirmatory studies, categories and the linkages among them are specified a prior since the theoretical development has proceeded to a reasonable level of maturity. However, validity of operationalization of concepts is a key task in evocative studies. Although general theoretical frameworks are available, the respondents speak a language of their own. Casting respondent terms into theoretical language is an important task to be completed.

4. Unit of analysis. The appropriate specification of the unit of analysis is critical, a feature that is common to both types of studies. As we have mentioned, causal maps have been employed as tools of verification at severa levels: individual, group, and organization. In evocative studies, unit of analysis, or the level of aggregation of causal maps employed, should correspond to the sampling criteria invoked. Of course, we should expect variation in causal maps around the criteria of heterogeneity; thus the constructed theory is open to differences among contexts, some sampled and others not yet sampled.

5. Inference procedures. Statistical inference procedures dominate confirmatory studies. In evocative studies, the output of inquiry is a valid theoretical representation of the domainspecific phenomena. One characteristic of a good theory is its efficiency or parsimony; hence we hope for convergence among respondents. The test, then, is how fast do responses converge? If maps constructed out of different sources of data differ widely, we should question the level of generalizability, and hence the adequacy, of the evoked theories.

6. Validity of findings. In confirmatory studies, the major test of the validity of findings is agreement with existing theories. If there is severe discrepancy, then we seek explanations for divergence and only in rare cases is the theory disputed. Validity is also an important issue in evocative studies. At least two questions need to be addressed before we can accept the validity of a constructed theory evoked from expert respondents. The first concerns the applicability of the general theoretical framework invoked: Do different criteria postulated to create heterogeneity in theories lead to meaningful differences in revealed causal maps? The second focuses on the maps as representations of knowledge: Do the sources of data, the experts who have offered their knowledge, find the representation of their theories accurate?

## Revealed Causal Mapping Process

We used a four step process to evoke the revealed causal maps of software operations support experts. The first step in this process is data elicitation, which includes site and sample selection. The second step is the actual construction of the RCMs. In the third step, the maps are subjected to a validation process. Finally, the RCMs are analyzed and interpreted. These steps are represented in Figure 1.

A pilot study of these four steps was performed before the actual study was completed.

## Pilot Study

A pilot study was performed in two organizations. These organizations were a Fortune 50 manufacturing company and a medium sized retailing company. The manufacturer has over 100,000 employees and represents the mature support environment. The technological environment of this organization is extremely complex and is a combination of legacy systems and newer, purchased technologies. This organization has had a relatively stable growth rate that has resulted in a slowly changing IS technical and business environment. Software operations support is the primary IS function with a ratio of 80:20 to software development. This organization has three primary IS groups with over 50 sub-groups and hundreds of IS teams. The retail organization, with about 15,000 employees, represents the evolving IS context. In this organization, software operations support is a minor area of the IS function and support personnel play a dual role, performing both support and development. The support to development ratio is approximately 20:80 and the legacy versus new technology ratio is also 20:80. The two organizations were chosen for the pilot due to their differing contexts and willingness to participate. Ten software operations support experts were interviewed from each of these contexts.

## Data Elicitation

The first step in data elicitation is the selection of research sites. The two sites used in the pilot study were selected for availability of experts and willingness to participate. The manufacturing firm had so many diverse IS groups and support experts available that it was judged by the researchers to be representative of the mature context. One of the researchers has worked closely with this organization for eight years, and in previous statistical studies, the various IS groups were found to be heterogeneous in their perceptions (Nelson and Ghods 2000). The retail organization, however, was felt to be somewhat homogenous by the researchers, so three more sites were found for the evolving context. These sites are a financial consulting firm, a university client server organization, and a business schoo computing group. Once again, this was a convenience sample, as organizations had to be willing to participate and had to contain support experts.

Software operations supports experts were located at the sites through peer and management nomination. Abdolmohammadi and Shanteau (1992) have shown that professionals in a field are competent to identify a consistent set of attributes they associate with experts. Therefore, nomination rather than factors such as job title and education level was used in identifying support experts. A total of 50 support experts were interviewed: 28 in the mature software operations support context and 22 in the evolving context. The number of required interviews was a judgement call by the researchers, since point of redundancy (Axelrod 1976), which calculates the adequacy of the sample, is not known until the RCMs are cast. In this study, the point of redundancy suggested after the fact that the sample was large enough to exhaust the concepts. If it was not, it would have been necessary to conduct more interviews. In addition to the 50 experts, 10 of the managers and co-workers who helped identify the experts were interviewed for background information on the software operation support environment.

This study used open interview techniques with probes (Rossi et al. 1983). An interview guide was used to facilitate the process. This guide was loosely developed from the available genera frameworks of expertise, but was designed to elicit and probe concepts mentioned by the experts. Respondents were asked about the characteristics of software operations support expertise. For example:

When I say the words expertise of software maintainers (the term respondents used to describe themselves), what characteristics does this make you think of?

Based on the response to this question, probing questions were asked to elicit further specific attributes of expertise. The interviewers did not constrain responses to questions (Payne 1951). Each interview lasted from 20 to 90 minutes. The interviews were transcribed to a document format, ranging from 15 to 20 pages. Table 3 describes the respondents in this study.

## Constructing Revealed Causal Maps

The revealed causal maps are derived from the transcribed interviews in five steps. This procedure (Dreher et al. 1999) is detailed in Appendix A. In the first step, the causal statements are derived from the interviews, following the guidelines suggested by Axelrod. The two main considerations in this step are to identify the causal connectors and to establish the reliability of the identification procedure. Causal linkages are identified by finding causal statements in the transcribed interviews. Causal statements are statements that imply an explicit cause-effect relationship. Some of the key words used in identifying causal statements are “if-then,” “because,” “so,” “as,” and so forth. Consistent with Narayanan and Fahey, all the causal statements and linkages were recorded in the actual language of the experts.

In the second step, the causal statements derived in the first step are broken into causes and effects to construct the raw causal maps. The raw causal maps of the first 22 respondents in the sample were derived independently by two researchers. The reliability between these researchers was calculated by measuring the level of agreement among them on terms and linkages. For example, the following causal statement was rated by both researchers as a link between formal education and cognitive abilities:

The third step in constructing RCMs is the identification of relevant concepts from the raw causal statements (Narayanan and Fahey 1990). This begins with grouping frequently mentioned words in the raw revealed causal maps. These groupings were then examined for theoretical or logical relevance and were placed into categories that would be understood and interpretable by researchers interested in IS and related fields. For example, from a theoretical perspective, Shanteau’s (1982) general framework of expertise has five constructs of expertise: domain knowledge, psychological traits, cognitive skills, decision strategies, and task characteristics. While we did not expect our evoked maps to reflect these exact constructs, we utilized the general framework for insight into our maps. Likewise, the IS literature has a great deal of discussion about technical expertise, and we used this to guide our interpretation and categorization of the concepts. In the fourth step, the concepts were recast into concept level revealed causal maps.

The last step of the mapping procedure is recasting the individual concept maps into construct maps and then aggregating these maps across individuals. Once the concepts were identified and mapped, they were grouped into constructs. This procedure was done by four individuals working independently: two information systems professors and two information systems professionals who were respondents in this study. The concepts were put on pieces of paper and each individual placed them into groups that they believed represented constructs. The coefficient of concordance for this process was 0.72. The four individuals then worked as a group and resolved any discrepancies in the identification of constructs. As discussed in detail in the results section of this paper, although the concepts evoked in this study showed some agreement with both existing general frameworks of expertise and the IS expertise literature, new, domain specific concepts emerged in this study.

Aggregation was done at two levels. First, a single construct level aggregated RCM was prepared by combining the individual revealed causal maps of all the respondents (Axelrod 1976; Bougon et al. 1977; Ford and Hegarty 1984). Second, we developed two RCMs, one for each support context: evolving and mature.

## Validation of Revealed Causal Maps

Member checks were used to validate the concepts and constructs in the aggregate RCMs (Lincoln and Guba 1985). This is done by going back to the original expert respondents and asking their opinion about the concepts, linkages, and constructs represented on the maps. The questions asked during the member checks related directly to the phrases used by the respondents in their interviews. The questions asked were open ended, but relatively structured. For instance:

## When you say mainframe environment do you mean hardware environment?

The process and purpose of this comprehensive check is to test for factual and interpretive accuracy and to provide evidence of credibility and trustworthiness similar to internal validity in confirmatory studies (Lincoln and Guba 1985).

## Analysis of Revealed Causal Maps

The analysis used in this study was based on our objective of defining the constructs of software operations support expertise. Borrowing from social network analysis (Knoke and Kuklinski 1982) as well as past research in causal mapping (Bougon et al. 1977; Ford and Hegarty 1984; Huff 1990; Narayanan and Fahey 1990), we focus on two matrices that capture direct and indirect causal linkages: the adjacency matrix and the reachability matrix. In addition, the point of redundancy is calculated to determine the convergence of concepts evoked from the respondents.

The association or the nature of the causal linkage between two concepts is analyzed using an adjacency matrix. Some adjacency matrices incorporate the directionality of the linkage by “+,” $^ { \scriptscriptstyle 4 } 0 , ^ { \prime \prime } 0 \Gamma ^ { \scriptscriptstyle 4 } - . ^ { \prime \prime } , ^ { \scriptscriptstyle 4 } + ^ { \prime \prime }$ indicates that two concepts are positively related whereas “–” indicates an inverse relationship. When researchers are only interested in the presence or absence of a causal relationship between concepts, which is the case in this study, the adjacency matrix contains only “0’s” and “1’s” (Carley and Palmquist 1992). In this study, we are also interested in the strength of the linkages in a revealed causal map. Therefore, the adjacency matrix was constructed with cells incorporating the frequency linkage between each pair of concepts (Knoke and Kulinski 1982) (Table 4).

The frequency of linkages between two constructs is calculated as a percentage of total linkage between two constructs (Ford and Hegarty 1983). The more a specific linkage is mentioned by the respondents, the greater the frequency of that relationship in the narrative and, in turn, the greater the frequency of that relationship in the final revealed causal maps.

The adjacency matrix shows only the direct causeeffect relationships between two constructs. However this relationship does not capture the indirect linkages between constructs. This makes it difficult to identify the cumulative influence of a construct on the revealed causal map. The reachability matrix allows a more complete picture of the causal relations specified in the adjacency matrix (Axelrod 1976; Ford and Hegarty 1984; Harary et al. 1965; Knoke & Kuklinski 1982) (Table 5).

## Results

We present the results of this study in three steps. First, we present the constructs revealed in the interviews that are central to understanding software operations support expertise. Second, we highlight the major characteristics of the revealed linkages between constructs in the aggregate RCM. We then highlight the differences in the RCMs of the mature and evolving support contexts.

## Constructs of Software Operations Support Expertise

Software operations support expertise was found to be related to five major constructs: personal competencies, environment, IS group outcomes, motivation, and IS policy. Table 7 presents these constructs and the 33 concepts that were evoked in the study. For ease of mapping, the 33 concepts were grouped in categories before being organized into constructs.

The personal competencies and environmenta constructs found in this study are somewhat consistent with generalized theories of expertise and previous research in IS expertise. However, the support experts revealed that the underlying concepts of these two constructs were linked in domain specific ways (concept level RCMs can be found in Appendix C). The other three constructs evoked in this study—IS group outcomes, motivation, and IS policy—have not been previously discussed in the expertise literature. The discovery that these constructs contribute to software operations support expertise demonstrates the power of revealed causal mapping as a methodology that builds mid-range theory in specific domains where generalized theories exist but the domain is unexplored. ## Personal Competencies

As shown in Table 7, seven categories of personal competencies appear to be important in the software operations support context: cognitive abilities, general IS related abilities, technical competencies, business application competencies, context specific competencies, social competencies, and mode of knowledge acquisition. The personal competencies construct evoked in this study has many of the same concepts as general frameworks of expertise. For example, Shanteau and Stewart (1992) list domain knowledge, psychological traits, and cognitive skills as characteristics of experts. Similarly, the technical competencies category revealed in this study is often the focus of studies of general IS expertise. However, the support experts in this study discussed mode of knowledge acquisition and context specific skills as also being components of expertise.

The experts see software operations support as a special kind of expertise. They described how support is not seen as a “glamorous” activity, and is often seen as less desirable than development.

A lot of it is in personalities and in what that person wants to do. Some people like trying to do things to see if they work or not and see them put together. Other people do not like that. Also, I think you have a tendency to see more aggressive people be developers than maintainers. I think it could also be an ego thing. People think being a developer is a lot nicer than being a maintainer. (Mature Context, Support Expert, Manufacturing Organization, Database Group, 6/96)

Conversely, the experts reported that it takes a broader range of skills to successfully perform the support function. Some of the technical skills required for support expertise included hardware, software, and support methods. These competencies are consistent with previous literature in IS (Banker et al. 1987,1991; Baxter 1992; Gibson and Senn 1989; Kafura and Reddy 1987; Peercy 1981; Rombach and Ulery 1989).

In some respects, knowledge of the business was seen as more important to support expertise than to development expertise. In the ongoing support environment, the business is constantly changing and support personnel have to understand and keep up with these changes. They become a critical factor in keeping the business current and functioning. Their ability to respond in a timely and accurate manner to business changes can be a significant factor in the bottom line success of the business.

There is kind of a different lingo that goes along with every business culture. They [experts] need to understand that and understand how things relate. For instance, you have a lot that falls into a classification of product that falls into a department of product, that falls into a category of product. So, you need to know how those hierarchies relate as you are maintaining the program. It is going to make much more sense to you. (Evolving Context, Support Manager and Expert, Retail Organization, Central Services Group, 8/96)

System specific competencies were described by our respondents as understanding how particular systems work together and with the overall environment.

In my particular case, there are lots of pieces to a large system in maintenance mode. It takes a long time to know where everything is, to know what data set to look in for certain information, to know how to communicate the changes we need to make to the people who have to make them. You can take an IMS qualified DBA and bring him/her into this job and they would know what the databases look like and what the structures were, but all the other pieces necessary to do it in our environment would take six months to a year to get. So most of the expertise is not in the discipline IMS, of programming, it is in the kind of informal expertise that it is takes to support those systems at our company. (Mature Context, Support Expert, Manufacturing Organization, Legacy System Group, 6/96)

Taken together, the respondents’ conception of personal competencies, as revealed in this study, is much broader in scope than the descriptions found in the general expertise literature. The experts viewed the software operations support job category as much more complex than is often perceived. This can create tension in organizations where support is viewed and rewarded as less important than development.

## Environment

This study also revealed the support environment to be an important construct impacting support expertise. This is consistent with generalized expertise theories that say the task domain is an important characteristic of expertise (Gaines 1987; Shanteau and Stewart 1992; Slatter 1990). People who are experts in one environment are not necessarily experts in a different environment. Prior research has found that the environment is a significant factor in IS group performance (Becker and Klimoski 1989; Bourgeois and Eisenhardt 1988; Lederer and Mendelow 1990).

According to the support experts, general environmental factors include business, hardware, and software environments. However, in addition to the general environmental factors, the respondents revealed context specific environment and system specific environment to be important components of the software operations support environment. One respondent commented:

I came from an environment that was supporting finance applications. Every year within the finance application there were changes because of new tax laws, accounting laws, new ways that accounting was going to be done. The maintainers have to understand that their system is changing. They have to have the flexibility to go back and pick that up. (Mature Context, Support Manager and Expert, Manufacturing Organization, Process Group, 7/96)

Software operations support personnel constantly face change in their environments. The complexity of the change and the system being impacted are two rich concepts that were revealed in this study.

Although software operations support is seen by some as a stagnant and repetitive activity, experts see continuous learning as a primary motivator and as a critical element of achieving successful outcomes. As organizations move to a more distributed environment, the skills required of support personnel become more diverse, triggering the need for continuous learning.

Whatever the language, there is a set of tools that make that environment productive. You cannot be productive if you do not know how to use the tools, those kinds of things. You can have all the detail skills: intuition, deductive reasoning, and analysis skills.…if you do not use the tools to your benefit, i.e., if you not able to take on new approaches to problem, as the development or support environment changes to be able to take that on or learn with it. Literally what we did 15 years ago does not work very well today. The tools are there from an editing or a debugging standpoint…if you do not use them you really cannot compete. Those are real critical. The ability to change or change with the environment is critical. (Evolving Context, Support Expert, Retail Organization, Corporate IS Group, 9/96)

## IS Group Outcomes

The IS group outcomes construct was described in terms of technical performance, organizational performance of the IS group, and IS system learning. The technical performance concepts were hardware and software performance, while organizational performance included productivity improvements, cost savings, and the contribution to business performance. The other dimension of this construct was IS system learning, which included system knowledge, updated documentation, and reverse engineering. It is interesting that the software operations support experts saw group as well as individual performance as a component of expertise. This finding indicates that expertise can reside in communities of practice as well as in individual experts (Duguid and Brown 1991).

## Motivation

Two dimensions of software operations support personnel motivation emerged in this investigation. These dimensions were labeled growth and accomplishment. In the growth category, the respondents emphasized satisfaction gained through “challenging work,” “growth,” “chance to work on difficult and multiple projects,” and “teaching others.” Work accomplishment was the satisfaction derived through the outcomes of the work rather than the work itself. This included “gaining reputation,” “professional development,” “promotion,” and “formal recognition such as awards.”

Let's say there is an X number of critical principles that you have to be able to maintain through your career and reputation is probably very close to the top. Do what you say you will do in the time you say you can to it and have credibility with the people you work for. Your reputation from a maintainer standpoint is literally producing quality within time and within budget. (Mature Context, Support Expert, Manufacturing Organization, Corporate IS Group, 6/96)

While both support and development experts may be motivated by reputation, growth, and challenge, our support experts did not cite working with leading edge technology as a motivator. The experts interviewed were more motivated by their results than by performing the task itself, suggesting that a different type of individual may be more attracted to this activity.

I think [being] the maintainer is more difficult because in the real world, just like targets and budgets, you do not meet expectations all of the time. If you have a system that was not written or documented well, it is much more difficult to come in and try to figure out what a program is doing. [It is easier] to come in with a blank sheet of paper and have a design and build to that design. The retrofitting or the changing is a more tasking job. (Mature Context, Support Expert, Manufacturing Group, Corporate IS Group, 6/96)

Many of our expert respondents indicated that support is often more abstract and difficult than development. This degree of difficulty and the sense of growth and accomplishment that results when a job is successfully completed intrinsically motivates these experts. This intrinsic motivation played a greater role than extrinsic motivators such as pay and promotion for our respondents. This finding may be an artifact of organizationa policies, which do not always outwardly value and reward the software operations support function. It also suggests that people attracted to this type of job may be more intrinsically motivated.

## IS Policy

The experts interviewed were somewhat skeptica of the impact of IS policies on expertise, although they revealed it to be a component of expertise. The IS policy construct consisted of personne policies, cost allocation policies, and goals set for the IS function as the dimensions of IS policy. The personnel policies mentioned by the experts were the selection and the training of IS personnel. The respondents were often frustrated with the way IS support personnel were hired by their organizations:

Basically in my organization, they go out and hire people who like to play with bits and bytes. We want somebody that will solve the problem for them, design it, and tell them to put it in some language. What they want is basically a coder. (Evolving Context, Support Manager and Expert, Retail Organization, Distribution Group, 9/96)

This finding is consistent with previous literature that suggests that IS policy is key to IS group success (Howard 1990; Tavakolian 1989). It appears that IS policies do not impact expertise in the short term; they affect expertise only if these policies consider the need for growth and accomplishment of software operations support personnel in their jobs. Another way of interpreting this result is that expertise cannot be legislated. Organizations cannot directly hire it, train it, or reorganize to achieve it. Expertise is a function of the individual support people being motivated and having opportunities for goal achievement.

In summary, the elicitation process produced a very finely textured description of the five major constructs of software operations support expertise with 33 underlying concepts. By turning these concepts and constructs into revealed causal maps, we also have insight into the linkages between constructs. The detailed concept level maps evoked in this study can be found in Appendix C of this document.

## Differences Between the Mature and Evolving Software Operations Support Contexts

Analyzing the causal statements of the software operations support experts in this study revealed the same five constructs of expertise for both the mature and evolving support contexts, but found differences in the number of and strengths of linkages between the constructs (Figure 4).

The aggregated RCM of the mature support environment reveals the same set of linkages found in the overall aggregate expertise map. The difference between the mature context map and the evolving context map is that the linkage between personal competencies and the environment is stronger. This result is not surprising since knowledge in the mature support context is often undocumented and resides within experts (Table 1). Similarly, the link between personal competencies and expertise is stronger in the mature versus the evolving environment. This is consistent with mature organizations where experts are assigned primarily to specific systems or specific tasks versus the evolving context where experts do both support and development is across a wide variety of systems.

Another difference between contexts is that, in the mature context, the relationship between personal competencies and motivation was mentioned more frequently by experts. This finding suggests that expertise appears to be a phenomenon more dependent on the individual in the mature context. This would not be surprising since the system support environment and support process environment (Table 1) are more heavily weighted to individual effort and performance. However, the context maps also reveal that in both contexts, experts view personal competencies as related to IS group outcomes, but that only in the mature context are these outcomes related to motivation,

The evolving context experts also believe that formal education is more related to expertise than experience:

I really think they [experts] need a Bachelor's degree in Computer Science or a Bachelor's degree in Business with an emphasis of about 15 to 20 hours of computer science work. (Support Expert, Retail Organization, Object Library Group, 9/96)

The experts in the mature environment believe that diverse experience is more important than formal education.

I think that work experience is more valuable than any academic credential. I think an expert needs to have hands-on experience with batch-type systems, online systems, compiled languages, uncompiled languages, i.e., sequential languages. (Support Expert, Manufacturing Organization, User Interface Group, 6/96)

These different viewpoints may partially explain why mature context experts believe that the support environment is related to their personal growth and performance; this is where they acquire their diverse experience. These findings reinforce the idea that expertise in one IS environment does not guarantee expertise in another environment. This indicates that environmental experts are not easily replaceable commodities and that mature IS organizations need to look for ways to motivate and retain the experts currently in the organization.

Another difference in the aggregated context RCMs is that, in the evolving context, there is no link between IS group outcome and motivation. Additionally, no links were revealed between IS policy and both IS group outcome and motivation. The evolving support context experts see both IS group outcomes and motivation only being related to their own personal competencies.

The differences found in the aggregated RCMs of the two software operations support contexts demonstrate the ability of the methodology to provide the richness and detail unique to qualitative methodologies. The linkages present in these maps help us understand that software operations support expertise is a complex phenomenon that is not the same in all organizational contexts.

## Discussion

Software operations support is an infrequently studied area of IS that is often mistakenly seen as a secondary activity in IS organizations. However, mature organizations spend as much as 90% of IS budgets on support activities and this amount may be rising with the advent of enterprise systems and purchased software applications (Bennett 1991; Gorla 1991; Soh et al. 2000). This study gives qualitative insight into the expertise of software operations support personnel. Additionally, the use of the revealed causal mapping methodology yields domain specific richness and structure to these insights. The methodology provided the tools to evoke constructs of software operations support expertise that go beyond general expertise theories and prior research on IS expertise.

This study extends prior research on expertise in three ways. The RCMs show new evoked constructs linked to software operations support expertise: motivation, IS group outcomes, and IS policy. This is a much broader conceptualization of software operations support expertise than has been previously found in either generalized expertise theories or the IS literature. Secondly, our results indicate that software operations support expertise operationalizes differently for two different IS contexts: mature and evolving. Finally, this study provides insights about where managerial efforts and resources in IS should be directed to foster software operations support expertise. While not currently seen as the “glamor” job in IS, we believe that this activity will become more and more prevalent in in-house IS organizations.

In summary, this research provides revealed causal maps of software operations support expertise that are rich in detail and future research possibilities. The constructs of these maps have some consistency with prior theories, yet appear to interact in meaningful ways not previously revealed by other research. The use of the RCM methodology has provided an understanding of software operations support expertise through the development of mid-range theory.

## Implications and Limitations

## Methodological Implications

This study demonstrates a qualitative methodology, revealed causal mapping, that can be used to develop mid-range IS theories. The methodology was demonstrated in a little understood domain: software operations support expertise. This methodology also has the benefit of developing maps that can be the basis for confirmatory empirical testing. Since IS is a relatively new discipline without many operationalized theories, this research also contributes new constructs and concepts to the body of literature. This study was conducted in five organizations representing the mature and evolving software environments. We believe the results of the study will be generalizable to software operations support organizations, but this external validity will be somewhat limited due to sample size and site availability. We recommend that large-scale, empirical studies be undertaken to validate, refute, or modify the RCMs developed in this study.

This evocative methodology may be used in examining other important issues in the IS discipline. Examples of these include outsourcing strategies, expertise in IS activity areas such as development, network administration, and end user support, or make versus buy strategies.

## Implications for Software Operations Support Expertise

There are three major practical implications of this study. First, the breadth of the personal competencies construct implies that organizations have to consider factors beyond technical skills and formal education when seeking support expertise. To gain software operations support expertise, specific competencies required by the unique IS environment in the organization need to be considered. The nature of education and experience required by IS support personnel may change from organization to organization depending on the IS environment and may change within the same environment over time. This is important for organizations to consider when selecting, recruiting, and training software operations support personnel.

Second, the type of IS support context appears to impact the nature of software operations support expertise. A mature support environment poses a greater variety of challenges due to the age of systems, number of different types of systems, and the complexity of having these systems interact. This environment requires experts to have better people skills and more diverse experience to coordinate and synchronize the complexity in the environment. The variety and age of systems also requires experts to have very strong software skills on the specific systems being maintained. This suggests that retaining inhouse experts is critical in the mature support environment.

Evolving support environments rely more strongly on formal education and individual growth to achieve software operations support expertise. Managers should understand the specific dimensions of personal competencies required to develop experts in their support context.

Third, rather than developing IS policies aimed directly at enhancing expertise, organizations should give support personnel opportunities to succeed, thereby affording them personal growth and accomplishment. The success of these IS policies will depend on how well they help motivate individuals and software operations support groups to match their knowledge with the environment. It is important that organizations understand the type of IS context they possess and reassess IS policies in light of this context.

## Future Directions

The revealed causal maps of software operations support expertise evoked in this study are specific to two different software operations support contexts: mature and evolving. Studies conducted in new contexts, such as e-business environments, might yield additional dimensions associated with this phenomenon. Theory building is a cumulative rather than an exhaustive process. We believe this study provides a mid-range theory that can be used as a foundation for future studies. The constructs developed in this study can serve as guidance for further qualitative inquiry into other areas of IS expertise.

## Appendix B

## Structural Analysis of the Causal Maps

1. Reachability Matrix: The reachability matrix (CR) is calculated by the formula:

$$
\mathsf {C R} = \mathsf {A} + \mathsf {A} ^ {2} + \mathsf {A} ^ {3} + \dots + \mathsf {A} ^ {n - 1}
$$

where A is the adjacency matrix and n is the number of variables in the adjacency matrix. Raising the adjacency matrix to successful power locates indirect paths between variables of the length of the power to which the matrix is raised. ${ \mathsf { A } } ^ { 2 }$ indicates the effects through paths of length 2 (e.g., the effect of B on D in $\mathsf { B } \to \mathsf { C } \to \mathsf { D } )$ ${ \mathsf { A } } ^ { 3 }$ the indirect effect through paths of length 3 (e.g., the effect of B on E in $\mathsf { B } \to \mathsf { C } \to \mathsf { D } \to \mathsf { E } )$ , and so on. If upon raising the adjacency matrix to some power, the value in cell ij goes to zero, then no indirect path of that length exists from variable i to variable $j .$ The reachability matrix, therefore, indicates the cumulative direct and indirect effects of a variable on all other variables. Summing the absolute values in row j of the reachability matrix gives the cumulative outdegrees of variable $j .$ This indicates the cumulative causality of variable j on all other variables. Similarly, summing the absolute values in column k gives the cumulative indegrees of variable j. This indicates the cumulative causality of all other variables on variable $j .$

2. Aggregation of RCMs: Aggregation of RCMs is done by combining (aggregating) the reachability matrices of all 50 respondents

3. Cumulative Binary Matrices: A binary matrix is an adjacency matrix that contains binary values in the cells (0,1). The linkages in the binary adjacency matrices were expressed as 0 or 1 and not as frequencies as in the regular adjacency matrix. If a linkage was identified, then 1 was recorded in the respective cell; if not, 0 was recorded. The binary matrices were cumulated by adding one additional matrix of a respondent at the time. Fifty different cumulative binary matrices were computed by combining the individual binary matrices of 1, 2, 3, ... up to 50 respondents. Each of these matrices represents a cumulative binary matrix of the respective number of respondents. The sequence of accumulation of cumulative binary matrices of the respondents was the same as the original theoretical sampling procedure. The total linkages identified in each of the 50 adjacency matrices were compared.
