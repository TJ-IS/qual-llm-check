# Robo-apocalypse cancelled?

Stefan Klein<sup>1</sup> and Mary Beth Watson-Manheim<sup>2</sup>

Willcocks (2020) aims to call attention to the problematic public discourse on artificial intelligence (AI), and shortcomings in the literature on employment effects of technology and the ensuing transformation of work. He offers not only relevant points of critique but works towards reframing the debate about technologies’ impact on work. We join him in this effort, however, we challenge him to deepen and sharpen his arguments. Moreover, we are uncomfortable with his style as it undermines the very credibility that we see as crucial to the ability of academia to contribute to public discourse.

In this commentary we examine what we find to be the two main contributions of his article and attempt to expand these perspectives to set the stage for a more nuanced view.

## Contribution 1: robo-apocalypse and the public discourse

Diagnosing the ‘robo-apocalypse’ in the public discourse is a no-brainer: The concern about jobless growth is older and refers more broadly to information technology (IT; Brynjolfsson and McAfee, 2012) or even to the early stages of automation (Chaplin, 1936). Yet it is commendable to address the issue time and again, as the concerns co-evolve with the technology.

Although we agree that automation will mean job displacement, and that societies, organizations and individuals will be ill-placed to respond to the rapid deployment of automation, we argue that the exaggerated language in the article minimizes credible exploration and conceptualization of the problem. Furthermore, Willcocks’ writing style, and superficial engagement with the literature and studies he is referencing, fit the genre of an opinion piece; this construction dangerously mirrors the style and shortcomings of the public discourse he is critiquing, prioritizing effects over substance and glossing over the literature without thoroughly engaging with it, and in our view undermines his efforts and credibility.

Obviously, authors should clarify what technology or technologies they are talking about. However, Leslie Willcocks (L.W.) does not do it himself: while introducing the distinction of three main technologies, he does not systematically use this distinction in the analysis of employment impacts of technology himself. Moreover, in his conclusions he claims that robotics needs to be discusses in the broader context of social media, mobile, analytics, cloud, blockchain, robotics, automation of knowledge work, Internet of things, digital fabrication and augmented reality (SMAC/BRAIDA) technologies, in other words as part of a much broader technological transformation. We agree with his point, yet this should not be the conclusion but the beginning of the analysis.

Second, a polarized debate of ‘hype or fear’, in which utopian views confront dystopian ones is prevalent in the public discourse. The phenomenon that technological innovation leads to the rise of utopias and dystopias is well known and established in the literature (see, e.g. Ogburn, 1964). However, what is missing from Willcocks’s reasoning is a critique of the implicit technological determinism underlying both utopias and dystopias. Yet, we would like to suggest to go a step further and look at the underlying assumptions about the nature of managerial and knowledge work, and assess what cannot and should not be delegated to ‘smart technology’? We are just beginning to understand its benefits and consequences. For example, finding one’s way has been widely delegated to GPS-based navigation systems, but what are the long-term implications for decision-making, judgement and perception? A recent study (Javadi et al., 2017) found less activity in the hippocampus of drivers using a GPS than those who had to develop their own mental navigation maps.

The more profound issue we raise is the relationship between human and machine agency: do we want to delegate agency to the machine or do we recognize technology as an increasingly powerful tool that requires human care and human competence to be used safely and to deliver on its promises of improving human capabilities? The responses, however, can go in (technologically determined) opposite directions: one side argues that we need more true automation to take the erring human out of the loop, the other side claims that human agency and control is essential for a humane society. It is a quandary: automation and new technologies can be a wonderful tool to relieve humans of tedious work and to achieve qualities that humans find difficult to match, as well as lead to new and unimagined innovations. Yet, they come with the inherent risk that the humans overstep the boundaries of their competence and even lose – or never develop – necessary capabilities. As well, this notion cautions us to prepare for ‘moments of truth’ when technology fails and we have to figure out what is going on and what is the right thing to do in the circumstance, and to highlight the critical importance of developing and nurturing the capabilities to follow through.

Finally, the methodological issues of assessing the employment effects of technology are profound. Academics, consultants, international organizations, governments, among many others are trying to figure out, how to come up with reliable forecasts. Willcocks adds his own approach of an extensive collections of case studies, but does not systematically engage with methodological issues. Yet, to address the methodological issues more profoundly, would be a separate piece of research on its own.

## Contribution 2: the qualifiers

The list of eight qualifiers provides a useful synopsis of shortcomings in the literature on employment effects of technology on the one side and a reflection of the literature on the transformation of work on the other side, even though it could have been argued more tightly. Providing alternative vista points, framing and reframing the issues, is essential in the current climate and discourse. They sensitize and remind the reader that human work has almost always implied the use of some tools and technology, and has been transformed as the tools and technology have developed and transformed, for example, significant changes in work in the industrial revolution. Substantial changes in tools of work led to societal transformation from the micro to the macro level, and has been subject to social, economic, regulatory and indeed technical innovations to create humane modes and environments of work.

Moreover, the prevailing rhetoric of technological pro gress foregrounds the substitution effect (and related reduction of personnel cost) while backgrounding the new tasks and changing work configuration, as well as innovations in work environments. The information systems community should propagate a more nuanced view.

## Author biography

Stefan Klein is Professor for Interorganizational Systems at the University of Münster, Germany. His current research areas are information infrastructures, network governance, digitization and risk, transformation of work. He studies practices of technology use and organizational transformation from an individual to an industry level.

Mary Beth Watson-Manheim is a Professor of Information Systems in the Information Decision Sciences Department and the Communication Department (affiliate) at the University of Illinois, Chicago. She is actively involved in research investigating individual, organizational and social implications of a global and ICT intensive workplace.
