# A narrative networks approach to understanding coordination practices in emergency response

Panos Constantinides <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Michael Barrett <sup>b,1</sup>

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 12 March 2012 Received in revised form 10 July 2012 Accepted 18 July 2012 Available online 4 August 2012

## a b s t r a c t

This paper examines coordination practices in emergency response by adopting a narrative networks approach (Pentland & Feldman, 2007). We apply this approach in the analysis of qualitative data collected in an empirical longitudinal study (2003–2006) of emergency response across a geographical region of Greece. We provide an in-depth narrative analysis of two different emergency incidents and the efforts to coordinate those. The paper concludes with the implications of using a narrative networks approach for understanding the temporal and situated nature of coordination, while placing emphasis on the performativity of coordination practices.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years there have been many advances in triage technologies, third-generation wireless connectivity. and geographical information systems. all of which have contributed to more accurate and real-time information for effective emergency response (Berman, 2004; Teich, Wagner, MacKenzie, & Schafer, 2002). However, many of the challenges faced in emergencies are not caused by shortages in technological resources, but rather from failures to coordinate their distribution with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant possibilities for error and destruction (McKinsey & Company, 2002a, 2002b).

Despite the evident advantages of computerized priority-dispatch protocols (Turoff, Chumer, Van de Walle, & Yao, 2004), key <sup>fi</sup>ndings reported from empirical case studies show that effective emergency management and response cannot be achieved by ritualistic behavior through ‘blind’ protocol-following (Drabek & McEntire, 2003; Tierney, Lindell, & Perry, 2001). This is because it is impossible to create work<sup>fl</sup>ow scenarios that will adequately handle every type of call a dispatcher will take (Dale et al., 2003; Deakin et al., 2006; Michael & Sporer, 2005). Further, the criteria in these work<sup>fl</sup>ow guides are not based on a strict yes or no answer and the questions are listed so that dispatchers can easily guide a caller into follow-up questions, but not necessarily to make a <sup>fi</sup>nal diagnosis (Buck et al., 2009; Reilly, 2006).

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are in tune with recent research in organizational coordination which has stressed the importance of shifting the focus from structured and shared cognitive models (e.g. standards and protocols of emergency dispatch) as to how coordination actually happens in practice (see Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). Coordination is thought to take place through improvization, which is in sharp contrast to relying solely on shared models (Bigley & Roberts, 2001; Weick & Roberts, 1993). Certainly, there is a tight coupling between shared cognitive models and the practices of a group of individuals in that improvization builds on routine (Weick, 1993). In turn, coordination follows a recognizable logic and is only partially improvized (Faraj & Xiao, 2006). However, even these partial improvizations challenge existing epistemic boundaries and require effective leadership and on-the-spot coordination (Majchrzak, Jarvenpaa, & Hollingshead, 2007).

While recognizing the tension between shared cognitive models and improvized coordination practices, it has more recently been argued that “in settings where work is contextualized and non-routine, traditional models of coordination are insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient to explain coordination as it occurs in practice” (Faraj & Xiao, 2006:1156). This is particularly true for pre-hospital emergency response, where the “setting” is constantly changing both temporally and spatially, as the trajectory of an emergency incident unfolds through the emergent practices of an emergency response team (Campeau, 2008). In these settings, it has been argued, we need alternative approaches to understand how coordination unfolds in practice.

Some have argued that focusing on the narratives produced in practice will move beyond extant approaches to understanding coordination, since “narratives do not represent a single shared understanding of a domain; rather they represent the multiplicity of events and actions a community is taking, as members are taking them” (Majchrzak et al., 2007:156). Thus, narratives can generate rich insights into a practice setting, providing observable records of different sets of actions, their interdependencies and con<sup>fl</sup>icts, as well as the ways in which they lead to the achievement of speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks.

This research responds to the call to focus on the narratives of coordination by adopting a “narrative networks” approach (Pentland & Feldman, 2007). Focusing on narrative networks is a way of “visualizing patterns of action that preserves the multiplicity of possibilities inherent in any organizational form” (Pentland & Feldman, 2007: 790). By focusing on the narrative networks constructed through the narratives of different actants, this paper develops implications toward a better understanding of the temporal and situated nature of coordination, while placing a great emphasis on the performativity of coordinating practices.

In contrast to the notion of ‘performance’, i.e. a representation of the doing of some activity (e.g. X performed Y), ‘performativity’ is the collective enactment of practice and discourse (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008). In other words, instead of placing emphasis on the performances of individual actors, in a performative understanding, narratives not only signify coordination practices, thereby serving a representative function, they also enact those practices.

In the next section we explore the extant literature on organizational coordination in more detail, and discuss how the narrative networks approach adds an appreciation of the performativity of coordinating practices. Then we describe in more detail our methods of data gathering and explain how we apply the narrative networks approach in the analysis of qualitative data collected in an empirical longitudinal study (2003–2006) of emergency response across a geographical region of Greece. Following this section, we provide an in-depth narrative analysis of two different emergency incidents and the efforts to coordinate those. We conclude with a discussion of the implications of using a narrative networks approach for research on organizational coordination.

## 2. Problematization of the extant literature on organizational coordination

In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss some key assumptions held in the literature on organizational coordination and propose a set of alternative assumptions by drawing on the narrative networks approach. We then discuss the value of the narrative networks approach to studying organizational coordination.

## 2.1. Existing and alternative assumptions on coordination

Early approaches to understanding coordination, such as information-processing models (e.g. Galbraith, 1973) and coordination theory (e.g. Malone & Crowston, 1990) shared the assumption “that the environment is predictable enough to characterize existing interdependencies and that prede<sup>fi</sup>ned mechanisms can be designed for various contingencies” (Faraj & Xiao, 2006:1156). In other words, they shared the belief that coordination can be designed in advance in models shared across an organization and that no improvized acts are needed because the environment is predictable. The reason why these assumptions weigh heavily on shared models was because they were formulated in the era in which manufacturing and production work was the dominant organizational form (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009).

Recent research on coordination has expanded the focus beyond manufacturing and production and has moved beyond the how of coordination (i.e. the shared cognitive models), toward the what and when of coordination (i.e. the temporal practices) (Faraj & Xiao, 2006). The key assumption underlying this new wave of research is that coordination introduces contingencies among individuals with diverse knowledge and skills, and, thus, requires the reconciliation and transformation of current practices (Bechky, 2003; Carlile, 2004; Faraj & Xiao, 2006; Kellogg, Orlikowski, & Yates, 2006; Levina & Vaast, 2005; Majchrzak et al. 2007). Researchers ascribing to this practice view of coordination emphasize the temporally unfolding and contextually situated nature of work processes.

We ascribe to the practice view of recent research on coordination, but argue that there is a need to better describe the interdependencies of different individuals with various technologies as they enact different practices toward a collective performance. In particular, there is a need to better explain the performativity of coordination beyond just physical acts, by focusing on the multiplicity of events and actions of coordination, as those are taking place in the narratives (cf. Majchrzak et al., 2007).

This need becomes even more apparent in the context of emergency management and response, where coordination moves across boundaries from the switchboard operator, to the dispatcher, and ultimately to the emergency response unit (Manning, 1989). In this context, narratives move and change in form, while mediated by technology, coding, interpretative work, and the roles and tasks of organizational members (Manning, 1989).

To this end, we adopt the methodological device of “narrative networks” (Pentland & Feldman, 2007) to acknowledge – like in the practice view – that coordination is a set of actions or events that embody coherence or unity of purpose (i.e. narrative), but to also emphasize that these actions or events can be interconnected in many different ways (i.e. network).

The notion of narrative networks is similar to what Czarniawska-Joerges (1998) has called “action nets.” As Pentland and Feldman (2007: 789) explain, the key difference between the two is that, narrative networks convey “the theoretical position that the combination of fragments forms meaningful structures of technology in use and organizational form". They add that, narrative networks “allow for analysis of not only fragments that have been enacted but also those that could be” (Pentland and Feldman (2007: 789)).

Narrative networks comprise actants and actions, narrative fragments, and narratives. Actants are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the human and nonhuman actors” involved in a narrative network, and “actions connect the actants to one another” (Pentland & Feldman, 2007:787). “Narrative fragments are the basic nodes within a narrative network” (Pentland & Feldman, 2007: 787), and “consist of at least two actants and some kind of action that occurs with them or between them” (Pentland & Feldman, 2007: 788). Finally, a narrative is “a coherent progression or sequence of events with a purpose or goal” and “a distinct beginning, middle, and end” (Pentland & Feldman, 2007: 788).

Different actants (e.g. an individual, an ICT) will produce different narratives (e.g. an oral recollection of actions and events, an electronic report of a collective performance), and together these will form narrative networks. What needs to be stressed here is that a narrative network is not irreducible to a single actant or group of actants. Rather, the sequence of events – including the possibilities they refer to – is produced by the sets of narrative fragments of the various actants involved.

The narrative networks approach holds the key assumption that “different interconnections will make different stories, transforming the context and, therefore, the nature of the things that are connected” (Pentland & Feldman, 2007: 781). Thus, narrative networks are not stable entities, but point at the multiple possibilities of enacted performances.

## 2.2. The value of the narrative networks approach for research on organizational coordination

The narrative networks approach provides a better understanding of the temporal and situated nature of coordination by contrasting and comparing the narratives of different actants on the same actions– events. Indeed. in contrast to the practice view. which assumes that coordination practices are shaped through the ongoing interaction between human improvization and the mediation from non-human actants (e.g. shared models of practice), we suggest that the narrative networks approach holds possibilities for a more holistic analysis of the performativity of the tension that ensues between the two. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, by focusing on combinations of narrative fragments as nodes of a narrative network – i.e. the relationality of temporal action–events – the narrative networks approach shifts the attention away from single, isolated actants (both human and non-human) and toward their holistic performativity.

This approach is relevant and useful to “visualize patterns of action without losing touch with the speci<sup>fi</sup>c performances that make up these patterns” (Pentland & Feldman, 2007:791). Most importantly, the narrative networks approach enables an understanding of the tension between <sup>fi</sup>xity and <sup>fl</sup>uidity or between shared models and improvized practices – an important problem for understanding organizations (Tsoukas & Chia, 2002; Weick & Sutcliffe, 2006) – and in extension how coordination is enacted in practice.

In the next section, we describe how we will apply the narrative networks approach in the analysis of qualitative data from an empirical case study.

## 3. Methods

This paper seeks a more in-depth understanding of the performativity of coordination practices by applying the narrative networks approach to the analysis of longitudinal, qualitative data on the coordination practices of the Regional Centre for Emergency Care (RCEC) in Crete, Greece.

RCEC Crete is part of the National Centre for Emergency Care (NCEC), the only public health organization in Greece offering pre-hospital care for chronic and emergency cases. NCEC is divided into a central unit located in Athens and regional units located throughout Greece.

RCEC Crete covers a geographical area of over 8000 km<sup>2</sup> and services a resident population of more than 500,000. RCEC Crete consists of the dispatch center and the ambulance <sup>fl</sup>eet. The central coordination center located at Heraklion (the capital) coordinates and manages the emergency services in each of the four districts of the island, Chania, Rethymnon, Heraklion and Lassithi. RCEC Crete staff includes, doctors trained in pre-hospital emergency care, ambulance crew (i.e. paramedics), and dispatchers. Daily and for 24 h, there are 5 fully equipped ambulances with a doctor (mobile units) and 13 Basic- and Advanced-Life Support (BLS, ALS) ambulances operating throughout Crete.

Access to the RCEC Crete was provided through the Director of the RCEC Crete and data collection progressed through six fieldtrips over different phases (January 2003, July 2003, June-July 2004, November– December 2004, September 2005, July 2006). During these <sup>fi</sup>eldtrips we conducted a total of 49 interviews and approximately 60 h of observations with (a) dispatchers, (b) ambulance crew, and (c) specialized doctors (including the director of the RCEC Crete). All interviews were taped in Greek and then translated in English by the <sup>fi</sup>rst author who is a Greek-Cypriot national. The second author was present in half of the interviews conducted.

The reason for using a functional categorization of interviews (i.e. dispatchers, ambulance crew, and specialized doctors) was that each of these actants has different tasks to perform during an emergency response. Thus, interview questions directed to dispatchers focused on the ways of interpreting and classifying emergency calls according to triage protocols, in addition to dispatching ambulance units to emergency sites according to resource availability, expertise, and the severity of incidents. Interview questions directed to ambulance crews focused on the communication between the instructions received from the dispatchers and the ‘physical image’ of the emergency site faced by the crews. Finally, the questions directed to specialized doctors (e.g. anesthesiologists, intensive care specialists) focused on their seniority in the division of labor, with an emphasis on their interventions in containing emergency situations from spiraling out of control. These interviews were supplemented by real-time observation of the practices of dispatchers at the central coordination center with audio access by radio communications to ambulance crews. We also observed the teleconsultation practices of specialized doctors also stationed at the central coordination center, and their interactions with ambulance crews either through radio or through telematic applications.

## 3.1. Data analysis: the narrative networks of emergency response

We approached the various narratives from the interviews and the observations as a narrative network, with a common boundary being the coordinating practices in and around an emergency response. The key objective was to compare the same practices from different points of view and develop an explanation of the temporal and situated nature of coordination practices in emergency management and response at the RCEC Crete. To structure our analysis we constructed narrative networks based on the concepts suggested by Pentland and Feldman (2007) namely, actants and actions, narrative fragments, and narratives. Dispatchers were responsible for triaging calls and dispatching appropriate ambulance units. Ambulance crew, in collaboration with the dispatchers at the central coordination center, were responsible for providing immediate care to the critically ill and injured, and for transporting the patient to a medical facility for further care. Finally, doctors were responsible for leading and coordinating disaster planning, training, and overall management of emergency response efforts. In 1997, the manual protocols used at the RCEC Crete were incorporated into a computerized pre-hospital emergency management system (PHEMS) — another non-human actant. PHEMS provides solutions for geographical tracking of ambulances and mobile units, acquisition, transmission, analysis and storage of vital signs for patient telemonitoring, and an electronic emergency incident archiving, and medical triage protocols for prioritizing emergency calls. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the system consists of the following applications: a dispatcher application; a doctor application including a telematics subsystem; an administrative application; and a GPS satellite geographical information system (GIS). Fig. 1 provides an illustration of the actions involved in the use of the PHEMS. These are described below.

The dispatcher application allows creating, completing and printing the electronic ‘Incident Card’. Based on triage protocols, it offers help with regard to incident severity estimate on and the selection of the most appropriate resources (e.g. ambulance or mobile unit). The application keeps track of all incidents being dispatched or waiting to be dispatched and displays them in a list.

Mobile units are equipped with appropriate technology to obtain and transmit vital signs and electrocardiographs (ECGs) directly to the doctor's application at the central coordination center allowing for remote monitoring and management of the patient within the ambulance. Using the ‘Multimedia Medical Card’, the doctor may record all relevant details for patient's condition and therapeutic actions throughout the incident. The application within the mobile unit acquires, transmits and stores vital signs and ECG automatically requiring minimum intervention while allowing the crew to concentrate fully on the patient.

Finally, the central coordination center operates a GPS satellite GIS system that depicts the exact position of the ambulance helping dispatchers calculate the time it would take to respond to an emergency call. All ambulances are equipped with this system.

## 3.1.2. Narrative fragments

As described earlier, narrative fragments are the basic nodes within a narrative network, and consist of at least two actants and some kind of action (Pentland & Feldman, 2007). For example, during our observations at the central coordination center, we observed ‘the phone ringing’, ‘dispatchers answering the call’, ‘dispatchers triaging the call’, etc. Each of these narrative fragments helped to “advance the plot” and answer the question “What happens next?” (Pentland & Feldman, 2007:788). Each of these fragments contained more than just the action or event. Rather, they occurred with or between actants (e.g. the dispatcher, the phone, the triage protocols, the PHEMS, etc.). It is suggested that narrative fragments are thought of as temporal instances of performed coordinating practices. These instances are institutionalized over time as part of shared, structured models of coordination. However, these structured models are temporally and contextually provisional in that, there is, in every use, always the possibility of a different practice being performed.

The performativity of coordinating practices becomes easier to understand if one considers how a range of narrative fragments may be produced by different actants around the same actions and events. Consider, for example, the following narrative fragments from two ambulance crews about the usefulness of telematics:

Telematics are fairly new… It would help us a lot in the simple ambulance because since we are not doctors to make a diagnosis, we could send the data to the doctor at the central coordination centre he could give us instructions. What we do now is give the vital signals over the radio. (Crew 1)

When we hook the patient up, everything is recorded in the [PHEMS] system so you know that if you are right, it will show. They can't say you made that up. (Crew 2)

Both sets of narrative fragments recognize the usefulness of telematics in the PHEMS, however they differ in their explanation of what currently is performed in practice. Crew 1 says that vital signals are currently sent over the radio, thus, implying that the PHEMS is not being used. In contrast, crew 2's narrative fragments seem to imply that the PHEMS is currently being used and that all data during an emergency response are recorded in the system. In fact, crew 2 makes reference to the security provided by the PHEMS to the crew, in case there is any questioning of performed coordinating practices in relation to a patient's trajectory (e.g. death).

Each of these sets of narrative fragments constitutes a node in the narrative network and together they help identify links, consistencies, and possible con<sup>fl</sup>icts in meaning. That is, the combination of fragments form meaningful structures of coordinating practice and shift the attention away from single actants and toward their collectively performed practices (Pentland & Feldman, 2007; also see Schatzki, 2005).

## 3.1.3. Narrative

A narrative is a coherent sequence of narrative fragments with a common unity of action or purpose (e.g. a coordinated response to an emergency incident). A narrative may be produced from different points of view, thus illustrating different possibilities of practice.

In one example, in an interview with an intensive care physician, we were told that. “the other day. I was on duty when we received a call from a primary care center to pick up a patient and transfer him to the University Hospital.” As we were told, the caring general practitioner had informed the intensive care physician at the RCEC Crete that the patient was suffering from chronic heart disease, and that he required immediate care at a tertiary healthcare facility. “When the ambulance arrived at the primary care center, we realized that the patient was having a heart attack,” the intensive care physician continued. “We were already late, the poor guy was dead,” he added. Despite this, however, when the ambulance arrived at the University Hospital, the receiving cardiologist there argued that it was the ambulance crew's fault for the patient's death. The incident was later leaked to the media and, as we were informed by the Director of RCEC Crete in a separate interview, “they [the University Hospital] are threatening to pursue legal action against us.” Apparently, in an effort to shift responsibility over to the RCEC Crete, the caring general practitioner argued that the patient was alive when he was picked up by the ambulance, so the patient must have died during transportation. RCEC Crete, though, argued otherwise and they had data (stored in the PHEMS) – the patient's vital signs before and after transportation – to prove it (cf. Constantinides, 2012).

As evident from the above data, narratives on and around a coordinated response to an emergency incident involve a number of human and non-human actants, including callers and patients, all of whom take part in different temporal instances of coordinating practice. Such narratives have a beginning (i.e. an emergency call), a middle (e.g. the ambulance dispatch), and an end (e.g. death) and are not irreducible to a single actant or group of actants. Rather, the sequence of events – including the possibilities they refer to – is produced by the sets of narrative fragments of the various actants involved.

In the next section we provide an analysis of two different narrative networks we observed in two separate emergency incidents.

## 4. Narrative networks in the coordination practices of the RCEC Crete

In the following sections, in order to construct a full-scale narrative network, each of the two emergency incidents is initially presented as a complete narrative, before proceeding with a breakdown of individual narrative fragments and the various actants and actions involved.

## 4.1. The first emergency incident

To better understand the trajectory of the incident, we broke down the narrative into key fragments according to four narrators, the dispatcher, the driver, the doctor, and the computerized protocols being drawn upon during the incident as illustrated in Fig. 2. Although there were multiple non-human actants present in the incident, we chose to focus only on the computerized protocols because these have a more clear ‘voice’ in the actions being performed. They can be said to provide a separate narrative about the incident and, thus, help in the construction of the narrative network under focus.

In each of the four columns in Fig. 2, there is a set of narrative fragments from the narrative as it was recorded. Each fragment is a functional event, which consists of two or more actants and some action. Each of the set of narrative fragments tells the story in a sequential <sup>fl</sup>ow in a straight line from beginning to end (vertical arrows pointing down). That is, one can be informed about the story (to some extent) by reading any one of the <sup>fi</sup>ve columns. However, there are many points along each of these <sup>fi</sup>ve sequences, where one could ‘jump’ between stories. It is by navigating the narrative network – i.e. tracing the links between the narrative fragments – that one is provided with opportunities to untangle the coordinating practices in the trajectory of the incident. The narrative fragments in these stories provide opportunities to make analytical cuts in the temporal action–events unfolding in the incident.

Analytical cuts provide contingent resolutions of the inseparability of narrative fragments within a narrative network and hence enable a more objective description of what is being enacted. That is, by ‘cutting’ the narrative network into individual narrative fragments as enacted by different actants across time, we are able to produce an unambiguous account of the marks of coordination in time–space term (cf. Barad, 2007). This is not to say that analytical cuts ontologically disentangle the narrative network into independent sub-networks; rather these analytical cuts are purely methodological in nature (Pentland & Feldman, 2007). Each narrative is read in isolation and in relation to other narratives.

Starting with the dispatcher's narrative, one is left asking questions about whether the ambulance was late or lost, or whether the patient's relatives were indeed late or lost. This is where the narrative of the doctor becomes insightful, as it is through his intervention that one starts to form an idea of what might have happened. The dispatcher's narrative helps to identify the signs of a possible cause: the patient is neither communicating nor breathing, he is unconscious. The doctor's narrative provides a diagnosis: the patient has swallowed a fruit nut and has choked (always based on the information provided by the caller). Both the dispatcher and the doctor had provided <sup>fi</sup>rst aid instructions to the caller, with the doctor providing more speci<sup>fi</sup>c instructions for helping to push the nut out of the patient's throat. However, there is a possibility that the patient was already dead before the call was placed, or at least this was what the doctor was claiming. This suspicion was raised after the driver had arrived at the site only to <sup>fi</sup>nd no one there. A few minutes later, the driver said that the crew “performed all possible actions but cannot get a pulse nor any breathing. We even gave him an adrenaline shot, but he is not responding”. The dispatcher con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that “the golden time”<sup>2</sup> was lost and directed the ambulance to transport the patient to the hospital. Finally, the PHEMS recorded the key temporal action–events of the incident, and the protocols provided a reference point as to whether those action–events adhered to the shared models of the team.

We do not have the caller's narrative to be able to draw an absolute conclusion, however, it is suggested that, in his panic and non-professional (mis-)judgment, the caller might have failed to notice that the patient was dead before placing the initial emergency call, and that there was some confusion about the pick-up point. Alternatively, the caller might have been aware of the patient's death, but was deliberately late in arriving at the pick-up point.

One could also argue that the dispatcher's decision to set the village cemetery as the pick-up point instead of picking up the patient from the site where the incident took place, was itself a possible cause for the horrible outcome. However, this was refuted by both the dispatcher and the ambulance driver on duty during later interviews with them. The dispatcher explained to us that this decision was made in an effort to minimize the response time of the ambulance crew. “The village cemetery is at the entrance of the village,” he told us. The driver added, “not all geographical areas are mapped in the GPS system and that is why we sometimes set key landmarks as pick-up points.” As both explained, Anogia is a mountainous village that had not been fully mapped in the system at the time of the incident. More importantly, the driver added that the death of the patient was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by the coroner at the hospital as having taken place prior to the arrival of the ambulance.

In addition to the change in the pick-up point, there was another, perhaps more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvization carried out by the crew: that of giving an adrenaline shot. According to the protocols, ambulance crews are not allowed to give an adrenaline shot without supervision by a specialized doctor. The key reason behind this constraint on ambulance crew's actions was that of medical accountability to a patient, which only a specialized doctor can assume. “They [the doctors] tell us not to do anything, just to give the patient air oxygen and pulmonary resuscitation” the same ambulance crew told us. Although there was a doctor at the coordination center during the incident, he gave no instructions for giving an adrenaline shot, but then again, he did not condemn the action after it was enacted either.

A choking incident would normally fall under the ‘respiratory failure’ protocol category (see Table 3 and Fig. 2). However, as a doctor explained in another interview, a choking incident may also be a consequence of an allergic reaction, with the extreme case being an anaphylactic shock, which can cause the throat to swell up and lead to a choke. In this case, the ‘allergies’ protocol instructs the crew, always under the supervision of a doctor, to give an adrenaline shot. The adrenaline counteracts the swelling and inhibits the production of histamine, which is responsible for the anaphylactic reaction. Thus, on the one hand, the protocol can be said to be encouraging the ambulance crew to give an adrenaline shot to a dying patient, as part of an improvized “moral act” (cf. Nelson, 1997). On the other hand, though, the crews are expected to restrain themselves from such improvization, as it falls outside their responsibilities.

Despite this evident tension between shared cognitive models (invoked through the protocols) and improvized practices (performed by and through different actants), all interviewees told us that it was “normal practice”. Improvizations were normal(-ized) in the sense that they were performed as part of ‘normal’ practice, in an effort to control the trajectory of the routine emergency incident (cf. Campeau, 2008).

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, improvizations were performed in an effort to minimize the time required to transfer the patient to a hospital, thus maximizing the patients' well-being. This clinical urgency of the incident, is what forced the dispatcher to attempt to ‘secure’ the ‘golden time’ by moving the pick-up point to the entrance of the village, although he was unsuccessful in the end. Improvizations were also performed out of an effort to account for the limited or partial information provided to the dispatcher and the ambulance crew by the caller, including the lack of fully computerized maps, and the unavailability of the address of the incident on the GIS system. These limitations and, in extension, the uncertainty in the clinical needs of the incident, are what caused some concerns about whether the patient was indeed dead prior to the call and under what circumstances (e.g. an anaphylactic shock), and what eventually led the crew to improvize by giving the adrenaline shot.

In other words, the dynamic unfolding of the incident, forced the rescue team to perform small improvizations on their shared cognitive models in an attempt to control the trajectory of the incident.

We next explore the narrative network of a mass accident, where, in contrast to the above incident, there was a more imminent need to control unfolding temporal action–events due to a denser relationality of coordinating practices.

## 4.2. The second emergency incident

Next, we provide excerpts from several interviews with the staff at the RCEC Crete, all describing emergency response efforts during a mass accident involving a passenger bus and a tanker carrying kerosene. We provide excerpts of these responses, starting with the dispatcher on duty that day:

We received a call from a civilian that there was a massive accident on the national highway between Heraklion and Rethymnon, close to Adelianos Kampos… The caller said that a tanker carrying kerosene crashed into a passenger bus while the bus stopped to unload passengers. … He said that he was not sure how many people were involved in the accident but there were a series of explosions and both vehicles were on fire… We dispatched one ambulance stationed in Heraklion, one stationed in Chania, three from Rethymnon and one more from Perama… we could see where every ambulance was at any given time in the [GIS] system.…After the first two ambulances arrived at the scene, the crew told us that there were nine passengers in the bus, three of whom suffered fatal burn wounds. The driver of the tanker was also dead from the explosion… The remaining six passengers and the bus driver were badly injured and required immediate assistance

## A crew who was dispatched to the accident site gave us his point of view on the incident:

The first few minutes of the battle with the fire were martyrdom. Nobody knew how many and who were trapped in the two vehicles … the specific nature of the fuel made it very difficult for the firefighters to extinguish the fire. There were large amounts of water coming out of hoses and cannons, but the water was only fueling the flames… One of my colleagues was ready to jump into the fire and grab one of the passengers in the bus, but we held him back … he could not take it any longer. We could hear their screams but we could not help them… Then two more firefighting trucks arrived fitted with foam and eventually they managed to extinguish the fire inside the bus… It was a horrible site when we got inside [the bus]. We found three people completely burnt… Six more people had extensive burns to the face and body as well as inhalant burns, some of them in critical condition… One by one, we started pulling them out of the bus and into the ambulances… I was in the first ambulance out. We were transferring one of the most critically injured, a guy with extensive facial burns who seemed to have inhaled a lot of smoke and was barely breathing. We intubated, ventilated him and inserted an IV line to restore his fluids before we transferred him to Rethymnon general hospital… As soon as we delivered the patient to the hospital we were dispatched back to the accident site again.

The director of the RCEC Crete who was in charge of the response efforts during the incident, gave us his point of view:

When you have a massive accident you are weighing the odds along a scale: on the one hand you have needs, which in this case we couldn't really know in advance but we did know they were many, and on the other hand you have available resources. This scale was constantly changing based on information we were receiving from ambulances that had already arrived at the accident site as well as our own experience with similar accidents… the GPS system helped us coordinate activities by enabling us to identify where exactly each of the ambulances was located… Through the radio we located hospital intensive care units across the region with available patient beds to which to transfer the patients… Overall we dispatched six ambulances with twelve rescue crew and six doctors to the accident site… two casualties were transferred to Heraklion's University Hospital, and Venizelion Hospital, and four to Rethymnon and Chania general hospitals… In twenty minutes we transferred all casualties.

…even though our staff are very well trained, in moments like those, emotions get in the way which may inhibit clear thinking; some of them were ready to jump into the fire. But we told them ‘don't cross the safety boundaries’… ‘If you enter the bus, at 700°… you will melt’… The role of the coordination centre is this, our cold-bloodedness; that we are outside of the fire; that we have clear thinking of our protocols. When you are on site your blood starts to boil.…After having received the first information from the site we painted an image that allowed us to coordinate activity. With that image in mind we kept giving instructions to the rescue staff; we didn't let them drift away… we filled their head with instructions… The doctor at the coordination centre is the safety of the rescue team.

## Finally, an intensive care doctor who was also dispatched at the site during the incident told us:

I was in the first ambulance dispatched to the accident… when we arrived we couldn't see much, there was so much smoke and the fire made it impossible to get close to the bus, which was our priority … I have to say that I trust our crew members, but when I saw one of them ready to jump into the bus and put himself and the whole team into danger I felt that I couldn't rely on him… In such cases, when you have to deal with somebody who is almost in a state of panic, the best thing you can do is to give them three, four the most, short, very accurate directives… Like, ‘check the airway’, ‘keep the airway open’, ‘look for a pulse’, ‘try to insert an IV line’… Usually they can't comprehend and they can't apply more than that… Even the best protocols won't be of any help if you don't understand how and when to put them into use…

Things changed for the better when our mobile unit arrived. Up to then it was more or less, organized chaos… I only started to feel some relief when our mobile unit arrived at the scene because then I knew things would start to get in the right order. … they [crew] received instructions from us [the doctors] on how to triage, how to do the first checking outs, which patients had to be dealt with first, which patients had to be moved first…

As with the <sup>fi</sup>rst incident, we broke down the narrative of the above incident into key fragments according to <sup>fi</sup>ve narratives as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Once again, each of the set of narrative fragments in Fig. 3 tells the story in a sequential <sup>fl</sup>ow in a straight line from beginning to end (vertical arrows pointing down). However, by navigating the narrative network – i.e. tracing the links between the narrative fragments – we are provided with opportunities to make analytical cuts in the temporal action–events unfolding in the incident and, thus, develop a more in-depth understanding of coordinating practices.

The dispatcher's narrative provides more factual information about the location and nature of the incident, the types of vehicles involved, the number of ambulance units dispatched and their origin (as visible in the GIS), as well as the number of casualties and fatalities involved. The crew's narrative is more graphic and emotional in nature, providing insights into the dangers involved for both the rescuers (including the <sup>fi</sup>re <sup>fi</sup>ghters) and the casualties, while also describing the coordinating practices of the dispatched rescuers, one of whom was found to be emotionally unstable and deviating from the shared cognitive models. The director's narrative provides a more global picture of the incident, focusing on coordination and leadership from a distance — through “our cold-bloodedness; that we are outside of the <sup>fi</sup>re… we kept giving instructions to the rescue staff; we didn't let them drift away.” The director's narrative con<sup>fi</sup>rmed many of the narrative fragments of the dispatcher and the crew as evident by the links between fragments in Fig. 3, including that, “some of them [the rescuers] were ready to jump into the <sup>fi</sup>re.”

The intensive care doctor's narrative also con<sup>fi</sup>rmed many of the previous narrative fragments, but also added another dimension to the story: commenting on the emotional instability of one of the crew, he argued that, prior to the arrival of the mobile unit, which is manned by a specialist doctor and usually a more experienced crew, “it was more or less, organized chaos”. He made speci<sup>fi</sup>c reference to the (lack of) competence of some of the crew, while praising the important role of the doctors in helping to coordinate the emergency response efforts and getting things “in the right order”. Finally, the computerized protocols provide a reference point as to which of the action–events performed by the team adhered to their shared models of practice. The <sup>fi</sup>ve narratives also point to a set of key coordinating practices performed in an effort to control unfolding temporal action–events due to the denser relationality of coordinating practices over the trajectory of the incident.

Firstly, rescuers sought to establish a safety zone by minimizing or removing potential risks and hazards from the incident site, not just for casualties, but especially for the rescue team itself. This is exactly what the director was suggesting when he said the “cold-bloodedness” of the central coordination center enabled a “clear thinking of our protocols” and the ability to control the emotional instability of the rescuers, by giving them constant instructions on what to do via the radio. “Don't cross the safety boundaries". he instructed. as he sought to control possible, hazardous improvizations (e.g. jumping into the <sup>fi</sup>re). “The doctor at the coordination centre is the safety of the rescue team,” the director added.

The crew's narrative also pointed at the importance of establishing a safety zone prior to any emergency response activity, when he mentioned that “we could hear their screams but we could not help them”. Instead, they had to wait for the <sup>fi</sup>re <sup>fi</sup>ghters to extinguish the <sup>fi</sup>re inside the bus, before they were able to go inside the bus themselves and start pulling out the casualties. This example shows how, in the mass accident the rescuers from the RCEC Crete had to establish a safety zone in coordination with not only their colleagues, but also members of other allied workers like the <sup>fi</sup>re <sup>fi</sup>ghters.

In this effort, the rescuers sought to draw epistemic boundaries according to their tasks, competences and experiences, but also those of their allied workers. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the mass accident invoked a number of protocols including those of ‘burns/electric shock’, ‘multi-trauma’, and ‘car accident’ (see Table 3 and Fig. 3). In the ‘burns/electric shock’ protocol, the <sup>fi</sup>rst actions are to “call the <sup>fi</sup>re brigade”, and “if you are safe remove the injured from the danger area; DO NOT do anything if you have the slightest suspicion of danger” (RCEC, 2004). Similarly, in the ‘car accident’ protocol, one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst actions is to “call the police to establish safety in the road” (RCEC, 2004). Thus, epistemic boundaries are already de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the protocols according to prioritized tasks, including those performed by the <sup>fi</sup>re <sup>fi</sup>ghters and the police of<sup>fi</sup>cers. However, these boundaries have to be re-drawn as part of establishing a safety zone in practice and on-site such as, for example, holding back any crew who may be emotionally unstable, as discussed above.

In addition to drawing the epistemic boundaries of the rescue team in relation to the tasks of allied workers, boundaries also had to be drawn according to the competences and experience of each crew member. As the intensive care doctor explained, some crew members were found to be more reliable to complete more advanced tasks, whereas others were less competent to do so. According to the intensive care doctor and the director, in incidents such as the one illustrated in Fig. 3, even competent crew members risked falling into an emotional state of panic, while bringing down their competence levels and posing signi<sup>fi</sup>cant risks for the safety zone of the incident. This drop in competence was addressed to some extent through an application of the protocols, which contributed to mapping out the epistemic boundaries of each rescuer, by pointing at who was supposed to do what and how each task contributed to the practices of the whole team. More importantly. however. it was the experience of the more senior members of the team and especially the doctors that helped to lift up the competence of the team. As the intensive care doctor said, only after the arrival of the mobile unit and more experienced crew, did he feel con<sup>fi</sup>dent in the competence of the rescue team. “Even the best protocols won't be of any help if you don't understand how and when to put them into use,” he argued, implying the importance of experience. When we asked the director to explain how he was able to draw from his experience to coordinate the rescue team while applying the protocols in practice he explained that “protocols are like amoebas, they constantly adapt.” He continued, “every incident is unique; you cannot <sup>fi</sup>t it into a protocol.” He was, thus, suggesting that there was a reciprocal relation between experience-based action and protocol-following, which is very much dependent on how the trajectory of the incident unfolds over time and space.

Managing the trajectory of the incident was another coordinating practice observed in the narratives. This practice referred to the ways by which rescuers were constantly under the pressure of the passage of time and the assessment of emerging, situated needs and available resources, as the director argued. Faced with the time constraints around the rapid deterioration in the casualties' condition in the mass accident, the intensive care doctor on site – like the director at the central coordination center – had to anticipate possible immediate futures, not just from their own point of view, but also from the perspective of the medical staff who would receive the casualties on arrival at the hospital. That is why they had to assess “which patients had to be dealt with <sup>fi</sup>rst, which patients had to be moved <sup>fi</sup>rst”, while the incident's trajectory unfolded, as the intensive care doctor explained. Time constraints were very much intertwined with situation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c constraints including both the medical conditions of each individual casualty, but also the environmental conditions of the overall incident. The leading coordinators – i.e. the doctors – sought to optimize the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of their efforts by looking for opportunities to trade-off the demands of, on the one hand, casualty care, and, on the other hand, overall environment management, and they utilized their colleagues in this regard. In this effort, the team at the RCEC Crete relied extensively on ICTs to coordinate and execute team tasks, from triaging incidents over the radio, to tracking and communicating with ambulance units through the GIS system, and to searching for available intensive care unit beds in near-by hospitals to transfer the casualties.

From this analysis, it becomes evident that the dynamic unfolding of the incident, forced the rescue team to perform small improvizations on their shared cognitive models in an attempt to control the trajectory of the incident. More importantly, the experience of more senior members (and especially the director) in shifting attention between protocols and combining available resources into effective rescue strategies, was what helped establish an orderly coordination of emergency response to and from the mass accident.

In the next section, we discuss these <sup>fi</sup>ndings while placing emphasis on the performativity of coordination practices.

## 5. Discussion

Our analysis builds on and extends the practice view of recent research on coordination (e.g. Faraj & Xiao, 2006; Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009), by providing an in-depth explanation of the performativity of coordination through a narrative networks approach (Pentland & Feldman, 2007). By looking at alternative narratives of the same emergency incident, as those were constructed by ensembles of different actants, including rescue staff, ICT, medical devices, medication and disposable items (e.g. adrenaline shot), we were able to examine the tension between shared models and improvization as a holistic practice, and not merely as a linear interaction.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our narrative networks analysis points at the co-existence of shared cognitive models and improvized coordination in practice. The co-existence of protocols and improvized practices can be observed in both emergency incidents, whereby coordinating practices were performed in an effort to deal with the relationality of temporal action–events. Relationality refers to the way practices performed in one temporal action–event had direct implications for the next as the incident's trajectory unfolded.

For example, in the <sup>fi</sup>rst incident, the ‘golden time’ during which there was the highest likelihood that prompt emergency care would have prevented the death of the patient was very much implicated in the relationality between the temporal action–events of the dispatcher in changing the pick-up point due to the limitations of the GIS system; the ambulance crew arriving there on time and providing care to the patient by improvizing protocols (i.e. giving an adrenaline shot); the patient's family transferring the patient to the pick-up point, but also informing the rescuers and the dispatcher of the circumstances and time of the incident; and of course the patient himself responding to the <sup>fi</sup>rst aid provided (although, as it was later con<sup>fi</sup>rmed, he was already dead — itself part of the relationality of temporal action–events).

In the second incident, the dense relationality of temporal action–events is especially evident in the efforts to draw the epistemic boundaries of each rescuer by prioritizing tasks, monitoring competence levels, and applying the experience of the team according to the seniority of the rescuers on site (including allied workers such as the <sup>fi</sup>re<sup>fi</sup>ghters). During this drawing of boundaries, extant protocols provided clear guidance on what needed to be done, when, how, and by whom. However, the changing dynamics of the incident (e.g. deterioration of casualties' condition, inability of rescuers to perform as trained because of emotional instability), necessitated the interventions of the leading coordinators. These leading coordinators sought to manage the trajectory of the incident by assessing changing needs and available resources, anticipating potential futures, and trading off the demands of casualty care versus those of environment care (cf. Campeau, 2008). Once again, the management of the trajectory of the incident bene<sup>fi</sup>ted from shared protocols, as those were implemented from a distance through the PHEMS. These protocols were not blindly followed, rather they were adapted “like amoebas” based on experience in assessing the clinical needs of each casualty, and the available resources on site (cf. Bigley & Roberts, 2001; Weick, 1993; Weick & Roberts, 1993).

Beyond the aforementioned support to extant research on coordination, however, our narrative networks approach builds some novel implications for further theorizing coordination, which we explore next.

## 5.1. Implications of the narrative networks approach for research on coordination

First, the narrative networks approach enables an in-depth understanding of the temporal and situated nature of coordination. By collecting narratives and analyzing fragments and their references on the same temporal action–events, researchers can gain a better understanding of the tension between <sup>fi</sup>xity and <sup>fl</sup>uidity or between shared models and improvized practices (Tsoukas & Chia, 2002; Weick & Sutcliffe, 2006) and in extension how coordination is enacted in practice.

Indeed, the contrasting and comparison between narratives as the trajectory of an incident unfolds, also help to understand the “non-locality” of an incident, i.e., where events in one time–space locality are instantly dependent upon events in another locality and vice versa (Pan, Bouwmeester, Daniell, Weinfurter, & Zeilinger, 2000). In other words, practices are not disconnected across localities; rather they continue to be performed in concert with one another, and facilitated by technology.

This becomes easier to understand through the narrative networks approach by making analytical cuts into speci<sup>fi</sup>c temporal action–events, which help to untangle the black box of actants and their actions. As illustrated in the empirical analysis of the data and Figs. 2 and 3, analytical cuts ‘cut’ the narrative network into individual narrative fragments as enacted by different actants across time, thus enabling a clear account of the marks of coordination in time–space terms. By observing the narrative fragments of different narrators, the narrative networks approach enables a more in-depth engagement of the temporal boundary-drawing practices, i.e. the constitutive exclusions that are enacted, and the questions of accountability and responsibility for the micro-changes of which every actant is a part (Pentland & Feldman, 2007:792).

For example, the narrative fragments of different actants in relation to the <sup>fi</sup>rst incident brought to the forefront a number of boundary-drawing practices around the death of the patient. There were questions of accountability and responsibility around who did what, when and how, and what implications did those action–events have on the outcome of the incident. For instance, was the dispatcher wrong in changing the pick-up point due to the limitations of the GIS, did the patient relatives withhold important information about when and how the incident took place, and were the crew to be held accountable for the death and for giving an adrenaline shot when the protocols explicitly stated not to do so unless supervised by a doctor? In the end, the holistic performativity of the actants involved in the incident (including the coroner's con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of the patient's death prior to the arrival of the ambulance) helped to provide answers to the above questions and to clarify issues of accountability and responsibility. In other words, the narrative networks approach enabled a visualization of patterns of actions across different time–space localities and a better understanding of their interconnectedness and performativity.

This renewed understanding of the performativity of coordination practices provides opportunities for further research in unpacking the relationship between narratives and material objects, thus, contributing to the recent wave of research in sociomateriality (e.g. Orlikowski & Scott. 2008). According to this line of research, “entities (whether humans or technologies) have no inherent properties, but acquire form, attributes, and capabilities through their interpenetration” (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008: 455–456). Accordingly, “any distinction of humans and technologies is analytical only, and done with the recognition that these entities necessarily entail each other in practice” (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008: 456).

Although drawing from a long tradition in sociotechnical studies (e.g. Knorr-Cetina, 1997; Latour, 2005; Pickering, 1995), research into sociomateriality has offered fresh insights for the study of technology in organizational settings. However, there is still the problem of how to study the constitutive entanglement of the social and the material; where does one start, methodologically and analytically, to trace the entanglement.

It is argued that the narrative networks approach can help to address the practical issue of how to study the entanglement of the social and the material. By focusing on combinations of narrative fragments as nodes of a narrative network – i.e. the relationality of temporal action–events – the narrative networks approach shifts the attention away from single, isolated actants (both human and non-human) and toward their holistic performativity (Barad, 2007; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008). Indeed, the narrative networks approach brings forward the materiality of narratives — i.e. narratives are the material conditions that de<sup>fi</sup>ne what counts as meaningful statements (Barad, 2007).

For instance, by focusing on the links between the narrative fragments of each actant, we were able to understand how they constructed both the conditions (e.g. constraints of the GIS system) and meaning (e.g. ‘code red’) of the materiality of their actions. In a continuum of narrative fragments, speci<sup>fi</sup>c actants and actions became materialized as determinate objects of practice (e.g. the death of a patient), while others were speci<sup>fi</sup>cally excluded (e.g. the change of pick-up point had no effect on the trajectory of the <sup>fi</sup>rst incident, at least, according to the diagnosis of the coroner). Thus, the narrative networks approach offers ways to make analytical cuts which enable researchers to relationally understand sociomaterial assemblages across different temporal action–events.

Pentland and Feldman (2007) suggest that, a narrative network is meant to be a methodological device and not an ontological construct. However, based on our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we suggest that, the narrative networks approach holds possibilities for reconceptualizing – at an ontological level – coordination practices as “constitutive entanglements” of the social and the material in everyday life (Barad, 2007; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008). Future research could apply the narrative networks approach through a sociomaterial lens, which can provide a broader theoretical ground upon which to understand how narratives entail material objects and vice versa.

Barad (2007) explains that this is an onto-epistemological position in that, if we are to truly understand the entanglement of narrative-material practices, we need to accept the inseparability of those practices from the ontological status of the phenomena which they are part, and which practices help to enact. For instance, the emotive elements of emergency incidents (e.g. emotions such as fear and despair in watching a victim burn, emotional reactions to death, etc..) are inseparable from the narrative networks being constructed by the actants involved, and affect the outcomes of coordination. Future research could pay particular attention at such emotive elements as part of the development of the narrative networks approach at an ontoepistemological level. Such a development would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from longitudinal ethnographic research and an acknowledgement that, how we observe particular phenomena has implications on what phenomena we eventually do observe (Barad, 2007).

## 6. Conclusions and re<sup>fl</sup>ections

This research has adopted a narrative networks approach to problematize extant assumptions found in the literature on organizational coordination. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, while ascribing to the practice view of recent research on coordination, we argued that there is a need to better describe the interdependencies between different individuals and the technologies they use toward a collective performance. To this end, we adopted the narrative networks approach (Pentland & Feldman, 2007) to acknowledge – as in the practice view – that coordination is a set of action–events that embody coherence or unity of purpose (i.e. narrative), but to also emphasize that these action–events can be interconnected in many different ways (i.e. network). This narrative approach holds the key assumption that different interconnections will make different stories, thus, pointing at the multiple possibilities of enacted performances.

We applied this narrative networks approach in the analysis of two emergency incidents and the efforts to coordinate those across a geographical region of Greece. Our analysis extends the practice view of recent research on coordination, by focusing on the links between speci<sup>fi</sup>c time–space events — i.e. narrative fragments. In doing so, the paper illustrates the holistic performativity of coordination by emphasizing the network of discursive-material practices, rather than individual performances. By making analytical cuts into the narratives of different actants and then tracing their interconnections – i.e. navigating the narrative network – the paper provides an unambiguous, richer, and more authentic account of how coordination takes place in time–space.

Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we acknowledge that our own interpretations of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings have implications on how the narrative networks of the two incidents were constructed. In other words, our own research narrative has taken place within a broader <sup>fi</sup>eld of knowledge production (Constantinides, Chiasson, & Introna, 2012). For instance, one could argue that there were many more actants involved in the two incidents, including the victim's relatives in the <sup>fi</sup>rst incident, and the kerosene truck in the second incident. However, in the first case, the victim's relatives were not accessible, and in the second case, the kerosene truck was challenging to <sup>fi</sup>gure into the narrative network. We recognize such limitations and have sought to cross-reference and re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the collected narratives (e.g. input from the coroner in the <sup>fi</sup>rst incident), in an effort to present as many possible pathways of narrative networks. Still, we do acknowledge that researchers are often faced with very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult methodological choices, which can have implications on how they eventually present their <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Moving beyond just methodological choices, it is argued that the narrative networks approach could be complemented by a sociomaterial lens. This lens can provide a broader onto-epistemological ground upon which to understand how the social and the material of a narrative network entail one another. Further research could usefully explore the possibilities offered by the narrative networks approach in revisiting extant onto-epistemological assumptions on organizational coordination. In so doing, researchers should be more attentive to their ethical responsibilities “not only for what we know but for what may come to be” (Barad, 2007:364).
