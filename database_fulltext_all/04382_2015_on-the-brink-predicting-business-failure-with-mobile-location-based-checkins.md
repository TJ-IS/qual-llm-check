## a b s t r a c t

Mobile-enabled location-based services are generating a huge amount of customer checkin data every day. It is vital to understand how small businesses, like restaurants, use this real-time data to make better-informed business operation decisions in this mobile marketing era. Using data collected from Foursquare, a leading location-based service provider, and Yelp, we aim to <sup>fi</sup>nd out the predictive power of customer checkins on business failure of restaurants in New York City by using several predictive modeling techniques, such as Neural Network, Logit model and K-nearest neighbor. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are encouraging. The customer checkin data from both a focal restaurant and its neighbors have shown strong predictive power on business failure, Compared to the baseline model in which we only use business characteristic variables to predict failure, incorporating the checkin data captured from location-based services gives a remarkable improvement on predictive accuracy. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide the foundation for future studies on the predictive power of information obtained from location-based services on business operations.

## 1. Introduction

Making an accurate prediction on bankruptcy or failure is a question that has been puzzling researchers and managers for decades. This problem has been studied in a variety of disciplines, such as <sup>fi</sup>nance and accounting, using different frameworks or models, for instance, ratio analysis [6,14,19], Altman Z-scores [2–4], rough sets [15], and Bayesian models [48]. The common feature of these models and frameworks is that they rely on <sup>fi</sup>nancial data, like stock prices, working capital, and debt. Therefore, they are very useful for medium and large businesses (especially publicly traded ones). However, such methods are of little help for small businesses such as restaurants, since they are not publicly traded and there is no <sup>fi</sup>nancial data or measures that we can adopt. Previous research has largely ignored small businesses because of the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of getting <sup>fi</sup>nancial data. Another issue is that the data is usually out-of-date. Traditional economic forecasts are mainly based on the statistics gathered by government agencies, such as the Census Bureau, or the Department of Labor. A clear drawback of these statistics is that there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant delay between data collection and data publication. Moreover, the data is too aggregate and is sometimes not suitable for small businesses.

Due to recent developments in mobile information technology, location-based services now provide us a unique opportunity to obtain highly disaggregate data on hundreds of billions of economic decisions almost instantaneously as they are made, at nearly zero cost. The data obtained from location-based services can potentially be leveraged to make predictions on large as well as small businesses. De<sup>fi</sup>ned as “information services accessible with mobile devices through the mobile network and utilizing the ability to make use of the location of the mobile device” [55], location-based services are gaining popularity due to their abilities to acquire new customers for small businesses [21,51]. Foursquare, a leading location-based service provider, has gained tremendous popularity in recent years. The growth of its registered users and the checkins made by these users is remarkable, from 7 million users and 0.45 billion checkins by March 2011 to 40 million users and 4.5 billion checkins by September 2013 [24]. Customers reveal their intention to have dinner at a restaurant by “checking in” on Foursquare using their mobile device. With the observations of billions of customers and their shopping intentions, as revealed by location-based services, in this paper we investigate whether the accuracy of predictions about retailers business performance can be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improved using checkin data. This paper addresses the following research questions: (1) can we use checkin information captured from location-based services to make a better prediction on business performance? (2) If yes, by how much can we improve our prediction compared to a prediction without such information?

Understanding the predictive power of the checkin information provided by location-based services is critical for business owners to understand their business performance in this mobile era. By adopting location-based services, businesses gain access to customers' visits statistics from their checkins. Businesses can use location-based services, such as Foursquare, to monitor their customer traf<sup>fi</sup>c. Information that can be collected from customer checkins include how many checkins a business gets per day and per week, who are loyal customers, and whether promotions and discounts help attract more customer traf<sup>fi</sup>c. Analysis of these <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained customer traf<sup>fi</sup>c data can help businesses understand their business performance in real-time. Exploring the predictive power of customer checkins can help businesses to identify any potential downward trends in their business performance, and provide timely warning to business owners to take any necessary corrective actions. Our method should be viewed as a diagnostic tool for businesses to detect symptoms of failure and possibly take remedial action before the trend towards ultimate business failure becomes irreversible.

In the electronic commerce era, the website of a business provides a critical interface between buyers and sellers. Researchers have shown that a well-developed website can be an effective vehicle for delivering services to customers, and businesses can leverage website technology to provide augmented customer services [9,32,58]. It has been found in literature that customer acquisition impacts a <sup>fi</sup>rm's business performance [47]. While in the e-commerce era, the metrics to measure customer acquisition were website visits or online orders, in this mobile era, with location-based services, customer checkins are a new metric that can measure real-time customer acquisition and foot traf<sup>fi</sup>c. Interesting insights could emerge from investigating how customer checkins offered through location-based services may affect business performance. Despite its importance and usefulness, empirical research on location-based services and its impact on business performance have been limited.

Therefore, in this paper, we utilize techniques from the literature on econometrics, forecasting, and predictive modeling to examine whether location-based services have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictive power with respect to retailers' business performance. In particular, we focus on using checkin data, along with other business characteristics, to predict business failure. Failure has been de<sup>fi</sup>ned in several different ways: a business declaring bankruptcy, defaulting on interest payments on its debt, or overdrawing its bank account [6]. In this paper, business failure is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a retailer having less than one checkin per day on Foursquare over a three-month period. In other words, a restaurant is virtually dead if it has very limited or no activity in the mobile world. The ultimate goal is to predict real world business failure. However, in our data set, very few restaurants have closed during our data collection period. Even so, we do observe that for all the closed restaurants, their real world closure is preceded by virtual death, and virtual death is a good indicator for real business failure. There is some recent work that identi<sup>fi</sup>es and analyzes factors that lead to business failure [10,43]. Motivated by these studies, as well as by unexplained and contradictory observations made in practice, this paper provides initial empirical evidence pertaining to the predictive power of customer checkin data associated with the failure of small businesses.

In this paper, we focus on the restaurant industry in New York City. To ensure statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance, we compiled a large data set from Foursquare and Yelp with customers' checkin information and other business characteristics, such as price and rating. We <sup>fi</sup>rst use a neural network to detect any possible complicated non-linear relationship among our independent variables and then apply a Logit model to show the predictive power of customer checkin data. After that, we adopt K-nearest neighbor, a nonparametric approach, to predict business failure. We then compare the predictive accuracy between the Logit and K-nearest neighbor models. We perform several robustness checks to show that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are very encouraging. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the total number of competitors a restaurant has, the growth rate of daily checkins, and the growth pattern of daily checkins all have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictive relationship with its business performance, failures in particular. The effect of the total number of competitors on the odds of failure is higher for restaurants with low ratings than restaurants with high ratings.

Besides its own checkin information, a restaurant's neighbors' checkin history is also highly associated with the odds of business failure. Our results suggest that incorporating the checkin information obtained for both the focal restaurant and its neighbors into a standard Logit model can yield more accurate predictions about the business's failure. For instance, there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvement in predictive accuracy, over a model without the checkin information-around a 60% drop for misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation rate and 50% decrease for both in and out-of-sample mean absolute deviation.

Our study contributes to the growing body of literature on locationbased services: to the best of our knowledge, this is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst studies that focus on the predictive power of the data from locationbased services. Location-based services are gaining a lot of attention because of their unique features. In particular, it is interesting to explore whether retailers can use information captured from these services as an indicator or predictor of their business performance. More concretely, this paper contributes to current work on location-based services by providing an empirical examination of whether checkins are a valuable and viable method of predicting business performance. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide the foundation for further research on the predictive power of information captured from location-based services.

In the remainder of this paper, we <sup>fi</sup>rst detail our research design in Section 2, and describe our data and the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of our variables in Section 3. We discuss the analysis of our results in Section 4 and also our robustness tests. Section 5 contains the discussion and possible directions for future research.

## 2. Research design

## 2.1. Previous studies on business failure prediction

Business failure is a term that can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned and explored in many aspects. The extent of failure can also be measured in different ways. Accurate or meaningful assessments are extremely dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to make given the nature of failure. Problems of business classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, various de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of failure, and data collection issues all provide underlying dif<sup>fi</sup>culties to study business failure [27].

From the 1960s onwards, there has been considerable interest in predicting business failures, as re<sup>fl</sup>ected in a large number of studies [2–4,6,16,19,44]. Early research on predicting business failure relies on discriminant analysis and <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios. Beaver [6] found that <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios are vital for classifying healthy versus bankrupt businesses. Using discriminant analysis, Altman [2] proposed the Z-score, a weighed combination of <sup>fi</sup>ve ratios, to detect possible future bankruptcies. Later, other methodologies have been applied to business failure prediction, such as the Logit and Probit models [10,40]. In order to develop a more accurate and generally applicable prediction approach, data mining and machine learning approaches have been applied in this prediction domain, such as, decision trees, Neural Network, rough sets [16], and Kohonen map [31]. Please see Dimitras et al. [15] and Kumar and Ravi [33] for complete reviews on the methods used for the prediction of business failure. However, most of the research in this area mainly focuses on predicting the failure of large and medium size enterprises using <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios as independent variables. Little attention has been paid to small businesses because of the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of collecting <sup>fi</sup>nancial data.

Two exceptions to this trend are the studies by Ciampi and Gordini [10] and Edmister [19]. Edmister [19] tested the usefulness of <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratio analysis for predicting small business failure. Ciampi and Gordini [10] applied the Logit model to a sample of small Italian <sup>fi</sup>rms using both <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratio and managerial variables. They found that incorporating managerial variables improves the prediction accuracy remarkably, and suggested that small <sup>fi</sup>rms should be assessed with speci<sup>fi</sup>cally designed models. In this study, we focus on predicting business failure for small businesses using real-time data obtained from location-based services.

There are two broadly de<sup>fi</sup>ned categories of predictive modeling methods: one is more black box style, such as Neural Network, in which we don't know how information is being processed during the analysis; the other category is more transparent and the results are easy to interpret, such as the Logit model and K-nearest neighbor. In this paper, we choose models from each category and illustrate them in the following subsections.

## 2.2. Neural Network

Neural Network was developed with the aim of developing computational methods to solve complex problems, and was initially inspired by how human brains process information [38]. Given an appropriate number of hidden-layer units and suf<sup>fi</sup>cient data, Neural Network is a nonlinear model that can approximate any nonlinear or linear function to an arbitrary degree of accuracy through the composition of a network of relatively simple functions [43]. Because of its <sup>fl</sup>exibility and simplicity, Neural Network has become a popular modeling and forecasting tool across different research areas. For example, in <sup>fi</sup>nancial <sup>fi</sup>rms, managers are employing Neural Network to tackle dif<sup>fi</sup>cult <sup>fi</sup>nancial and investment decision-making [53]. It has also been used to model municipal credit rating problem [26]. Please see Ripley [45,46] and Hertz et al. [29] for a more detailed introduction to Neural Network models. In this research, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y describe the Neural Network we used to model small business failure.

Let n denote the number of hidden-layers for a Neural Network. A large n may cause Neural Network over<sup>fi</sup>ts, in which case the in-sample errors will be very small but the out-of-sample errors may be large. How to choose an appropriate n depends on the number of independent variables and the nature of the underlying relationship [43]. Besides the number of hidden-layers, another important variable that we have to choose is the number of neurons in the hidden layers. Heaton [28] suggested, “Problems that require two hidden layers are rarely encountered. There is currently no theoretical reason to use neural networks with any more than two hidden layers. In fact, for many practical problems, there is no reason to use any more than one hidden layer.” Using too many or too few neurons will cause problems. A rule-of-thumb method for determining the correct number of neurons to use in the hidden layers is that the number of neurons in the hidden layer should be the mean of the neurons in the input and output layers [28]. This method provides us a starting point to consider. The ultimate selection of a network will be based on certain criteria, such as Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

## 2.3. Logit model

The Logit model has been widely used to make predictions on different disciplines [5,10,43]. It is typically used for situations in which one needs to predict the presence or absence of a characteristic or outcome based on values of a set of predictors. Because our dependent variable is binary (fail/no fail), we propose a Logit model to demonstrate how information provided by location-based services can be used to make a reliable prediction on a small business failure.

The standard Logit approach models the probability that $y _ { i } =$ 1, denoted by $\pi _ { i } = \mathrm { P r } [ y _ { i } = 1 ]$ . The standard Logit model can be written as:

$$
\operatorname{logit} \left(\pi_ {i}\right) = \log \left(\frac {\pi_ {i}}{1 - \pi_ {i}}\right) = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} x _ {i 1} + \beta_ {2} x _ {i 2} + \dots + \beta_ {j} x _ {i j}
$$

where, as de<sup>fi</sup>ned before, $x _ { i 1 } ~ \ldots ~ x _ { i j }$ are our independent variables, including business characteristics variables and checkin variables. $\beta _ { 0 } . . . \beta _ { . }$ are the corresponding coef<sup>fi</sup>cients. The Logit model described above implies that business failure is guided by the checkin information available from location-based services, and also their business characteristics, such as price and rating.

The Logit model and multinomial Logit model have been widely used in marketing problems. For instance, it has been used to study the impact of marketing mix variables on customers' brand choice [25], their reactions to promotions [35], their response to advertising [52], and customer segmentation and targeted marketing [41]. A key assumption of a Logit model is the linear utility function, which makes it a little bit harder to model some non-linear effects. Adding some quadratic terms is one way to handle this drawback. However, this approach may potentially lead to some misinterpretations [7]. Several general models that are capable of modeling non-linear utility functions can be used to overcome this limitation. Neural Network is one of these non-linear non-parametric models, which do not require a priori knowledge of the relationships to be modeled.

## 2.4. Linear discriminate analysis and K-nearest neighbor

A Logit model is a popular parametric binary response model that is used to estimate the probabilities for binary outcomes. Other methods that can be used to predict binary outcomes are linear discriminant analysis and K-nearest neighbor. These are supervised machine learning methods for point prediction. Unlike Logit model, which needs transforming a continuous probability into a binary point prediction, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) and K-nearest neighbors are binary classi<sup>fi</sup>cation models that don't involve probability transformation [20,34]. However, a fundamental assumption of the LDA method is that the independent variables are normally distributed, which is not required for logistic regression. Since in this research, we do have independent variables that are not normally distributed, we will not adopt the LDA model in the prediction analysis.

K-nearest neighbor is a nonparametric model for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, which means that it does not make any assumptions on the underlying data distribution. This is a useful feature since in reality, the practical data does not obey theoretical assumptions like normality, linear separability, etc. This method is used to classify an object or event based on a majority vote of its K-nearest neighbors. It is a lazy learning algorithm, or instance-based learning, in which all computation is done locally. It is one of the simplest machine learning algorithms but works incredibly well in different scenarios. In a binary classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, we typically choose an odd number of neighbors to avoid tied votes. A detail discussion on K-nearest neighbor can be found in Duda et al. [18].

Although Neural Network is able to model highly complex relationships, the interpretation becomes very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. The model is a “black box”, and it is very hard to explain how Neural Network reaches an outcome or provides an explicit insight from the estimated network. On the other hand, Logit model provides interpretable coef<sup>fi</sup>cients and signi<sup>fi</sup>cance statistics. When we study consumer behavior and marketing problems, interpretability is as important as predictive power. Therefore, the choice between the two models depends on whether the complexity of the data justi<sup>fi</sup>es the use of the more complex model. In other words, if the complexity of the data is low and there is no or low level of non-linear relationship in our data, the Logit model is appropriate for our analysis. If the complexity of the data is high, then Neural Network is the preferred model to analyze our data. In this research, we <sup>fi</sup>rst estimate the Neural Network model with different hidden-layer units. If the results suggest that the Logit model is a better choice, then we adopt the Logit model to show the predictive power of customer checkins and marketing mix variables. After we <sup>fi</sup>nd the variables that are helpful to predict business failure using Logit model, we compare the predictive accuracy between Logit model and K-nearest neighbor model to <sup>fi</sup>nd out which model performs better.

## 3. Data and de<sup>fi</sup>nition of variables

## 3.1. Data

To accurately assess the impact of business failure, it is crucial that businesses and researchers utilize reliable data and an appropriate measure of failure. We focus on the restaurant industry in New York City and publicly available data is collected from July 2012 to January 2013 (around 6 months) from Foursquare and Yelp. We aim to use a cross-sectional data set to show that customer checkin data, available from location-based services, can be used to predict business performance, failures in particular. The spirit of prediction is that previous information or data can be used to predict the future outcome. Therefore, in this research, we use the checkin information in the <sup>fi</sup>rst three months to predict restaurants failure in the second three-month period.

The available information on small business failure is limited and confusing because of the variety of de<sup>fi</sup>nitions/proxies used in literature. It has been recognized that a lack of a reliable measure of failure is the major obstacle to understand and alleviate the causes of small business failure [11]. There are four de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of failure [56,57]: (1) discontinuance of a business for any reason [22]; (2) bankruptcy or loss to creditors [17]; (3) disposal through sale or liquidation to prevent further losses [54]; (4) failing to “make a go of it” [11]. The last de<sup>fi</sup>nition is wider than the <sup>fi</sup>rst three. Failing to “make a go of it” includes failed any business that are not earning an adequate return or are not meeting other owner objectives [11]. One of a restaurant owner's major objectives is to get enough customer visits, which is crucial for their survival and success. Checkins can only be made when a customer is physically close to a restaurant. Thus, more customers checking in at a restaurant through location-based services means more customers have visited the place or at least very close, which may lead to better business performance. Therefore, in this study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne failure as having less than one customer checkin per day during a three-month period. Our de<sup>fi</sup>nition of failure falls into the last category of business failure. We choose one customer checkin per day for the following reasons: (1) more and more customers are using their mobile devices and location-based services to search nearby restaurants, check in the ones they visit, and share their review comments [21]. In this mobile ear, if a restaurant cannot get enough mobile visits, it is probably not attractive to modern customers and this is very likely to increase its probability of total business failure. Companies and marketers have also expressed interest in and need for mobile data monitoring solutions in order to makes sense of mobile data and improve the real-time consumer insight and business performance [49]. (2) The median number of customer checkins is 3.25 per day. Therefore, we believe one customer checkin per day is a fair measure for business failure. If a restaurant cannot maintain one customer checkin every day, it would be hard for them to survive in this mobile commerce era. Later we will try different de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of failure to show the robustness of our results.

Preprocessing data is a key step in our data modeling process. Two steps have been taken to get our data ready for analysis: <sup>fi</sup>rst, subscribers to Foursquare can indicate their interest in a restaurant when they are physically present at or close to it by ‘checking in’ to the Foursquare application using their smartphones. We divide our six-month data set to two periods: the <sup>fi</sup>rst three-month period and the second three-month period. If a restaurant has at least one checkin on the last day of the <sup>fi</sup>rst three-month period, we classify this restaurant as “active” and include it in our <sup>fi</sup>nal data set. Second, if these active restaurants have less than one checkin per day during the second three months, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne them as “Failed” restaurants, and code them as 1. Otherwise we classify them as healthy restaurant and label them as 0. Our <sup>fi</sup>nal data set contains 686 restaurants. Fig. 1 shows the locations of these 686 restaurants.

## 3.2. Definition of variables

CTN is the number of competitors a restaurant has on Foursquare, which is directly collected from Foursquare. In order to show the predictive power of the customer checkin information available on Foursquare, we construct the following measures: (1) Average\_Daily\_Checkin is the average daily checkin for each restaurant over the <sup>fi</sup>rst three-month period. All three checkin variables we have discussed so far capture checkin information for a focal restaurant. However, the impact of checkin data from its neighbors cannot be ignored. We use 1 km as the cutoff radius and all restaurants within 1 km are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a focal restaurant's neighbors. Therefore, two other variables are created to capture neighbors' checkin information: (1) Average\_Daily\_Checkin\_Neighbors is used to capture how many checkins neighbors can get per day.

We perform the Pearson correlation analysis among these independent variables. Most of the correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients reported in Table 2 are very small, indicating no or very weak correlations. Some of the correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients are relatively high (above 0.5) and may raise concerns about multicollinearity. We later report results of the Variance In<sup>fl</sup>ation Factor (VIF) test to address the possible multicollinearity issue. Although we have attempted to control for the predominant variables associated with a restaurant's failure, at this point, we recognize that there may be other correlated but omitted variables associated with a restaurant's failure.

In predictive modeling, data partitioning is especially important for model assessment. Hence, we randomly split our data into two parts: 500 restaurants are used for the purpose of model estimation and the remaining 186 are used as a holdout sample to validate our model. Later, several different holdout sample sizes are tested to show the robustness of our results.

In terms of performance measures and test statistics, in this paper, we use Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to select the best-<sup>fi</sup>tted model [1]. A smaller AIC value indicates a better model. Therefore, our preferred model is the one with the lowest AIC value. Mean absolute deviation (MAD) is another measure that is adopted in this paper. MAD is the average of the absolute values of the deviations between actual and forecast data. The prediction accuracy of our model is measured by in- and out-of-sample MAD. It has been widely used to compare several forecasting models [13,43,59]. A model with smaller MAD is preferred to a model with larger MAD. The Hosmer and Lemeshow test [30] is a goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t test that is widely used to assess whether there is evidence for lack of <sup>fi</sup>t in a logistic regression model. It is used to compare the observed and expected number of events in bins de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the predicted probability of the outcome. The rule of thumb for the number of bin is the number of covariates plus 1, and the default number is 10 if the number of covariates is less than 9. This test statistic follows a chi-square distribution. A small p-value indicates a poor <sup>fi</sup>t.

In predictive analytics, a confusion matrix is a 2 by 2 table that contains information about actual and predicted classi<sup>fi</sup>cations done by a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation approach [50]. Each row represents the instances in an observed class and each column represents the instances in a predicted class. The <sup>fi</sup>rst step in most predictive modeling methods, such as Logit model, is to estimate the probability that a case belongs to a certain class. Then, we compare the estimated probability of belonging to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c class to a cutoff value [50]. In a two-class (0/1) case, the default cutoff value is 0.5. Thus, if the estimated probability of a case as class 1 is greater than 0.5, we classify this case as 1. Previously, people thought 90 % of restaurants failed during the <sup>fi</sup>rst year of operation. However, it is just a myth. A research study [42] has demonstrated that around 60% of restaurants fail during the <sup>fi</sup>rst three years of operation, which means the average failure rate per year is around 20%. Therefore, we use 20% as our cutoff probability when we calculate the percentage of correct prediction. In a Logit regression, a restaurant is predicted to fail if the predicted probability of failure is greater than or equal to 0.2. This cut off value is only required in Logit Model, but not in Neural Network or K-nearest neighbor model.

## 4.1. Neural network results

In our study, Neural Network is primarily used to handle the nonlinear relationships. If our independent variables are linearly separable (meaning there are no interaction terms or other non-linear terms), we will adopt the Logit model. Two decisions have to be made before we start creating Neural Network structures: how many hidden layers we need and how many neurons for each layer. The rule of thumb for these two numbers is: (1) one hidden-layer is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for the large majority of problems; (2) the number of neurons in that layer is the mean of the neurons in the input and output layers [28]. Based on this rule of thumb, our Neural Network should have one hidden layer and up to six neurons in that hidden layer. We have tried seven Neural Networks to <sup>fi</sup>nd out whether non-linear relationships exist or not, and reported the results in Table 4. The Neural Network without a neuron in the hidden layer has the lowest pair of AIC and BIC values, which suggest that the model without a neuron in the hidden layer is the best one. Therefore, our independent variables are linearly separable and Logistic regression is preferred in this study.

## 4.3.4. Test on different cutoff values used for business failure definition

Parsa et al. [42] <sup>fi</sup>nd that the restaurant failure rate is around 20%. Based on the data provided by National Restaurant Association, the restaurant failure rate is about 30% [12]. In the book ‘Contagious’, Berger [8] mentioned “more than 25% fail within twelve months of opening their doors, Sixty percent are gone within the first three vears."¹ Different failure rates have been found in previous studies or reports, in order to demonstrate that our results are robust and do not depend on the cut off value we used for the failure de<sup>fi</sup>nition, we estimate our logistic model again using three different cut off values, 0.2, 0.25 and 0.3. The VIF in Table 7 is large for CTN and the interaction term, CTN Rating. One concern with higher VIF is the possible multicollinearity issue. Therefore, in order to alleviate any concerns about multicollinearity issue, we remove the interaction term and run the logistic model again. The estimation results are reported in Table 11.

From Table 11, it is clear that our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings are very consistent no matter which cut off value we use. Table 11 also provides the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation rate for the hold out sample. It is obvious that Model 2 and Model 3 outperform Model 1. The misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation rate has dropped from around 30% (Model 1) to 11% (Models 2 and 3). Changing the cut off value for failure de<sup>fi</sup>nition only affects the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation rate for Model 1. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant drop in misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation rate is almost identical no matter which cut off value we adopt. It again demonstrates the robustness of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 5. Implications and conclusions

Our central contention in the paper is that mobile-enabled locationbased services that capture customer shopping behavior data hold signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictive power. Customers reveal their shopping history or shopping intention by checking into the places they have visited through mobile-enabled location-based services. These data are available to regular customers, retailers, and researchers at almost zero-cost and at remarkably levels of disaggregation compared to the data available from government bureaus. It has been shown that these disaggregate, real-time data can be used to predict economic activities, such as buying a house [59], predicting <sup>fi</sup>rm equity value [37], and the success of telemarketing calls for selling bank deposits [39]. These real-time social network data can also be used to predict crime. Gerber has shown the strong predictive power of twitter data on forecasting crime [23].

In this research, we aim to demonstrate that these timely, disaggregate customer data captured from location-based services can be used to predict business performance, failures in particular. We adopt several different approaches, such as Neural Network, Logit model and K-nearest neighbors. Results show that compared to Neural Network, standard Logit model is preferred since no non-linear relationship has been found and our independent variables are linearly separable. We then apply a standard Logit model to predict the odds of business failure with the help of these micro data we collected from Foursquare and Yelp. To assess the predictive power of checkin information, we compare our full models (Models 2 and 3 with both checkin information and business characteristics) with our baseline model (Model 1 with only business characteristic variables). In order to relax some assumptions required in Logit model, we also test several K-nearest neighbor models and <sup>fi</sup>nd that they are very similar to the Logit model in terms of predicative accuracy. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that a restaurant's odds of failure are correlated with its own customer checkin history and the customer checkin history of its nearby neighbors. A counter-intuitive <sup>fi</sup>nding we obtained in this research is that the effect of a restaurant's competitor number on the odds of its failure is higher for a low-rating restaurant than a high-rating restaurant. In order to make sure that our results are robust, we use a different failure measure, which is less than two checkins per day in the second three-month period. We con<sup>fi</sup>rm that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are very robust: the customer checkin from both the focal restaurant and the nearby competitors have strong predictive power. The misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation rate drops by more than 60% by adding checkin variables into the Logit model. In addition, we also generate different holdout samples and use different radius to further evaluate the performance and robustness of our results.

These correlations we <sup>fi</sup>nd in this study support the hypothesis that data captured in mobile-enabled location-based services can be used to predict business failure, especially for small businesses. For retailers like restaurants or coffee shops, obtaining accurate and timely customer visit information can help them make better informed business operation decisions and potentially save them on the edge of closing. Our results have shown that the checkin information from location-based services has very strong predictive power. Analyzing customers' checkin data could help us uncover the failure trends before they really appear.

Our study has some limitations. First, businesses fail for different reasons. Some unobserved variables might lead to business failure. For instance, the shop owner may sell the place because he/she runs out of cash <sup>fl</sup>ow. Information like this is very private and hard to know in advance, but they can be easily incorporate into our model that will lead to a new level of insights. Second, our data is about the restaurant industry in New York City. It will be an interesting exercise to apply the techniques presented here to data sets from other industries and other cities, and see if the checkin information from location-based services can still have predictive power on business performance. Third, our de<sup>fi</sup>nition of failure is based on customers' checkin failure on location-based services. We acknowledge that this failure is limited to the mobile world, not a real-world closure or bankruptcy that has happened. However, for these real-world closures we captured in our data set, we do observe that they are preceded by virtual death. Therefore, this virtual death or failure is a good indicator for real business failure, and it will be extremely useful for small business if they can capture the virtual death and adapt their business operations to turn over the trend. Fourth, in this research, we focus on restaurants and their customer check-ins captured by Foursquare. Foursquare is also a location-based social network. In the future, as pointed out by Lee et al. [36], it will be interesting to study the social network formed by Foursquare users, and incorporate the user proximity measures into the model and test how they can help to improve predictive accuracy.
