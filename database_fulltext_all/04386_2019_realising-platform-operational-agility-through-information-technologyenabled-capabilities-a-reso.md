## Abstract

Intensifying competition in recent times requires businessto‐consumer (B2C) e‐commerce platforms to develop operational agility for competitive advantage. Despite the agreement on an increasing competitive landscape, the process for realising information technology (IT)‐driven operational agility on e‐business platforms has received little scrutiny. Through an investigation of M.com, a leading multisided online retailing platform in China, this study provides a clear thesis of resource interdependencies and IT‐enabled capabilities forged during operational processes to realise operational agility. Our investigation, observations, and reflections from practice substantiate a model of how a platform like M.com cultivates IT‐driven resource‐interdependent competencies and capabilities tantamount to realising operational agility. The model shows how IT enables three capabilities—localised, synergistic, and optimised—which reflect the effective coordination of three resource interdependencies (pooled, sequential, and reciprocal) during M.com's complex operations and processes, and explicates the consequences. For research, we postulate that IT‐enabled operational agility in complex organisational forms, cultivated through the development of resource‐interdependent capabilities to deliver effective sensing and response mechanisms, forms effective strategies for the B2C platform in dynamic marketplace conditions.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

As competition between contemporary business‐to‐consumer (B2C) firms in a digital economy intensifies, opera tional agility, or the ability of a firm's business processes to accomplish speed, accuracy, and cost economy, is con sidered imperative for the exploitation of marketplace opportunities for innovation and competitive actio (Sambamurthy, Bharadwaj, & Grover, 2003). It is a cultivated capability that ensures firms can rapidly redesign existing processes and creates new processes and organisational forms to reduce information asymmetrie between buyers and sellers under dynamic marketplace conditions. Operational agility underpins the ability to detect and seize market opportunities within a focused business model to achieve firm performanc (Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Sull, 2010).

Information technology (IT) shapes agility models by enhancing a firm's capacity to gather and process information (Overby, Bharadwaj, & Sambamurthy, 2006). Not only do digitalisation and IT investments play a significant role in shaping agility for many firms, they simultaneously stimulate platform capabilities (Tiwana, Konsynski, & Bush, 2010; Yoo, Boland Jr, Lyytinen, & Majchrzak, 2012) and cultivate emergent organisational forms (Chu & Smithson, 2007). Increasingly, such technology‐mediated shifts are appearing where firms develop as platforms (Yoo et al., 2012). Ingram Micro (see Sambamurthy et al., 2003), eBay (Tiwana et al., 2010), Amazon, and Alibaba (Sawhney & Khosla, 2014) operate as platforms that facilitate the transactions between two or more constituent sides in large and complex networks of suppliers, intermediaries, and customers (Hagiu & Wright, 2013). Unlike traditional pipelin businesses that create value by controlling a linear series of activities, platform businesses facilitate the exchange o goods, services, or social currency, thereby enabling value creation for all participants (Van Alstyne, Parker, & Choudary, 2016).

Amid the growth of the marketplace and intensifying competition, platforms increasingly face environmenta and organisational challenges posed by their surrounding ecosystems (Tiwana, 2013) such that it is becoming increasingly complex for platforms to establish, manage, and sustain their multisided markets (Eisenmann, Parker, & Alstyne, 2006). Unlike traditional pipeline businesses, platforms must be sensitive to network effects between multiple sides of the market and managing regulatory issues, including access, compatibility, data privacy and security, and national control of information assets unique to large platform businesses (Parker, Van Alstyne, & Choudary, 2016). Hence, multisided platforms require substantial resources and co‐ordination (Gawer & Cusumano, 2014). Analogously, scholars argue that understanding the co‐ordination of organisational resources is often more important an issue than IT (Hsu, 2013a) and that interdependencies need to be developed in response to competitive environments (Hillman, Withers, & Collins, 2009). Understanding various types of interde pendence affects the appropriate configurations of firm resources (Siggelkow & Levinthal, 2003; Sirmon & Hitt, 2009) and shapes business processes (Gözübüyük, 2013) and a firm's capacity to respond quickly and effectively to market forces (Rockart & Short, 2012).

The notion of resource interdependencies is widely adopted in management and information systems (IS) to study organisational change phenomenon (Kumar & Van Dissel, 1996; Lenox, Rockart, & Lewin, 2007; Sirmon & Hitt, 2009). Resource interdependencies exist within the transaction of resources between units, such that actions in one organisational unit affect important outcomes in another unit, However, uncertainty occurs when resource flow fluctuates or transactions are subject to competitive pressures. Understanding the function of resource interdependencies is significant as it helps us answers important questions on managing the number of resource provision frequency of resource sharing, and determining the criticality of resources in transactions, such as financial resource budgeting and control (Pfeffer & Salancik, 2003). In other words, failing to determine resource interdependencies may complicate the task of co‐ordination, planning, and subsequently control and in turn ensue to incur higher firm costs and resources in environmental scanning and forecasting processes (Kumar & Van Dissel, 1996). Hence, addi tional theoretical treatment and scrutiny is needed to understand how the resource interdependencies in a firm ar tied to IT‐enabled capabilities that are required to achieve agility.

Despite the favourable attention surrounding agility for platforms, the process for realising IT‐driven operational agility on multisided platforms has received neither consistent treatment in IS literature nor a coherent theory of its meaning. The current body of knowledge in agility research remains largely theoretical, and further focus is needed to understand how IT‐enabled operational agility is cultivated in complex and emergent organisational forms (Richardson, Kettinger, Banks. & Ouintana. 2014). Although prior research suggests that operational agility is technol ogy mediated, it is still a socially embedded organisational capability that requires more than a simple activation of technology (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). Hence, despite the key role of IT systems in organisational change, few scholars agree that they are sufficient for complex and diversified firms (Merali, Papadopoulos, & Nadkarni, 2012 Napier, Rivers, Wagner, & Napier, 2006). Any assumption that IT systems automatically enable business integration and that co‐operation between departments naturally ensues it is inaccurate. In many cases, only tight co‐ordination between discrete business activities to form a seemingly single function ultimately enables an e‐commerce firm to respond to pressing competitive forces, deliver value, and enhance firm performance (Markus, 2000; Zhu, Kraemer, & Xu, 2006). Researchers call for a closer examination of the reconfiguration of their resource base and th development of new capabilities (Chu & Smithson, 2007) for internal business change (Yoo et al., 2012).

Against the above backdrop, this study addresses the following research question: How does a multisided platform cultivate IT‐driven resource‐interdependent capabilities to realise operational agility? To answer this question, we conducted a case study of a renowned e‐commerce and multisided platform in China, M.com (a pseudonym). M.com is a major competitor to another multisided platform, Alibaba (Tan, Tan, & Pan, 2016), by providing diverse small and medium enterprises access to production, logistics, distribution, and payments, which challenges traditiona online business models. The qualitative analysis in this study focuses on combining insights from M.com executives and cross‐examining significant events in the transition and growth of the firm. Drawing on the resource interdepen dencies as a theoretical lens, this study identifies how platforms use technology to acquire resources that increase alternative options for new product markets to co‐ordinate different types of resources and exploit critica interdependencies among them. Findings from the study substantiate theory building on the process of realising how IT‐enabled operational agility responds under dynamic marketplace conditions.

Our paper contributes to IS and organisational studies and holds implications for both research and practice. Directed by principles of resource interdependencies purported in organisational studies, our study provides valuable guidance for managers looking for new organisational strategies to address the firm challenges associated with limited resources, and conditions of uncertainties in a dynamic and increasingly competitive business landscape We consider effects of the different types of interdependencies on new practices and capabilities that improve firm operations. For organisational studies, this research provides a guide for a systematic programme of research to crystallise operational agility as an organisational capability (Sanchez, 1995; Sanchez & Mahoney, 1996) for platforms and their mechanisms and explains the conseguences. This examination is important because these capabilities in turn facilitate the integration of multiple functions, such as sales, production, and distribution to continually identify opportunities in the market and orchestrate resources to seize those opportunities (Overby et al.. 2006 Sambamurthy et al., 2003). For IS, our study builds on management aspects of platforms and interdependencies to offer a characterisation of the relationship between IS, resource interdependencies, and platform operational agility. Deeper knowledge of the processes underlying the effective management of resource interdependencies and the role of IS offered by this study enables new practices that will potentially optimise resource utilisation and subsequently navigate a platform business towards operational agility and its competitive advantage. Conversely, ineffective management of resource interdependencies can impact the costs/benefits of projects significantly, including unnecessary resource wastage and improper allocation of resources in IS proiects. Bevond insistence of a relationship between IS and operational agility, we find that various strategies to facilitate IT/IS‐enabled operational agility imply different levels of coordination that can vary widely, depending on associated factors. Lessons learnt from our study are also particularly relevant for managers who are developing strategies to obtain resources to overcome resource uncertainty for IS project portfolios.

## 2 | LITERATURE REVIEW

In this section, we examine the notions relevant to this study. In particular, we discuss the background of multisided platforms, the gaps in IT‐driven operational agility and platform research, and present resource interdependencies as the theoretical lens for this study

## 2.1 | Multisided platforms and operational agility

The rapid emergence of B2C platforms and electronic marketplaces has put IS scholars on notice. This emergence reflects a rapid shift in attention to the platform‐centric ecosystem (Tiwana et al., 2010), namely, the combination of a platform and the constituents specific to it (Boudreau, 2010; Boudreau, 2012) now found in marketplaces. This shift presents significant challenges and opportunities for IS researchers. Many of these combinations develop int multisided platforms that operate in an IT‐enabled commercial network comprising two or more constituent sides, including suppliers, intermediaries, and customers, and facilitate the transactions between these different constituent sides of the market to add value (Gawer & Cusumano, 2008; Hagiu, 2014).

Fundamentally, a multisided platform performs two functions: (1) reducing the search costs incurred by platform constituents when seeking transaction partners and (2) reducing the shared costs incurred during the actual transaction. Notable multisided platforms include Alibaba and eBay. The increasing popularity of e‐commerce,<sup>1</sup> the intense competition between e‐commerce platforms, and the dramatic increase in the volume of economic activity generated through e‐commerce place new forms of scrutiny on firms' emergent platform strategies and business models (Hsu, 2013b).

Given the emergence of platforms, scholars encourage further investigations, suggesting that conventiona mechanisms prescribed in IS development literature cannot be readily applied beyond individual firms to platforms because the scope is significantly wider (Boudreau & Hagiu, 2009; Tiwana et al., 2010). Unlike traditional pipeline businesses, platform businesses operate on the principles of openness, transparency, and participation, and the value of a platform is derived primarily from network effects. Given this, governance mechanisms unique to platforms must be introduced to prevent failures caused by information asymmetry, externalities, monopoly power, and risk focus (Parker et al., 2016). This is significant given that platforms invert companies and transform firm's traditional inward focus into outward focus, using resources they do not own or control to create value (Parker et al., 2016). For example, push strategies (such as advertising and public relations) used in conventional marketing is less important in platform strategy.

The IS literature postulates that more agile platforms enjoy competitive advantage and enhanced firm performance because they continually sense opportunities in their environment and marshal the resources necessary for responding to those opportunities (Huang, Pan, & Ouyang, 2014; Overby et al., 2006). As previously mentioned, operational agility reflects the ability of a firm's business processes to develop speed, accuracy, and cost economy in the exploitation of opportunities for innovation and competitive action (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). According to more recent studies (Huang et al., 2014; Ngai, Chau, & Chan, 2011), operational agility further describes the sensing and responding capabilities within the supply chain to address unexpected market changes and reduce uncertainty For example, a firm can enhance its operational agility by developing flexible, reusable IT‐platform capabilities that facilitate cost reduction (Richardson et al., 2014). Based on an empirical study of 109 electronic marketplaces Chakravarty, Grewal, and Sambamurthy (2013) propose that IT plays an enabling and facilitating role in building and shaping firm agility and performance.

However, agility cannot simply be implemented by procedures or rules, because it is an inimitable and nonsubstitutable strategic advantage (Ngai et al., 2011) that results in superior performance in the long term. Studies have identified several factors enabling supply chain agility, including flexibility (Ngai et al., 2011), market sensitivity (White, Daniel, & Mohdzain, 2005), scalability (Holmqvist & Pessi, 2006), and integrative processes (Sarker, Munson, Sarker, & Chakraborty, 2009). For many platforms, achieving agility requires e‐commerce activities to be integrated into the value chain at both the front end (marketing and sales, customer services, and outbound logistics) and the back end (operations, technology development, and procurement) (Markus, 2000; Zhu & Kraemer, 2005). In short, organisational resources are perceived to be more important than IT in generating business integration capabilities in an e‐business context (Hsu, 2013a). However, the strategic role of organisational IS (Kumar & Van Dissel, 1996) and the interdependencies between sides that are crucial for collaboration (Lenox et al., 2007; Munksgaard, 2010 are neglected topics in IS research, particularly in e‐commerce literature that has largely ignored the deep‐rooted practices of business integration (Markus, 2000). Robust functional and resource‐based models are required to address the diverse needs for resource‐based logic (Staudenmayer, 1997).

Although a more complex, larger base of resources is advantageous to firms launching competitive action (Ferrier, Smith, & Grimm, 1999), it increases the need for adjustments between organisations, departments, and business units (Jansen, Simsek, & Cao, 2012). From a resource‐based view, a firm's competitive advantage is attributed to the capabilities and competencies that co‐ordinate, redeploy, and leverage resources (Teece & Pisano, 1994). While competencies often result from a blending of technology and diverse production skills to leverage corporate resources (Prahalad, 1993) (eg, Casio's product portfolio and Canon's expertise in optics), capabilities are rooted in processes and business routines that are not necessarily built on a technological foundation (eg, Marriot's ability to design training protocols and create a hospitality culture) (Kenneth, 1996). While recent discussions have shifted from competencies to capabilities, competencies are inextricably linked to firm‐specific capabilities, and differentiating them is a distraction (Kenneth, 1996). Competencies should provide potential access to a wide variety of markets, make a contribution to the customer benefits of the product, and be difficult for competitors to imitate (Prahalad & Hamel, 2006). Service innovation, for example, is a competency of service organisations (Kandampully, 2002), as firms increasingly compete on the basis of services rather than physical products. Organising around competencies and capabilities requires a radical change in corporate organisation and a redesign of internal operations for continua organisational learning. Operations are the key context within such activities and issues of routines, capabilities, and competencies take place.

## 2.2 | A resource‐interdependence perspective

In this study, resource interdependence describes the extent to which organisational units depend on each other for resources to accomplish tasks (Gerdin, 2005; Jansen et al., 2012). From an organisational perspective, the development of the body of knowledge around resource interdependence lies in the fundamental notion that each organisa tion or business unit has only a portion of the resources, information, or materials necessary to achieve a goa (Johnson, Johnson, & Stanne, 1989). The richness of the information that flows between parties is an important ele ment in the smooth functioning and development of relationships (Tomkins. 2001). Studies of interdependence not only focus on the allocation and distribution of critical resources (Comeau & Griffith, 2005) but also examine how organisations acquire resources to reduce uncertainty and interdependence (Ulrich & Barney, 1984) to complete tasks. Additionally, examining resource interdependence is important when addressing information complexity and resource scarcity (Ettlie & Reza, 1992). The notion of resource interdependence has featured prominently in studies on firm resource management (Hillman et al., 2009; Ulrich & Barney, 1984), psychology of relationships (Johnson et al 1989) and IS development (Parolia Jiang, Klein & Sheu 2011) Over the years numerous scholars have adopted this notion of interdependencies to create alternative theories and contribute important insights to the bod of knowledge (Lenox et al., 2007; Levinthal, 1997). Here, we elaborate on the conceptualisation of resource interdependencies.

The foundation of the notion of resource interdependencies in organisations lies in the seminal work o Thompson (1967). He argues that three types of interdependencies exist and arise from the completion of organisational tasks—pooled, sequential, and reciprocal—such that the extent of the relationship and workflow between work units can be identified by one of these three types. In a pooled interdependence model, units perform separate business functions, imply the least contingencies (eg, conflicts or incidents), and typically have the highes physical dispersion between them (eg, when different independent work units like marketing and accounting rely little on one another for their work function), Hence, pooled resource interdependence typically arises when there is a higher level of predictability in the resources available that typically meets the requirements for decision making, and the outcome is fairly well predicted. Sequential interdependence between units occurs in tasks when operations have a clearly defined process and a unit engages in a chain of structured activities that support the addition of value to the goods or services for the next unit or internal beneficiary (eg, steps in a procurement cycle or goods assembly line). Reciprocal interdependence, which describes a cyclical exchange of value, goods, or services between units, reflects most contingencies and typically the lowest physical dispersion (eg, front desk and housekeeping departments in a hotel where the output of one unit becomes the input of another and vice versa) (Lenox et al., 2007; Pfeffer & Salancik, 2003; Staudenmayer, 1997). All organisations have sequential interdependence, and the more complex firms have all three types (Gözübüyük, 2013)

Interdependencies are an important source of power to subunits, regardless of the measure used to assess them (Astley & Zajac, 1990; Astley & Zajac, 1991). Hence, in this study, we refer to the state of resource interdependencies to reflect the specific nature of the relationship and workflow between work units. At the firm level, interdependencies are recognised as a critical contingency (Chenhall & Morris, 1986) and as “boundary spanners” because th nature of these relationships determine effective collaboration (Caglio & Ditillo, 2012). This is significant as collaboration between constituents in a multisided platform ecosystem is not a simple “co‐operate or not” decision and demands scrutiny of different strategies of co‐ordination between multiple constituents (Yoo, 2013; Yoo Henfridsson, & Lyytinen, 2010). The development and co‐ordination of the interdependencies between busines units can potentially alleviate conflict, which is considered a typical barrier to sustained co‐operation

It is unknown how the resource interdependencies in a platform are tied to the IT‐enabled capabilities required to achieve agility. Extant literature points to the need for better understanding of the effective management of interdependencies to address uncertainties found during business integration in complex, networked businesses. This knowledge is important as the interdependence types identified above demand different co‐ordination methods in terms of rules and operating procedures, adaptive planning and scheduling, and mutual adiustments (Munksgaard 2010). In other words, levels of interdependence complicate the task of co‐ordination and tend to affect planning and control (Chenhall & Morris, 1986). The premise is that uncertainty and organisational conflicts—typically over resources—can pose a threat to co‐operative processes (Kumar & Van Dissel, 1996); that is, uncertainty influences the extent to which a future circumstance is conditioned by the provision of resources (Verwaal, Commandeur, & Verbeke, 2009). Every organisation faces resource constraints, so resources tend to be allocated where they are needed the most.

The availability of internal resources is also an important contingency affecting the decisions of firms (eg, the decision to upgrade to a new system) (Khoo & Robey. 2007). For a long time, scholars have emphasised uncertainty as a critical aspect that managers must contend with when seeking to achieve a resource‐based advantage (Sirmon & Hitt, 2009; Verwaal et al., 2009). Firms that experience uncertainty incur higher costs and spend more resources. For example, firms that are uncertain about the state of their environment are likely to spend greater amounts of time and resources on environmental scanning and forecasting (Milliken, 1987). Therefore, we refer to the effect of uncertainty between interdependent units arising from resource interdependence across units as something that must be managed. The literature has, identified that the reduction of uncertainty involves understanding changing societa trends and developments in IT (Kumar & Van Dissel, 1996), and the knowledge of options and/or the likely consequences of a choice of resource allocation on the organisation and its systems, processes, or resources.

From our review of the literature, we identified the key conceptual themes with which to guide our investigative research in Table 1. Drawing on the perspective of resource interdependencies forged during operational processes of multisided platforms, our investigation seeks to uncover insights into a platform's development of IT‐enabled mechanisms, competencies, and capabilities required to achieve operational agility.

## 3 | CASE STUDY RESEARCH METHOD

We adopted an interpretivist case study research approach (Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1995) to address th question regarding how IT‐driven operational agility on a multisided e‐commerce platform is developed from the per spective of resource interdependencies. The case study approach is appropriate, as the phenomena under study ar complex, multifaceted, and embedded within a firm context that makes examining them through stakeholders' inter pretations more suitable than using a quantitative approach (Klein & Myers, 1999). Our interpretivist case study approach seeks to provide empirical contributions (Ågerfalk, 2014) and reveal insights into a novel phenomenon without relying explicitly on a priori conceptualisations (Mays & POPE, 2000).

The case firm selection was based on the following criteria. First, the case firm should be a multisided platform situated in a competitive environment such that there is a need to improve operational agility to generate business value and enhance firm performance. The case should experience complex changes in work processes, organisation and management. We aimed to extract the directors' and managers' knowledge and experience regarding the management of associated conflicts and uncertainty over resources and the improvement of business process integration Second, the firm should have demonstrated its capabilities (both IT and non‐IT) in creating tighter co‐ordination between discrete business functions and activities (eg, operations planning, order processing, and product research and development) to create unified business processes.

## 3.2 | Coding and data analysis

We performed data analysis concurrently with data collection (Eisenhardt, 1989) to compare the initial findings of the case against the initial statements and our theoretical lens and to reach confidence (per Pan & Tan, 2011). We compared th revisions with subsequent interview data, sifting through the empirical data, theoretical perspectives, relevant literature and other sources to build an explanation (Walsham. 2006) of the resource interdependencies formed and to learn when what, and most importantly how (Walsham, 1995) the strategies around tighter co‐ordination of discrete business activ ities influenced performance under specified operating conditions. To achieve this, we used a combination of tempora bracketing, narrative, and visual mapping strategies to organise the empirical data (Langley, 2009) to obtain a deeper com prehension of the evidence. In addition, we performed open, axial, and selective coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1990) on translated notes and documents from the secondary data collection. During open coding, we assigned conceptual labels to the data to capture interfirm activities and operational processes to reduce the data to manageable categories and to identify recurring themes. The literature offered some conceptual labels, including “mutual adjustments,” “standard data,” and “specify rules,” while the data offered others, including “decentralisation” and “co‐opetition.”

Next, we linked similar concepts from the open coding to form axial themes. “Standard data models” and “regular meetings,” for instance, were mechanisms for co‐ordinating the pooled interdependence between sides. The concepts of “data standards” and “regulated communication channels,” which refer to co‐ordinating mechanisms for reporting and planning, have not been discussed in the literature. ## 4 | HOW DOES THE M.COM PLATFORM CULTIVATE RESOURCE‐INTERDEPENDENT CAPABILITIES?

M.com was founded in the late 1990s and began as a chain of computer electronics stores. Because of the SARS epi demic in 2003, falling consumer confidence caused retail sales to plummet across its stores. Faced with the distinct possibility of closing all its stores, the firm considered the option of changing its mode of operation to an e‐business model. In late 2003, the CEO made an executive decision to shift the firm to an online mode of operation, leading a group of IT personnel to leverage its knowledge in electronics and develop a system to support the transformation to an e‐business. Over the next 3 years, the business flourished. Taking advantage of China's rise in personal computing, the emerging technological wave, and changing customer behaviour, M.com began offering an extended range of products and services to an expanded customer base. This customer base included raw material manufacturers, agents, distributors, retailers, stores, and other e‐commerce outlets

A number of the senior executives we interviewed felt the impact of the complexity and rapidity of changes driven by the competitive marketplace. As the director of the research and development centre (Chengdu) explained “I have seen a lot of changes [in the marketplace] since the four years that I have joined, such as that the IT department had to grow from six to now hundreds. Our back‐end business logic is now very complicated.” The director of the Testing Department added, “As the business [has] diversified and grown, so has the number of departments we have to manage ... it's easy to say cancel a stock order, but it affects restocking, financials, and other processes and systems.” Despite the competitive environment, in less than 10 years, M.com has pushed through to become one of China's most influential multisided B2C platforms in terms of visits, click‐through rates, sales, and industry reputation. In 2013, the company announced that it would expand bevond the traditional B2C model to become a total solutions provider in the areas of technology, logistics, customer service, and financial services to help its constituents in th marketplace achieve steady and sustainable growth. The sections that follow contain an examination of how M. com realised operational agility by managing resource interdependencies and exploiting technologies and capabilities more effectively than its competitors to achieve disproportionate profits and rapid growth

## 4.1 | Developing localised capabilities for IT‐driven operational agility

## 4.1.1 | Pooling resource interdependence at M.com

As M.com attempted to establish itself as a platform, reporting and strategic planning were crucial in consolidating its rapidly growing client base and reputation. Fundamentally, these reporting and strategic planning tasks involved the pooling of data where each department discretely contributed to the whole by collating (or pooling) its information and knowledge without regard for other departments' activities. According to the directors at M.com, this translating of all departments' knowledge—where some share low resource interdependence in day‐to‐day operations—is required to plan the strategies moving forward. The director of the Business Data and Warehouse Department explained, “We have to collate data from every one of the six departments [in the back office], then, based on the requirements of the business, create analyses and other reports. There are some scientific reports, too, that we prepare.” These data are required for other divisions to develop new opportunities and the ability to build a base from which the firm could adapt and extend its operations. For instance, call logs. transactions, and service logs are reguirec to determine why a certain customer performance index has risen or fallen and to decide how to deal with this According to the director of the Information Control Department, “[The data came] from multiple offices … one set is the users' information exchange data … or when they click on the respective log data. Then there is the transaction data … these may have a considerable amount of extended data. Then, there is data between us and them during customer service.” The availability and certainty of information becomes an important foundation for these reports.

## 4.1.2 | The role of IT in managing pooling resource interdependence at M.com

When interdependent data sources are not well specified, uncertainty is added into data gathering between departments and, consequently, strategic planning. Hence, and to support the requirements of pooling data for interdependent reporting and strategic planning by departments, M.com's directors established a routine and regulated communications strategy supported by a combination of in‐house systems and social media platforms. The “Big Ears” programme at M.com is an example of an initiative that promotes open communication and collaboration across interdependent departments for data resources. The human resources manager explained the initiative, which is underpinned by in‐house systems: “We use ‘Big Ears’ [a literal translation of the name of the programme the company introduced] to connect line workers directly with senior management. It is an internal digital channel to feed back your concerns or to propose an innovation. Every year the “golden idea” prize is given to the employee who proposes an innovation of value.” This initiative is contrary to reports that in China, IT systems are often used to maintain power and control and that applications that are consistent with directives rather than participative management are more likely to suit (Martinsons & Westwood, 1997). Furthermore, the director of the research and development centre (Chengdu) outlined the collection and distribution of data using social media platforms: “We use Weibo [the Chinese version of instant messenger/Twitter]. However, its capability of [transferring] information is relatively low, so we use that to share connections and links to technical articles and blogs.”

To enhance the use of data, managers moved beyond complicated IT functions to adopt compatible standards and models for the classification of data that departments relied upon. The director of the Business Data and Warehouse Department noted, “We use data models provided by the Data Department at the front office to catalogue the data. We report on total running costs, timetabling of delivery, marketing analysis reports, complaints, after‐sales feedback, technical reports, external partnership information, and results.” For example, the Business Data and Warehouse Department analysed the back‐office operations using the data parameters and models developed by the Information Control Department. The front‐office departments consequently operated in compatible ways, performing IT service management according to Western standards through use of the IT infrastructure library (ITIL) for service continuity management, operation processes, and programme management. According to a director of the Software Quality Assurance Department, this procedure sets the reporting standards for front‐office operations.

## 4.1.3 | Localised competencies and capabilities for operational agility at M.com

In M.com, the translating of protocols and models through technology enables competence in managing pooled resource interdependencies. From data, departments like Information Control and Business Data and Warehouse were able to generate a large number of extended indicators to help identify new segments of customers using the site. For example, the director of platform architecture (research division) stated that its customers largely were male and aged 18 to 35 years, and many were white‐collar workers, civil servants, public sector employees, college students, and technology enthusiasts who did not have enough time to go shopping. This generation of consumers tends to have a steady income, strong consumption ability, and a familiarity with online shopping. These consumers are more likely to be loyal customers. According to the same director, the firm customised the platform with products that were closer in terms of what the demographic needed Then based on these different segments the front-office departments were able to segregate and plan their marketing and service strategies specific to the demographic. As the director of platform architecture (research division) outlined, “Our goods are now classified into A, B, and C types. A goods are those that we have eyes on or we think that will do well, so the exposure is higher than the rest, the have priority when it comes to stocking.” Similarly, the director of information control observed, “We now have a lot of client and product groups. Some of them are digital enthusiasts, some are more of book readers, etc. … we can locate the users' preferences.”

The examples highlighted above describe the unique adaptive profiling of both clients and products. For example in cities like Chengdu and Wuhan, where people have a relatively low income, are younger, and have lower levels o education, the firm would not advertise products such as the iPhone 4 or brands such as Gucci. In turn, the departments used this information to communicate with the associated operations levels. This adaptive profiling capability enabled the firm to develop more localised responses that were both specific and effective. As the directo of the Business Data and Warehouse department noted, “We decided to automate the procurement process for goods that are daily essentials; the 100 things that people use every day are automatically procured by the system.” This leads to our first proposition:

Proposition 1. Towards operational agility, multisided platforms leverage IT to cultivate competence in

the co‐ordination of pooling resource interdependencies and to realise localised operational capabilities

Localised operational capabilities represent the firm's capacity and its IT function to translate, adapt, and dispense resources necessary for localised responses in a marketplace. These capabilities are enabled by compatible IT systems to improve connectivity among organisational functions, particularly those that are dispersed du to geographical differences, to empower the exchange of common information and data. Based on our investigations, IT compatibility and enhanced organisational processes not only determine certainty in its cumulative resources but also foster adaptiveness and alignment within the organisation to emerging business imperatives Especially in instances where departments operate in compatible but not necessarily integrated ways in pooling tasks, discrete resources and knowledge kept locally can be called upon quickly when required. This allows fo adaptive profiling of customers and products, an enhanced capability that allows the firm to quickly sense and respond to targeted demographics with the implementation of specific strategies. This process enjoys a highe level of predictability and speed in the available information that typically meets the requirements for decisio making (Gerdin, 2005).

## 4.2 | Developing synergistic capabilities for IT‐driven operational agility

## 4.2.1 | Sequential resource interdependence at M.com

Towards the end of 2007, M.com's strategies for maintaining its high transaction volume were in doubt. In 4 years (2004‐2008), M.com had achieved a 340% growth rate in sales, and it was handling 10 million registered users by late 2009. Daily new orders exceeded 1500, and with an inventory turnover of only 12 days, the demands of order processing–related activities on the firm's technological infrastructure became extremely high. The firm rapidly moved from maintaining a Web presence to managing order processing transactions end to end online. Orders became more complicated with customer cancellations and amendments. As the director of product research and development explained, ‘For example, cancelling an order: You have 30 minutes on Amazon to make changes or to cancel your order otherwise there will be charges; we have no time limit. This may sound good, but creates a lot of troubles, because in 30 minutes the order is well processed. Hence, if there were to be changes, we have to refresh the process and that would involve many parties.”

## 4.2.2 | The role of IT in managing sequential resource interdependence in M.com

An expansion, and subsequently a division, of the IS Department was considered necessary to address the growth i orders but also was viewed as a risky move. Thus, in October 2009, M.com announced a split of the IS Department into two departments—the front office and the back office—amid some resistance from managers. Product managers were particularly concerned about the impact of emergent communication problems between front end and back‐end operations, especially when attempting to avoid system bottlenecks during larger orders, product refunds, or new promotions. As the director of the Business Warehouse and Data Department pointed out, “They [the front offices] deal with the website, sales, volume, and all other customer‐facing operations. The back‐end offices deal with picking and packing goods, logistics, supply chain, orders from [the] phone or Internet, orders going to warehouse, from warehouse to production, production then delivery. We also dea with customers' returns.” Higher resource interdependence between departments arose due to the decentralising of IS office responsibilities and changes in the sequence of order fulfilment. When whole processes are fragmented and/or the linkages between departments are not visible, it adds uncertainty to sequential workflows in order processing. As the process winds its way through different offices, handovers cause delay, add expense, and introduce errors. Some of these uncertainty‐induced problems include opera tional downtime because of a departure in workflow from the existing process, such as cancellations or amendments to orders.

To support the requirements of sales order processing and a sequential workflow, a range of IT systems were adopted to facilitate collaboration between departments. This includes an in‐house Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) and logistics system at its back end and the customer service system (supported by its nationa customer service centre). Order processing became highly structured and mostly automated through the firm's in‐house ordering and ERP system. With narrower business functions and specialisations, each office becomes closer to the conduct of such activities, and the ERP and ordering system becomes a critical resource for integrating business functions. While ERP aims at integration of enterprise‐wide business units into a coherent whole, ERP challenges and decisions often are resource sensitive themselves (He, 2004). The director of the Supply Chain Management Department explained that IT needs to monitor the distribution of resources: “Besides an in‐house ERP, we have a series of systems to support the order centre. Systems allow us to monitor the distribution of sales orders to the warehouses for processing, and it will simultaneously report‐back information on the status of warehouse and delivery.” IT systems are also used to simulate critical processes, as the directo of product management (back‐end operations) stated: “When we are analysing customer requirements, we mock up the sales and delivery process. For instance, if front office is able to absorb the orders, what pressures are there on back office, how many orders will come, do we have stock in reserve, if not, do we need to replenish our stock quickly?” This was in addition to a fully optimised Web interface supported by a large‐scale Webcontent repository.

While IT supports the platform, the processing of orders cannot be relegated to only IT mechanisms as the logic of these systems would need to be immensely complex and have a detailed understanding of the fulfilment process. According to the director of the Product Management Department (back‐end operations), “The order triggers the next system in each warehouse called WMS [warehouse management system]; when the warehouse systems see the job it's another process—package, scan, and send out of warehouse. All the information flows back t the OSC [Operations Support Centre]. Information also goes to a DMS [distribution management system] at each delivery centre for delivery.” In addition, the capacity to handle growing transaction volumes heightened the perception that the IS Department (understood in the firm to be a combination of the Information Control, Software Quality Assurance, and Platform Architecture Departments) was playing a supporting role rather than leading firm changes. The director of platform architecture (research division) observed that many emplovees believed that M.com was essentially not a new IT business but a traditional business operating on a new IT platform: “The business grew so quickly … rather than being the driver, the IT department has gone passive, supporting product tracking and order processing.” By late 2011, the size of the IS Department had reached nearly 800 employees (more than 400 workers for front‐end operations and more than 300 workers for back‐end operations). Directors and teams were appointed to various front‐end and back‐end operations to oversee the integration of skills, pro cesses, and technologies

## 4.2.3 | Synergistic competencies and capabilities for operational agility at M.com

The bundling of enterprise systems routines and changing the capacity of the IS Department to co‐ordinate tasks like order processing reveal how M.com developed competence in managing sequential resource interdependencies. As result, M.com has developed a reputation in the industry as an optimal logistics company. M.com now possesses wholly owned logistics subsidiaries in two southern states and four large logistics centres in four major cities. It als established its own courier company to expand the logistics and distribution network and to provide more efficient distribution services across China. Customers identify M.com by its 211 promise, which is the guarantee the firm makes on the delivery of an order. Because M.com owns and controls the supply chain end to end, many other popular e‐commerce platforms in the country cannot compete on that scale in terms of shipping and distribution The result is a highly synergistic supply chain that not only reduces costs but also is closely monitored by M.com. The director of product management (back office) explained this competency: “The difference is that Alibaba uses the society's resources, using their supplier's resources and reputation, using the power of masses to promote their products and drive their operations; M.com, however, is self‐managed, with no borrowing of reputation or power.” This created additional synergies between each functional office to counter overall process inefficiencies, capturing intermediate sensing and response achievements that are reflected by increasing customer intimacy at the front end and operational efficiency at the back end.

Proposition 2. Towards operational agility, multisided platforms leverage IT to cultivate competence in the co‐ordination of sequential resource interdependencies and to realise synergistic operational capabilities.

Synergistic operational capabilities represent the firm's capacity to operate in an integrated manner such that intermediate resources are utilised to maximise throughput and to improve efficiency. These capabilities are enabled by a robust IT infrastructure that supports various system components quickly to react to changes and uncertainties found in sequential tasks, such as order processing. The sequential linkage between departments on order processing places greater emphasis on resources, targets, scheduling, monitoring, and feedback. At M.com, the decoupling of front‐end and back‐end activities and IT‐enabled routines fulfil that requirement, ultimately leading to improved delivery (Zomerdijk & De Vries, 2007) and an integrated business culture (Kappos & Rivard, 2008; Sawhney, Wolcott & Arroniz, 2007). This business integration means that back‐end operations have a stronger influence on firm performance than front‐end functionality does, which is consistent with resource‐based logics. However, specifying new schedules and routines requires the capacity for destabilising old sources of competitive advantage and dismantling outmoded capabilities and endowments (Merali et al., 2012). This co‐ordination between specialised and resourcedependent departments along the sequence (Gerdin, 2005; Macintosh & Daft, 1987) becomes crucial to provide relative certainty about tasks and highly standardised processes so that the decision‐making process is straightfor ward and the outcome fairly well predicted.

## 4.3 | Developing optimising capabilities for IT‐driven operational agility

## 4.3.1 | Reciprocal resource interdependence at M.com

At the time of data collection, M.com offered over 30 000 types of products grouped into different product lines. The Product Research and Development Department recruited research developers to develop customer products, including working on the source code, development framework, and error checking. The Product Management Department monitored the sales of the products, found innovative means to market the products, and repositioned goods into new product lines where necessary. The department also handled the recruitment of product managers. The director of product research and development pointed out that “[e]ach product line requires ten people to develop it, [so] the demand for the attention of our [Product] Research and Development Department and guys are very high, especially from the product managers and understandably so: they want the guys with the most experience and most knowledge of resources.” Product managers depended on developers in the Product Research and Development Department. The director of product management (back‐end operations) noted, “Product manager and product developers' ratio used to be 1:20, now its 1:10. We were very tight on people, as you can imagine. He [product manager] is in charge of the entire line of products and its innovation, too. He has to interview, investigate then establish a [product] programme in our department before resources can be allocated; before that happens, h will have no resources so he has to apply. Once [a] resource is allocated, he can then start the work; he can then form a team to research on the project.”

Hence, while product managers enlist product developers to develop a product line, reciprocally product developers who are responsible with maintaining a product line rely on product managers for this work. With the high quality and flexible product lines at M.com, uncertainty occurs—product managers and developers are faced with multiple and conflicting objectives in the pursuit of the objectives of their departments. Over the years, the rapid rise in the types of products and lines introduced, coupled with the dip in the ratio of product managers to product developers, meant there was growing uncertainty and scarcity of resources, particularly for product development This mutual requirement for work and resources (ie, product developers) made for a unique reciprocal and interde pendent relationship between product development and product management at M.com.

## 4.3.2 | The role of IT in managing reciprocal resource interdependence at M.com

To support the requirements of the reciprocal need for development resources between interdependent departments for product management, the role of a product manager is crucial in linking the Product Research and Development Department, Product Management Department, and other departments in the firm that developed a product line. The director of product research and development explains that it was impractical to have an outsid hire managing a product line and new hires had to undertake long periods of training to understand a product line, hence prompting a high dependence on product management and managers. The director of the Software Quality Assurance Department explained, “They [project managers] are the bridge between departments working together. They are most familiar with ROI, the length of [product] development process, potential conflicting resources, scheduling, and discussion with our sales departments help us determine which product line we should be focusin our efforts on, which is why everyone wants good product managers.”

The internal IT systems not only functions to capture the development and management of product lines but concomitantly monitors the development of potential product managers and their expertise. On one hand, obtaining competitive data about employees (the product managers) during department restructuring could not rely on the rules and standardisation purported in systems. Where resource uncertainty and scarcity surfaced around tasks with potentially high interdependence and competition, for example, the in‐demand product managers, the data supplied in system reports were accompanied by direct managerial involvement in face‐to‐face co‐ordination. In addition to social media as an open feedback channel, the director of the Product Management Department (back‐end operations) highlighted email as a mediating tool: “We also use emails to follow up. It's all mutual respect and communication.” Furthermore, the fair allocation of product managers to tasks within the firm also meant that potential conflicts or misunderstandings could be avoided since department personnel were already aware of one, another and their work according to the HR manager. On the other hand, increasing the volume and quality of information through IT mitigated uncertainty around reciprocal activities. IT functions to provide a combination of information on returns on investment, production schedules, and length of development process to help the product managers determine which product line they should be focusing their efforts on and ultimately where to allocate further resources.

Our findings conform to the view of Caglio and Ditillo (2012) and Tomkins (2001) that information and trust are both uncertainty‐absorbing mechanisms. Higher levels of trust are directly associated with an increase in information to enable mutual adjustment between highly interdependent departments. Daily meetings and other informal inter ventions, such as monthly employee gazettes that acknowledge the achievements of departments and employees create awareness and ultimately deepen trust It is noteworthy that some studies suggest that resource interdependence is not dependent on trust, and vice versa (Saidel, 1991). This is interesting, especially where relationship‐based commerce in China has been found to have important implications for the use of complementary IT applications (Martinsons, 2008), including social media platforms.

## 4.3.3 | Optimised competencies and capabilities for operational agility at M.com

In summary, the bundling of co‐opetition and IT‐enabled adjustments reveals how M.com develop the competence for managing reciprocal interdependencies found in product development tasks, for example. Although departments were working together to improve products, they were also working against each other, especially in terms of human resources (eg, product developers). As the director of product research and development observed, “Ultimately, dif ferent product managers tend to want to push for their product lines and safeguard their interests. There is going to be a conflict for resources when product managers claim the importance of their programmes, hence we evaluat which is more important and which programme should go first. It ties back to our 211 promise.”

Through actively promoting co‐operation and competition (without agreed contribution), M.com has developed the competencies and capabilities to offer more products and more product lines than any of its competitors and maintains cost leadership even while offering rebates to manufacturers. Furthermore, departments operate in a highly compatible manner, such that competitive resources are shared to create a convergent value that is greater than the individual. The director of platform architecture (research division) illustrated the emergent perception of research and development departments: “The research and development team is a train's engine. It [hooks] the other cars [departments] behind and drags them along. If the R&D department is the train's engine, the product department hence is the signals on the tracks. Before 2009, it seems that sales departments were leading the business but things have reversed, we are led by IT.” This leads us to our third proposition:

Proposition 3. Towards operational agility, multisided platforms leverage IT to cultivate competence in the co‐ordination of reciprocal resource interdependencies and to realise optimising operationa capabilities.

Optimising operational capabilities represent the firm's capacity to operate in a flexible manner to realise struc tural changes and to transform firm resources into business value quickly. These capabilities require enhanced IT functions to take better advantage of existing IT resources to exercise business strategies in order to react and adiust to changing business conditions. Based on our findings, when interdependence between business units becomes reciprocal, the information requirements begin to outstrip the data supplied by formal control systems, such as an ERP. The IT function is particularly important in situations where resources are scarce, as in the case of product development resources in M.com, the lack of resources usually dominates the interaction (Khoo & Robey, 2007) and creates uncertainty when there is recognition that common pool resources are finite (Hatcher, 2011; Larkin, 2011). Hence, IT systems primarily used for planning and scheduling give way to those suited for enabling communication, co‐operation, and adjustments, and employees have a strong incentive for using these newly developed sys tems at M.com for dividing and consuming resources quickly and fairly

The failure to co-ordinate reciprocal interdependencies is costly as it involves dealing with the conflicts that arise when departments compete to divide resources, including the effects of unequal distribution and envy (Bengtsson & Kock, 2000; Lehmann, 2001). Co‐opetition for resources (where departments compete for resources from a coopetition pool) may arise and cause departments working together to improve products to also work against each other. In the management literature, co‐opetition refers to the simultaneous occurrence of co‐operation (without agreed contribution) and competition. To preserve limited resources, previously competitive entities co‐operate to manage the scarce resources. Simply recognising that there is a common pool resource problem is insufficient for modifying behaviour. This process can occur only after extensive contact and the building of trust through deep relationships (Lim, Tan, Cyr, Pan, & Xiao, 2011). M.com illustrates this by the recruitment of departmental employees to move up to management roles rather than hiring new executives from outside the firm. Departments may also assume the role of mid‐offices that act as a bridge to integrate the disparate parts of a firm (Zomerdijk and de Vries, 2007). In our case firm (in the Product Management Department), although the creation of a mid‐office was unplanned, its role at a process level was nonetheless crucial for developing interdependencies between disparat offices. This complex form gives rise to a partially convergent goal structure (Dagnino & Padula, 2002), creating a new kind of optimised system for value creation.

In the visual summary of our investigation in Figure 1, we illustrate how the M.com platform cultivates ITenabled resource‐interdependent capabilities for operational agility. We postulate that towards IT‐driven operational agility, managers must first identify the extent of resource interdependencies between departments including pooled, sequential, or reciprocal during their completion of operational tasks. Our model reveals that consideration for and management of all interdependency types across firm processes are necessary for the subsistence of a complex e‐commerce multisided platform. Based on the resource interdependence that departments have with one another, managers will be able to estimate the cost of new processes and the implementation of IT‐enabled mechanisms, and hence the extent to which co‐ordination of resources must occur. The extent of resource interdependence determines the bundling of co‐ordination mechanisms and IT systems, allowing firms to better manage and leverage resource interdependencies. In our case study, the IS Department acts as a cornerstone to cope with the technology competence of the departments and provides support for system and process maturity (akin to Chen, Chen, & Yen, 2005), and at times manages the priorities among departments (Baranowski, Dun, & Varano, 1994). This involves the active participation of interdependent departments and higher‐level management to foster the openness, operational efficiency, and commitment to firm culture (Siegel, 2000) that is ultimately beneficial to the maintenance of relationships. Finally, the leveraging process involves the development o competencies and capabilities that emphasise efficiencies in sensing and response, and technological expertise at points along the value chain, ultimately achieving operational agility.

## 5 | RESEARCH IMPLICATIONS ON THEORY AND PRACTICE

Our investigation provides insights into how a leading multisided B2C platform realises operational agility and the role of IT. Our study observations and reflections contribute to theory and practice in the following ways

First, we purport incremental insights to the growing body of knowledge in IS on operational agility, We build on prior IS studies that insists operational agility and digital options are imperative for competitive action in a market place (eg, Sambamurthy et al., 2003). For theory, we found beyond the adoption of technology, there is scope for operational agility on a platform to be shaped through IT capability building. Furthermore, our findings address some what the question raised in prior literature if and how resources should be combined to effectively enhance opera tional agility; we focus on its process (as illustrated by Figure 1). We reveal that for a multisided platform, operationa agility requires the careful integration of IT and business resources into organisational competencies and capabilities. Specifically, we reveal how the multisided platform can leverage on IT to co‐ordinate resource interdependencies found in operational tasks and between business units. Subsequently, we suggest that multisided platforms that rec ognise the importance of bundling IT‐enabled co‐ordination and organisational mechanisms during firm operation are more likely to gain specific competencies and operational capabilities to the firm's advantage.

Second, we present an approach that address how types of resource interdependences are tied to operationa agility. Our approach suggests that resource interdependencies must not only be discussed as a concept embeddec in tasks but also be considered in each context, and there is no single best way to manage these interdependencies at an interfirm level. Building on the extant IS research particularly by Kumar and Van Dissel (1996), we purport the unique composition of interdependent units and IT capabilities (Rockart & Short, 2012) as co‐ordinating mechanisms for different forms of interdependencies in an interorganisational setting. Beyond patterns of tasks and treating uncertainty as a consequence of interdependence, there is scope for interdependency as a pattern of relationships in a given context, treating uncertainty with respect to the environment and resources as the antecedent and consequence of interdependence conflict. Our theoretical propositions add greater understanding to established conceptualizations of interdependencies (Staudenmayer, 1997; Thompson, 1967) by considering the implications of heterogeneity and how one type of interdependence relates to another in a complex organisation. We also address th somewhat lack of empirical studies (Gerdin, 2005) on the relationship between IT and interdepartmental interdepen dencies; our model enriches the body of knowledge in IS of IT use by demonstrating how an array of resource‐base interdependencies are enacted and developed through IT.

Third, because our study reveals how a leading multisided platform realises operational agility in a competitive e‐business environment, we add to the body of knowledge in IS on multisided platform function and strategies Reflecting on our investigations, we purport that platforms provide opportunities to investigate the means of governing platforms and the tensions that such means can create, Our approach is IS oriented and underscores the important interactions between a multisided platform, IT artefacts and their affordances, and the platform's internal and external environments, which is an aspect largely absent in the body of platform literature. Our approach responds to calls for further IS research endeavours on platforms (Agarwal, Gupta, & Kraut, 2008; Tiwana et al., 2010) and addresses how the resource interdependencies present in a B2C platform are tied to the sensing and responding capabilities required to achieve agility. Moreover, this study adds to a handful of examples (see Zhu & Kraemer, 2005) that adds incremental insights on how interdependent departments in an e‐business firm interact and identifies the inherent complex sociotechnical issues (Chu & Smithson, 2007 Treiblmaier & Strebinger, 2008).

Fourth, our research on the IS‐enabled operational agility of platforms offer platform owners and managers practical insights into organisational settings and operational strategies. We reveal that beyond paying attention to the depart ments' service delivery (eg, Cenfetelli, Benbasat, & Al‐Natour, 2008; Éthier, Hadaya, Talbot, & Cadieux, 2006) and (product and service) design implications (eg, Zhu, Benbasat, & Jiang, 2010) in e‐business transformation, managers need to be aware that the process of attaining operational agility in an increasingly competitive digital marketplace begins with the resource‐based actions of individual constituent members of the platform. We build on e‐business studies that focus on firm development and deployment issues (Phan, 2003), firm assimilation, and e‐commerce technologies (Chatterjee, Grewal, & Sambamurthy, 2002) to address the question raised in prior literature of the strategies needed for technolog enabled innovation (Hackbarth & Kettinger, 2004) necessary for operational agility and firm survival

## 6 | CONCLUSIONS

In closing, competition in the marketplace invigorates the transformation firms require for operational agility. As operations become increasingly complex and interconnected, it is important to develop models to account for the competencies and capabilities these businesses must acquire to leverage resources. Existing literature suggests that operational agility on platforms and complex networked businesses requires IT‐enabled capabilities and the effective management of interdependencies. Using resource interdependencies logic, this study reveals that while collaboration between business units for interdependent reporting, processing, and design tasks complicates the process of internal business integration, it is necessary for operational agility. Our premise is that systems integration itsel may not always be sufficient for business integration, especially if the systems integration was overinvested or wrong. The results of this study confirm that the effective management of interdependencies enables firms to increase their capacity to respond quickly and effectively to market forces. to improve the quality of conformance to customer requirements, and to reduce costs.

This study is not without its limitations. First, a common criticism of case study research approach is the problem of transferability or generalizability (Walsham, 2006). While we acknowledge that our findings are drawn from a single case study of M.com, we contend that our findings on IT‐driven capabilities for operational agility are nevertheless generalizable beyond the current context, and they are corroborated by, and built on, the findings of other studies in th literature, A second limitation of this study concerns the dependability (Trochim. 2006) of our interview data, given the general inability of any researcher to account fully for the dynamic context within which research occurs. We were restricted in who we could speak to, and as such, our interview data may be susceptible to bias and errors of recall. Future case studies can reinforce existing findings and reveal perspectives that may have been missed

Nonetheless, our study makes several practical contributions from observations and reflections from practice Our research establishes a platform for a systematic programme of research to crystallise operational agility as an organisational capability for platforms. Our findings lead the way for further research into mechanisms and consequences of IT‐enabled operational agility, as well as research on structural synergies and antagonism between the departments of a firm in resource‐interdependent relationships
