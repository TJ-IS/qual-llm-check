# Boundary Organization Practices for Collaboration in Enterprise Integration

<sub>Abstract.</sub> Multiparty collaboration is particularly challenging in large-scale enterprise integration (EI) implementations as diverse organizational subunits and external consultants need to work together to enact transformational change. We argue from prior research that a boundary organization—a formal organizational space that enables participants with divergent interests to collaborate—provides a relatively durable structure to manage emergent tensions and conflicts among stakeholders in EI projects. To better understand how a boundary organization enables ongoing, dynamic, multiparty collaboration, we introduce the concept of boundary organization practices to describe practices enacted as part of the boundary organization to define the working rules and arrangements for diverse stakeholders to work together. We conducted a longitudinal exploratory case study of a multiyear EI implementation in a large logistics organization. Our analysis of the longitudinal data led us to identify three sets of boundary organization practices (i.e., organizing to negotiate, to contain, and to sustain). These sets of practices emerged to address the divergence that arose in each of the three phases (designing, realizing, and leveraging) of EI implementation. We also find artifacts to be an integral part of the boundary organization practices as they motivated, coordinated, and enabled collaboration among diverse stakeholders.

## Introduction

The core of any enterprise integration (EI) implementation is the transformation of a traditional, siloed, functional organization into an integrated enterprise. Such EI implementations have large project teams made up of members from diverse business units and external consultants (Lee et al. 2003, Markus 2004, Mørk et al. 2012, Sandoe and Saharia 1999). These team members need to work together on joint tasks, combining their expertise and domain knowledge; in other words, there has to be efective multiparty collaboration in EI projects (Levina 2005, Sarker and Sahay 2003).

However, there are characteristics of EI projects that make multiparty collaboration very challenging. First, EI projects are often transformational, requiring standardization of common processes and radical organizational change. These transformations involve not only technical but also operational and strategic aspects of the organization (Markus 2004, Sia and Yeo 2009). Second, since the goal is to integrate heterogeneous business units that have diferent knowledge domains, practices, cultures, resources, and power (Davidson 2002, Wagner and Newell 2004), the project team has to deal with the multiple existing boundaries among their team members. These boundaries pose severe challenges to efective collaboration by inhibiting the flow of information, sharing of knowledge, and coordination of tasks, which in turn exacerbates the diferences in interests, views, and agendas (Bechky 2003b, Carlile 2004, Levina and Orlikowski 2009, Pawlowski and Robey 2004). Third, the scale of EI implementations is relatively extensive (Lee et al. 2003, Soh et al. 2000). EI projects typically involve multiple subprojects that require an extended period of time to complete, and often span multiple geographical locations (Elbanna 2010). Project teams have to coordinate their work across subprojects and across time to achieve the overarching project goals (Gkeredakis 2014). Finally, the centrality of a highly complex artifact in EI also adds to the coordination challenges. Enterprise systems are a critical part of EI projects and much collaboration work revolves around developing and implementing highly complex enterprise systems (Sia and Soh 2007, Volkof et al. 2007).

Organizational and information systems (IS) studies have examined boundary spanning practices and shown that while they are necessary for multiparty collaboration, they are not suficient for efective collaboration when there are contextual issues such as intergroup conflicts (Barki and Hartwick 1994, Wagner and Newell 2004, Yeow and Sia 2008) or incompatible motivations (Azad and Faraj 2008, Yeow and Sia 2008). Several studies have suggested that boundary spanning practices could be more efective if they were better organized (Carlile 2004, Levina and Vaast 2014). Given the nature of EI projects, the boundary organization concept, i.e., a formal organizational space that enables members with divergent interests to collaborate—provides a useful perspective as it sensitizes us to the organizing of boundary spanning work across diferent parts of an organization. Because the boundary organization has access to formal authority and critical resources, it provides a relatively durable structure to manage inherent diferences among stakeholders and emergent tensions and conflicts in EI projects. While existing research on boundary organizations tends to emphasize their structural aspects such as governance and membership structures that enable cooptation of diferent stakeholders (Guston 2001, O’Mahony and Bechky 2008), research on boundary spanning has found that situated practices enacted by the project team are important to boundary spanning and are shaped by these governance and membership structures. To better understand how a boundary organization enables multiparty collaboration, we draw from the practice perspective on boundary spanning, and seek to understand the diferent practices enacted within the boundary organization (Levina 2005; Levina and Vaast 2005, 2014). We introduce the <sup>concept</sup> <sup>of</sup> boundary organization practices <sup>to</sup> <sup>describe</sup> practices enacted within a boundary organization to define the rules and arrangements for diverse stakeholders to work together. As such, our research question is: What boundary organization practices are created and how do they support multiparty collaboration in an EI project?

To address this question, we conducted a longitudinal exploratory case study of a multiyear EI implementation in a large logistics organization. The implementation involved heterogeneous business units and consultants and had significant challenges to collaboration. The organization created a core project team that served as the boundary organization. Our analysis of the longitudinal data led us to identify three sets of boundary organization practices that enabled multiparty collaboration in EI. These sets of practices emerged over time and addressed the separate divergences that arose in each of the three phases (designing, realizing, and leveraging) of EI implementation. We also find artifacts to be an integral part of the boundary organization practices as they motivated, coordinated, and enabled collaboration among diverse stakeholders. The next section presents existing research on multiparty collaboration in EI projects through boundary spanning, and also discusses how the boundary organization perspective and boundary organization practices may be useful in the complex collaborative context of EI.

## Multiparty Collaboration in EI Projects

EI projects are large-scale eforts in constituting “distinct and interdependent enterprise components as a unified whole” (Barki and Pinsonneault 2005, p. 166). The goal is to integrate processes and systems across disparate units within an enterprise, usually through an enterprise system (Markus and Tanis 2000, Sandoe and Saharia 1999). Prior research often focuses on the negotiated outcomes of EI in achieving a workable enterprise system, i.e., through customization, workarounds, and loose coupling of work processes (e.g., Berente and Yoo 2012, Soh et al. 2000, Wagner and Newell 2004). Research also highlights the power struggles, conflicts, and tensions among multiple stakeholders (Lee et al. 2003, Soh et al. 2000). For example, they show how users engage in active resistance (Lapointe and Rivard 2005, Volkof et al. 2005), adopt workarounds (Boudreau and Robey 2005, Sia and Soh 2007), or enact strategic loose coupling between work practices and the system (Berente and Yoo 2012). Others have also shed light on ways by which these separate groups negotiate their diferences, using various social (e.g., reciprocity), discursive (e.g., framing), and political actions to enroll each other and develop a shared understanding of the system (Wagner and Newell 2006, Yeow and Sia 2008).

While these insights are important to our understanding of the emergent process of negotiations among groups, they tend to put less emphasis on the ongoing collaborative work, and take for granted the boundaries crisscrossing EI projects. In EI projects, there is not just one predefined boundary, but multiple boundaries including hierarchical/functional, geographical/ spatial, temporal, discipline/vocational, social, cultural, and epistemic boundaries (Carlile 2002, Espinosa et al. 2003, Levina and Vaast 2005). These boundaries pose severe challenges to collaboration by inhibiting information sharing and task coordination (Bechky 2003b, Carlile 2004, Levina 2005).

As such, we turn to boundary spanning research to understand multiparty collaboration in EI projects (Faraj and Xiao 2006, Gkeredakis 2014, Kellogg et al. 2006, Majchrzak et al. 2012). Drawing on the practice perspective (Feldman and Orlikowski 2011), boundary spanning research has examined the situated boundary spanning practices used to overcome the challenges of internal boundaries during multiparty collaboration. These situated work practices enacted by boundary spanners help to overcome knowledge and power diferences across disparate groups (Bechky 2003b, Carlile 2002). They are supported by various workplace artifacts such as information technology (IT) systems (Pawlowski and Robey 2004) and engineering drawings (Bechky 2003a). These practices and artifacts help to coordinate tasks and mediate knowledge exchange among the groups (Carlile 2002, Nicolini et al. 2012).

IS research has also shown that diferent boundary spanning practices help to relate diferent stakeholders across boundaries and enable efective collaboration (Levina 2005; Levina and Vaast 2008, 2014) Extant research has focused on examining various aspects of collaborative boundary spanning work. For example, one approach is to focus on the background and motivation of boundary spanners, as those appointed to be boundary spanners do not always carry out their responsibilities, while others may assume such roles informally (Levina and Vaast 2005). Some studies look at the diferences and conflicts across boundaries and how informal practices as well as boundary objects emerge to negotiate these challenges and bring about efective collaboration (Carlile 2004, Levina 2005, Majchrzak et al. 2012, Star and Griesemer 1989). Other studies examine the issue of power and the institutional field’s influence on the boundary spanning work (Levina and Vaast 2008, 2014). Depending on the context of collaboration and actions taken by the boundary spanners, collaboration may occur either through practices in existing shared fields of practice or through new joint fields of practice that emerge as local practices and power relations are changed (Levina and Vaast 2005, 2006, 2014).

While these approaches to examining organizing boundary spanning practices addresses the role of boundary spanners, boundary objects, power relations, and influence of institutional fields, most of these studies focus on emergent practices in localized collaborative contexts (Carlile 2004, Kellogg et al. 2006, Levina and Vaast 2005). These emergent practices address very specific “local” collaboration challenges, but may not scale beyond the specific situation. For larger enterprise-wide projects (e.g., an EI project), which require a high degree of collaboration among many groups that remain distinct throughout collaboration efort, emergent boundary spanning practices that are specific to localized, rather than an enterprise-wide context may not be efective. Moreover, some studies have hinted that formal organizing of the collaboration context may directly and indirectly influence boundary spanning practices, for example, through the assignment of formal organizational roles (Levina 2005, Levina and Vaast 2005). This approach of examining formal ways of organizing for collaboration has not been a focus in prior research on boundary spanning. A helpful theoretical perspective that allows us to focus on formal processes of organizing the collaboration context to enable multiparty collaboration in EI projects is the boundary organization perspective.

## Boundary Organization Practices

The boundary organization perspective is used by organizational researchers and sociologists to understand how diferent organizations from divergent worlds collaborate (Guston 1999, O’Mahony and Bechky 2008). This perspective is typically studied in the interorganizational context. An example would be the American National Institute of Health’s Ofice of Technology Transfer. The ofice serves as a boundary organization between the U.S. government and the scientific community. Guston (1999) defined boundary organizations as a formal organizational space that makes multiparty collaboration possible by enrolling participants from diferent groups while remaining accountable to each of these groups. Likewise, O’Mahony and Bechky (2008) emphasize the role of boundary organizations as a collaboration platform for stakeholders who are from diverse worlds and have competing interests. The uniqueness of this concept is the facilitation of multiparty collaboration through a formal organizational form.

Specifically, a boundary organization is a formal structure that operates in parallel to the existing stakeholder groups to facilitate efective collaboration. It has a degree of formal authority as the participating organizations agree to delegate authority to the boundary organization (Guston 2001). A boundary organization thus has the mandate and resources from the participating organizations to incentivize boundary spanners to collaborate with one another. Within the boundary organization, participants can confront and articulate their interests and understand how their interests converge while being mindful of their divergent interests (O’Mahony and Bechky 2008). O’Mahony and Bechky’s (2008) study of open source community projects shows how boundary organizations are formally structured through membership (project representation and control rules), governance (rights of members and contributors), ownership (agreements on code ownership), and controls (code production control and contribution). Through its formal processes and structures, boundary organizations provide a durable structure to sustain collaboration among stakeholders.

The concept of boundary organizations is applicable to and useful for understanding EI projects. While boundary organizations have been studied in the interorganizational context, the essence of the boundary organization is about organizing collaboration between stakeholders from divergent worlds. This essence is reflected in a large-scale EI project since EI project participants are from autonomous business units with unique cultures, practices, and goals within a large organization. Thus, apart from some diferences in terms of the nature of authority and control (an interorganizational boundary organization’s authority is delegated by the participants, but an intraorganizational boundary organization’s authoritative control is based on top management mandate), a boundary organization perspective can be used to understand the dynamics in collaboration within the intraorganizational context. In fact, the boundary organization perspective helps illuminate aspects in EI that extant boundary spanning practice and traditional project management research have neglected.

First, the boundary organization perspective helps point to the formal context in which boundary spanning practices and boundary spanners are situated. In EI, we can take this wider perspective to understand the formal project team as the initial boundary organization. The boundary organization perspective enables us to take into account how boundary spanning practices are formally organized and enacted across the entire organization. At the same time, because a boundary organization is not an ad hoc, provisional space but a formally organized structure in parallel to existing stakeholder groups, it serves to manage boundary spanning work across space and time (Levina and Vaast 2014). Specifically, if the boundary organization is formally created, it is more likely to persist throughout the duration of an EI project and addresses the challenges of project participant turnover and changing commitment. Finally, applying the boundary organization perspective in the intraorganizational context highlights that access to formal authority and critical resources is instrumental in addressing issues of cross-boundary power struggles and tensions inherent in EI projects.

However, the few boundary organization studies have mainly investigated the boundary organization’s structural aspects but do not directly theorize on how participants dynamically create and develop practices within the boundary organization to remove obstacles or demarcate interests that impede collaboration. In O’Mahony and Bechky’s (2008) study, while they discuss how the participating stakeholders adapted organizing practices to work together through the boundary organization, they only highlighted the structural aspects of boundary organizations (e.g., pluralistic membership, shared ownership, and control) and did not discuss specific practices within the boundary organization. While the extant literature informs us that participants have to adopt adaptive, provisional approaches to achieve efective collaboration (Kellogg et al. 2006, Majchrzak et al. 2012), there is little understanding about what and how participant actions and practices are enacted within the boundary organization to engender collaboration in a large and highly complex context.

Drawing on the practice perspective on boundary spanning, we seek to understand the diferent situated practices enacted within the boundary organization. We introduce the concept of <sub>boundary</sub> <sub>organization</sub> <sub>practices</sub> to describe practices enacted within boundary organizations to define the working rules and arrangements for boundary spanners to collaborate efectively. As such, we understand boundary organization practice as a type of boundary spanning practice enacted within a formal boundary organization that helps us analyze how boundary spanners and their practices are structured, managed, and formally organized across these boundaries.

Thus, in our longitudinal study of an EI project, we carefully trace how members of the EI team enact different boundary organization practices, and how these shape boundary spanning work and influence the overall multiparty collaboration within EI.

## Methodology

## Research Design and Context

We applied the boundary organization perspective to study a multiyear EI project in a large Asian logistics organization. The case had several attributes that made it suitable for this study. First, the EI project had multiple boundaries as it involved several corporate units and traditionally autonomous business units. Second, there was evidence of efective collaboration among these business units as the EI project was considered to have met both the enterprise integration objectives, as well as local business unit needs. Third, we were able to obtain a good level of access to the EI process from inception to completion, which allowed us to conduct a longitudinal study of this exemplar case.

The large Asian logistics organization, which we call “Trilog,” employs over 10,000 employees and is over 40 years old. It is organized in a multidivisional form, with headquarters providing strategic direction and corporate support functions, and three relatively autonomous business units—Sea, Land, and Air Logistics. Each business unit had developed a separate set of business practices and operating culture given its particular business demands. The Land business unit was the largest in terms of staf, assets, and the number of subunits. The Land business unit staf were less educated and turned over more frequently, thus, this business unit’s culture was more people-control oriented. The Air and Sea business units were smaller in terms of staf and assets, and their operations were concentrated in fewer locations. Their operations were more high-tech and their staf is more technology proficient. The Air business unit culture emphasized safety and adherence to stringent airworthiness standards. They also operated at a much faster operating tempo. For example, a flight would last less than 24 hours. The Sea business unit, on the other hand, upheld a professional culture focused on coordination through close tracking of processes and resources as most sea operations occurred ofshore for longer periods of time. Corporate Logistics first put forward the EI initiative in 2000, and after several rounds of proposals, funding approval was obtained in 2002. The project kicked of in 2003, and was finally completed in 2008.

## Data Collection

We were observers of the EI project as it unfolded, being asked by the organization to develop a series of case studies to document their transformation journey, for use in their internal management training. We collected data from several sources: project documentation, interviews, organizational events, and project meetings for stronger triangulation (Yin 2003). Project documentation comprised project proposals, minutes of meetings, presentation slides, organizational charts, budgets, schedules, project management plans, and periodic progress review reports. Figure 1 presents the timeline of key events.

Two or three researchers visited the site once every six to eight weeks throughout most of the EI implementation (January 2003 to August 2008) to conduct interviews. Besides the Corporate management group (Logistics, IT, and Finance), Sea, Land, and Air business units, we also interviewed other actors (e.g., vendors) to triangulate our findings. A total of 169 interviews were conducted—see Table 1 for a summary of interviewee profiles. Before each visit, researchers reviewed project minutes and identified people to interview as well as issues to explore during interviews. The interviews were with senior and key middle management involved in the EI implementation. The interviews focused on the roles of the Project Team members in EI, the implementation arrangements they put in place, the challenges they encountered across phases, the actions they undertook, and the related EI outcomes. Most interviews lasted at least an hour and were transcribed within 24 hours.

## Data Analysis

We first developed our understanding of the overall sequence of events and actions, key conditions, and outcomes by writing several case studies as part of our deliverables to corporate management—one for each phase of the EI project. The reports ranged from 25 to 76 pages long and were verified by various individuals in the organization. Their feedback and subsequent clarifications increased our confidence in the accuracy of the reports (Golden-Biddle and Locke 1993). The collection of case studies formed the in-depth narrative of the EI project.

Next, we used an iterative approach of “travelling back and forth between the data, pertinent literature, and emerging theory” (Elsbach and Sutton 1992, p. 705). The boundary organization perspective was not our original lens for our study, but it emerged as our theoretical framework after we found it had strong empirical relevance with the data (Berente and Yoo 2012). Once we adopted the boundary organization perspective, the next step in our analysis involved identifying the focal analytical unit, i.e., boundary organization practice. From our case study data, we focused on the EI team and systematically traced and unpacked actions they undertook to define the working rules and arrangements for boundary spanners. We based our initial coding on the O’Mahony and Bechky (2008) study that identifies various actions undertaken by the open source foundation participants to change the structure of membership, ownership, and governance to enable collaboration. Using those actions as a guide, we coded the first two boundary organization practices, i.e., composing representation and coordinating work. As we coded more project team actions, we quickly realized that the other boundary organization practices were very diferent from this initial set and we proceeded to code in an open, iterative fashion, focusing on coding emergent boundary organization practices, their interaction with artifacts, and their impact on boundary spanning and collaboration outcomes (Miles et al. 2014). For each boundary organization practice, we coded the actions related to the practice (e.g., for aligning incentives practice we coded the actions of assigning responsibility of scenario design to specific project members) and traced how they shaped the collaboration outcomes. As we coded the actions, we also realized that artifacts played critical roles within each practice. Drawing on the extant literature on artifacts, we coded two types of artifacts based on their roles—boundary object (Star and Griesemer 1989) and primary object of collaboration (Nicolini et al. 2012). These concepts are elaborated within the Discussion section. Through this process, we surfaced a total of 11 boundary organization practices and their attending artifacts (see Table 2).

We then reviewed the collaboration challenges in view of the convergent and divergent interests and their relationship with the boundary organization practices and artifacts. We compared across the different boundary organization practices and reflected on the relationships among practices and the conditions surrounding the practices (Miles et al. 2014). At this point, we noted several key changes in the project context that led to divergence, which challenged the ongoing multiparty collaboration. We surfaced three key phases of the EI project based on these changes— designing, realizing, and leveraging. Within each of these EI project phases, we analyzed how diferent boundary organization practices emerged to deal with those changes. The boundary organization practices complemented each other in dealing with specific collaboration challenges within each phase. For example, the five boundary organization practices in the designing phase addressed the divergence because of diverse interests, enabling negotiation and developing convergent interests among the business units. Based on this analysis, we grouped the boundary organization practices into three sets: organizing to negotiate, organizing to contain, and organizing to sustain. ## Findings

## Background of Boundary Organization—Trilog EI Team

Trilog sought to enhance its competitive position by ofering an eficient, seamless, and responsive logistics service across its Land, Sea, and Air business units. A first step was the creation of a Corporate Logistics department in 2000. Corporate Logistics was given control of funding resources for systems and infrastructure, and was tasked with the mandate to drive the implementation of the strategic vision. To that end, Corporate Logistics proposed an EI project.

As Corporate Logistics iteratively refined the proposal, it found natural partners in Corporate IT and Corporate Finance. These two corporate departments were important as the EI transformation would require replacing the current legacy logistics systems in all of the business units, and integration with finance systems and processes for approval, payment, control, and analysis. While these corporate departments came from diferent functional domains, they found common ground in that standardized and integrated processes and systems would enable them to operate more efectively. For example, Corporate IT would have fewer legacy systems to manage and Corporate Finance saw the benefits of stronger compliance and easier access to data across the enterprise.

In the following sections, we present the findings of the challenges, boundary organization practices, and impact on collaboration outcomes across the three phases of designing, realizing, and leveraging as they unfolded in Trilog’s EI journey (see Table 2).

## Designing Phase

Corporate Logistics found it challenging to engage the Sea, Land, and Air business units in the EI project. The three business units had traditionally operated autonomously. Each business unit focused primarily on improving its own operational eficiency, and believed that they were the specialists in their particular logistic domain. Thus, while they were all part of Trilog, they had not seen the need to understand the other business units. The operating mindsets among the diverse stakeholders were also diferent. The Air business unit had a profession-centric orientation (with largely full-time and more qualified specialists) while the Land business unit was more control oriented, bureaucratic, and procedural (given that it had to manage a much larger, less qualified, and distributed network of employees). Over the years, each business unit had evolved their own systems and processes, and very specific domain knowledge and practices. Boundary Organization Practices. <sup>To</sup> <sup>address</sup> <sup>these</sup> challenges, Corporate Logistics and IT (with the mandate from Trilog’s management) initiated the development of a boundary organization to facilitate collaboration among the diverse stakeholders. The boundary organization took the form of three project committees, reflecting the diferent levels of management. At the highest level was the Enterprise Steering Committee, which was chaired by the Chief Executive Oficer (CEO) and included the heads of the business units, Corporate Logistics, IT, and Finance. The Global Project Management Team, which reported to the Enterprise Steering Committee, was cochaired by the heads of Corporate Logistics and IT, and included the deputy heads from the business units and the Corporate Logistics, IT, and Finance departments. Finally, the Project Team consisting of representatives from each of the business units, Logistics, Finance, and IT reported to the Global Project Management Team. The Project Team was organized along functional tracks (i.e., supply, engineering, and finance).

The composition of these three central committees was carefully designed to ensure representation not only at all levels but also from all key stakeholder groups. There was also some overlap in membership across the three committees to strengthen coordination across levels. For example, the head of Corporate Logistics was in both the Enterprise Steering Committee and the Global Project Management Team. The deputy heads of Corporate Logistics and Corporate IT were in both the Global Project Management Team and Project Team.

These committees had distinct roles. The Enterprise Steering Committee provided high-level oversight— shaping the strategic direction, scope modification, and resource allocation decisions. The Global Project Management Team oversaw the EI implementation. Finally, the Project Team ran the project on a dayto-day basis. The structure ensured that while Corporate Logistics and IT drove the EI implementation, there was still room for joint decision making on major issues. In cases where there were disagreements, the issues would be escalated up to the next level committee for collective resolution.

The members in each committee also had diferent roles. For example, on the Project Team, the business units’ operations managers and key users were to provide their respective business requirements. Corporate Logistics was to drive cross-business unit synergies. IT was to deal with the technical feasibility of EI and liaise between the business units and IT consultants to derive the enterprise systems solutions. Finance ensured that their requirements for payment transactions, control, and auditability were designed into the enterprise solutions. While each had its own role, the CEO and the head of Corporate Logistics were careful to reiterate that EI was a collective efort to achieve enterprise synergies.

The discussions in the Enterprise Steering Committee and the Global Project Management Team at the start of the project led to the adoption of a particular vendor package as the enabling technology platform for EI. While Corporate Logistics and IT initially wanted a high degree of data and process standardization, the target was set to at least 60% standardization, after taking into consideration the business units’ concerns about the EI transformation given their diferent operating environments.

<sub>Challenge.</sub> With these broad agreements in place, the Project Team began its work. The consultants and fulltime business unit user representatives began articulating requirements and creating a common design for logistic processes across the three business units. Within six weeks, it became clear that there were problems with collaboration within the Project Team. Within each functional track, business unit user representatives and consultants were often unable to agree on a common design.

How do cross-business unit diferences get surfaced? . . . usual way is for consultant to say—this is how we do it, . . . try and map your processes to it—then business units will say how they are diferent. When the consultant tries to understand one business unit, the rest feel bored. (Consultant—project manager)

The business units’ user representatives perceived their role narrowly, i.e., to provide requirements to the consultant, and expected the consultant to come up with the new process design. They were focused on having a system that met their needs, and failed to see their role in facilitating the development of a common process design that would meet the requirements of all business units. Lacking in-depth knowledge of user processes, the consultants were unable to reconcile the diverse operational requirements. Each time the consultants presented a new process design, the business unit user representatives would criticize where the design failed to meet their business unit’s requirements.

The business units’ entrenched silo mindset remained an obstacle to their collaboration. In other instances, some process designs that needed to be linked to other process designs were found unconnected. This was because the functional tracks in the Project Team were not talking to each other.

Integration is not very good. . . tracks will put boxes where they need a link with another track and assume the other track knows. (Sea business unit representative)

New Boundary Organization Practices. <sup>The</sup> <sup>Project</sup> Team leaders, with the support of the Global Project Management Team, began adapting the boundary organization to deal with these challenges. They realized that while the initial boundary organization practice ensured equitable representation, it did not address the issue of collective ownership of the operational processes and the need for arbitration within the Project Team. In fact, the definition of members’ roles and the initial assignment of work reinforced the business units’ silo mindset and operational fragmentation. The responsibility for joint process design was “outsourced” to the consultants who were left to reconcile the diverse interests. There was insuficient collective ownership to build common processes for EI.

The Project Team leaders first redesigned the subprocesses within the functional tracks as end-to-end scenarios that cut across the tracks. Each scenario was then assigned to a specific business unit user representative and an IT consultant as joint scenario owners. Each pair was responsible for negotiating with representatives from all of the groups afected by the scenario, and getting their agreement on the process design. The new boundary organization practice encouraged the business unit representatives to shift from their silo mindset to having to consider other business units’ interests for a specific process scenario.

Moreover, the final process scenario had to be signedof by all business unit representatives to constitute the global design for Trilog’s EI. This ensured that all business units continued to have influence over the design process.

We restructured whole project; clear responsibilities; smaller teams. About 120 scenarios, each with 2 owners-1 user, 1 consultant <sub>.</sub> <sub>.</sub> <sub>.</sub> now, clear who’s in charge; good thing because process owners were afraid earlier, and stepped back; process owners get clear mandate, support and instructions that they are to do this.

(Project Team director)

To further incentivize scenario owners to negotiate with other business unit representatives, a “blue-pink” peer voting rule was introduced for all of the scenario teams. This peer-voting rule specified that when two or more business units agreed on a process design that process was coded “blue.” When only one business unit insisted on a specific process, these would be coded as “pink,” i.e., a nonstandard process. For pink processes, the cost of customizing would be fully borne by that business unit. The blue-pink peer voting rule was reinforced with the creation of the role of executive owners who each had oversight of a cluster of scenarios. The executive owners were four senior Corporate Logistics and IT executives. They were given the authority to make the final decision if the business unit user representatives could not reconcile the divergent interests at their level.

The leaders of the Project Team also acted to build a common identity among Project Team members, through a set of formal and informal practices. Formally, they mandated the use of a common process modeling tool across all of the process scenario teams. Standardizing the production of these process maps helped to facilitate the mutual understanding of tasks performed across the enterprise. It also ensured consistent documentation of the final global design document and eased their integration with other processes. Informally, they created opportunities for social interaction among the representatives from the various stakeholder groups. “Brown bag” sessions where team members could come together to share their project issues were held. The issues were noted and followed up by the Project Team leaders, and resolutions and progress were reported back to the group. Other social activities such as karaoke and soccer were also organized on a regular basis.

The karaoke at [corporate ofice] and clubhouses [helped] build trust and relationships. After karaoke, [we will] do some problem solving. (Corporate Logistics deputy head)

Collaboration Outcomes. <sup>These</sup> <sup>adaptations</sup> <sup>to</sup> <sup>the</sup> boundary organization practices (see Table 3 for a summary) created a more conducive EI context that facilitated collaboration among the diverse stakeholders. Each user representative and consultant pair now began to work with each other on their assigned cross-track scenario to create the initial design. The user representative no longer just raised objections when the design did not meet the business units’ needs, but would now reach out to user representatives from the other business units and corporate functions to get their inputs on the emerging scenario design. It started becoming common to see a user representative sitting with their consultant, as they iteratively designed their scenario, taking into consideration the inputs they collected from other stakeholders. People began working later as their sense of ownership and motivation rose.

People are working till 8 or 9 p.m. I had to ask for the [daily] server backup to be delayed to midnight [so that teams could work uninterrupted on their designs]. (Corporate Logistics deputy head)

The blue-pink peer voting also facilitated interactions among the user representatives of various scenarios. User representatives now sought to find a common ground as they knew that if they did not agree with the design, then they would have to pay for the cost of customization. As their interactions grew, a sense of reciprocity was developed, where business unit user A would agree to business unit user B’s proposed scenario design, and vice versa.

Before June, people were shouting, unhappy with each other; now, (after the changes were made), more professional and things went very well. There were questions but very professional ones. (Project Team director)

When an agreement could not be reached via interactions at the user representative level, the scenarios were escalated to the executive owners who weighed the various business units’ points of view and made a judgment call. By and large, the interactions among user representatives and consultants worked well, and very few scenarios had to be escalated to the executive owners.

When the business unit user representatives converged on each scenario, it was then signed of by all key stakeholders and became part of the global design document. Each scenario that was signed of was an indication of progress toward the goal of common processes and systems. The global design document was the key motivating artifact, which eventually contained over 120 cross-functional scenarios.

The outcome of these scenario level negotiations was a global design that greatly exceeded the original 60% target for common processes. Indeed, the percentage of common processes for supply management was more than 90%, for engineering management, more than 80%, and for finance, close to 100%. Each business unit only had a handful of local customized scenarios.

## Realizing Phase

With the global design document completed, the next step was to implement the EI design sequentially in the Sea, Land, and Air business units over three years (see Figure 1 for the timeline).

<sub>Challenge.</sub> While the global design document reflected the agreements that arose from a multitude of design negotiations among stakeholders, these agreed designs were still relatively high level. Next, the business units together with the vendors and Project Team had to work on translating those designs into specific configurations of systems and processes—down to the level of deciding the configuration of screen menus. As the user representatives worked on the actual implementation, they became more aware of the extent of changes they would have to make to their day-to-day operations. Each business unit began submitting separate change requests. The number of change requests ballooned. Up to this point, the focus had been on getting convergence on the global design and not on monitoring the change requests vis-à-vis the “development bucket” budget.

As systems implementation moved forward, it became evident that the required efort to accommodate the change requests far exceeded the budgeted development bucket. At one point, the change requests were estimated to require 16,000 man-days, greatly exceeding the original development bucket of 9,500 mandays. Not only was this a cost issue, it also had significant implications in terms of project delays.

. . . For a long time, the vendors said there was no problem, the number of man-days required was ‘still within the bucket.’ Then suddenly, they increased the mandays. . . required! [We] raised some questions, [like] have they been monitoring? [It was a] tense time. Also, vendors wanted to reduce the number of bucketed mandays to less than that allocated [because they] needed some man-days for management overhead. However, users didn’t want to compromise on new requirements because that would mean losing features. (Senior corporate Logistics manager)

The Project Team saw an urgent need to bring down the change requests both from a budget and scheduling standpoint. However, each business unit insisted on pushing through its change requests, demonstrating the underlying tension between the business units’ self-interest of operational eficiency and Corporate Logistics’ interest in developing an integrated and standardized system.

New Boundary Organization Practices. <sup>In</sup> <sup>light</sup> <sup>of</sup> these new challenges, the Project Team made several adaptations to the boundary organization’s practices, to minimize business unit-specific “biases” in the system configuration and reduce the number of change requests (see Table 4).

The Project Team sought clearer visibility about the size of the problem by appointing an independent external consultant to audit user change requests and IT consultants’ man-day estimates for these change requests. The Project Team then developed and implemented a set of rules for the prioritization of change requests. The business units had to work with Corporate Logistics and IT to prioritize all their change requests by categorizing them as follows: “showstoppers” that would seriously impede the system’s functioning, “good-to-haves,” which increased eficiency or efectiveness but were less mission critical, and “nice-to-haves,” which were usually for improving ease of use.

The global design document played an important role in enhancing accountability through these new boundary organization practices. Since all parties had signed of on it, the global design document from the first phase served as a benchmark against which changes could be compared and thereby reflected the extent of deviation each change request created. The budget for the development bucket was another important artifact in this new practice, as the change requests from each business unit was monitored against its allocated share of the budget.

While the categorizing priorities and monitoring boundary organization practices facilitated negotiations that significantly reduced the change requests, some challenges remained. The negotiations identified some of the change requests as important to business units’ efective functioning, but that could not be included within the current project budget and time frame. For these requests, the Global Project Management Team recommended the setting up of an Extended EI team with its own budget and schedule. The Extended EI team would work in parallel with the existing implementation teams to analyze the requirements and work on the design of the approved change requests. To coordinate between the Extended EI team and the main Project Team, several experienced Project Team members were moved to the Extended EI team, and the Extended EI team also reported to the same Global Project Management Team and Enterprise Steering Committee. The Extended EI team was an important concession from Corporate Logistics and IT as it assisted in breaking the implementation deadlock by deferring business units’ individual operational interests to a later stage and enabling them to refocus on the processes and systems that had been agreed on.

The other related challenge was that after the first (Sea) business unit’s system was implemented, there was concern that future change requests might undermine enterprise integration. To address the concern, the boundary organization was extended through the creation of a new committee—the Integration Management Committee. The Integration Management Committee ensured that any change requests proposed would not adversely afect integration with system functionalities that had been implemented earlier or that were to be implemented later. The Integration Management Committee was appointed as a single formal body that reviewed and resolved all change requests and integration issues.

Collaboration Outcomes. The independent external review of the change requests established that the estimates were often based on guesses and incomplete information. This finding gave the Project Team a basis to bring all of the business units and consultants back to the table to revise the change requests. The process of prioritizing the change requests was painful, as each group had to rethink their change requests from the enterprise rather than their business units’ perspective.

. . . first kicking-out the ‘nice-to-haves’ and considering the possibility of workarounds without the change request. . . A painful process with some users refusing to budge, insisting that they could not do without (these things). The consultants approach the business process managers to talk to the users to persuade them to defer requirements to future phases. (If the manager was unsuccessful) then (we) escalate. . . upward. (Supply management track team lead)

The new prioritization categories were helpful in getting business unit users to compare their requests across all stakeholders, surfacing inconsistent prioritizing, and forcing them to question their original assumptions. As representatives from the stakeholders altered the priority of their change requests, the Corporate Logistics and IT members of the Project Team carefully tracked the business units’ change requests against their respective customization budgets. The resource limitations became more salient to the business units as they saw the cost impacts of each change request submitted. In cases where agreement on priorities could not be reached, the change requests were escalated to the executive owners of the process scenarios (established during the previous phase) for final resolution.

These collaborations and work patterns continued through the subsequent Land and Air implementations, and helped to manage the change requests for those implementations as well. One vignette of how this process played out was in the Land business unit’s change request for “nonstandardized” user interfaces. The Land business unit argued their users were generally less educated and tech-savvy than the Sea and

Air business unit users and wanted the user interface of the EI system to be customized for ease of use. They also wanted a diferent training approach that took into account their users’ education and skill profile. The other senior business unit representatives together with Corporate Logistics and IT agreed to these change requests, on the grounds that these did not unduly afect enterprise integration, while significantly increasing system acceptance among the Land business unit users. At the same time, Corporate Logistics and IT carefully tracked the Land business unit’s change requests against the Land business unit’s development budget. The cost of the change request was estimated to be approximately one-third of the budget. The careful tracking against its development bucket resulted in the Land business unit dropping many of its other change requests.

With these new boundary organization practices to manage the challenge of ballooning change requests, the three business units’ EI implementations were completed on time and within budget. With the revised change requests, the overall level of common processes remained above 70% (above the original target of 60%, but below the 80%–90% figure reached in the original global design document).

## Leveraging Phase

The successful rollout of enterprise systems for the Sea, Land, and Air business units and the gradual acceptance of the enterprise systems by users meant that the enterprise systems had slowly become part of Trilog’s operational infrastructure. However, the EI journey was not yet over. Trilog wanted to explore how they could leverage the implemented enterprise systems to obtain added benefits. There was also the need to extend EI for logistics-finance integration. The EI (Finance) implementation at this phase continued to be guided closely by forums established earlier, prior design documents, and the implemented systems. The overall control of the EI project was still the responsibility of the Global Project Management Team, who continued to report to the Enterprise Steering Committee.

As the various finance-related EI initiatives were implemented (e.g., retirement of old financial systems, linkages to external payment interfaces, consolidation of procurements), Finance began taking a more active role in driving the project. Finance injected new energy into EI with a series of strategic transformation initiatives under the umbrella of the Enhanced Financial Framework.

The core of enterprise systems is not just about logistics. There is an implication on money expended to do ops, there is an implication on the budget. Business unit managers understand that enterprise system is the enabler to give current and reliable information on how their resources are deployed. . . Many operational decisions in the past were made without such visibility. (Finance manager)

<sub>Challenge.</sub> The first challenge was how to create additional enterprise value from the large investment that had been made in the EI infrastructure. To date, the focus of EI had been on creating the common systems, processes, and data to support the logistics operations of the respective business units. Yet the organization had not actively explored the EI’s enterprise level benefits. To reap enterprise-wide value would require collaboration across diferent stakeholders, but now they had to go beyond the operational level to focus on leveraging the implemented EI infrastructure for enterprise level value. The second challenge related to this was how the potential value from EI would be distributed across the diferent stakeholders.

A key element of the (new financial approach) is that we want business unit heads to make decisions not just on operational needs but also on financial considerations. They can make sharper educated decisions, not guesses. To us, this is empowerment, but to them, it’s workload. (Finance manager)

The initiatives undertaken by Finance to reap enterprise-level benefits triggered some unexpected reactions from other stakeholders. This was because of their perception that Finance was going to reap the benefits of EI at the expense of the business units. They believed that many of the proposed EI initiatives brought only finance-related benefits with no direct impact in improving the cost eficiency of Corporate Logistics or the operational outcomes of the business units. Consequently, there was strong resistance to the eforts of Finance. Various concerns were raised by the business units. Examples included concerns that the granularity of the cross-business unit financial information collected by Finance translated to heavier upfront data entry workload by the business units as well as concerns over the sensitivity of who could access the cross-business unit financial information. Some even questioned the value of financial visibility across the business units.

The nature of our business is fairly dynamic [hence the need to operate independently]. I’m not sure how much we can stretch to reach a state of total resource visibility [across business units]. In terms of money, we know how much to spend and where we are spending. But how much information is to be pumped into the system? I don’t know. (Land business unit user)

New Boundary Organization Practices. <sup>The</sup> <sup>Enter-</sup> prise Steering Committee realized that there was a need to leverage on the EI infrastructure that had been implemented, and that the strategic transformation proposed by Finance was important for Trilog, but that there was also a need to provide enterprise value and synergies for the other key stakeholders. After much deliberation, the Enterprise Steering Committee decided to set up three separate strategic level committees to derive ways to reap EI value in Finance, Supply Chain, and Operations Readiness.

We have now three diferent parties studying to see how we can capitalize the value in enterprise systems. One is financial. There’s the performance tracking that builds models in terms of how to project things. There’s the logistics in terms of how to deal with supply chain itself. And third, still logistics, but it links to the operations side to shorten cycle time, and thus improve operational readiness. (Corporate Logistics manager)

Each committee was comprised of representatives from Corporate Finance, Logistics, IT, and the business units, typically senior executives at the director level who had an enterprise perspective and the mandate to deliberate on key decisions. The first committee (Finance) focused on the needs and goals of Finance, the second (Supply Chain) focused on the cross-business unit logistic needs of Corporate Logistics, and the last (Operations) focused on the needs of the business units, specifically in expediting the cycle times of their business operations. These committees developed synergistic cross-business unit scenarios that enabled them to generate and extract enterprise-level EI value. Any issue in balancing between EI value to one stakeholder and the costs to be borne by other stakeholders was escalated to the Enterprise Steering Committee and collectively addressed.

As these committees met and deliberated on ways to reap cross-business unit synergies, they appointed executive sponsors for the identified cross-business unit processes. Their roles were to ensure that these cross-business unit processes were properly incentivized and implemented, and that the EI benefits were realized as planned. These executive sponsors were accountable for the tracking and monitoring of the performance outcomes of these cross-business unit processes.

The Enterprise Steering Committee also proposed the design and development of an enterprise performance dashboard as an extension to the enterprise system. The new dashboard would capture not only the set of key performance indicators on Finance, Supply Chain, and Operational Readiness to be reported in a timely manner but also the agreement among the business units, Corporate Logistics, and Finance, on what constituted key performance indicators, how the key performance indicators would be compiled, and the appropriate level of granularity of the key performance indicators (see Table 5 for a summary).

<sub>Collaboration</sub> <sub>Outcomes.</sub> The setup of the three strategic level committees boosted the willingness of the business units to participate, and to give and take. Finance, for example, moderated their push for the Enhanced Financial Framework, agreeing to bear a portion of the “pains” the business units sufered. Specifically, they transferred some financial analysts to business units to help them develop expertise in financial analysis of logistics operations.

The value extraction and distribution focus of the three committees together with balanced representation also enabled stakeholders to delineate their convergent and divergent interests more clearly in the context of leveraging cross-business unit synergies. For example, the committees identified that procurement and specialized maintenance were key processes that would benefit from cross-business unit collaboration. In discussing these cross-business unit processes, the stakeholders could concretely clarify and agree on details such as the definition of new job scopes and workflows for these cross-business unit processes (e.g., who inputs data, who authorizes and buys, who maintains, who analyzes), and determine which business units would best perform these activities. For example, new cross-business unit procedures were laid out for one business unit to procure their transportation vehicles through another business unit’s procurement unit, since the latter had the best expertise and economies of scale.

Moreover, through the three strategic committees, the senior executives actively engaged their respective management team to define and operationalize the important strategic cross-business unit outcomes. Key metrics for these outcomes were agreed on, formalized, and designed into the enterprise performance dashboard. These indicators served as the basis for continuing discussions on cross-business unit benchmarking, and on the perceived fairness of cost and benefit distribution.

As a result, each stakeholder was relatively happy with the business value they derived from EI. For example, Finance not only strengthened its financial control and streamlined its financial processes but also enjoyed greater financial visibility for decision making.

At the click of the system I can see the budget of all the business units. I can do a comparison across similar units or for an activity. . . If unit A incurs 10 times more cost I want to know why. . . . At the end of the day I can do performance measures on the budget itself. (Finance manager)

Similarly, EI also provided better cross-business unit visibility for Corporate Logistics to manage logistic resources (e.g., warehouse management) across the enterprise.

Because now Corporate Logistics manages all warehouses, we use enterprise systems to . . . see across all warehouses. We see data in terms of inventory management, in terms of cost across all 3 diferent contracts. We are also the contract managers, so we use that to standardize inventory management across all 3 business units, and also influence operational details across 3 business units. (Corporate Logistics manager)

Even the business units were warming to how EI could help to optimize their operations. For example, the visibility through EI prompted the Sea business unit to investigate fuel consumption, which in turn led to the determination of optimum sailing speeds for ships with particular cargo weights.

There is more cost awareness now. We do ABB [Activity Based Budgeting]. We track the sea hours of the ship and see the cost implications of sailing. . . . This translates to real savings in sea hours and in maintenance. (Sea business unit user)

At this point of its multiyear EI journey, Trilog had become an enterprise that was integrated not just operationally, but also strategically, as it learned to reap cross-business unit synergies. The overall distribution of the value of EI was also more balanced across all stakeholders.

## Discussion

Our study sought to understand the process by which boundary organization practices enable efective multiparty collaboration in EI. We found that EI collaboration had cycles of divergence and convergence. This is consistent with prior research that found cycles of divergence and convergence within IS projects as multiple stakeholders collaborate on designing and building complex IT systems (Hansen and Rennecker 2010, Levina 2005). In our study of EI, the cycles occurred within each of the three phases, which we labeled designing, realizing, and leveraging—reflecting the main focus of each phase. More importantly, we found that the nature of divergence changed depending on the phase, and that specific boundary organization practices were enacted to address the divergence that arose in each phase. In the following sections, we discuss the nature of divergence in each EI project phase as well as how the three sets of boundary organization practices with their attending artifacts were enacted to address the specific divergence and promote convergence (see Figure 2).

## The Changing Nature of Divergence in EI

The nature of divergence difered across the three phases of EI as it reflected the changing context of each EI phase. In the designing phase—the key divergence arose from Project Team members who came from different business units. They were typically entrenched in their functional silo, and their interests and goals did not align well with the transformational EI goals. As the project shifted to the realizing phase, divergence arose because of diferences in users’ understanding of agreed design and how it should be implemented in the respective business units. Finally, in the leveraging phase, new divergence emerged as the organization sought to obtain greater value from the investments in EI, and stakeholders had diferent views about the distribution of the benefits and costs.

The recurring and changing nature of divergence across the three phases explains, in part, the need for a formal structure such as a boundary organization in an EI project. Our study found that the boundary organization did not remain static but evolved as diferent boundary organization practices were enacted in each phase at various levels of the organization (e.g., business unit representatives to senior executives to enterprise steering committee) to manage each new divergence (see Table 6).

## Enabling Convergence Through Boundary Organization Practices and Artifacts

Our findings revealed a variety of boundary organization practices across the three phases of the EI project. In addition to boundary organization practices that were similar to those in extant research (i.e., composing representation and coordinating work) (O’Mahony and Bechky 2008), we found others that were enacted to address the divergence that arose in each phase.

In the designing phase the divergence arose from diferences among the business units given their disparate interests and goals. This divergence resulted in potential gridlock as the various business units would not collaborate on creating common designs. This led to the emergence of a set of boundary organization practices—<sub>organizing</sub> <sub>to</sub> <sub>negotiate</sub> (see Figure 2)— that helped to organize the project members to efectively negotiate and compromise on process design for common goals. These boundary organization practices—aligning incentives, escalating issues, building collective identity—complemented one another by (a) aligning business units’ incentives toward building a set of convergent interests; (b) providing a way for each business unit to negotiate reciprocally with each other (blue-pink peer voting) and escalate disagreements to the appropriate people (executive owners); (c) encouraging the boundary spanners to develop a sense of collective identity (through social interactions). The aligning incentives and escalating issues practices helped the members focus on the convergent interests (blue process) while acknowledging the importance of divergent interests (pink process and executive owners). Artifacts served important roles in the boundary organization practices. For example, blue and pink process documentation and common process modeling tools served as boundary objects that constituted the aligning incentives practice. The global design document served as a primary object of collaboration that was critical for aligning incentives and escalating issues practices as it was the focus of the Project Team member eforts as they collaborated to create and modify the common processes inscribed in the global design document. We elaborate on the role of artifacts in the next section.

In the realizing phase, divergence was because of differences in how to implement agreed designs as business units began to understand the changes necessitated by the implementation of the common design. In view of this divergence, the EI team enacted boundary organization practices—<sub>organizing</sub> <sub>to</sub> <sub>contain</sub>—that organized the members to explicate their diferences with regards to implementation and to minimize the degree and impact of divergence so as not to derail the EI goals. This set of boundary organization practices included explicating divergence, categorizing priorities, monitoring, and deferring practices. The explicating divergence practice enabled the EI team, through boundary objects like global design document and independent audit report, to make visible how far the team had deviated from their prior converged interests. Building on the explicating divergence practice, the EI team introduced the categorizing priorities practice whereby members had to jointly categorize and negotiate their change requests, thus confronting and negotiating their divergent interests vis-à-vis the overall project objectives. The monitoring practice used artifacts like the budget document to help the EI team carefully track change requests against allocated business unit budgets and thus held each member accountable for their decisions with regards to the change requests. The deferring practice was important as it accommodated divergent interests that were found to be important to the organization, but that could not be met within the current budget and schedule. In this study, the deferring practice took the form of a sub-EI project with its own budget and timeline to further study and implement these significant change requests at a later time. By enacting this set of boundary organization practices, the EI team was able to deal with this new divergence in the realizing phase.

In the leveraging phase, another set of boundary organization practices—<sub>organizing</sub> <sub>to</sub> <sub>sustain</sub>—was enacted by the EI team to help organize stakeholders to generate and ensure a fair distribution of EI value for sustained collaboration. First, the EI team sought to extract greater value for EI by setting up three strategic committees with representatives from diferent stakeholder groups, specifically targeting senior executives. This practice served to focus senior executives from separate business units on how to leverage the EI infrastructure that was now in place by exploiting cross-unit synergies. Second, the EI team enacted the distributing value practice that helped put in place executive sponsors for cross-business unit responsibilities and processes to align and balance the distribution of costs and benefits among business units and corporate functions. The enterprise performance dashboard was a boundary object supporting the distributing value practice as the executive sponsors could use it to track and monitor the key performance indicators as well as to formalize and inscribe their agreement of “balanced” distribution of EI benefits. These boundary organization practices enabled Trilog to explore how the EI system could be leveraged for value and to reduce the divergence because of perceived inequitability of benefit distribution.

In sum, we have highlighted three sets of boundary organization practices—organizing to negotiate, organizing to contain, and organizing to sustain—that were used to address specific divergences and to shepherd diferent stakeholders to collaborate together. These boundary organization practices were not enacted independently but worked to complement each other, not only within each phase but also across phases. For example, in the realizing phase, the EI project continued working within the diferent subteams that had been formed in the designing phase. Also, in the leveraging phase, the composing representation practice used in earlier phases was enacted for the Finance implementation and complemented by strategic level committees enacted in the extracting value practice.

The boundary organization practices also work at diferent hierarchical levels of the enterprise (e.g., the aligning incentives practice of blue-pink voting was enacted among peers on the Project Team, while the extracting and distributing value practices were enacted at the senior management levels) and in some cases across hierarchical levels (e.g., the escalating issues practice was enacted between Project Team members and senior executives).

## The Role of Artifacts in Boundary Organization Practices

Our findings showed that artifacts played critical roles in the diferent boundary organization practices. In line with extant research on boundary object and boundary work, we observed that many artifacts served as boundary objects, i.e., representative objects that enable coordination of understanding across diferent groups so as to assist in boundary spanning work across space and time (Carlile 2002, Levina and Vaast 2014, Star and Griesemer 1989). Boundary organization practices, like aligning incentive practice, could efectively coordinate and build a shared understanding among diferent stakeholders via artifacts like common process modeling tools (see Table 6, first row). Thus boundary object artifacts with its shared schema of knowledge representation help to manage knowledge flow and coordination of work within boundary organization practices through embedded collaborative rules, standardized practices, and common consensus (Doolin and McLeod 2012, Østerlund 2008, Star and Ruhleder 1996).

Prior literature on boundary objects has also noted and discussed how artifacts as boundary object can serve to represent joint concerns, interests, and identity and diferences among diferent groups, and thereby enable negotiation and alignment of concerns and interests (Carlile 2004, Levina and Vaast 2005, Star and Griesemer 1989). This was evident in our finding where boundary organization practice like explicating divergence used the completed global design document (see Table 6, second row) as the basis to negotiate and align diferences among the business units.

While artifacts as boundary objects help explain <sub>how</sub> boundary practices are supported, recent research has suggested that artifacts help explain <sub>why</sub> collaboration occurs (Knorr Cetina 1997, Nicolini et al. 2012). Drawing on various sociological perspectives for theorizing artifacts such as epistemic object (Knorr Cetina 1997) and activity theory (Engeström and Blackler 2005), Nicolini et al. (2012) argued that artifacts help trigger, sustain, and motivate collaboration by taking on the role of primary objects of collaboration. Artifacts that act as primary objects of collaboration are partly emergent, multifaceted (since each group may have its own view of the object), and constantly evolving as they are being constructed through collaboration (Engeström and Blackler 2005, Knorr Cetina 1999, Miettinen 2005) and, as such, they tend to be partial and incomplete (because they tend not to be visible in their entirety to all of the participants).

Artifacts that act as primary objects of collaboration support boundary organization practices that were focused on creating and building new or novel systems, knowledge, or structures. The primary object of collaboration captures the multifaceted interests of the collaborating parties but also reveals emergent problems and what the group does not know (Knorr Cetina 1997). In our study, the tentative global design document acted as a primary object of collaboration (Table 6, first row) in that it helped expose the complexity of an end-to-end process, documented emergent issues as cross-track scenarios were discussed, and revealed what the scenario teams did not know. This thus motivated the business unit representatives and IT consultants to collaborate closely on constructing the scenario designs (Miettinen 2005).

Finally, prior literature on boundary objects have highlighted that artifacts may perform diferent roles in diferent contexts (Barrett and Oborn 2010). This is because artifacts perform specific roles as they are constituted and realized in the performance of the boundary organization practices (Doolin and McLeod 2012, Levina and Vaast 2005, Orlikowski and Scott 2008). The global design document artifact is a prime example in that it performed the role of primary object of collaboration in the designing phase as part of the aligning incentive practice and then its role changed to boundary object in the realizing phase when it was performed as part of the explicating divergence practice. Thus, artifacts are not confined to one static role but rather their roles can change as they become part of diferent practices across changing project contexts (Barrett and Oborn 2010, Nicolini et al. 2012).

Prior literature not only recognizes the changing role of artifacts, they have also pointed to the durability of artifacts and its ability to sustain collaboration over time (Star and Griesemer 1989). In our study, we noted that the focal artifact of the EI enterprise system was not only changing but also highly durable. In the first phase, the Project Team worked on the enterprise system in the form of the tentative global design document—where they documented and signed of on agreed on designs for EI. By the realizing phase, the Project Team had moved from the completed global design document to the development of mockup screens and system configurations that later cumulated in the Sea business unit enterprise system. By the third phase, Trilog had adopted the three integrated Sea, Land, and Air enterprise systems as part of its operational logistic infrastructure (Star and Ruhleder 1996).

Note that the durability of artifacts contributes to the durability of boundary organization practices and, in turn, the boundary organization. First, artifacts contribute to the durability of the boundary organization practices by physically capturing interests, negotiations, and converged agreements (Yeow and Sia 2008). Business unit representatives in the Project Team could continue their collaboration as they moved from one phase to another by referring to the agreements and negotiations represented in the global design document artifact. Second, Project Team leaders also represented their collaborative work arrangements in artifacts so that they act as physical templates of how boundary organization practices are to be enacted by the other stakeholders (Feldman and Pentland 2003). For example, the enterprise performance dashboard built in the enterprise system helped cross business-unit executive sponsors track and monitor key performance indicators so as to ensure a balanced distribution of EI benefits (see Table $6 ,$ third row). Third, as artifacts become part of the organization’s physical infrastructure, they become formal symbols of the organization’s values and logics of integration and standardization that the Project Team could draw on to legitimatize and support the boundary organization and its practices (Gosain 2004, Star and Ruhleder 1996, Volkof et al. 2007). For example, the three business units’ enterprise systems became the symbol of Trilog’s integrated enterprise objective and gave legitimacy to Finance who was running the EI Project boundary organization during the leveraging phase (Table 6, third row). In these ways, we show that the durability of artifacts contributes to persistence of the formal boundary organization structure and its practices.

## Implications for Research and Practice

Prior research on EI acknowledges the need for efective multiparty collaboration in EI to resolve the tensions among multiple stakeholders (Lee et al. 2003, Soh et al. 2000), resulting in compromises, customizations, workarounds, and loose coupling of work processes (Berente and Yoo 2012, Soh et al. 2000, Wagner and Newell 2004). However, they do not fully explicate the collaboration dynamics or how collaboration can be managed. Our research explicitly highlights the need for ongoing boundary spanning work that goes beyond typical project management approaches discussed in the EI literature (Sandoe and Saharia 1999).

Our findings suggest that in managing EI projects it is useful to take the perspective of the boundary organization, a formal but dynamic structure that carries a mandate to facilitate multiparty collaboration. Extant research of boundary organizations have been in interorganizational contexts where they often face constraints on resources and legitimacy issues (O’Mahony and Bechky 2008). Within the interorganization context, collaboration is among peer organizations who lack hierarchical authority over one another; they mainly employ practices that encourage collaborative negotiation and consensus-driven resolution. By applying the boundary organization concept to an <sub>intraorganizational</sub> EI context, we found that the practices of the boundary organization were combined with the hierarchical authority of traditional project management approaches. For example, the boundary organization practices of aligning incentives and escalating issues were used to promote convergence toward a common design, supplementing the traditional project team hierarchy and roles. More broadly, we found three distinct sets of boundary organization practices—organizing to negotiate, organizing to contain, and organizing to sustain, together with key EI artifacts—that address the three divergences, which we believe are typical of EI initiatives as they move from designing to realizing and finally to the leveraging phase.

Our study also contributes to the boundary organization literature. By exploring practices, we add a more dynamic perspective to the relatively structural view of boundary organizations in the literature (Guston 1999, 2001; O’Mahony and Bechky 2008). We explicate the diferent types of boundary organization practices and show how they work in combination to enable efective multiparty collaboration. We also show that the boundary organization has to continually evolve its practices because of the emergence of new kinds of divergence across the EI phases as the context of the EI project changes. It is not enough for the boundary organization to focus on the initial development of convergent interests—which can be tenuous—but also to continue to find ways to sustain the convergence by addressing new divergence. We believe this dynamic understanding of boundary organization through the practice perspective is important, given the growing stream of work that focuses on highly adaptive and dynamic collaboration practices (Kellogg et al. 2006, Majchrzak et al. 2012).

In addition, our observations highlight the role of artifacts in boundary organizations. Prior research in boundary organizations tends to emphasize the organizational aspects, e.g., membership and governance (O’Mahony and Bechky 2008), with little discussion on the related artifacts. This study shows that artifacts, as boundary objects or primary objects of collaboration are integral to boundary organization practices. Artifacts contribute to the durability of the boundary organization practices by materially capturing negotiated agreements, embedding collaborative work arrangements, as well as serving as formal symbols of organization’s values. This in turn provides stability for sustained collaboration among diverse stakeholders (Orlikowski and Scott 2008). Such durability is important in initiatives or projects that span many years. It is also interesting to note that the role of each artifact can change when constituted in diferent boundary organization practices—in one practice, it is a primary object of collaboration and in another practice, it is a boundary object. Future research could adopt a sociomaterial view of boundary organizations to build a more holistic understanding of boundary organization practices with their attending artifacts (Nicolini et al. 2012, Orlikowski and Scott 2008).

Finally, the insights from our study also bear strong practical implications for managing EI projects. Successful EI projects require project managers to go beyond managing project resources or promoting system use to proactively facilitating collaborative work across diferent boundaries. EI project managers could use our framework of boundary organization practices (i.e., organizing to negotiate, organizing to contain, and organizing to sustain) as cognitive anchors to guide the organizing of diverse stakeholders for efective collaboration. It is important that project managers understand the changing nature of divergence across the different phases of the project. They cannot just rely on an initial set of boundary organization practices, but must be alert to emergent divergence and be ready to deploy other boundary organization practices to deal with these challenges. Together, these practices and the attendant artifacts constitute a formal boundary organization that manages emergent divergence and facilitates multiparty collaborative work.
