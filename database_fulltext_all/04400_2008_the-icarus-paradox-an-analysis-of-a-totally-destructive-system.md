## Abstract

This paper uses a case study to illustrate and further extend the notion of ‘socially destructive systems’. Baskerville and Land define a destructive information system as either coming apart internally or threatening to take their environment apart. Building upon their insights, this paper suggests that destructive systems emerge from the very attributes that attract organisations to information systems in the first place – a phenomenon encapsulated by the so-called ‘Icarus paradox’. Icarus flew so close to the Sun that his wax wings that had enabled him to fly in the first place melted sending him plunging to his death. The case study of an insurance company shows how a system that increased claims processing productivity in the short run resulted in higher costs and lower customer satisfaction in the long run. The paradox is explained by a disconnect between ‘espousedtheory’ and ‘theory-in-use’. Issues for further research include developing techniques of impact analysis to measure recursive losses arising from ‘destructive systems’ and for gauging the level of complexity at which computer-based automation becomes counterproductive. An important recommendation for practice is that computer-based systems are efficient only to a point. For more complex tasks organisations may need to put more emphasis upon developing people.

## Introduction

C <sup>omputers</sup> <sup>have</sup> <sup>become</sup> <sup>so</sup> <sup>ubiquitous</sup> <sup>that</sup> <sup>it</sup> <sup>is</sup> <sup>easy</sup> <sup>to</sup>forget that hardware and software are not mandatory. forget that hardware and software are not mandatory. Tablets of clay, bells, flags and hand-written letters served as information systems long before computers were invented (Daniels, 1993 citing Land). The appeal of computers over older methods is to shorten communication time.

In medieval times, horseback was the fastest medium for transporting information long distance. Papal edicts were despatched with the recipient’s name left blank in case the recipient died before it arrived (Duggan, 1980). Communication was also extremely costly and uncertain. Horse and rider might need to stay in inns for weeks or even months en route plus the risks of travelling on unlit roads infested by brigands chocked with dust in summer and flooded in winter. Yet for all the difficulties, medieval information systems were surprisingly efficient. In emergencies, certain types of information, such as the arrival of an enemy, could be rapidly passed along a line of beacons. The Pastons, a famous Norfolk family, exchanged hundreds of letters over long distances between about 1430 and 1504. Few if any letters were lost in transit (Davis, 1983, see Introduction). Moreover, even in medieval times the architecture of organisations was largely determined by communication systems, but these enabled quite sophisticated trade networks to develop. For example, some medieval inns offered currency exchange and payments through book transfer (Spufford, 2002).

Even so imagine conducting business today with medieval communications! Significantly many organisations give higher priority to restoring computer systems than getting people back to work following a crisis (Larsen, 2005a).

Yet not everyone likes computers. Some executives have jettisoned their electronic diaries and reverted to ‘old-fashioned’ paper ones. When the UK brokering firm Cazenove merged with the giant US investment house J. P. Morgan in 2004, Morgan’s executives were perplexed by

Cazenove’s former partner’s refusal to carry ‘Blackberries’ (Larsen, 2005b). In a seminal chapter, Baskerville and Land (2004; see also Argyris and Schon, 1978) suggest that such seemingly trivial incidents may represent a serious and under-researched problem. The problem is that computerbased information systems can undermine or even destroy the organisation that they were meant to support. The notion of destructive systems is explained later in this paper. Here we need only note that they differ from the more familiar problem of information systems failures such as project TAURUS (Drummond, 1996) and the Denver airport baggage handling system (Monteaglre and Keil, 2000), because superficially they seem successful. Their destructive properties only emerge post-installation. For example, Baskerville and Land analysed a defence communications system intended to enhance efficiency by eliminating the need for meetings as military commanders could brief one another and answer to higher command via the system. The new system became destructive precisely because commanders no longer needed to meet face-to-face and consequently mutual trust began to weaken.

The destructive property of the command system was that it reduced communication to an instrumental activity. This paper attempts to build upon Baskerville and Land’s work by attaching new literature to it, namely the so-called ‘Icarus paradox’ (Miller, 1992). The fabled Icarus of Greek mythology plunged to his death because he flew so close to the Sun that his wax wings melted. Likewise, the intention is to suggest that it may be precisely what makes computerbased information systems useful that can also make them destructive. The demonstration invokes a case study of an organisation that installed a new information system in order to improve efficiency. The new system was an immediate success as it enabled ambitious key targets to be met. Yet the very factors that made the system successful are under-mining the organisation in the longer term.

## Theoretical context

It is assumed that destructiveness is unintentional because the purpose of installing computer-based information systems is to maximise utility. The theoretical question therefore is how can individual computer users act rationally and yet the totality of their actions produces irrational consequences?

The intellectual vehicle for addressing this question is the trope of irony. The word trope is derived from the Greek language meaning ‘turn, twist’ (Manning, 1979; Morgan, 1980; Gibbs, 1993). Tropes provide a form of notation for telling reality in a particular way in order to facilitate the conceptualisation of experience (Brown, 1989; Watson, 1994). To be more precise what is seen in data depends upon the conceptual lens used to analyse it. This is because different analytical perspectives define differently what are to be taken as the basic ‘facts’ of the situation (e.g., Brown, 1989, 1977; Morgan, 1990). The reader may be most familiar with the trope of metonymy. Metonymy reflects a positivist view of the science that approaches the study of social phenomena as the same way as the natural sciences (e.g., Burrell and Morgan, 1979; Brown, 1989; Giddens, 1995). Metonymy expresses contiguous relations between objects such as cause and effect with its root metaphor of mechanism (e.g., Morgan, 1980; Brown, 1989; Gibbs, 1993). For example, what causes occupational stress?

Irony is defined as, ‘a reflective form of metaphorical imagination that involves the interplay of opposites and creates insight through paradox and contradiction’ (Brown, 1977: 172). What all ironies have in common is that they are a structure of ambiguities and contradictions that are revealed over time as events ripen to yield an unexpected yet inevitable outcome. Strictly speaking if something is inevitable it can hardly be unexpected. Analysis seeks to penetrate this paradox by focusing upon the structure of contradictions inherent in the situation itself in order to explain the contrast between expectations and outcomes.

## Rationality

Rationality is the key to maximising utility. Rationality means calculation of the most efficient means of accomplishing a particular end. It is hardly a new idea. Medieval monastic foundations applied rational methods of production in order to maximise the time available for prayer. For instance, forecasting models for bread were used, ‘The number is always the same, unless through some accident there should be a need for fewer rations; for there is never a need for more y. ’ (Keiser, 1987: 112).

The next major advance was mass production. Whereas a pin maker might produce one pin a day, a system whereby, ‘One man draws out the wire, another straightens it, a third cuts it, a fourth points it,’ (Smith, 1983 ed: 110) division of labour could enable labourers in a small manufactory to achieve exponential increases in production. produce 12 pounds of pins in 1 day. Management’s most influential exponent of rationality was Taylor (1947). He recommended separating conception of work from its actual execution, that is, management assume control of work processes and methods, while employees merely discharge their allotted tasks. Tasks moreover were deskilled, that is, divided into miniscule components to facilitate rapid accomplishment and to require minimal training or knowledge thereby minimising expense and rendering operatives dispensable.

## The ‘irrationality of rationality

We are not concerned with the allegedly de-humanising aspects of Taylor’s ideas (for a detailed discussion see Braverman, 1974), but whether and to what extent they do actually optimise efficiency when applied to the design of information systems. For our purposes system architecture stands surrogate for the conceptualisation of work and execution means ‘following the system’.

In theory, Taylor’s brand of rationality offers four interrelated benefits, namely efficiency, predictability, calculability and control by enabling organisations to deliver services of uniform quality and quantity, via a production system that maximises output for a given cost (Ritzer, 1993). Beyond a certain point, however, the result may be a paradox of consequences known as ‘the irrationality of rationality’ whereby rational systems of production produce results that are patently absurd (Ritzer, 1993; see also Watson, 1994; Drummond, 1998a). For example, there are often long queues at automatic cash dispensers while bank tellers stand idle.

One reason for the paradox is that our notions of rationality reflect linear intuitive thinking that assumes ‘more is better’. Yet intensification can become counterproductive (e.g., Cameron and Quinn, 1988). Rational systems of production enabled monastic foundations to become rich but excessive wealth tempted the monks into gluttony and idleness thus undermining the legitimacy of monastic foundations and providing a pretext for their eventual abolition (Keiser, 1987). More recently intense commitment to quality in Japan and concomitant fear of failure has led to dangerous products being marketed and a train driver fatally exceeding speed limits rather than be a few minutes late (Pilling and Sawada, 2005).

This paper will try to show that computer-based information systems may become destructive for the same reason. More specifically, using computers to assume control over conception of work and to de-skill execution may be efficient, but beyond a certain point applying Taylor’s ideas becomes counter-productive.

The Icarus metaphor will be invoked for the purposes of this demonstration. A metaphor is a linguistic device whereby one thing is understood by its resemblance to another. For example, ‘life is a journey’. In social sciences research metaphors are used to pre-figure the ground to be studied (discussed further in Morgan, 1980). Hirschheim and Newman (1991) have argued, for example, that the metaphors used in information systems development such as ‘sign off’ and ‘system test’ resonate scientific precision that is seldom realised in practice. The paper raises an important issue because it implies that failure may be more apparent than real – a reflection of unrealistic expectations created by the metaphors employed.

There are two types of metaphor, the iconic and the analogue (further discussed in Brown, 1977). An iconic metaphor such as a statute tells you what something is, whereas an analogue tells you what something is like. It is the difference between apprehension and comprehension. All metaphors fulfil both functions to a greater or lesser extent. The attraction of the Icarus metaphor is that it enables us to apprehend the potentially paradoxical nature of system dynamics and to comprehend the resultant destructive tendencies.

## How do we recognise a destructive system?

An information system becomes destructive when it undermines the organisational structure (Baskerville and Land, 2004). Structure refers to a template for action – a set of instructions for what will happen (Brown, 1978; Giddens, 1979). No matter how diligently actors follow their instructions, the results seldom correspond exactly to the plan. Just as coroners must decide between ‘death by suicide’ and ‘death by miss-adventure’, rules become a set of instructions for generating difficulties that actors are required to solve (Brown, 1978; Weick, 1979; Feldman, 2000). How actors regularly solve problems becomes the realised structure or ‘theory-in-use’ as distinct from espoused-theory (Baskerville and Land, 2004). While organisations cannot function without some flexibility (ironically the first stage of industrial action is often ‘working to rule’), they can also be deflected by a mismatch theory and practice:

An incompatible system design represents a departure of espoused-theory from theory-in-use y. Conflicts then arise between desired actor behaviour (theory-in-use) and the IS constraints (espoused-theory), because the in use and espoused assumption spaces become incompatible. (Baskerville and Land, 2004: 267)

The present study focuses upon the gap between espousedtheory and theory-in-action. Specifically, how and why do they become separated by the information system and with what results? For example, how does the resultant conflict between espoused-theory and theory-in-action manifest itself? How is it resolved and how might the resolution of that conflict inform theory and practice?

## Method

The present study invokes a case study as the vehicle for analysis. The case concerns a unit of a large organisation named ABC (a pseudonym) processing medical insurance claims. The organisation is located in Europe. It has been in existence for over a decade. A case study may serve three potentially over-lapping purposes namely description, exploration and explanation (Yin, 1994). The present study is primarily exploratory aimed at elucidating the dynamics of a destructive system prototype and their interactions. Generating understanding places emphasis upon telling the story (e.g., Mintzberg, 1979; Dyer and Wilkins, 1991). Validity requires triangulation, namely if important facts are confirmed by three independent data sources, the researcher can be confident that the story has not been distorted.

Triangulation was achieved in the present study by utilising:

(a) semi-structured interviews with key organisational actors;

(b) documentary evidence, and

(c) observation.

For example, observation of a management meeting showed that systems development occupied almost an hour of discussion whereas staff development occupied barely 20 minutes. An analysis of minutes of meetings dating back 3 years confirmed that this pattern was typical. Interview data also revealed that key actors were more committed to developing systems than people. Collectively these sources reliably indicate where the organisation’s priorities lie.

The main informants were the Information Officer responsible for the day-to-day system development and maintenance; Case Administrators, semi-professionals who oversee claims; and Supervisors, typically qualified to UK ‘A’ level responsible for day-to-day activities. Interviews were also conducted with ABC’s Chief Executive and the Chief Information Officer, who has overall responsibility for IT within the firm. The interviews took place after the new system had been operational for about 3 years. Interviews were recorded in a notebook in a form of shorthand and subsequently written up. Observation was conducted over three sessions, one morning and two afternoons. The Chief Executive Officer, Chief Information Officer and Information Officer were interviewed twice, initially for familiarisation purposes and then to explore issues raised in interviews with Case Administrators and Supervisors. Three Case Administrators and three Supervisors were interviewed. This number of supervisors interviewed was determined by the principle of saturation (for an example of the principle being applied see Ross and Staw, 1993). That is, the issues raised by informants during first and second interviews were similar. Interviewing ceased when the third interviews added nothing new. It was also obvious by the second session of observation that office activity followed a pattern. Nothing new was discovered during the third session and therefore observation ceased. The researcher also observed a management team meeting as part of the familiarisation exercise. The main documents gathered were the business case for the new system, minutes of management team meetings, a Supervisor’s diary sheet for a week and samples of systemgenerated documentation.

## Approach to data analysis

A variant of pattern matching was employed to analyse interview data (Yin, 1994). The aim was to assess ‘goodness of fit’ between espoused-theory and theory-in-use. In order to do this, a template was devised comparing anticipated benefits (see below) of the new system to stand surrogate for espoused theory with actual practice. Diary analysis followed a similar approach. Interview data suggested that contrary to espoused-theory, Supervisors seldom found time to do productive work or check out-going letters. Observation also revealed that Supervisors were constantly on the telephone. A Supervisor was then asked to maintain a diary for a week. This exercise showed that no productive work was undertaken and that only four letters were checked instead of the 20% stipulated by the rules – a miniscule proportion of out-going correspondence. Taken together, these sources reliably indicated a significant gap between espoused-theory and theory-in-use’.

## Case study

ABC decided to automate claims processing as part of a business process re-engineering exercise aimed at improving efficiency. A model originally developed for the banking industry was adapted for use. The business case for the new electronic workflow system promised seven inter-related benefits resulting from improved control:

(a) comprehensive case management,

(b) instant overview of cases in the system,

(c) no more documents mis-placed,

(d) processing no longer delayed pending filing of documents,

(e) elimination of manual files,

(f) release supervisors to train staff and do productive work,

(g) customer satisfaction resulting from faster service and improved accuracy.

‘The system is ok for routine stuff

The promise of faster service was soon achieved. Processing times fell from an average of 200 days to the target level of 95% of claims being processed within 60 days. Accuracy levels also rose from 80% to the target level of 95%. The key to such an achievement was simplicity. The new system requires only a single key-stroke to generate routine letters such as, ‘please complete and return the enclosed form’. Likewise if a claim for compensation is agreed the clerk merely presses a single key and enters the amount offered in settlement or a different key if a claim is refused. For example, a lorry driver involved in a minor explosion while delivering to a chemical factory was offered d3000 compensation for a damage to two front teeth. The notification letter was dispatched in minutes.

Not all claims are as simple, however. Some involve multiple items where some may be refused. For instance, an insurer may allow a claim for pain and distress resulting from a tripping injury but refuse to meet bills for aromatherapy and massage to relieve depression allegedly resulting from the accident. The new system with its highly restricted free space cannot handle such complexity. A Supervisor said:

It (the software) doesn’t allow for anything else obviously the easiest thing to do is press ‘yes’ and a notification is issued. But claims one, three and five may have been allowed and two and four rejected and we are saying that your claim has been agreed. The system is OK for routine stuff, ‘Dear Joe Bloggs, here is your leaflet.

Frustrated customers frequently telephone Supervisors for more information. Handling such enquiries is time consuming as Supervisors have to read about the case, sometimes hunt for documents (see below), return the call and then write a letter of confirmation. A Supervisor said:

The thing is every case is different. Say you have an accident and suffer a broken leg. Right, you’ve got a broken leg – same as the next person. But you may be left limping badly and in constant pain whereas the other person makes a complete recovery. The outcome may be similar but the claims are different. If the system can’t bring itself to tell you, ‘We (the insurers) have taken into account the fact you now have a limp and are experiencing constant pain y.’ (sic) you’re going to think we’ve only done half a job. Then you’re going to come back to us and we have to spend time explaining. And then we have to write another letter.

Letters are dispatched remotely. It speeds up processing but makes it difficult for staff to check their work. For example, when a clerk mistakenly pressed ‘yes’ instead of ‘no’ the letter of notification said, ‘Your claim for compensation has been agreed. Amount awarded: nil.

‘It took all morning to sort that one out,’ said a Supervisor.

## ‘Why are you sending me all this?

ABC has tried to fix the problem by loading more standard letters culled from past cases onto the system. The resultant database is cumbersome and a poor fit. That is, because ‘every case is different’, it can be difficult for clerks to match the precise requirements of a particular case with templates available ‘on the system’. Consequently, the resultant letters are sometimes mis-aligned. For example, a clerk processing a claim for an industrial injury called up a standard letter that almost fitted the case but left in a paragraph concerning provisions under Scots law relevant to a previous claim but irrelevant to the present claim. The confused recipient was soon on the telephone. A Supervisor said, ‘We regularly get complaints from customers, ‘‘Why are you sending me all this?’’’

The database, moreover, is continually expanding as business evolves but also because additional paragraphs are required to correct previous errors, making it even more cumbersome. The firm’s Chief Information Officer said, ‘The trouble is, systems get built and then people starting adding things on but if we didn’t make so many mistakes in the first place, we wouldn’t need half of these (new additions).

‘Oh! no! how did that ever get through the post tray?

If staff cannot locate a suitable template they may write the letter themselves using Microsoft Word. A Supervisor said, ‘You can’t blame them y but it does cause problems.’

Poor English is one problem. For example, ‘please tell us what you want doing with this matter.’ instead of, ‘please let us know how you wish to proceed.

Supervisors should check 20% of out-going letters but they never have time (see earlier reference to diary keeping). A Case Administrator said, ‘You look at a (copy of a) letter and you just think, ‘‘Oh! No! How did that ever get through the post tray?’’’

Another problem is that the electronic file, the cornerstone of automation, is incomplete. Staff should scan the letters into the system but such is the pressure to meet targets that they are typically either filed manually or left on hard drives. Clerks should ‘flag’ that documentation is missing but frequently omit to do so and besides the signal is vague. A Case Administrator said, ‘That (flag) can be anything y. They (clerks) can put an expanded comment on Notepad so at least someone checking the file knows to go into the manual system but not everyone does’.

Incomplete files can result in duplication of effort and confusion. For example, a customer was diagnosed with dementia and was therefore no longer eligible to administer his affairs. Unable to locate a suitable template on the system, the clerk wrote to the customer’s wife advising that in the absence of power of attorney, compensation would be paid via the Court of Protection. Shortly afterwards another clerk unaware of this development wrote directly to the customer requesting bank details to enable compensation to be paid in the ordinary way. The supervisor who dealt with the enquiry from the customer’s wife said, ‘It’s embarrassing and customers think we don’t care about them as individuals. But how can you tell a customer, ‘‘I’m sorry Mrs So and So but the left hand doesn’t know what the right hand’s doing in this organisation?’’’

The database was then expanded to include paragraphs covering dementia and payments made through the Court of Protection. The Information Officer doubted whether the exercise was worthwhile, ‘I don’t see why we have to have a system for everything when we might only do a couple of cases a year,’ she said.

‘There is a credibility gap’

The free space available on the system was then expanded to allow a measure of flexibility. The results were encouraging, ‘Staff love it,’ said the Information Officer. ‘They feel they’ve got more control of the case and can do a better job.

The facility is only available to ‘hand-picked’ staff, however. The researcher asked why it was not extended to office juniors. The Information Officer replied:

They’d die! Some would be pleased but a lot of people are scared of letter writing. A lot of school leavers are dreadful. Give them free space and yeah, they’ll use it but they’ll put capitals in the middle of a word! Letter writing skills should be part of customer service training – but you’ve got to prove that it saves money. The use of computer technology is very intensive and it encourages staff to leave their brains at the gate. Some would welcome it but a lot would panic.

Case Administrators also had to be convinced. The Information Officer said:

They were worried that it would stop them from meeting their targets. We said, ‘‘Just have a bit of faith in what we’ve done’’. In fact it has had no impact on targets because staff are not having to do as much follow up work but its work that has to be fitted in with everything else.

ABC’s Chief Executive was not enthusiastic about extending the facility. ‘At least the gobbledygook (sic) produced by the computer is correctly spelled,’ he said.

ABC is considering employing a customer relationship manager to handle complaints. The Chief Executive is more worried about the uncontrollable risks, ‘What keeps me awake at night is the customer who doesn’t complain to us but gets straight on the phone to ‘Watchdog’, he said.

‘Watchdog’ is a consumer affairs television programme instrumental in the collapse of firms doing similar work to ABC (Guthrie, 2002).

A more pressing problem concerns the 17% fall in turnover since the system was installed coupled with higher than anticipated staffing costs. The problem partly reflects ending the so-called ‘claims culture’ (Guthrie, 2002; Harris and Orr, 2003) and organisational arrangements for apportioning costs to units. ‘Even so’, said the Chief Executive, ‘I have to admit there is a credibility gap.’

## Analysis

Baskerville and Land (2004) identify three destructive forces. Inwardly destructive systems prove unsustainable and eventually collapse upon themselves. Outwardly destructive systems threaten to destroy the host organisation. Totally destructive systems are both inwardly and outwardly destructive. The system in ABC exhibits both properties. It is not literally totally destructive as the organisation continues to exist. On the other hand, its efficiency is being constantly under-mined by the new system.

The new system is successful in that de-skilling work by utilising standard letters, ‘please complete and return’;

restricting free space helps to cut processing times and improve accuracy. The system becomes outwardly destructive, however, as the conflict between what the system can bring itself to do and what customers want is resolved by staff taking the easiest option. Consequently theory-in-use is supervisors almost totally absorbed in non-value adding activities instead of engaging in training and productive work (espoused-theory).

The adverse reaction from customers forces a re-think. Espoused-theory is that using standard letters is more efficient than staff writing letters because standardisation eliminates duplication of effort. Espoused-theory further dictates that standardisation guarantees quality because staff cannot leave capitals in the middle of words. Theoryin-use, however, is that the standard letters slow work down because by the time staff find what they need (if they find it) they could have written the letter themselves. So while processing is no longer delayed pending filing of documents, the new system creates new delays. Moreover, the conflict between what customers want and what the system offers is frequently resolved by mindless behaviour – brains left at gate with letters containing superfluous information that confuses customers. We then see the structural template deforming further as the facilitator becomes outwardly destructive by creating rework as customers query letters, ‘Why are you sending me all this? (sic)’

The result is a deviation amplification loop as the system becomes over-loaded making it slower and increasing the risk of letters containing ‘gobbledygook’ albeit correctly spelled.

Ultimately the conflict may be resolved by circumnavigating the system but only to trigger another wave of outwardly destructive dynamics. Destructiveness is evidenced in duplication of effort and rework as customers query receipt of contradictory information. Moreover, espoused-theory of comprehensive case management, instant case over-view and the elimination of manual files are contradicted by theory-in-use. Contrary to espousedtheory supervisors are almost back where they started under the old system – wasting time hunting for missing documentation.

## Discussion

The theoretical question raised by the present study was how organisational actors may act rationally and yet the sum total of their actions produces the opposite of what was intended. ABC’s system fails on all four dimensions of rationality (Ritzer, 1993). Efficiency fails because the system generates constant rework. Predictability fails because the outputs are unreliable ‘Oh! No!’ Calculability fails as turnover declines and the system needs more staff than anticipated. Incomplete records and inconsistencies of approach mean that the system is not fully under control. Paradoxically, the gap between expectations and outcomes arises from the very thing that made the system successful in the first place, that is, automation enabled by using the computer to maintain control and to achieve de-skilling.

Before elaborating on this observation, it is emphasised that what is seen in data largely depends upon the conceptual lens used to analyse it. The rational actor paradigm assumes that outcomes reflect objective calculation of advantages. This perspective implies that actors get what they want regardless of the impact of legacy and vested interests (for a discussion of the rational actor model see Allison and Zelikow, 1999: 5–15). Utilising the trope of irony to prefigure the study inevitably casts the spotlight upon negative outcomes thus obscuring the portion of work that is executed satisfactorily. As Gall (1975) notes systems do accomplish a measurable fraction of what they set out to do. Processing times have been drastically reduced. Not all customers query the response they receive. Moreover, problems like ‘claim agreed, compensation nil’ could be cured by amending the software. The database could be reorganised to reduce searching time. Targets could be revised to reflect re-work (see below).

## Vicious circles

Mindful of such partiality what does the present study add to our understanding? Recall the trope of irony focuses upon the contrast between expectations and outcomes. It was expected that automation would enhance efficiency and customer satisfaction. The outcome is a downward spiral as evidenced by a credibility gap and a perceived risk of damaging exposure in the media.

What explains the contrast? Baskerville and Land (2004) suggest failure results when systems depend upon organisational forms unsuitable to their mission. This is ABC’s problem. A portion of the work is routine ‘here is your leaflet’ and ‘please complete and return the enclosed form’. This does not mean, however, that claims processing can be automated and de-skilled to the extent essayed by ABC. Icarus fell to his death not because he flew, but because he flew too close to the Sun.

To be more precise, computers can only deal with wellstructured problems (for discussions see Penrose, 1995; Angell and Ilharco, 2004). Consequently, complex cases get reduced to what the computer can handle, ‘yes/no’. The result is a proliferation of vicious circles. A vicious circle begins with a transformational problem (need to process claims faster) whereby individuals act purposefully yet the sum total of their interactions contradicts their intentions (Masuch, 1985). To create a structure actions have to be repeated (Weick, 1979). It is this repetition that pulls the structural template apart – not a single correctable act:

Action structures are reproductive by definition. Consequently, any nuisance that is not simply a passing problem, but is a structural sub-optimality y is also reproduced. Since sub-optimality cannot by definition, be intended, any structural sub-optimality must somehow be based on stable vicious circles. (Masuch, 1985: 19)

A downward spiral results in the present study because actors cause problems (structural sub-optimality) whether they follow the system mindlessly or whether they try to use their initiative.

Vicious circles can implode and cause organisations to collapse. Alternatively they can become the normal yet suboptimal state of affairs (Masuch, 1985). In ABC, it is the Supervisors who mitigate the destructive forces unleashed by the information system and ensure that the conflict between theory-in-use and espoused-theory does not completely de-stabilise the organisation.

The illusion of normality is also sustained by meeting key targets. Yet targets merely reflect what organisations regard as important and choose to pay attention to. Problem definition is not a neutral activity. A problem can be seen as an ideological molecule shaped by the values and priorities of organisational actors sufficiently puissant to define the organisational reality and specify what constitutes evidence of an acceptable level of performance (see especially Starbuck, 1983, also Clegg, 1981; Alvesson and Willmott, 1996) – in this case correctly spelled ‘gobbledygook’.

Besides statistical representations merely stand surrogate for reality (discussed further in Drummond (2001: Chapter 4), see also Brown, 1989). Angell and Ilharco (2004) cite the building society that used customer throughput as a surrogate for customer satisfaction and efficiency only to discover that clerks were giving wrong information on mortgages. Even ABCs more direct statistical representations of efficiency are misleading. Target levels for processing and accuracy may have been achieved but those statistics ignore the amount of re-work that may be required.

## Implications for research

Just as many drugs have side effects, conceivably most computer-based information systems are potentially destructive. Yet in what ways may systems become destructive? For example, what role do information systems play in creating permanently failing organisations such as the UK’s Child Support Agency? Researchers might also re-visit Weick’s (1985) work on the dangers of over-reliance upon computer-generated information in decision making. Seemingly trivial rejections of computer-based technology like electronic diaries may warrant re-examination. Why do users sometimes revert to technically sub-optimal solutions?

The present study (see also Baskerville and Land, 2004) suggests that computer-based information systems become destructive by transforming organisations. Anecdotal evidence suggests another possibility, namely that information systems can change how organisations are perceived. That is, thanks to the internet customers now perceive banking as a commodity. The resultant unprecedented waves of migration are forcing banks to consider re-opening branches in order to re-establish their ‘brand’ image (King, 2005). Research is needed to identify destructive prototypes and their properties.

## Identifying destructive properties

Willcocks and Lester (1996, see also Strassmann, 1990) suggest that the un-substantiated assumption that competitive viability demands massive investment in IT survives partly because organisations neglect post-implementation reviews and because many benefits of IT are intangible. While standard measures such as response time and downtime and calculating input error rate (see Smithson and Tisavos, 2004) might not reveal destructiveness, rework and hunting for lost files can be computed. Techniques of impact analysis might be developed to embrace these and other recursive losses emanating from information systems. For example, ‘re-work’ could be conceptualised and computed as a form of manual work-around.

Another issue concerns the role of facilitators and inhibitors. The present study tentatively suggests that inhibitors like restricted free space may be more destructive than facilitators because they invite mindless behaviour. Resistance in organisations can be very subtle – as we saw here, the most effective form of sabotage may be to leave one’s brains at the gate and simply follow the system (e.g., Collinson, 1994; Drummond, 1998b).

Studying constructive systems may yield further insight into the nature of destructive systems (for a discussion on studying opposites see Camerer and Weber, 1999). For example, given the centrality of E-mail, the hand-written note tactically deployed can have a positive impact upon social relations because of what it symbolises aside from the instrumental content of the communication.

## Predicting destructiveness

De-skilling and automation can be efficient in stable environments where tasks lend themselves to sub-division (‘the number of loaves is always the same’). The present study suggests, however, that techniques become counterproductive where complex nuanced work is involved. While more sophisticated technology may be invented to cope with complexity, nevertheless the scope for automation and de-skilling may be limited as computers observe the law of diminishing returns (Angell and Ilharco, 2004). We need heuristics to estimate those limits. In other words, how high can Icarus safely fly?

Not flying high enough may be wasteful as organisations can make the opposite error of lavishing resources on standard tasks. The model used by ABC was successful in the banking industry because tasks like approving loan applications are highly amenable to automation. Metaphor is not theory but it may be the precursor of theoretical development. The image of Icarus plunging to his death could be the genesis of a mathematical model for predicting at what level of complexity the wings of automation start to melt.

## Implications for practice

Since destructiveness only emerges post instillation, prevention through better requirements definition is better than cure – but whose requirements? ABC should have followed Deming’s (1986) advice and put the customer first and conducted a pilot study before implementing major change. To re-frame the issue, the key to avoiding destructiveness may be to strive for authoritative systems. Designers cannot confer authority on a system. Rather just as a textbook becomes an authority through being consulted regularly (Wrong, 1979), an authoritative information system reflects the quality of a relationship between parties based on mutual recognition of each other’s needs. As we saw here, when one party puts their instrumental concerns over the needs of another party, the relationship is destroyed and authority fails (Knights and Roberts, 1982).

## Breakout strategy

Masuch (1985, see also Watzlawick, 1993) argues that vicious circles are virtually inescapable. Certainly, it is easier to treat the symptoms, for example, by employing a customer relations manager. Perhaps the key word is ‘virtually’. Escape is possible if we are willing to break bounding assumptions. Problems of mindlessness and alienation are hardly new in the IT literature. Previous research on the effective integration of social and technical systems assumed, however, that systems will be computer driven (comprehensively reviewed in Mumford, 2003). Emphatically, it is not suggested that ABC should abandon automation. The question is one of balance that is, how to combine technical and human capability and whether the optimal solution lies in diverting some resources to bolster staff training.

Will it ever happen? According to Fabrizio et al. (2005a, see also Merton, 1948, 1957: 475–490) wrong theories can seem right because they modify the reality they purport to explain. For example, a new options pricing model that contradicted published data eventually matched reality because practitioners began basing strategies on the model. The dominance of computer-based information systems may reflect a similar self-fulfilling prophesy as their very ubiquity may lead organisations to assume that they maximise efficiency. Herding behaviour means decisions appear to be based on reliable information but it is like choosing a restaurant assuming that because it is full, it must be good, or in this case, because automation has been successful in other industries.

Fabrizio et al. (2005b: 33, see also Klein and Lyytinen, 1985) subsequently argued that valid and important prescriptions for action (like developing people) are often ignored because of the cost implications or if change contradicts dominant ideological and theoretical world views. Ostensibly organisations deploy computers to enhance efficiency. Critical theorists might argue, however, that their latent function is control (see Merton (1957: 115–121) for a discussion of the significance of latency). Power in organisations derives from ownership and means of control of the means of production ‘overlain with knowledge of the methods of production’ (Clegg, 1981: 547, see also Alvesson and Willmott, 1996: 145–151). Computers place all three elements of control firmly in the hands of the organisation – that is why ‘we have to have a system for everything’ rather than empower employees to do a better job.

## Shattering the old frame of vision

In contrast to this disturbing thesis, Brown (1977) argues that by exposing the supposedly efficient as inefficient and wasteful, irony can shatter the old frame of vision. Indeed, the influential Financial Times newspaper (Hunt, 2005) recently reported that organisations are becoming sceptical about whether IT really is capable of producing promised quantum leaps in functionality and flexibility. The present study suggests that such questioning is healthy. Besides, the shattering may come from an unexpected quarter. The present study also suggests that perceived entry barriers created by technology requirements may be more apparent than real in certain industries (see Willcocks and Lester,

1996). Just as the internet has enabled independent estate agents and small solicitors’ firms to survive despite seemingly poor odds, systems driven monoliths like ABC could be challenged by enterprising ‘minnows’ using relatively primitive technology.

## Concluding comments

Baskerville and Land’s (2004) seminal work challenges the popular assumption that computer-based information systems invariably benefit organisations. The present study built upon the author’s insights by attaching new literature to the topic predicting that the seeds of destruction may lie in the very things that attract organisations to computers in the first place.

Both studies show that computers can become destructive when complex and nuanced work gets reduced to what a machine can handle, whether it is human communication or data processing. In addition, the present study suggests that destructiveness can manifest itself as the production of vicious circles that are not detectable by standard measures of efficiency and all the more invidious for becoming the ‘normal’ state of affairs.

The most important lesson for practice is that making optimal use of IT does not mean using computers for everything. Conceivably better, that is, more intelligent technology capable of handling complexity may be invented. Until then the most effective organisations will not be those with the most powerful or comprehensive computer-based information systems but those who recognise the limits of their technology.

Helga Drummond is Professor of Decision-Sciences at the University of Liverpool Management School.
