## a b s t r a c t

Healthcare alliance networks have developed rapidly in the US. Yet, research has not kept pace. This study examines two interrelated healthcare research needs—the value of information technology (IT) and the complementarity of IT with process and decision-making (PDM) integration. Hypotheses relate (i) IT integration, (ii) PDM integration, and (iii) alliance network integration (complementarity of i and ii) to performance. The mixed results lend support to the complementarity of IT with PDM integration and call for a more holistic perspective of healthcare alliance network.

## 1. Introduction

When describing the healthcare industry (an industry which now accounts for one-sixth of the US gross domestic product and is responsible for the betterment and maintenance of human life) Herzlinger [36, p. 58] stated:

Health Care—in the United States, certainly, but also in most other developed countries—is ailing and in need of help…The wellknown problems range from medical errors…to soaring costs… Such problems beg for innovative solutions involving every aspect of healthcare—its delivery to consumers, its technology, and its business models.

In an attempt to mitigate the problems found in the healthcare industry, organizations have actively formed strategic alliances, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “clusters of organizations that make decisions jointly and integrate their efforts to provide a service” [1, p. 2]. Strategic alliances are further de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “two or more organizations that contractually pool resources to achieve a long-term strategic purpose that is not possible for a single organization” [40, p. 71]. The key premise for forming these alliances is to integrate the otherwise autonomous organizations [1,24,60,70,86], to promote collaboration and cooperation [9,56,71], to improve the quality of medical services [42], to reduce costs [73] and, in general, to achieve performance improvement and competitive advantage [40,48,59,63,74].

Alliance formation continues to gain popularity across industries, with the number of new alliances growing by approximately 25% per year [53]; yet the failure rate of alliances is estimated at about 50% [41]. Much of this failure is attributed to the inability to assess the value and performance bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the alliance [41,53]. The same issues have been observed within the healthcare industry, causing many to question the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of alliance arrangements [18]. For example, [68] observed slowed or stalled progress in the medical service quality and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of healthcare alliance networks. In fact, some have considered the dissolution of these alliances due to the inability to quantify the anticipated improvements in either medical service quality or <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance [49,61].

In this paper, we argue that strategic alliances in healthcare can provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, but the formation of the alliance network is a starting point of integration and for bene<sup>fi</sup>t realization. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we hypothesize that three integration measures will signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly explain the bene<sup>fi</sup>t differences among healthcare alliance networks. While we will further explain each of these, we believe it is important to introduce them:

Information technology (IT) integration—Alliance network capability that provides seamless access to timely information within and among the network members.

Process and decision-making integration—Alliance network capability that allows for collaborative and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient decision-making within and among the network members.

Alliance network integration—Alliance network capability that is created from the synergistic effect of IT and Process/decisionmaking integration.

The literature base related to integration (e.g., supply chain integration, buyer–supplier integration) is quite informative, but there are several interrelated research needs with respect to integration in healthcare alliances—these needs are discussed in the following three paragraphs.

First, it is unclear if the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the majority of the integration research will transcend to the healthcare setting. While there is an abundance of integration research efforts [e.g., 21–23,27,31,46,52,58,75,76], the majority of them focus on product-based chains (i.e., in a manufacturing setting) and thus primarily exhibit sequential interdependence [72,78]. Healthcare alliances, which are service-based chains, exhibit reciprocal interdependence, which is the highest form of interdependence [c.f., 72,78], thus the ef<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fl</sup>ow and use of information will likely be more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to accomplish, resulting in an even greater need for effective integration [57,72,74]. Therefore, integration needs to be further examined in service-based settings such as in healthcare alliances.

Second, although there are studies that have focused on integration in healthcare alliances, they have focused on either process and decision-making integration [14,20,43,67,70,71] or IT integration [45,73], but not both simultaneously. By not analyzing both simultaneously, researchers have not captured the potential for a synergistic effect between them (the potential synergistic effect is supported by the concept of complementarity [87] which we discuss in the next section of this paper). This shortcoming may result in the value of integration being underestimated in healthcare alliances— this could partially explain the questions that have arisen relative to the value of these alliances [18,49,61,68]. Therefore, healthcare alliance integration needs to be more fully examined to capture the potential synergistic effects among IT and process and decisionmaking integration.

Finally, virtually all of the studies in the healthcare area on alliance networks are case studies. Although informative, the maturing of the literature base warrants larger scale, empirically-based, examinations of integration efforts and potential resulting performance enhancements. For example, while integration of business processes and IT has been purported to be critical to healthcare alliance network success, larger scale evidence of this relationship is lacking in the literature [40,51,70,74].

These three interrelated needs found in the healthcare alliance integration body of knowledge provide the motivation for this study. This study centers on integration in alliance networks within the healthcare industry known as integrated delivery systems (IDSs)—it should be noted that IDS are not information systems, they are strategic alliances. There are approximately 450 IDSs in existence within the United States [37]. IDSs take on various legal and contractual forms [20,57,84], but are typically governed by a formal organizational structure with a network level executive management team that oversees the diverse and exclusive combinations of hospitals, physician practices, Home healthcare, laboratories, pharmacies, and other medical care providers (see Appendix A for an example of an IDS). Each of the medical providers within the IDS may be directly or indirectly involved in the care of a particular patient, depending upon the nature and severity of the illness or condition [86]. The goal of IDSs is to provide a diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed, comprehensive continuum of care [20,57,86].

## 2. Theoretical foundation

Thompson's Interdependence Theory proposes that organizations, although natural and open systems, will work to achieve bounded rationality at different levels of interdependence [78]. This rationality is most often achieved through effective coordination [72]. Labeled pooled, sequential, and reciprocal interdependence, the strength of interdependence increases across each of these levels [78]. In addition, each level exhibits a greater degree of complexity in terms of the <sup>fl</sup>ow of inputs and outputs. For example, pooled interdependence is the weakest form, characterized by loosely coupled entities that provide direct input to a common node but have no connection to one another. At the other extreme, reciprocal interdependence represents the strongest level of interdependence. All entities may be directly linked to all other nodes, depending on the interaction; and these linkages may change with each new interaction. Further, the outputs of each entity may be direct inputs to any other node and vice versa. This results in a high level of complexity and a great need for effective coordination [72,78]. Thompson's levels are not mutually-exclusive [78]. In other words, an organization exhibiting reciprocal interdependence most likely also exhibits sequential and pooled interdependence among different sub-components of the network. Research suggests that reciprocal interdependence is most prevalent in service organizations such as airlines and healthcare providers [72,78]. Reciprocal interdependence, due to the complexity of the relationships, warrants a high level of coordination and collaboration [59].

Coordination mechanisms are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a means to link entities of the network to enable communication and common action, while also building a common pool of knowledge [72,78]. Thompson [78] argues that coordination becomes increasingly important across the three levels of interdependence, and this coordination is best accomplished through effective knowledge-sharing and communication. He suggests that using an appropriate coordination mechanism will enable and enhance coordination, and he offers mutual adjustment as a valuable mechanism for those networks at reciprocal interdependence. Mutual adjustment allows for the transmission of information during action and is best suited for those networks exhibiting high levels of unpredictability and variability, such as that evidenced in healthcare alliance networks. Further, mutual adjustment allows for joint problem-solving and decision-making [72,78].

Coordination among healthcare networks is facilitated by the network's resources and capabilities. These resources and capabilities can be both tangible and intangible, but should enable integration across all entities of the network [85]. When effectively utilized, the network will be able to integrate both IT and business processes, thereby enhancing coordination and collaboration across the network [60]. One weakness in recent studies of the value of IS resources is the lack of consideration of the complementary role of IS. IS has not often been shown to directly provide the necessary levels of mutual adjustment needed in reciprocal interdependence, perhaps because the technologies, though valuable, cannot accomplish effective coordination in isolation. Instead, it is the complementarity of the IS resources with the business resources that often provides the desired levels of communication and common action [87]. Complementarity between organizational resources arises when the enhancement of one also raises the value of the other and vice versa [87]. To further explain, take two resources in an organization where each provides some value to that organization. Taken together, we might see that the value added is simply the summation of the value provided by the two resources. However, if the two resources, say A and B, are complementary, the combined value of the two will be greater because in some way resource A either accelerates, increases, intensi<sup>fi</sup>es, promotes, or in some other way adds to resource B so that the value provided by B is now greater than it was without its interaction with A. Resource B, likewise, affect A in one of these ways so that now resource A provides more value than before its interaction with B. The overall effect is that the complementary effect of the two resources, A and B, has produced an overall value greater than the sum of the two.

Therefore, more overall value is created without an increase in the amount of resources. Thus, a call exists for examinations of the value of resource complementarity [81].

Extending Interdependence Theory to the healthcare alliance network, the value of the chosen resources, both assets and capabilities, should be de<sup>fi</sup>ned by their ability to enable mutual adjustment through ef<sup>fi</sup>cient, effective knowledge-sharing and joint decision-making [78,85]. In turn, this should reduce the level of unpredictability and variability present in reciprocal interdependence. In addition, the effective emphasis on both IT integration and process and decision-making integration, and the complementarity of the two, should result in resources that are rare and appropriate, thereby providing sustainable performance improvement for the healthcare alliance network [81]. The present study investigates the nature of IT integration, process and decision-making integration, and the complementarity of these among healthcare alliance networks. We examine the value of these resources to aid in the improvement of cost and quality outcomes for the healthcare alliance network. ## 4. Hypothesized relationships

## 4.1. IT integration to performance outcomes

Interdependence Theory posits that alliance networks will strive for rationality across different levels of interdependence. In particular, healthcare alliance networks operate at a level of reciprocal interdependence, the highest level identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by [78]. Reciprocal interdependence, characterized by a need for immediate knowledgesharing and joint decision-making, requires the greatest efforts at coordination if the network arrangement is to be successful. This is crucial in the healthcare industry, as immediate access to reliable knowledge is critical for effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient patient care. Coordination across and within network entities is most effectively enabled and enhanced by IT [60,72]. The most appropriate IT resources for an alliance network should be those that offer spanning capabilities to enable integration of all network participants [81]. These spanning capabilities are probably best observed in network systems. Network systems are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as combinations of IT components integrated to enable and enhance the goals of the alliance network by providing effective knowledge-sharing and communication for all participants [47]. Key to this de<sup>fi</sup>nition is “combination”, which suggests that the integration of IT resources across network participants is much more bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for network performance than would be realized from each of the components in isolation [72]. While the resources contributed by individual entities may not be rare and inimitable of their own accord, it is the unique combination of these resources at the network level that provides real value, rarity, and appropriateness that will, in turn, lead to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant performance improvements for the alliance network. By enhancing coordination efforts, IT integration should lead to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant performance improvements and competitive advantage in [2,81]. Thus, the value of IT integration is most fully realized through improved ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness that results in these performance improvements [2,81].

Empirical evidence of the performance bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of IT integration is abundant in the literature [e.g., 35,48,77,83], particularly in investigations of performance at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level or among product-based supply chains. In most of these studies, the performance bene<sup>fi</sup>ts were realized through the ability of IT integration to enhance communication and access to information. In turn, effective communication and information sharing enhance the coordination of activities and decisions across the network, thereby improving alliance network performance [60,72,78]. These prior studies offer valuable insight for our study, limited only by the level of analysis. Considering these empirical examples along with Interdependence Theory and complementarity, we argue that similar results should be expected in healthcare alliance networks. Because network systems are expected to bring about better performance than that of individual components, we argue that IT integration across the complementary resources of the network participants should lead to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant performance improvements at the network level.

Recognizing that the healthcare industry places great importance on cost reduction and patient care quality as signi<sup>fi</sup>cant indicators of success and competitive advantage, it is necessary for healthcare alliance networks to employ an integrated set of IT assets and capabilities that will ultimately result in lower costs and higher quality patient care. Therefore, based on the theories and arguments presented, we offer the following hypotheses:

H1a. IT integration will have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on healthcare alliance network quality performance improvement.

H1b. IT integration will have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on healthcare alliance network <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance improvement.

## 4.2. Process and decision-making integration to performance outcomes

The very formation of an alliance network suggests that alliance members expect the network to bring about greater performance improvements than they have experienced individually. In fact, some have argued that an alliance network itself is a resource under the Resource-Based View (RBV), as it provides rarity, value, and inimitability through its unique organization and combination of entities [28,33]. Additionally, most network members choose to join an alliance network because of a de<sup>fi</sup>ciency within their own organization. This de<sup>fi</sup>ciency may be centered on resources, knowledge, or expertise. By joining with other network participants, the interdependence on the assets, capabilities, knowledge, and expertise among network entities encourages a higher level of coordination and communication among and across members [48,79]. In particular, reciprocal interdependence exhibited in healthcare alliance networks necessitates a high level of coordination to ensure process and decision-making integration; and this integration will lead to <sup>fl</sup>exibility, collaboration, and expedited delivery of products and services that, in turn, result in cost reductions and higher quality for the alliance network [8,72].

Research has shown that “organizations who make decisions jointly and integrate their efforts to produce a product or service adjust more rapidly to changing technologies and market conditions, develop new products and services in a shorter time period, and provide more creative solutions in the process” [1, p. 2]. Prior studies have supported this argument and have observed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cost and quality improvements through process and decision-making integration [20,27,52]. Most have found that effective process and decisionmaking integration enables a necessary level of collaboration and coordination for mutual adjustment that will provide the alliance network with a more in depth understanding of the customers' needs, thereby positioning the alliance network to provide customers with higher quality services while simultaneously reducing costs.

While much of the research on process and decision-making integration has commenced around product-based alliance networks, a few studies have investigated this among service-based alliances in the healthcare industry with similar <sup>fi</sup>ndings [14,43,67]. In each of these studies, process and decision-making integration provided assets and capabilities to the healthcare entities across the network that enabled a complete and coordinated continuum of patient care. In turn, the value of these assets and capabilities was realized through cost reductions and higher quality patient care.

Based on the theories and prior research discussed here, we hypothesize the following:

H2a. Process and decision-making integration will have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on healthcare alliance network quality performance improvement.

H2b. Process and decision-making integration will have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on healthcare alliance network <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance improvement.

## 4.3. Alliance network integration to performance outcomes

Interdependence Theory tells us that alliance networks exhibiting reciprocal interdependence use mutual adjustment as an optimal coordinating mechanism to ensure maximum performance [78]. Mutual adjustment is a process of joint problem-solving and decision-making that relies on instant feedback and access to information [72]. Skipper et al. [72] also tell us that accomplishing mutual adjustment can be complicated, as information is being contributed and used across a number of entities, often simultaneously. This complication may be even further exacerbated in a healthcare setting, where successful mutual adjustment must address the need for information management, optimization of resource and information sharing, optimal clinical decision-making, and an overall network-wide focus on the patient [29,30]. The RBV argues that alliance networks will need a set of valuable and rare assets and capabilities that will not only enable mutual adjustment, but that will ultimately lead to competitive advantage for the alliance network.

Given the complexity of healthcare alliance networks and the intricacies of providing patient care, we argue that IT integration and process and decision-making integration in isolation may not provide the value, rarity, and inimitability necessary for sustained performance improvement. While each of these should indeed lead to reduced costs and higher quality patient care, an isolated emphasis on IT integration and process and decision-making integration is likely only a starting point toward sustained performance improvement and competitive advantage. We argue that the desired value, rarity, and inimitability will be more readily observed through the complementarity of IT integration and process and decision-making integration. Complementarity between organizational resources arises when the enhancement of one also raises the value of the other and vice versa [87].

Researchers support the idea that maximum value is achieved through an emphasis on the synergies of IT and business resources [7,66,81], yet these synergies have often been overlooked in IT research. Some argue that IT resources are best suited to enhance or enable business processes so as to create competitive advantage [39].

In fact, one of the key concepts of complementarity is that the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the synergistic combination of two or more organizational resources are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater than the sum of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the individual resources [4]. Complementarity offers a set of resources that is not easily diagnosed and duplicated by competitors, thereby offering greater competitive advantage than would be achieved by individual resources.

While evidence certainly exists to suggest that IT integration has a direct and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on performance, there is doubt about the longevity of this impact. Some suggest that IT integration alone may offer a short-term performance bene<sup>fi</sup>t, but it lacks rarity and will soon be imitated by others [81]. Thus, the opportunity for competitive advantage as a result of IT integration alone may be lost in the longer term. On the other hand, if IT integration is used in support of process and decision-making integration, the complementarity of the two will result in a rare, valuable, and inimitable resource that should indeed result in sustained competitive advantage. We argue that alliance network integration is itself a resource, as the complementarity of IT integration and process and decision-making integration offers a set of unique assets and capabilities for the alliance network. Thus, based on the theories and discussion above, we offer the following hypotheses:

H3a. Alliance network integration will have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on healthcare alliance network quality performance improvement.

H3b. Alliance network integration will have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on healthcare alliance network <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance improvement.

## 5. Methodology

## 5.1. Unit of analysis and participants

The population of interest was the approximately 450 IDSs (healthcare alliance networks) currently active in the United States. The targeted participant was the corporate level CIO for the IDS network (i.e., not a <sup>fi</sup>rm-level CIO). While individual <sup>fi</sup>rms within the network may have their own IT directors or CIOs, those invited to participate in this study are part of the executive management team for the IDS. Their job is to provide IT direction and oversight for the entire alliance network. Thus, they were chosen due to the need for survey participants to have a broad level of understanding and knowledge regarding the structure of the healthcare alliance network and the current state of both IT integration and process and decisionmaking integration within the healthcare alliance network.

## 5.2. Secondary data

Two important sources of data for the study were the HIMSS Analytics Database and the American Hospital Directory. Secondary data from these sources was used to measure the six quality and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance variables. This performance data was aggregated to the IDS level. While this is appropriate given the intent of our study, we believe there is a potential limitation of this aggregation related to the mortality rate measure. Patients with terminal illnesses may be transferred from smaller hospitals to larger ones due to quality of life issues and thus could affect the mortality rates. To the extent that patients are transferred from smaller hospitals to larger ones within the same IDS, our results would not be affected. Although this appears to be appropriate given that the intent of IDSs is to provide a seamless continuum of care, we have no way of determining if transfers are within the same IDS or not and therefore we believe it is important to note this as a potential limitation. Despite this, we believe the secondary data is appropriate for our study and overcomes one of the biggest threats to pure survey research, common method bias.

Common method bias is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “variance that is attributable to the measurement method rather than to the construct of interest” [64, p. 879]. The potential for common method bias is particularly problematic within survey research when the independent and dependent variables are captured from a single respondent. As recommended [64], the use of secondary data for the performance outcomes allowed us to gather data for the independent and dependent variables through different methods and from distinct sources, thereby reducing the likelihood of common method bias in our results.

## 5.3. Primary data

A survey instrument was used to collect primary data to represent the independent variables. The survey was developed primarily from multiple existing scales rather than introducing a new instrument [10,50,54]. A pretest of the survey instrument was conducted using doctoral students and professors in the areas of management information systems, organizational behavior, strategic management, and human resource management. The results of the pretest indicated no major issues with regard to the instrument; however, the wording of a few items was re<sup>fi</sup>ned to improve clarity. The items, shown in Table 2, were measured on a <sup>fi</sup>ve-point Likert scale that ranged from strongly disagree to strongly agree.

## 5.3.1. Survey administration

Participation invitations were emailed to the corporate level CIO of all active US healthcare alliance networks (IDSs) listed in the HIMSS Analytics Database (450 total) with published email addresses. Included in the request was a description of the study, the purpose, and the objectives, as well as a link to the survey. Participants were offered a copy of the summarized study results for their willingness to complete the survey. Reminder emails were sent at 2-week intervals, for a total of 4 emails.

5.3.2. Measurement—IT, process and decision-making and alliance network integration

In line with Kilbridge's [42] suggestions, three facets of IT integration were measured (see Table 2). First, IT <sup>fl</sup>exibility and accessibility measured the <sup>fl</sup>exibility of IT resources to provide integration and seamless sharing of information throughout the organization [10]. Second, IT modularity measured the modularity and ease of application development within the organization [10]. Third, IT planning measured the existence and appropriateness of organizational IT plans and the degree of widespread participation in, and effectiveness of, an IT steering team [50].

Four facets of process and decision-making integration were measured (see Table 2). Cross-functional teams measured the level of horizontal integration of employees from various functions and organizations for the completion of work [54]. Communication measured the speed, ease, and abundance of communication within and across levels of the organization [54]. Collaboration and business process integration (both developed for this study) measured the collaboration present among the participants within the alliance network to solve problems and address the needs of patients and the integration and centrality of administrative functions across the alliance network, respectively.

Alliance network integration was measured as the interaction of IT integration and process and decision-making integration. Moderation [15] was used to calculate the synergistic effect of these two measures. This is in line with the concept of complementarity, which purports that the effects of these two resources will likely be substantially greater than their sum. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, each factor of IT integration was multiplied by each factor of process and decision-making integration.

## 6. Results

## 6.1. Sample and sample bias test

From the total population of 450 IDSs contained in the HIMSS Analytics Database, an initial sample of interest consisting of 394 CIOs with published email addresses was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Of the 394 IDSs sent the <sup>fi</sup>rst email invitation, 25 were returned due to invalid email addresses or electronic refusals of delivery. Five email addresses were corrected and the email invitation was resent, resulting in a <sup>fi</sup>nal sample of interest of 374 IDSs. After the initial email invitation and three subsequent email reminders, 105 IDSs responded, for a response rate of 28%. Five were removed for incomplete data; and the only response from a for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t IDS was removed to ensure that the <sup>fi</sup>nancial goals of the alliance networks would be more consistent by analyzing only not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t IDSs. Descriptive statistics for the <sup>fi</sup>nal sample of 99 respondent healthcare alliance networks are provided in Table 3. A test for sample bias (respondents versus non-respondents) was conducted using t-tests based on the variables in Table 3 that were obtained from the HIMSS Analytics Database. No signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences were found.

## 6.2. Data analyses

Empirical examinations of the hypotheses were conducted in two parts. First, steps were taken to ensure validity of the survey instrument and the scales and items contained within the instrument. Con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis was conducted for all latent constructs in the model. Goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t for the measurement model was assessed using several <sup>fi</sup>t indices [34]. In addition, the strength of factor loadings was examined. Potential adjustments to the measurement model were considered only if appropriate and theoretically supported. Second, hypothesis testing was conducted using Partial Least Squares (PLS) [16].

## 6.3. Goodness of fit and validity

Con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis was conducted in AMOS to assess the goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t and validity of the constructs. ITM3 and ITI2 were removed from the IT Modularity scale and IT Flexibility and Accessibility scale due to low factor loadings of .26 and .43. Convergent validity was demonstrated, as the factor loadings for all remaining items on their respective constructs were acceptable, ranging from .54 to .86 [11,34,44]. Further, modi<sup>fi</sup>cation indices were examined for possible cross-loadings across scales. No signi<sup>fi</sup>cant modi<sup>fi</sup>cation indices were suggested, thereby supporting discriminant validity [44]. As presented in Table 4, the goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t indices indicated acceptable <sup>fi</sup>t for all constructs.

## 6.4. Scale reliability

Cronbach's alpha, obtained from AMOS , and the composite reliability, obtained from PLS-Graph 3, were used to assess scale reliabilities (Table 5). All factors demonstrated acceptable Cronbach's Alpha and composite reliability levels, ranging from .71 to .89 and .82 to .90, respectively [34,38,55]. A measure of factorial validity common to PLS is the Average Variance Extracted (AVE). While strict thresholds for this measure are yet to be determined, an AVE of approximately .50 is desirable [32,34]. As such, all factors demonstrated AVEs above the suggested threshold, ranging from .51 to .65.

## 6.5. Analysis of statistical power

Statistical power for the study was conducted according to the recommendations of [3] and the standards established by [19]. Because effect sizes for the scales were not readily available, we assumed a medium effect size, deemed to be appropriate for IS research [3,19]. Based on Cohen's [19] tables, a sample size ranging from 68 (pb.05) to 108 (pb.01) is necessary to produce a desired power level of at least .80 for medium effect sizes. Our sample of 99 falls within this range, thereby suggesting that our hypotheses tests demonstrate acceptable power levels.

## 7. Discussion

## 7.1. Theoretical contribution

Strategic alliances in healthcare networks are frequently being adopted as a means to improve performance and create competitive advantage [63,74]. Healthcare alliance network participants are organized jointly to enhance quality and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance across the network [1,49,63,74]. The integration of business processes, functions, and tasks, enabled largely by IT, appears to be critical to healthcare alliance network success; yet empirical evidence of this relationship is lacking [70,74]. Therefore, this study has provided some much needed attention to this area by examining the relationships of IT integration, process and decisionmaking integration, and alliance network integration to quality and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance improvement. Patient days in the hospital, mortality rate, and hospital admissions were used as measures of quality that are appropriate for the healthcare industry [5,26]; operational cost, administrative cost, and clinical cost represented <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance outcomes [25,73].

## 7.1.1. IT Integration to performance

Hypotheses 1a and 1b predicted that IT integration would have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on both quality and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. Regarding quality, IT integration was linked to reductions in hospital admissions and mortality rate. Financial improvements in terms of reduced clinical cost and administrative cost as a result of IT integration were also demonstrated. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are in line with prior healthcare research, which has suggested a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvement in both cost and quality attributed to the use of IT and greater integration of IT resources [5,35,79].

The results of the present study support the work of [79] and [35], indicating that IT integration leads to improvements in patientcentered measures, such as mortality rate and admissions. Further, these results are in line with [5], who demonstrated a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cost savings associated with physicians' use of electronic order entry. Finally, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings lend support to the <sup>fi</sup>rm-level evidence within other industries. For example, in their investigation of IT integration in the airline industry, [77] also found a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between increasing levels of IT integration and cost reductions. Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the present study further emphasize the need for continual efforts to increase and improve IT integration for greater alliance network success, both in terms of quality and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance [84].

## 7.1.2. Process and decision-making integration to performance

Hypotheses 2a and 2b relate to the effects of process and decisionmaking integration on both quality and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance improvements. Support was lacking for all relationships between process and decision-making integration and quality and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings somewhat contradict prior research at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level [31,75], which suggested that as the business processes, functions, and tasks of the alliance network are increasingly integrated, costs should be reduced.

Although contrary to the anticipated results, the lack of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationships between process and decision-making integration and both quality and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance improvement is in line with the observations of [18]. These authors suggested that although healthcare alliance network formation had historically been popular for its perceived ability to improve cost performance, results had not shown this. The use of patient-centered quality outcomes as opposed to more user-centered operational outcomes may have contributed to these <sup>fi</sup>ndings. The push to form healthcare alliance networks began as recently as the early 1990s. Thus, because the healthcare alliance network is a relatively young concept, these networks may still be largely focused on organizational integration for business process improvement, as posited in the earlier stages of both the IT-Enabled Business Transformation Framework [80] and the IT integration life cycle [84]. Along those lines, process and decision-making integration at earlier stages of development may be more likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence changes to measures of administrative and operational quality, as opposed to those regarding patient care. It should be noted, however, that the paths from process and decision-making integration to patient days in the hospital, mortality rate, and admissions were negative, suggesting that favorable progress is being made in this area.

A second possible explanation for the lack of support regarding process and decision-making integration may be the high level of organizational complexity associated with newly formed and expanding healthcare alliance networks [18]. These alliances are formed by the union of otherwise autonomous organizations, each with its own governance structure, business processes, assets, and resources. Thus, it is reasonable that simply the formation of a healthcare alliance network is insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient to evoke performance improvement. Instead, improvements may be more readily realized through IT integration and the complementarity of IT integration and process and decision-making integration.

## 7.1.3. Alliance network integration to performance

Hypotheses 3a and 3b stated that alliance network integration would lead to improvements in both quality and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. Support was demonstrated for a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative relationship between alliance network integration and patient days in the hospital, mortality rate, hospital admissions, operational cost, administrative cost, and clinical cost.

The complementary view of alliance network integration employed in the current study, along with the data analysis results, support the arguments of prior literature [12,18,65] and suggest that both quality and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improved when IT integration and process and decision-making integration are jointly emphasized and achieved. Process and decision-making integration alone produced insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant results, and IT integration produced mixed results across quality and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. The notion of complementarity of resources has substantial support based on our results. The results indicate the likelihood of underestimating the value of IT when only the IT capabilities are evaluated. The combination of the IT and process variables in the form of alliance network integration produces highly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and consistent results across all measures of quality and cost. The value of IT integration in the complementary relationship in this study is clear, evident, and unambiguous in these results. This is a major contribution of this study and differentiates it from any previous examination of alliance network issues.

## 7.2. Implications for managerial decision-making

Healthcare organizations have actively formed strategic alliances in an attempt to improve patient care quality [42] and to reduce costs [73]. However, many healthcare alliance network administrators have expressed a hesitance to make additional investments in IT and these alliances given the pressures to control costs. In fact, some have begun to consider the dissolution of these alliances due to an inability to quantify the anticipated improvements in either quality or <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance [49,61]. Therefore, the results of the present study are timely and should aid in the justi<sup>fi</sup>cation of decisions by management to implement additional innovations, integration, and IT-enabled change within the alliance network.

The present study has demonstrated a number of important <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding alliance networks. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the empirical model suggest that greater alliance network integration is an important and necessary pursuit for the success of the healthcare alliance network. In particular, IT has been demonstrated to be a critical enabling force for alliance network integration through its enhancement of process and decision-making integration. This argument supports past researchers who have touted IT as one of the most critical enabling resources in the formation and success of alliance networks [51,63,74]. Therefore, healthcare alliance networks may seek even higher levels of IT and process and decision-making integration as important and valuable components of alliance network integration.

One important contribution of the present study is the use of patient-centered, objective measures of quality. The results of the present study suggest that IT integration in the healthcare industry serves not only to improve administrative costs, but also directly and favorably impacts the medical care of patients. Of those measures examined, healthcare administrators have identi-<sup>fi</sup>ed mortality rate as the most critical measure of patient care quality, noting the importance of identifying innovations, techniques, and medical discoveries that can aid in reducing the number of patient deaths [26]. Thus, the results of the present study suggest that both IT integration and alliance network integration have a direct and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on reductions in patient deaths. These results alone should spur the interest of healthcare alliance network administrators and should serve as a catalyst to promote the diligent pursuit of increased IT and alliance network integration.

It is rather IT's ability to enhance process and decision-making integration that affords a healthcare alliance network the greatest performance improvement, both in terms of quality and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. Thus, as healthcare alliance network administrators contemplate further investments in IT resources and tighter integration of the participating organizations, perhaps this research and the associated results can aid in the justi<sup>fi</sup>cation of these investments.

Based on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and discussion of the present study, instead of dissolution, healthcare alliance network administrators would be well-served to adopt a practice of continual dynamic innovation in terms of both IT resources and organizational functions and processes. As the lag effect of IT suggests, the maximum quality and <sup>fi</sup>nancial bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of integration may not have been fully realized due to the young age of many of these networks [11]. Consider the argument of the lag effect which suggests that new IT investments may require at least two to three years and even more to generate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant performance bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Then, allow ample time for healthcare alliance networks to expand, diversify, and integrate the IT and organizational resources of all network participants. This leaves very few years for the maximum performance bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to have materialized. In fact, [6] suggest that within the healthcare industry, organizational transformations of the magnitude required by healthcare alliance network expansion and sustained by IT integration may take as long as <sup>fi</sup>ve to seven years to fully produce tangible <sup>fi</sup>nancial bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Yet, the results presented in this study are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and favorable, thereby encouraging healthcare alliance networks to stay the course, to continue to pursue appropriate integration, and to exercise patience in terms of quantifying the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of these investments.

As healthcare alliance network administrators make the choice to expand and integrate, they must recognize that neither the formation of the healthcare alliance network nor the purchase of an ERP system is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient in isolation to bring about extensive improvements. As this study has demonstrated, IT integration includes the hardware and software, planning processes, and modularity and <sup>fl</sup>exibility of the chosen solutions. Similarly, as participants join the healthcare alliance network, the contract between providers is simply not enough to spawn performance improvement. Administrators must also encourage an atmosphere of effective communication, collaboration, and business process integration among all network participants. The synergy brought about by suf<sup>fi</sup>cient attention to each factor of process and decisionmaking integration, and the recognition and emphasis on IT to promote and enable information sharing, is ultimately what brings about performance improvement.

## 7.3. Concluding thoughts and future research

This study has presented a more holistic view of integration within the healthcare alliance network and has laid the groundwork upon which to build and extend alliance network research to other industries. Future research should build upon this study to consider the impact of integration, over time, on more complex treatments and medical procedures, and patient satisfaction measures. Methodologically, future studies should view our results with longitudinal data and consider the potential in<sup>fl</sup>uence of network complexity and diversity. Finally, future studies need to fully explore the synergistic effects of IT and process and decisionmaking integration. Studies that could provide detailed insights into how one form of integration complements the other would be particularly bene<sup>fi</sup>cial.
