# Do (how) digital natives adopt a new technology di<sup>f</sup>erently than digital immigrants? A longitudinal study

Ankit Kesharwani

Indian Institute of Foreign Trade, New Delhi, India

A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Digital natives Digital immigrants Computer engagement Longitudinal stud Decomposed theory of planned behavior Multi-group analysis

## A B S T R A C T

Although the information systems (IS) literature has revealed a variety of mechanisms involved in technology adoption and postadoption use, the literature lacks insights about how individuals with di<sup>f</sup>erent usage characteristics process the information related to new IS and how their belief judgments and use behavior unfold over time. This study <sup>fi</sup>lls this void in the literature by conceptualizing and testing a comprehensive model to investigate the impact of user orientation toward technology use by digital natives (DNs) and digital immigrants (DIs) on technology continuous use behavior. The e<sup>f</sup>ect of DNs and DIs is currently gaining the attention of researchers. This study investigated the postadoption and use behavior of these groups using a three-wave panel model and with decomposed theory of planned behavior (DTPB) as the initial adoption model. The longitudinal model is a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed framework that sheds light on four di<sup>f</sup>erent mechanisms underlying postadoption phenomena: (1) the belief judgment evaluation processes suggested by the DTPB model, (2) sequential updating mechanism, (3) feedback mechanism, and (4) habit mechanism. Based on multigroup analysis, we show that a clear pattern of di<sup>f</sup>erences in e<sup>f</sup>ect exists between DNs and DIs with respect to the sequential belief updating mechanism and that these results are relatively stable over time.

## 1. Introduction

Every generation is shaped by the major developments and move ments that take place during its time. The current generation is shaped by digital devices and ubiquitous technology. In today’s digitalized times, everything from tablets to health applications for parents to children’s games on computers has been a<sup>f</sup>ected by new era technologies. Major part of our daily activities, such as communication, meetings, reading, gaming, and entertainment, have now been digitalized ([1], 2016). Observing these behavioral preferences for the use of digital gadgets or technology, Prensky [2] has coined the terms “digital natives” and “digital immigrants” for the current tech-savvy generation. Digital immigrants (DIs) represent individuals born before the 1980s, or before the existence of digital technology; digital natives (DNs) refer to those born after the 1980s and exposed to these digital technologies at a very early stage of their lives. DIs too uses digital devices but only as adults; however, unlike DNs, their exposure to digital technology came at a much later stage of their lives. DNs, with early access to digital technologies, usually engrossed themselves in a networked world and were able to learn and use digital technology in a manner better than their counterpart DIs. Although many DIs have transformed themselves into expert users of digital technology, their attitudes toward technology di<sup>f</sup>er from those of the DNs [3]. These behavioral di<sup>f</sup>erences highlight a wide generation gap. Most studies have treated DNs and DIs as mutually exclusive [4–6].<sup>1</sup> Such studies place younger users born after the 1980s on one side of the gap (i.e., DNs) and adults born prior to the 1980s on the other side (i.e., DIs). Such a distinction, based on age, between these two types of users is problematic because the literature has focused primarily on the young.

Researchers also have criticized this age-based dichotomous classi-<sup>fi</sup>cation, advising instead that these two types of digital users (DNs and DIs) should be studied along a continuum depending upon their level of preference to use digital technologies [7,8,3]. These researchers argue that although DNs are assumed to be more tech-savvy, they share the same digital world with DIs, in which many DIs, older in age, not only command a superior knowledge but also are more pro<sup>fi</sup>cient users of digital devices than are young people. Additionally, Herring [9] said that neglected in most of this discourse about the internet generation and its transformative potential is the continued presence and in<sup>fl</sup>uence of adults in the larger digital landscape inhabited by young people. She particularly emphasized the characteristics of young people and their online and communication behaviors. While agreeing to the revolutionary impact of the digital disruptions, she recommends a paradigm shift from focusing only on the young generation to considering all generations. Thus, the present research has adopted the behavioral-based explanation of DN and DI classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Studies have shown that apart from age factor, these behavioral di<sup>f</sup>erences are due to digital environment and individual’s experience with and the breadth of technology used [10,11]. Shara<sup>fi</sup> et al. [12] has found that personality orientations in conjunction with socio-demographic variables can result in di<sup>f</sup>erential engagement modes and <sup>fl</sup>ow experiences. Therefore, even several older generation but socially advantaged people are found to be more tech-savvy than younger but socially disadvantaged people [13].

In this research, computer engagement has been used as an individual di<sup>f</sup>erence variable to study DNs and DIs. Because there is a lack of any accepted measurement scale for delineating DNs/DIs, an attempt was made to identify the closest variables that could be utilized to delineate both the groups and to match with the de<sup>fi</sup>nitional criteria of DNs/DIs. The rationale behind adopting the “computer engagement” scale to identify DNs/DIs is as follows: (1) the research attempts to delineate the digital users based on their behavioral di<sup>f</sup>erences rather than age. As there is an absence of an established measurement instrument (i.e., scale) to measure DNs/DIs, the study looked into lit erature to <sup>fi</sup>nd a suitable (proxy) scale to capture the behavioral differences (particularly with regards to the usage of digital devices such as computers and smartphones) between DNs and DIs. Additionally, as the study context was the implementation of a learning management system, which was at that time only accessible through computer (desktop/laptop compatible), the use of “computer engagement” scale was deemed as suitable; and (2) though age may provide opportunities to gain exposure to and familiarity with technologies and thereby lessen anxiety and promote interest and usage, it is engagement that is the key driver for them being comfortable with these technologies and thereby leading to greater usage. Thus, more than age, the engagement level identi<sup>fi</sup>es whether an individual is classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as DNs or DIs [3,8].

The academia and business worlds are also witnessing a similar paradigm shift in technology use behavior. Therefore, it becomes imperative to investigate whether DNs and DIs are accepting new information systems (IS) similarly, and, more importantly, to foster sustained use of the new IS, how their belief judgments and use behavior unfold over time. Each time users are exposed to a new technology, they develop beliefs concerning system use, such as ease of use and self e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and it is on these beliefs, they conduct their usage behavior. Although the initial adoption phenomenon has been studied through various initial adoption models, the eventual success of a new tech nology relies upon both its <sup>fi</sup>rst-time adoption (initial use) and the subsequent usage behavior (continued usage) of the users [13–17]. Continued usage behavior by an individual depends upon four pillars: (a) initial adoption, i.e., the e<sup>f</sup>ect of current belief evaluation on current use behavior; (b) sequential belief updating mechanisms, i.e., the e<sup>f</sup>ect of current belief evaluations on subsequent belief evaluations [18]; (c) feedback mechanisms, i.e., the e<sup>f</sup>ect of current use behavior on subsequent belief evaluations [19]; and (d) habit, i.e., the e<sup>f</sup>ect of current use behavior on subsequent use behavior [20].

Four leading IT/IS journals were chosen for a content analysis. These included Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Management Information Systems Quarterly, and Management Science. Their content from 2005 to 2015 revealed that though initial adoption was studied in over a hundred articles, literature on continued usage is fairly limited. Even for this literature, only two research articles [16,17] study it longitudinally. These two works investigated the important predictors of continued usage behavior by integrating the three postadoption mechanisms (sequential belief up dating, feedback, and habit) within an initial adoption theoretical fra mework of the technology acceptance model (TAM) [17] and the theory of planned behavior (TPB) [16]. Only Kim and Malhotra’s [17] study used primary data. This sole study, however, was limited by its use of a two-wave panel data model, where relationships found could be methodological artifacts. The sequential updating, feedback, and habit mechanisms appear only once in a two-wave panel model, thus not o<sup>f</sup>ering robust support to long-term continuous usage behavior. Kim and Malhotra [17] quote, “To gain deeper insight into continued IS usage, researchers are encouraged to conduct more than two waves of panel surveys” (p.753). The three-wave panel model should be preferred over the two-wave model because previous judgments could have long-term e<sup>f</sup>ects on the current judgment [16]. Even though Kim [16] used a three-wave panel model, the author employed secondary data represented by factor correlations. As the original studies did not consider issues of measurement errors when calculating factor correlations, the results of Kim [16] are likely to be erroneous. In addition to the above research gaps, assessment of common method variance and social desirability bias (SDB) were not performed in some of these studies.

Considering the above stated concerns, this study re-examines the postadoption mechanisms that in<sup>fl</sup>uence continued usage behavior of DNs and DIs. Thus, the objective of this research is to study postadoption phenomena of information technology usage of DNs and DIs. This study <sup>fi</sup>lls a void in the literature by conceptualizing and testing a longitudinal adoption model based on the decomposed theory of planned behavior (DTPB) [21]. Although Kim and Malhotra [17] and Kim [16] have used the TAM and the TPB, respectively, as their initial adoption models, our study uses DTPB as the initial model. The DTPB model has advantages over the other models because it includes salient beliefs that a<sup>f</sup>ect IT usage [22]. DTPB is better than TPB because of its increased explanatory power; further, DTPB provides a fuller understanding of usage behavior than that with TAM [21]. Our study investigates the continuous use behavior of DNs and DIs using a three-wave panel model based on primary data, with DTPB as the base model. Following e<sup>f</sup>orts in past research, this longitudinal model integrates the four postadoption phenomena/mechanisms. This study undertakes a multigroup analysis to examine the (non) invariance of the three-wave model between the two groups: DNs and DIs. In this research, the data are validated for common method bias (CMB) and SDB. Thus, our research plugs gaps identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in previous research.

To enhance organizational productivity, it is important for managers to help their workers develop a habit of using desirable technology features. The outcomes of this study may provide managers useful insights about how to ensure the optimum utilization of in formation technology keeping in mind the DI-DN classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of their workers. The model proposed in this study may further help in the deployment of new technologies. The organization of the remaining paper is as follows: Section 2 presents a review of the literature on DNs/ DIs and postadoption mechanisms. Section 3 describes the proposed model and hypothesis development. Section 4 discusses our research methodology and data collection. Section 5 provides details about the data analysis. Next, section 6 provides the results obtained from the analysis. Section 7 o<sup>f</sup>ers a discussion based on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings, and section 8 to section 11 present the theoretical contribution, managerial im plications, limitations and scope for future research, and conclusion of the study, respectively.

## 2.1. Digital Natives (DNs) and digital immigrants (DIs)

DIs refer to individuals born before the existence of digital tech nology [2]. Although DIs are also users of digital devices or information and communication technology, they started using these digital devices and technologies during their adult lives. For DIs, the saying “you can’t teach an old dog new tricks” is perhaps applicable here. Conversely, DNs are those born after the 1980s and exposed to these digital technologies at a very early stage of life, even before their teenage years. With early access to digital technologies, DNs usually engross themselves in a networked world and are more comfortable adopting digital technology than their DI counterparts. Prensky [2] uses this dichotomy to highlight di<sup>f</sup>erences in digital technology use preferences and to recommend changes in approaches to educate young tech-savvy students. Table 1 presents the key di<sup>f</sup>erences between DIs and DNs.

DNs are characterized as digitally <sup>fl</sup>uent with a variety of technol ogies because of their immersion in digital technology during their childhood and adolescence, when neural plasticity is high. This phenomenon habituates the young generation to certain behaviors that a<sup>f</sup>ect how these generations think and learn [23]. There has been a lack of evidence found in technology usage pattern between younger (most likely DN) and older (most likely DI) students and between students and faculty [24]. Immersion in digital technology is not age-dependent as the popular press suggests. Older populations can become quite comfortable with digital technologies, and DNs can limit themselves to using a smaller set of technologies and features [23]. Thus, age cannot be used as a measurement to distinguish between DNs and DIs. Most previous research that used age as the criterion to distinguish between DNs and DIs has serious limitations. Despite practitioners’ concerns about the DNs and DIs skill di<sup>f</sup>erences in the workplace, particularly concerning ubiquitous technology use, there is a scarcity of studies examining continued usage behavior with respect to di<sup>f</sup>erences in DNs and DIs.

Additionally, Yoo and Gothenburg [25] call for research on experiential computing that includes artifacts, actors, space, and time. The space and time elements de<sup>fi</sup>ne the context in terms of “where” and “when” the actors engage with the artifacts. Vodanovich et al. [3] suggests four key dimensions that appear to be relevant for technology use: users (DIs vs. DNs), systems (traditional IS vs. ubiquitous IS), activity (professional vs. personal), and context (o<sup>fi</sup>ce versus home) (Fig 1).

Although literature is abundant on the subject of traditional IS used by DIs for professional purposes (e.g., at the workplace), there is scarcity of research on ubiquitous IS used by DNs for professional and personal purposes at both the home and workplace.

Four primary approaches for measuring the variables were proposed in prior research, which could be adopted for classifying DNs and DIs in the research context. First, individuals’ technological characteristics can be captured with measures of technology anxiety, a closely related concept used in previous IT-adoption research (Venkatesh 2000). Second, a computer anxiety and apathy scale measure the three a<sup>f</sup>ective dimensions of computer use behavior, namely computer anxiety, computer apathy, and societal overemphasis of computers [26]. As both

DNs and DIs have realized the importance of ubiquitous technologies, the new generations of IS users typically have previous computer experience and have no hesitation in learning and/or accepting new technologies, the use of “technology anxiety” scale and “computer apathy and anxiety” scales to delineate DNs and DIs has been avoided.

Third approach, digital natives assessment scale (DNAS) measures the self-reported instrument designed to measure students’ perception of the degree to which they are DNs [27]. The 21-items scale measures the individual nativity of an individual across four factors, namely grow up with technology, comfortable with multitasking, reliant on graphics for communication, and thrive on instant grati<sup>fi</sup>cations and rewards. Though the scale was contemporary, in a validation study, Teo [28] himself has found that these factors were subject to computer experience of the individual, i.e., the factor-structure of the DNAS was different for low computer experience as compared to that for high computer experience. Finally, fourth approach, computer engagement re<sup>fl</sup>ects the degree of computer usage and does not have any negative e<sup>f</sup>ects on others’ behavior [29].<sup>2</sup> The type of computer engagement distinguishes between DNs and DIs – it is the innate urge, excitement, and psychological dependency in using technology and the resultant usage behavior that distinguishes DNs from DIs. In addition, the construct of high computing engagement <sup>fi</sup>rst arose in the factor analytic study of the “Computer Apathy and Anxiety Scale” [26], with high engagement being the other end of the apathy continuum, indicating a high degree of positive involvement in computing and being shown to be positively related to computing course performance. Given the context of high computer usage, the use of “computer engagement” scale was appropriate to conceiving DNs and DIs, as it will o<sup>f</sup>er following advantages:

a) It o<sup>f</sup>ers the mechanisms through which the DIs can progress to the DNs, i.e., it provides opportunities such that DIs can be made to become DNs by modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the systems, activities, and the other contextual factors such as task involvement.

b) It incorporates preferences and behavioral tendencies into the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, in line with suggestions of Vodanovich et al. [3] and Tufts [8] above.

c) Computer engagement-based conception of DNs/DIs is applicable to all types and generations of technologies and is compatible for all contexts be it learning or gaming, professional or personal, mandatory or choice-based, etc. This conceptualization based on computer engagement can stand steady even under circumstances when old technologies are to be re-launched or exported to technologi cally lagging markets.

Further, such a conceptualization is not a time-point-based permanent dyadic classi<sup>fi</sup>cation but an approach to conceive the DNs/DIs distinction as a dynamic concept that includes cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective elements and behavioral aspects of the users. Thus, making this study of adoption more real, holistic, and conceptually richer. Therefore, this study adopted the Charlton and Danforth [29] “computer engagement” scale to measure the degree to which an individual is a<sup>f</sup>ectively and cognitively involved with computer usage behavior, which is used to distinguish between DNs and DIs.

## 2.2. Postadoption mechanisms

For continuous use behavior, users’ prior belief judgments about the same technology could in<sup>fl</sup>uence their current belief judgments because information acquired from system exposure is de<sup>fi</sup>nitely an important source of information for current belief-judgment evaluations. As presented in Table 2, studies demonstrate that any discussion concerning continued usage of technology should consider the three postadoption mechanisms $[ 1 6 , 1 7 ] { : }$ (i) the e<sup>f</sup>ect of current belief evaluation on sub sequent belief evaluation (i.e., sequential belief updating), (ii) the e<sup>f</sup>ect of current behavior on subsequent belief evaluation (i.e., feedback), and (iii) the e<sup>f</sup>ect of current behavior on subsequent behavior (i.e., habit).

The theory of sequential belief updating provides the theoretical underpinnings for the often-observed intertemporal updates of user evaluations [30,31]. The theory posits that individuals do not comprehend information or cues in their original forms as they are presented. Rather, as Hogarth and Einhorn [31] said, “the prior knowledge is adjusted by the e<sup>f</sup>ect of succeeding pieces of evidence” (pg. 8). In the innovation adoption context, prior knowledge is referred to as an anchor, and the new piece of information is considered an adjustment (Venkatesh 2000). Studies have proven that individual technology use behavior is also cognitively regulated and monitored to a certain degree [32,33]. These studies have also empirically shown that prior behavior is not necessarily a good predictor of subsequent behavior. Thus, new information, if persuasive and relevant, can change and/or establish belief judgments, which eventually bring changes in later behavior.

As opposed to the common prejudice that individual behavior is mostly led by beliefs and attitudes, feedback mechanisms support an opposite causal relationship, i.e., that use behavior can also a<sup>f</sup>ect belief evaluations [34]. In adoption literature, there is su<sup>fi</sup>cient evidence that demonstrates the causal e<sup>f</sup>ect of past behavior on framing current belief judgments [35,36]. Moreover, when doing a meta-analysis of articles studying TPB and past behavior, Conner and Armitage [37] also <sup>fi</sup>nd a positive association between past behavior and current belief evaluations.

Habit demonstrates behavioral persistence over time. Although people perform a behavior for which they have strong behavioral intentions, sometimes they fail on intention and instead relapse to past behavior patterns, i.e., habit. Such behavior is said to be habitual and is guided by speci<sup>fi</sup>c situational cues that are not usually led by behavioral intentions [38]. Such repeated behavioral patterns develop a mental linkage between a particular behavior (e.g., for teenagers and visiting Facebook) and a situational cue (e.g., before sleeping at night) and do not require any conscious decision-making. Studies supporting habit mechanisms have consistently found that frequency of a past behavior explains more variance of current behavior than other behavioral variables such as intention. Additionally, in conditions in which other things remains constant, whether individual behavior was driven by habit or belief evaluations, the individuals are expected to follow their past behavioral patterns.

## 2.3. Integrated framework of technology use

Only two studies [16,17] researched the continued usage behavior of an innovation adoption utilizing the three postadoption mechanisms discussed above. Kim and Malhotra [17] proposed an integrated framework of technology use (IFTU) to understand the mechanisms through which users update their belief judgments on the use of a new technology, and gradually form a usage behavior and conform to their continued usage behavior. Keeping TAM as a base model, they integrated all three postadoption mechanisms in a two-wave panel model. Data were collected from 298 university students. The participants were asked to provide their response for their university intranet portal. This portal served as a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed access and dissemination point for a variety of student information such as campus news/announcements, personal/ academic/<sup>fi</sup>nancial information access, and e-mail access. Path mod eling was performed to analyze the data. According to their <sup>fi</sup>ndings that compared initial adoption models with proposed continuance usage IFTU model, IFTU explained more variance of both behavioral intention and the actual usage behavior.

Kim [16] extended the IFTU model proposed by Kim and Malhotra [17] by borrowing the process model of memory. His model illustrates how past experiences, such as prior evaluations and behavior, are accumulated into memory and how the outcomes of learning stored in the memory regulate subsequent evaluations and behavior. The memory perspective is particularly relevant in a longitudinal setting because ultimate memory, either explicitly or implicitly, will be the repository of a person’s experiences with a technology application and the source of any subjective viewpoint on the interpretation of new information related to the use of the application. Overall, the memory perspective is expected not only to o<sup>f</sup>er a more complete view of the four mechanisms but also to highlight the hidden process that is critical to the discussion of proximal versus distal e<sup>f</sup>ects (i.e., the e<sup>f</sup>ect of belief judgments or behavior at time “t” on belief judgment or evaluation on time “t+2”). To test this extended framework, Kim [16] has studied a three-wave panel model. Kim [16] used three di<sup>f</sup>erent datasets from two published articles (n = 195, 160, and 342), and the results lent support to the extended IFTU. In addition to proximal e<sup>f</sup>ects, two postadoption mechanisms, sequential updating and habit, were also found to have distal e<sup>f</sup>ects on the postadoption phenomena. This study contributes to the adoption literature by broadening the application of IFTU for postadoption phenomena in conditions in which an individual s memory can serve as a source of past use behavior or use experiences.

## 2.4. Research gaps

The following are some of the gaps that were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in existing research and which prompted our study. Although both longitudinal studies on IT adoption show that postadoption mechanisms can be in tegrated into one theoretical framework to predict postadoption beha vior, they su<sup>f</sup>er from the following limitations: (i) only Kim and Malhotra [17] use primary data; and (ii) although they used primary data, Kim and Malhotra [17] utilized a two-wave panel model in which the relationships found signi<sup>fi</sup>cant/insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant could be a matter of chance.

When studying di<sup>f</sup>usion of innovation in longitudinal settings, role of moderators (DNs vs. DIs) has been ignored. Whereas most previous IS research has used DIs as their subjects, the rise of DNs has created a paradigm shift in IS research [3]. Little is known about how these user groups (DNs and DIs) can a<sup>f</sup>ect shaping of continued usage behavior. Furthermore, previous research on DNs is erroneous because age is used as the criteria to di<sup>f</sup>erentiate the two groups, resulting in mixed conclusions [23]. However, engagement in technology does not depend upon age because older populations can gain expertise in using tech nology and younger generations may achieve only limited knowledge in its use [39].

Because of the limitations of previous research, it is imperative to re-test a three-wave longitudinal model by duly considering the differential IT usage characteristics of DNs and DIs. In the present research, we examine the continued IT usage behaviors of DNs and DIs and their di<sup>f</sup>erences based on DTPB, a more advanced model, for initia adoption and by employing primary data. We use theoretical scales to distinguish between DNs and DIs. A multigroup analysis is conducted to examine (non) invariance of the three-wave panel model between the two groups (i.e., DN and DIs). Before assessing the psychometric properties of constructs, the data are checked for CMB and SDB.

## 3. Research model and hypothesis development

To address the research gaps mentioned above, we build and vali date a three-wave continued usage model by integrating the postadoption mechanisms into the DTPB-based initial adoption model. Furthermore, we examine the e<sup>f</sup>ect of user types (DNs vs. DIs) on continued usage by incorporating these types into the continued usage model.

The present study will consider only proximal e<sup>f</sup>ects because the technology being studied requires high-frequency use, and memory literature shows that to make their evaluations, people are expected to quickly recall previous incidents of technology use for only a certain period of time (e.g., a week or a month). Then, the number of incidents recalled from previous incidents serves as a basis to form current judgments. As is the case with previous studies, we hypothesize that continued usage of a new information technology by individuals is in-<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the three postadoption mechanisms discussed above. Furthermore, we propose that the e<sup>f</sup>ects of these mechanisms are different for DNs and DIs. Additionally, we use a three-wave panel model by using primary data. Thus, this study further extends previous research in multiple directions.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst assess a nomological network of initial adoption model and then conceptualize a longitudinal model by integrating three postadoption mechanisms into the DTPB-based initial adoption model. When testing for the longitudinal model, we test all relationships hypothesized in the DTPB model at each wave. Researchers $[ 1 6 , 1 7 ]$ state that continued usage of technology should simultaneously consider the following three mechanisms: (i) the past behavior to current evaluation relationship (i.e., feedback), (ii) the past evaluation to the current evaluation relationship (i.e., sequential belief updating), and (iii) the past behavior to the current behavior relationship (i.e., habit). ## 3.1. Sequential belief updating

As presented in the literature review section, the sequential belief updating mechanism provides a theoretical basis for the intertemporal updates of user evaluations [30]. The sequential belief updating mechanism works similarly to the anchor–adjustment framework suggested by Venkatesh (2000). Anchor refers to the users’ past familiarity with a new IS, and adjustment is considered the users’ updated/refreshed familiarity with the system (Venkatesh 2000). Applying this sequential belief updating mechanism in a longitudinal setting, we propose that beliefs at $\mathrm { T } = 1$ will be the basis for subsequent evalua tions at T = 2, and, similarly, beliefs at T = 2 will act as an anchor for subsequent belief evaluations at $\mathrm { T } = 3 .$ . Using a neurobehavioral view, high computer engagement can rewire users’ brains and make them hypersensitive to rewards and other stimuli that modify their perception systems [40]. ## 3.2. Feedback mechanism

The feedback mechanism highlights the importance of current experience on subsequent belief evaluation. A positive relationship is found to exist between past behavior and current belief evaluations, such as attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and intention [37]. In routine tasks, individuals tend to elaborate and base their judgments on past behaviors [17]. Integrating a feedback mechanism with the DTPB-based initial adoption model, this study proposes that individuals (both DNs and DIs combined) use behavior (USE) at T = 1 will have a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on their PU, PEOU, CMPT, ATT, INI, FRI, SN, CSE, FC, and BI at T = 2 (and similarly for $\mathrm { T } = 3 )$ beyond the inter-temporal e<sup>f</sup>ects of user evaluations, as discussed earlier (Fig. 2).

Although positive association between past usage behavior and current belief is expected, overall, the association tends to be stronger for DNs than that to DIs. Because of computer usage a<sup>fi</sup>nity processes, individuals tend to engage in more high-risk behaviors, exhibiting im paired views of reality and developing in<sup>fl</sup>ated system evaluations [42]. DNs may actually utilize the system di<sup>f</sup>erently, in a way that is easier to use, making the system more useful and compatible to their needs. Because DNs spend more time with computers and enjoy using them, their positive use episodes will augment users’ positive perceptions of the system regardless of the features of the system [19]. Thus, a person with a high computer engagement is likely to have a higher level of positive appreciation for the features of the system, including useful ness, ease of use, perceived behavioral control, and compatibility. Accordingly, DNs who are highly engaged in computer technology will <sup>fi</sup>nd their system enjoyable, easier to use, and more useful, which contrasts with DIs with relatively lower use and enjoyment and, consequently, less positive evaluations of the system. We postulate:

H2a. Overall, for DIs and DNs combined, current use behavior (at time t) has a significant efect on subsequent belief evaluations (at time t + 1) when using DTPB as the initial adoption model.

H2b. DN/DI di<sup>f</sup>erence moderates the e<sup>f</sup>ect of current use behavior (at time t) on subsequent belief evaluation (at time $\mathbf { t } + \mathbf { 1 } )$ such that e<sup>f</sup>ect will be higher for DIs’ continued use behavior than those of DNs’.

Thus, we contend that USE at T = t will have a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on USE at $\mathrm { T } = \mathrm { t } + 1$ (refer Table 7). Because DNs are always willing to experiment with new innovations, <sup>fi</sup>nd new technologies easy to use, consider themselves to be technologically savvy, and rate innovation to be better than the status quo, they tend to adopt (and continue using) new technologies readily. The attitudes of DNs are distinctly di<sup>f</sup>erent from those of DIs because DNs are keen to try out new technologies and new ways of doing things without considering associated potential failures [46]. According to user acceptance theories, users (i.e., DIs) traditionally resist using new technologies and systems [47]. In contrast, DNs are eager to try new technologies including social networking tools. DNs are attracted to new technologies and often use them when they add value to their lifestyles [48]. ## 4. Research methodology

To restate, the objectives of the present research are (1) to re-test the continued usage behavior model using DTPB as the base model and (2) to assess the di<sup>f</sup>erential e<sup>f</sup>ect of DNs/DIs on continued usage behavior. To achieve the <sup>fi</sup>rst objective, a three-wave panel model was tested to investigate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of three postadoption mechanisms, namely sequential belief updating mechanism, feedback mechanism, and habit behavior on continued usage with DTPB as the initial adop tion model. For the second objective, a multigroup analysis was conducted to examine the (non) invariance of the three-wave panel model between the two groups (i.e., DNs and DIs).

To assess the impact of social desirability biasness on computer engagement scale, a correlation analysis was performed. The lack of correlation between them suggests the absence of SDB among respondents. The correlation analysis reveals that only three correlations were more than 0.30 and only one correlation was more than 0.40. The absence of high correlation among items of these two scales suggests the lack of SDB in target respondents while <sup>fi</sup>lling the survey instrument [76].

The main study was conducted in a premier management institute in India, where a new learning management system, “Quicforce,” had been implemented. Quicforce enables both instructors and students of post-graduate program in business management courses to deliver/receive course-related information and promote topic discussion/parti cipation online. Besides, it also facilitates co-creation and collaboration among students and course instructors through an online discussion forum, and also enables the students to take their class test/quiz online. The use of Quicforce was voluntary and students could access the same course and/or evaluation-related information through “CampusNet” (university Intranet) and Google groups. The <sup>fi</sup>rst phase of data collection (i.e., wave 1) was conducted only after a brie<sup>fi</sup>ng about the study (t = 1) was given to the participants. We then conducted the second phase (i.e., wave 2) two months after wave 1 (t = 2), and the third survey (i.e., wave 3) four months after wave 1 (t = 3). At wave 1, each respondent was provided with a consent form brie<sup>fi</sup>ng him/her about the research work and a request to participate honestly and to the best of their knowledge and understanding.

The measurement instrument was developed based primarily on existing, established scales from the literature (Appendix A). All items were measured on a 7-point Likert scale, with 1 representing “strongly disagree” and 7 representing “strongly agree.” At wave 1, we approached second semester (1054) students for their response. We received 948 completed questionnaires, representing a response rate of 90%. We found that 62 respondents had not completed the questionnaire properly, i.e., marked more than one score in most of the items, entered the same score in all items, and so forth. Hence, these 62 respondents were not included in the <sup>fi</sup>nal sample. Excluding these 62 responses from the wave 1 dataset, we were left with 886 survey responses. The median age of the respondents was approximately 22 years, and 62% of the respondents were male. Two months after wave 1, wave 2 survey was conducted on all of the 886 respondents who had successfully completed the wave 1 survey. As with wave 1 survey, we sent each respondent an invitation message reminding them about a lucky draw. Of the original 886 respondents, 810 completed this wave 2 survey, leading to a response rate of 91%. Four months after wave 1, 810 wave 2 respondents were approached to participate in wave 3, and 750 respondents (response rate 93%) returned the completed questionnaire. To assess nonresponse bias, a mean comparison was performed with respect to demographic variables (e.g., age, gender, and education) and all theoretical constructs. Thus, two T-tests were performed: <sup>fi</sup>rst between the respondents and nonrespondents at wave 2, and the second between the respondents and nonrespondents at wave 3. In both tests, the result showed that means values were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent for either group, hence, suggesting an absence of nonresponse bias in the dataset.

Discriminant validity is a measure to test whether the constructs intended to measure di<sup>f</sup>erent theoretical concepts do not highly correlate with one another. There are two ways to ensure discriminant validity: a pair-wise construct comparison method [55–57] and comparison of correlations between factors with the square root of average variance explained by individual factors [54]. In the pair-wise comparison method, we compare all 45 possible pairs for the 10 factors separately. For each pair, the chi-square value of the full model is compared with the chi-square value of the collapsed model. More precisely, the collapsed model is the same as the full model except that one pair of target factors is constrained to have a correlation of 1. Anderson and Gerbing [56] suggest that if the collapsed model is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and its chi-square value is greater than the values of the full model by four or more, then the free model re<sup>fl</sup>ects a better <sup>fi</sup>t than does the collapsed one, indicating that collapsed factors are not measuring the same concept. Hence, the increased chi-square values, i.e., collapsed factors, are discriminant from one another. As shown in Table 6, for each possible combination of 45 collapsed models, the chisquare value increases by more than four. Hence, as shown in Table 6, all factors are discriminants from one another.

Moreover, to ensure discriminant validity, Fornell and Larcker [54] recommend the comparison of correlations among constructs with the square-root value of average variance explained. The authors suggested that to achieve discriminant validity, the diagonal value should be greater than the nondiagonal values. As Table 7 clearly shows all eleven factors are di<sup>f</sup>erent from one another.

Here, though three correlation coe<sup>fi</sup>cients in Table 7(PU - CMPT, PU – BI, and CMPT – BI) have higher loadings than the recommended criterion of 0.70, chi-square di<sup>f</sup>erence values, as shown in Table 6 of pair-wise construct comparison for discriminant validity, suggest that the measurement model with these constructs separate has a better model <sup>fi</sup>t statistics than the measurement model where the correlation between these two constructs is constrained to one. Thus, based on both the approaches, study shows that all eleven factors are meeting the discriminant validity criteria, and hence, they are di<sup>f</sup>erent from each other. A similar procedure is also followed for wave 2 and wave 3 separately, which shows that all psychometric properties of the measurement instrument meet the cut-o<sup>f</sup> requirements across all measurement points.

## 6.1. Findings of multi-group analysis

For testing hypotheses, we perform multigroup analysis to test the invariance between the two groups (DNs and DIs). Establishing multi group invariance implies three key features: (1) the research model behaves invariant across each separate sample group, i.e., DN and DI (con<sup>fi</sup>gural invariance); (2) measurement instruments behave invariant across both sample groups (metric invariance); and (3) individual hy pothesized relationships are also invariant across the sample groups (structural invariance) [58].

When analyzing invariance for multiple groups, a baseline model is <sup>fi</sup>rst established. To create a baseline model, the longitudinal research model is tested for the overall sample (i.e., for both the DNs and the DIs). This longitudinal research model with the overall sample is referred to as the con<sup>fi</sup>gural model. To establish whether the con<sup>fi</sup>gural model <sup>fi</sup>ts well with the data, as suggested by Hu and Bentler [53], we use several model-<sup>fi</sup>t statistics. Achieving con<sup>fi</sup>gural model invariance is critical because it serves as a baseline model for subsequent invariance testing. Results show a $\chi ^ { 2 }$ (6586) value of 11995.042 $( \chi ^ { 2 } /$ df = 1.821) with a CFI value of 0.927, and a RMSEA value of 0.034, indicating that con<sup>fi</sup>gural invariance is attained. Besides, these results also support the notion that the pattern of <sup>fi</sup>xed and non<sup>fi</sup>xed parameters in the research model is identical for the DN and DI samples. Additionally, the numbers and patterns of <sup>fi</sup>xed and non<sup>fi</sup>xed parameter values across groups are not found to be di<sup>f</sup>erent when tested separately for each group.

Establishing metric invariance implies that the measurement in strument behaves similarly across both sample groups, i.e., DNs and DIs. This behavior can be achieved if path coe<sup>fi</sup>cients of measurement instrument variables on their respective constructs do not show dif ferent results across both the groups, i.e., path coe<sup>fi</sup>cients across groups are not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent. Failure to establish metric invariance may lead to di<sup>f</sup>ering meanings of the factors despite their same factor label [59]. Once metric invariance is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed, and comparison of di<sup>f</sup>erent scores on items across groups will be meaningful. Then, the observed item di<sup>f</sup>erences would correctly indicate di<sup>f</sup>erences across groups in underlying latent constructs [60].

To test for metric invariance, the path coe<sup>fi</sup>cients are <sup>fi</sup>xed across groups, increasing the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value from 11995.042 to 12067.833 (Table 9). Because the metric invariance model (Model 2) is nested within the baseline model (Model 1), we perform a $\chi ^ { 2 }$ di<sup>f</sup>erence test. Given that the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ di<sup>f</sup>erence of 72.790 with 63 degrees of freedom is not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at $\mathtt { p } = 0 . 1 8 7$ , we can conclude that the metric invariance is supported (Table 9).

Although the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ di<sup>f</sup>erence test is the most accepted approach for model comparison, the use of CFI and RMSEA to assess the model <sup>fi</sup>t [52] is recommended. As shown in Table 10, the $\mathbb { X } ^ { 2 }$ di<sup>f</sup>erence is insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant, and ΔCFI and ΔRMSEA values are less than the cut-o<sup>f</sup> value of 0.005 ([60,59,61]. Thus, both sample groups, i.e., DN and DI, behave similarly in terms of metric invariance (Table 10).

## 7. Discussion

The main objective of this study was to conceptualize and test a new innovation model, with DTPB as the base model that could be used to explain continued technology use behavior across two user groups: DNs and DIs. The three postadoption mechanisms, namely sequential belief updating, feedback, and habit, have been integrated into DTPB to study how belief judgments and use experience shape new technology sustained use. The proposed three-wave panel model and associated hypotheses were used on users of a new learning management system. The results of data analysis on the overall model show that all three postadoption mechanisms (sequential belief updating, feedback and habit) have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ects on postadoption phenomena. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings can be explained using a motivational model suggested by Davis et al. [63] and Venkatesh and Davis [64]. The motivational model suggests that an individual’s continuous technology use decision can be understood via two routes: developing extrinsic motivation, i.e., continuous improve ments in belief judgments through belief updates/experience, and developing an intrinsic motivation, $\mathrm { i . e . , }$ to develop a habit.

Next, the di<sup>f</sup>erential e<sup>f</sup>ects of DNs and DIs on continuous use be havior of technology were examined. Similar to an overall continuous use research model, both DNs and DIs individually support the research model. However, the di<sup>f</sup>erential e<sup>f</sup>ects between DNs and DIs are seen only in some of the mechanisms underlying the postadoption phe nomenon. The results support the notion that only two the sequential belief updating mechanism and the feedback mechanism – di<sup>f</sup>erentiate the DIs from the DNs in continued usage behavior. These results suggest the need to increase demonstration sessions provided to both DNs and DIs (Table 11) and provide as many as opportunity interact with the friends; and provide instructor’s guidance to build their familiarity with the new technology interface, which would further enhance their compatibility with the system being used (Table 12). Further, there is need to encourage them to increase their frequency to use the learning management system such that they develop a habit to refer to it when required (Table 13). Thus, <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study complement the digital inequality study of Hsieh et al. [13], which focused on socio-economic criteria. They have referred to digital inequality as “inequality in the access and use of information technology” (pg. 3). Similar to DNs and DIs of present study, they studied the users as “advantaged” and “disadvantaged” groups. They contend that individuals who are socio economically challenged $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ disadvantaged group) re<sup>fl</sup>ect lesser con tinued usage behavior of an innovation than those who are not $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ advantaged group). As seen in the Table 3, though income levels are similar, the present study has taken a behavioral classi<sup>fi</sup>cation approach to complement the study “digital inequality,” i.e., DNs vs. DIs. As ex plained above in literature review section, this computer engagementbased classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is expected to be more enduring than demographic factors (age, income, and education)

The theoretical framework presented in this study also provides practitioners with insights into the adoption processes leading to sustained postadoption use behavior. In addition, the <sup>fi</sup>nding of this study highlights the question of whether DNs or DIs prefer the technology that they are used to/habituated with, which is similar to the habi tuation behavior of Ajzen [65]. For instance, traditionally, the approach to education was to focus on learning-about and to build stocks of knowledge and some cognitive skills in the student that would be de ployed later in appropriate situations. This approach to education worked well in a relatively stable, slowly changing world, where a student could expect to use one set of skills throughout life. But, the concept of lifelong learning – a term used all too glibly – is now more important than ever. When technical jobs change, we can no longer send a person back to school to be re-trained or to learn a new profession because by the time the formal educational process is completed, the domain is apt to have morphed yet again. A di<sup>f</sup>erent approach is called for, an approach suggestively called a “demand-pull” rather than the traditional “supply-push” strategy. In the new approach, the focus in education moves from building up stocks of knowledge (learning-about) to enabling students to participate in <sup>fl</sup>ows of action, where the focus is on learning to be through enculturation and on collateral learning. Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the present study encourage the university administration or education policy makers to stress on sequential belief updating mechanism to facilitate continuous learning, and on habit mechanism to nurture mental linkage between situational cue and desired behavior. Thus, organizations may stabilize the employment of new technologies for considerable time, which would result in sustained use behavior for both DNs and DIs.

## 8. Theoretical contributions

First, as opposed to the previous research that stresses on the sole dependence on the initial adoption models $[ 6 6 , 4 7 ]$ , the <sup>fi</sup>nding of this study reveals that postadoption mechanisms, such as sequential belief updating, feedback, and habit, are also important to adoption and continue use decisions. Thus, study <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that exclusive focus on the reasoned-oriented action, i.e., initial adoption phenomena, can easily result in biased inferences. For example, researchers in IS have often discretely analyzed the panel data wave-by-wave without taking a holistic view. Though integrated framework of technology has been used before by Kim and Malhotra [17] and Kim [16], they have not tested it in a three-wave setting using primary data. This study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to examine integrated framework to study an IFTU in a three-wave setting with primary <sup>fi</sup>eld data.

Second, the role of any individual di<sup>f</sup>erence variable in shaping continuous use behavior of IS has largely been ignored in innovation adoption research. Little was known about how user characteristics (DNs vs. DIs) could a<sup>f</sup>ect shaping of continued use behavior for the two user groups, given their di<sup>f</sup>erences in technology familiarity, experience, and comfort. A major contribution of this research is that it studies the postadoption models of two types of IS users and maps how their belief judgments and experiences of technology use unfold over time.

Third, the study contributes to the literature by identifying the factors that should be considered in the postadoption situation. Unlike previous studies, which suggest the three postadoption mechanisms and those are expected to work similar across all IS user groups, this study identi<sup>fi</sup>es selected factors separately for each set of IS user groups. Thus, <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the study are focused more to examine the di<sup>f</sup>erential impact of di<sup>f</sup>erent postadoption mechanisms across two groups. For example, H2b <sup>fi</sup>nds that under sequential belief updating, the study of perceived usefulness, compatibility, instructors’ in<sup>fl</sup>uence, and behavioral intention would better explain the DNs’ adoption and use behavior of an IS than that of DIs. Similarly, H2a <sup>fi</sup>nds that during the initial phase (at wave 1 and wave 2), the opinions and in<sup>fl</sup>uence of instructors and friends matter more to the DIs than DNs, though this di<sup>f</sup>erential e<sup>f</sup>ect decays over time. Further, H2b <sup>fi</sup>nds that feedback mechanisms work better in favor of DIs, if they can easily see the relative advantage of using new IS, and friends/management are able to demonstrate how the use of the new IS helps to achieve those bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Finally, H3a and H3b also reiterate the importance of habit in the success of an IS. Though habit does not play a di<sup>f</sup>erential role in the present study, it has been found to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on subsequent use behavior (H3a). These observations are true not only for the overall sample but also for DNs and DIs individual subgroups, and they extend the un derstanding of postadoption mechanism to explain behavioral di<sup>f</sup>erence phenomena as well. This highlights the three-stage processing model of memory proposed by Atkins and Schi<sup>f</sup>rin [67], which o<sup>f</sup>ers a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed account of how human experiences are processed in the memory system [68,16]. These three stages are sensory memory (very short-term memory), working memory (short-term memory), and long term memory. Kim [16] has found that long-term memory is responsible for continued use of technology. Taking initial technology use as an example, a user will carefully develop and execute a detailed plan for interaction with a new technology application to complete the task at hand. Conscious e<sup>f</sup>ort will be required in this case, but the sequence of the actions performed will remain as memory traces in working memory. As a person becomes more experienced with the application, the “hard-wired” links in long-term memory will be strengthened [69]. Eventually, when the same person is faced with a similar condition, the entire procedure for using the application will be executed unconsciously without recourse to working memory. Thus, this study has presented the path through which universities can enforce the usage of learning management systems by providing multiple touch points between LMS and its users.

Fourth, this study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to prefer non age-based classi<sup>fi</sup>cation to classify DNs and DIs. Previous research on technology use by DNs has certain drawbacks because age is used as the criteria to di<sup>f</sup>erentiate DNs from DIs. These studies have drawn several criticisms and drawn mixed conclusions [23,70]. However, immersion in digital technology does not depend on age because older populations can become comfortable with technology and younger generations can limit their learning to fewer new technologies and their features [39]. In this research, computer engagement scale has been used to di<sup>f</sup>erentiate between the groups because these instruments measure the users’ skills, comfort, and perceived importance of technology. Thus, our research <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the continuous technology use of DNs and DIs are based on theoretical distinction of the characteristics between the two user groups.

In addition to above contributions, this study extends previous research on continued use behavior of IS use from the two-wave panel model of Kim and Malhotra [17] to a three-wave panel model, and from secondary data as used in Kim [16] to primary data. Additionally, the study employs robust research methodologies, including assessment of common methods bias and SDB to make it more valid and reliable.

## 9. Managerial implications

Findings from our multigroup analysis highlight Smith et al. [70] reference to the “DN” myth, i.e., not all young people are DNs. It further vindicates our attempt to use a nonage-based DN/DI classi<sup>fi</sup>er. Further, one important <sup>fi</sup>nding of multigroup analysis suggests that DIs could continue the use of an innovation backed by continuous improvement in their belief judgments through routine training or demonstrations. Additionally, <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study suggest that although the DNs are using the new technology themselves, DIs require more technology demonstration and regular reminder to use it. In the organizational context, the phenomena of DNs and DIs can be applied to the set of employees exhibiting di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of preference and the use of available technologies in the workplace. One reason for a new IS becoming unsuccessful is the non usage behavior by existing employees. Although most organizations conduct/organize training sessions, it is found that they do not induce employees to use the new IS. The reasons cited in the literature are low self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy or motivation.

After DNs and DIs are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed among their existing or new employees, managers can plan training or induction programs according to their respective characteristics. This might help managers ensure that the new employees, categorized as DIs or DNs, can be trained on different technologies di<sup>f</sup>erently. This may be done on the basis of the technological skills. Thus, an organization can opt for separate training sessions for DN (i.e., high on computer engagement) and DI (i.e., low on computer engagement) employees. This is likely to increase both groups’ self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy and motivation to adopt the new IS.

Similarly, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this study can also be useful to address the continued usage issue by reducing the employee burnout problem due to the overuse of the same technology over a period. The <sup>fi</sup>nding can also be used to design di<sup>f</sup>erent workshops and training programs for DNs and DIs about new features and updates in the currently used IS technology. Initiatives such as these might help organizations to retain worker interest given their orientations toward the technology. Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the study can be potentially utilized by managers to identify optimal user–technology–induction combinations.

To facilitate a better learning experience for DNs, organizations have integrated various ubiquitous technologies such as tablet computers, software applications, and cloud servers into their business, suggesting a paradigm shift in their existing working philosophy. Although all these changes are welcome by the new DNs employees in organizations, not all employees are alike, i.e., not all employees are DNs; among them, many remain Dis, i.e., not as comfortable with technology as their DN counterparts. Hence, companies across the world should also observe how employees learn how they like to learn and how they solve problems, and most importantly, what creates meaning for them and helps them to construct their sense of self [2,71]. Answers to these questions would provide an insight into the technological orientation of employees and, thus, provide companies an opportunity to formulate/revise strategies from initial training to increasing productivity to performance appraisal.

The results of base model (DTPB) clearly suggest that di<sup>f</sup>erent constituencies of employees consider di<sup>f</sup>erent factors in making technology adoption and use decisions. The results of H2b imply that user analysis cannot be taken for granted and, rather, should become the centerpiece for design (to re<sup>fl</sup>ect compatibility, and usefulness), parti cularly in the early stages of the systems development and implementation process. This availability is particularly important because, as shown by the results of all three postadoption mechanisms (Table 11–13), early perceptions can have a lasting impression on subsequent intentions (through sequential belief updating) and behavior (through habit). Thus, training programs should follow made-toorder approaches to stress factors that are important to each group. For example, trainers should be aware of the need to highlight motivating factors, such as perceived usefulness and compatibility, etc., because factors such as these are very attractive to DNs (H1b). At the same time, management should also ensure that these motivating factors (e.g., perceived usefulness and compatibility for DNs) are not provided at the expense of another technology user group, e.g., DIs. Similarly, internal and external marketing e<sup>f</sup>orts should bene<sup>fi</sup>t from these <sup>fi</sup>ndings by planning tailored information for di<sup>f</sup>erent groups, thereby giving each group something to like about new technology products.

Our results show that perceived usefulness (PU), instructor s in<sup>fl</sup>u ence (INI), compatibility (CMPT), and behavioral intention (BI) have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher path coe<sup>fi</sup>cients in sequential update mechanisms for DNs than those in DIs.<sup>4</sup> This suggests that to provide higher motivation to use the available system, DNs need to be more sensitized to ward the current system. Thus, such programs can be designed and delivered for better continued usage. As DNs are more likely to be in-<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the instructor than DIs, the instructors should focus more attention on employing technology that is more attractive to DNs. This suggestion is consistent with those given by previous researchers that a younger population might be using a limited set of tools and that a teacher has a role to play in exploiting the full bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of these technologies [23]. The rationale for such advice is that compared with Dis, DNs may not evaluate the usefulness of systems accurately [72]. This is empirically sound as the path coe<sup>fi</sup>cients are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher for usefulness in the sequential updating mechanism. Thus, DNs should be the prime focus of such training programs to gain maximum bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the system as DNs are better than DIs in evaluating IS and their usefulness. Employing IS that are compatible (CMPT) with DNs’ habits and tastes will increase continuous use of technology by DNs. Our results concerning PU and CMPT with respect to DNs are in line with the views of Vodanovich et al. (2010), who state, “As organization’s attempt to balance the social needs of DNs with business requirements for task completion, viewing task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t in conjunction with people-technology <sup>fi</sup>t because a driver of IT usage appears to be highly relevant and necessary (pp. 718)”.

The results of the study will be useful in helping educational institutions revamp their undergraduate and graduate programs in IS such that the curriculum is matched to the type of incoming students (most of whom are DNs). Thus, such e<sup>f</sup>orts to understand the importance of the DN/DI distinction and applying it in the context of continued usage of technologies can lead to the coveted “right users – right technology – right induction” combination.

## 10. Limitations and scope for future research

Just like any other research work, our study has certain limitations. We list two that we believe are important to note. First, this study received support for only partial e<sup>f</sup>ects of feedback mechanisms in describing sustained usage behavior among users of a new technology for either DNs or DIs. This is unlike the results of previous research work in the area. A possible rationale could be the underutilization of the LMS by its users. Therefore, we encourage studies in which the IS is well established and is frequently used by its users. This could provide additional or full support for feedback mechanism on postadoption usage.

Second, this study has not considered distal e<sup>f</sup>ects (i.e., e<sup>f</sup>ect of T = t on T = t+2). The focus has been limited to proximal e<sup>f</sup>ects (i.e., the e<sup>f</sup>ect of T = t on T = t + 1). Given the requirements of the tech nology being studied to be one where use behavior frequency is high, we note that to infer their evaluations people are expected to quickly recall previous incidents of technology use for a certain period of time (e.g., a week or a month). Thus, the context provides possibilities of distal e<sup>f</sup>ects. A study that integrates both the proximal as well as and distal e<sup>f</sup>ects would possibly enhance further the explanatory power of the present study. Such a study could result in interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings, such as DNs having di<sup>f</sup>erent distal e<sup>f</sup>ects rather than DIs. This could possibly be explored on the basis of memory-related characteristics such as memory power and memory-processing capabilities

Third, this study has measured “computer engagement” scale during all three waves. A cross-wave comparison of computer engagement scores shows that roughly 10% of the students migrated from DIs to DNs. Therefore, responses of wave 2 and wave 3 could be biased toward DNs. Though this problem is implicit in nature as, irrespective of the scale used, users’ beliefs, judgments, and usage behavior would improve over time and hence their responses to computer engagement scale would also improve. A study to address this issue is required to highlight the unbiased in<sup>fl</sup>uence of DNs vs. DIs on adoption and postadoption behavior. Additionally, as this - DNs vs. DIs - classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is dynamic in nature, with DNs and DIs being two important groups in social system, researchers are advised to explore the societal perspective of this classi<sup>fi</sup>cation in detail. As several researchers have suggested, such research would further explore the possibilities to under stand these digital generations more aptly [73–75].

## 11. Conclusion

In the domain of innovation adoption, the study of user acceptance is perhaps the most actively researched area. Nevertheless, explanation of continued usage of technology has not been actively pursued for contexts where individuals di<sup>f</sup>er on usage characteristics. The objective of the present study is to investigate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of user orientation toward technology use (DNs/DIs) on technology continued use behavior. The longitudinal model is a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed framework that sheds light on four di<sup>f</sup>erent mechanisms underlying postadoption phenomena: (1) the belief judgment evaluation processes suggested by the initial adoption model, (2) the sequential updating mechanism, (3) the feedback mechanism, and (4) the habit mechanism. We use DTPB as the initial adoption model to integrate the three postadoption mechanisms to explain continuous usage. From a theoretical perspective, this study indicates that whereas DTPB is useful in explaining technology adop tion and use, extending the theory to include individual technology orientation for the moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects of DNs/DIs signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increases our understanding of the underlying phenomenon. From a practical individual behavior perspective, based on prior research, managers might reasonably wonder whether user technology orientation matters when implementing new technology in organizations. Our results indicate that the answer to this question is more complex than it may appear and is based on the dynamic relationship between the initial adoption model, three postadoption mechanisms, and user technology–engagement characteristics. Results indicate that there exists a clear pattern of di<sup>f</sup>erences between DNs and DIs when considering the postadoption phenomena of sequential belief updating mechanism. Finally, the present study can act as a precursor to explore other avenues of DN – DI behavioral di<sup>f</sup>erences and their e<sup>f</sup>ect on IS research issues.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the study can be potentially utilized by managers in identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of optimal user–technology–induction combinations. This might help managers ensure that the new employees, who may be categorized as DIS or DNs, can be trained on di<sup>f</sup>erent technologies differently. This may be done on the basis of the technological skills. Thus, an organization can opt for separate training sessions for DNs (i.e., high on computer engagement) and for DIs (i.e., low on computer engagement) employees. Similarly, the results of the study will help educational institutions revamp their undergraduate and graduate programs in IS such that the curriculum is better matched to the type of incoming students (most of whom are DNs).
