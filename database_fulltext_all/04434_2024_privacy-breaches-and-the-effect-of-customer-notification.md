# PRIVACY BREACHES AND THE EFFECT OF CUSTOMER NOTIFICATION<sup>1</sup>

Jeong-Bon Kim Beedie School of Business, Simon Fraser University Vancouver, BC, Canada {jbk3@sfu.ca}

Chong Wang School of Accounting and Finance, Hong Kong Polytechnic University Kowloon, Hong Kong SAR {chong.wang@polyu.edu.hk}

Feng (Harry) Wu Department of Accountancy, Faculty of Business, Lingnan University New Territories, Hong Kong SAR {harrywu@ln.edu.hk}

Laws requiring firms to disclose privacy breaches to their customers have been adopted extensively worldwide. However, the manner in which these laws affect the security protection behavior of firms disclosing a data breach is poorly understood. To shed light on this issue, we leveraged institutional theory and examined how U.S. state data breach notification laws (DBNLs), under which firms must notify customers of personal information breaches, influenced firm-level incidence of security breaches and how such influence manifested heterogeneously across firms. Exploiting the staggered enactments of DBNLs in a difference-in-differences analysis, we found that firms experienced a significant reduction in data breach incidents after the implementation of DBNLs. This effect was more pronounced among firms that were more reliant on sensitive customer data, operated in stricter privacy protection environments, or held more intangible and digital assets. We document evidence that compared to firms not subject to DBNLs, firms subject to these laws are more likel to appoint IT-specialized executives and remediate IT-related internal control weaknesses, which suggests potential channels that may facilitate DBNLs’ curbing of data breaches. We also found that the reduction in breach incidences following DBNL-mandated disclosure policies relates to both endogenous breaches and exogenous cyberattacks.

Keywords: Data breach notification laws, data breaches, institutional theory, difference-in-differences

## Introduction

Data breaches have become increasingly frequent and consequential, leading to extensive consumer, media, regulatory, academic, and public attention.<sup>2</sup> Information systems research has identified an array of factors affecting the likelihood of breaches, including technical security controls, various firm characteristics, and managerial features (e.g., Kwon & Johnson, 2014; Wang et al., 2015; Angst et al.,

2017; Hui et al., 2017; D’Arcy et al., 2020; Haislip et al., 2021). Though many studies have examined the effect of government-imposed mandatory information security standards (e.g., Lee et al., 2016), another important regulatory approach—data breach notification—remains largely underexplored. As a widely adopted practice, legislators around the world have introduced data breach notification laws (DBNLs) that compel firms to notify customers when their private information is acquired by unauthorized parties. In the U.S., DBNLs have been adopted in every state, as well as

Washington, DC.<sup>3</sup> We study the economic consequences of this notification approach; specifically, we examine the influence of the mandatory disclosure of data breaches on firms’ efforts to safeguard data and, thus, the subsequent incidence of breaches.

Drawing on institutional theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Powell & DiMaggio, 1991; Scott, 1995), we posit that DBNLs impose coercive and normative pressures on firms to improve cybersecurity. Coercive pressure originates from the regulatory mandates that publicize firms’ failures in protecting customer privacy and from customers, who, as the victims of the breaches, exert sanctions that can compromise firm performance and supply chain sustainability. Normative pressure comes from the threat of exposing breached firms’ deviation from the institutional legitimacy established among their peers with regard to privacy safeguarding. This exposure, in turn, attracts media attention, which can damage firms’ reputations, and provokes investor resistance, which can hinder firms’ access to economic resources. Together, these pressures compel firms to adopt measures that reduce the likelihood of data breaches.

To identify the impact of customer notification on the incidence of data breaches, we exploited the staggered adoptions of DBNLs across U.S. states from 2005 to 2018 as a quasi-natural experiment. In this institutional context, DBNL enactment can be viewed as largely exogenous to individual firms’ data security status. We applied a difference-in-differences (DiD) analysis to compare the change in data breaches among treatment firms (i.e., firms located in states that enacted DBNLs) before and after DBNL enactment with the corresponding change among benchmark control firms (i.e., firms in states that did not enact a DBNL) over the same period. Based on data breach and financial data for publicly listed firms in the U.S. during our sample period, we document robust evidence that the incidence of data breaches decreased significantly among treatment firms after the implementation of DBNLs, as compared to control firms. Our findings suggest that mandating firms to notify their customers about data breaches induces a significant change in corporate behavior that helps firms improve their information security.

In further analyses, we show that DBNLs exhibited a stronger suppressive impact on data loss incidents for firms that rely more heavily on customer information, operate in stricter privacy protection environments, or hold more substantial intangible assets, indicating that DBNLs are most effective in preventing data breaches among firms that are sensitive to the statutory notification requirements and subject to substantial institutional pressures imposed by DBNLs. We also found that firms made efforts to enhance data safeguarding after DBNL enactment: they were more likely to add an IT-related executive such as a chief information officer (CIO) or chief data officer (CDO) to their top management teams and were more likely to significantly remediate their IT-related internal control weaknesses. These findings help identify potential channels for the observed post-DBNL reductions in security breach incidents.

Our study was conducted in the context of various jurisdictions introducing customer notification laws.<sup>4</sup> The primary aim of DBNLs is to warn affected individuals in a timely manner so that they can take preemptive actions to avoid potential misuse of their leaked data.<sup>5</sup> Our study, however, explored such laws from the perspective of corporate insiders. That is, we investigated the behavior of a subject (i.e., the breached firm) that was not the direct focus of the legislation (i.e., the firm’s customers).

Our firm-level analysis supplements prior research on the state-level outcomes of data breach disclosure laws. In particular, Romanosky et al. (2011) examined the aggregate level of a state’s identity theft that could be influenced by the protective actions of customers who were notified of data leaks involving them.<sup>6</sup> Identity theft is a more constrained concept and only a small fraction of data breaches result in personal information being maliciously stolen. Identity theft also differs from data breaches in that the former can arise for other reasons unrelated to the latter.<sup>7</sup> As such, our study adds to this literature by focusing on the security-enhancing actions of firms involved in a data breach incident following their data breach disclosure.

Unlike prior research examining the consequences of breaches such as successful cyberattacks (e.g., Kamiya et al., 2021), we focus on the reactions of corporate insiders to the enactment of DBNLs and reveal a determinant of the likelihood of data breaches. In this way, our study addresses a novel and significant issue regarding a disclosure-based means of preventing privacy breaches, particularly in the framework of institutional theory.

Our study also contributes to the literature on the economics of cyber risk regulation (Kashyap & Wetherilt, 2019), the economics of privacy (Acquisti et al., 2016), and the digital economy (Goldfarb & Tucker, 2019). A major theme in this literature is that as privacy becomes a key issue in the digital economy, customers often find themselves in an environment of imperfect or asymmetric information regarding the status of their personal data that firms hold. Efforts to protect personal data can enhance individual and societal welfare, but such efforts require regulatory support to build cyber resilience into firms’ systems. In this regard, our analysis sheds light on the role of mandatory disclosure of cyber risk in both protecting privacy and fueling economic development through the sophisticated use of digital technology.<sup>8</sup>

Lastly, our study has important policy implications. The effect of DBNLs on firm behaviors is of increasing interest as policymakers around the world adopt or consider adopting similar consumer notification legislation. The findings of our comprehensive analysis of U.S. state-level consumer notification statutes have direct implications for data breach policies and proposals in other jurisdictions; in particular, they reveal the significance of requirements to disclose data breaches not only for the design of information security models but also for improving cyber policies going forward.

## Theory and Hypotheses

## Effect of DBNLs on the Incidence of Data Breaches

Business organizations do not operate in a vacuum. Institutional theory postulates that the institutional environment shapes the behavior of organizations because, to survive and succeed, organizations must conform to established values, norms, and rules to secure their legitimacy (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Powell & DiMaggio, 1991; Scott, 1995). An important feature of this theory is that organizations respond to institutional pressures exerted by a variety of stakeholders by inhibiting behaviors deemed to be undesirable. Prior research (e.g., Doshi et al., 2013; Leong & Hazelton, 2019) has shown that mandatory disclosure provides information that fuels such institutional pressures, which, in turn, disciplines misconduct and prompts firms to improve along the disclosed dimension.

Within this theoretical framework, we propose that, by mandating that firms disclose defects in their data security protection, DBNLs facilitate the formation and strengthening of institutional pressures that compel firms to be more cautious in safeguarding customer privacy, thus reducing the incidence of data breaches. Specifically, following DiMaggio and Powell (1983), Powell and DiMaggio (1991), and Scott (1995), we theorize that DBNL implementation relates to coercive pressures and normative pressures.

Coercive pressures are embedded in regulations related to data protection. DBNLs are a specific type of legislation governing firms that experience data breach incidents. The compulsory nature, enforcement, compliance costs, and legal consequences of DBNLs are likely to impose nontrivial pressures on firms disclosing data breaches, especially those with poor privacy protections. A regulatory mandate to disclose firms’ data breach information could serve as a signal that the government is serious about data protection and may impose further regulations, such as higher information systems security standards or sanctions. Consistent with this rationale, prior studies (Stechemesser & Guenther, 2012; Leong & Hazelton, 2019; Mateo-Márquez et al., 2020) have shown that sustainability reporting, such as environment-related disclosures, are associated with environmental laws and regulations. Firms’ perceptions of these pressures incentivize them to adopt behaviors to avoid the issue (e.g., data breaches) that prompted the need for disclosure in the first place.

Coercive pressures can also come from customers, who are the users of a firm’s products and services. DiMaggio and Powell (1983, p. 150) define coercive pressures as those “exerted on organizations by other organizations upon which (the firms) … are dependent.” Many studies treat the pressures from customers as coercive (e.g., Williams et al., 2009; Hoejmose et al., 2014; Foltean et al., 2019; Lu et al., 2019). Customer-related coercive pressures are especially pertinent to the unique setting of DBNLs in which customers are the major targets in the data breach notification scheme because, unlike other corporate assets, the information compromised in data breaches is owned by and important to customers. Customers are likely to be disturbed by the prying into their lives that firms’ compilation of personal information represents, and unauthorized access to such information should heighten their concerns.<sup>9</sup> Consequently, customers will be especially alert to personal data breaches, and customer notification could trigger backlashes against firms because affected customers have a direct stake in the disclosed breaches. The negative feelings triggered by corporate data breaches may cause large numbers of customers to cease doing business with firms and may increase firms’ cost of acquiring new customers (IBM, 2017).<sup>10</sup> Moreover, DBNLs enable affected customers to identify the specific party (i.e., firms disclosing the data breach) responsible for allowing breaches to occur, which could facilitate potential legal actions against the firms. As a result, firms disclosing data breaches may face significant deterioration in their fundamental performance and a significant increase in their litigation risk, which are likely to pressure such firms to avoid these negative consequences by better protecting customer privacy.

Normative pressures stem from expectations from the community in which norms and standards of the operating environment are formed. Organizations are compelled to conform to the generally accepted institutional values in order to establish and maintain their legitimacy (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Suchman, 1995; Appari et al., 2009). The importance of privacy protection is recognized around the world, leading to a firmly established legitimacy related to information security (Appari et al., 2009; Bulgurcu et al., 2010; AlKalbani et al., 2015). By publicizing data breach events and improving information transparency, DBNLs facilitate collective actions to develop privacy protection norms among firms and their peers, which helps strengthen data security and prevent data breaches. Frequent disclosures of privacy breaches via DBNLs also intensify people’s focus on cybersecurity, which exerts normative pressures on firms in terms of the depletion of reputational capital and moral responsibility if they fail to protect their customers’ private information (Culnan & Williams, 2009). The circulation of news about negative data protection behaviors (e.g., DBNL disclosures) may have a profound effect on firms because they are concerned about their reputation and perceived legitimacy. Specifically, normative pressures include institutional peer pressure because DBNLs make the comparison of data protection performance among peer organizations easier and less costly. Such a comparison clearly reveals any deviations from the established norms; if a firm’s data protection performance is significantly inferior relative to its peers, then the firm’s legitimacy could be severely undermined. Peer pressure thus obligates firms to follow the norms and better safeguard data security. Moreover, particularized data breach notifications tell consumers, the media, and legislators a story about data security gone awry.

Firms that experience data breaches are thus likely to become a focal point for consumer resentment, media attention, and legislative scrutiny, which can severely harm their public image, brand loyalty, and overall competitiveness. The revelation of a privacy meltdown may invite social sanctions against a firm and negatively affect the professional reputations of its managers.

Normative pressures can also hinder firms’ access to critical resources for their operations. When the main providers of firm capital, such as equity investors and creditors, become aware of data breaches, they may exert additional pressures on firms disclosing data breaches. Fund providers are naturally concerned about firms’ ability to generate earnings and are therefore likely to react to any adverse impacts of DBNLs. In particular, the disclosure of data breach information could increase firms’ cost of capital; recent studies have documented substantial negative impacts of corporate security failures on equity costs and bank loan terms (Huang & Wang, 2021; Ashraf & Sunder, 2023). Likewise, stock price and firm value have been found to decrease significantly following data breach announcements (Cummins et al., 2006; Gatzlaff & McCullough, 2010; Amir et al., 2018; Kamiya et al., 2021; Tosun, 2021). These market reactions highlight the concerns of capital providers and can substantially hamper firms’ capital budgeting, project investments, and financial growth. Publicizing a data breach event can facilitate the enforcement of market sanctions by directing investor and creditor attention to a firm’s information security weaknesses. These weaknesses can also increase the risk of cash flow shortages (Boasiako & Keefe, 2021). Reducing the incidence of data breaches can minimize these negative outcomes.

Without notification from a firm about a data breach, it would be difficult for customers to detect any risk to their personal information. DBNLs thus make otherwise untraceable security breaches traceable. Customers’ response to and dissemination of such breaches across a wide range of stakeholders can threaten a firm’s legitimacy, as defined by societal norms of data protection; therefore firms disclosing a data breach will be particularly cautious when processing sensitive data. In this way, mandatory disclosure requirements tend to induce firms to make strenuous efforts to prevent data breaches. In light of these arguments, we formulate the following main hypothesis, stated in alternative form:

H1: Data breach notification legislation reduces the incidence of security breaches among firms disclosing data breaches, all other things being equal.

## Cross-Firm Heterogeneity in the DBNL Effect on Data Breaches

According to institutional theory, the degree to which DBNLs compel firms disclosing data breaches (i.e., “disclosing firms”) to reduce the incidence of data breaches hinges on the level of external institutional pressures imposed on the firms. One prominent factor relating to external pressures is a firm’s reliance on sensitive customer data. Concerns about data loss naturally increase with more sensitive data, such as those related to personal information and financial security, and the adverse consequences of a data breach (e.g., impairment of a firm’s institutional legitimacy) will likely be more severe for firms holding a large amount of highly sensitive data. Also, the associated compliance and litigation costs are likely to be higher for such firms, especially when countermeasures are required. Consistent with this reasoning, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2a: The effect of data breach notification legislation on reducing the incidence of security breaches is more pronounced for disclosing firms that are more reliant on sensitive customer data.

Institutional pressures regarding data safeguarding increase with the level of privacy protection in the environment in which a firm operates. This environment relates to legislation for internet privacy. A strong legal framework of privacy protection tends to heighten coercive and normative pressures as privacy-related rhetoric among customers and the general public intensifies. Overall, in an environment with stricter privacy protection, data breaches are expected to be more detrimental to a firm’s legitimacy, and DBNLs should thus play a more important role in curbing data breaches. This rationale leads to the following hypothesis:

H2b: The effect of data breach notification legislation on reducing the incidence of security breaches is more pronounced for disclosing firms that operate in stricter privacy protection environments.

Potential pressures induced by DBNLs are associated with a firm’s dependence on intangible assets. Intangible, especially information-based, assets are more susceptible to the impact of cyber risks.<sup>11</sup> DBNLs are thus more relevant to and should have a larger impact on firms with more intangible assets. For these firms, the institutional norms of information security are more prominent, and DBNLs play a more important role in facilitating the formation of such norms. To the extent that data hold greater significance for information-intensive firms, the comparison of cybersecurity performances among them, as facilitated by mandatory data breach disclosures, tends to be more salient, and an underperforming firm is likely to face higher risks of losing credibility and institutional legitimacy. As such, both the coercive and normative pressures could be escalated for firms with heavier reliance on intangible assets. Based on these arguments, we hypothesize the following:

H2c: The effect of data breach notification legislation on reducing the incidence of security breaches is more pronounced for disclosing firms that hold more intangible assets.

## Institutional Background and Empirical Design

## Legislative Background of State-Level Data Breach Notification Requirements

In 2002, California became the first U.S. state to pass a statute mandating the disclosure of data breaches, which went into effect in 2003. The enactment of the statute represented a milestone legislative event in the sense that the California law established the basic outline that other states have followed. By 2018, all 50 states and Washington, DC had enacted similar legislation (see the appendix for a list of the dates on which DBNL first came into force in each state). These statelevel DBNLs share key features; in particular, the residents of the state must be notified if they become victims of data breaches involving their personally identifiable information, and, as part of the notification process, the breached firm must identify itself as the party responsible for the data loss.

In examining the effect of DBNLs on future data breach incidents, it is important to note that the adoption of the statelevel disclosure legislation is largely exogenous to individual firms’ information security conditions. Joseph Simitian, then California state senator who co-introduced California’s DBNL, described the legislative process as “random, dramatic, and idiosyncratic” (Simitian, 2009, p. 1009), in that he initially planned to introduce a bill requiring firms to post their privacy policies and then, less than 48 hours before the legislative deadline for bill introduction, he coordinated an after-hours conference call “on an occasional, and ad hoc, basis” (Simitian, 2009, p. 1010). Simitian recalls asking at the end of the conference call whether there was any other issue to address, at which point the data breach disclosure requirement was proposed, and the Online Privacy and Disclosure Act of 2002 was introduced one day before the deadline.<sup>12</sup> As discussed, other states followed suit with similar legislations in the manner of a “diffusion of innovation” (Walker, 1969); that is, a legislative issue quickly dissipates when legislators observe that other states have successfully adopted a law so that passing it becomes “recognized as a legitimate state responsibility” (Walker, 1969, p. 890). Overall, the process by which different states enacted their DBNLs appeared similarly unsystematic and noisy in that the precise moment at which the legislators took action on the issue was unpredictable and generally exogenous to the severity of local data breach problems.<sup>13</sup>

## Main Empirical Design

Our main econometric design exploits the staggered enactments of DBNLs. As explained, we view these enactments as exogenous regulatory shocks to firms suffering a breach. Accordingly, we conducted a quasi-natural experiment applying a standard DiD analysis. Specifically, we estimated the following regression model:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Data   Breach } _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} D B N L + \beta_ {2} L n (S i z e) _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} R O E _ {i t} + \beta_ {4} M B _ {i t} + \\ \beta_ {5} L E V _ {i t} + \beta_ {6} L n (P P E) _ {i t} + \beta_ {7} O p e r a t i o n a l R i s k _ {i t} + \beta_ {8} I n t a n g i b l e A s s e t s _ {i t} + \\ \text { Firm   Fixed   Effects } + \text { Year   Fixed   Effects } + \varepsilon \end{array}
$$

The dependent variable Data Breach<sub>it</sub> refers to the likelihood that firm i experiences at least one data breach incident in year t during our sample period of 2005–2018; specifically, this variable is coded as 1 if one or more data breach events occur at the firm, and 0 otherwise. The DiD effect is captured by the coefficient β<sub>1</sub> on the key variable of interest, DBNL, after firm and year fixed effects are accounted for. DBNL is a variable with its value changing upon the enactments of DBNLs in states where a firm operates, and is constructed in two steps. First, for a particular year, we identified each DBNL implementation event in each state and assigned a value of 1 to each firm with headquarters or subsidiaries located in the state, and 0 otherwise (i.e., if the firm does not operate in that state). For a firm operating in multiple states, we identified the value (1 or 0) indicating DBNL implementation status for each state where it operated and then computed the average of all values.<sup>19</sup> As such, an increase in the average value for a firm indicates that the firm was affected by the implementation of DBNLs and thus is a treatment firm; a firm without a value change during the same period was unaffected by DBNL enactment and thus serves as a control firm.

Next, to address variation in the timing of treatment, we set for each state a weight as the share of years firms spent as a treated group during our sample period from 2005 to 2018. For example, the weight for Mississippi, which implemented its DBNL in 2011, is [(2018-2011)+1]/14 = 0.571. In this construction, 2018 refers to the last year in the sample period,

2011 indicates the state’s DBNL implementation year, and 14 represents the total years in our sample.<sup>20</sup> We applied these weights to the values obtained in the first step to obtain the final measure for DBNL.<sup>21</sup> The rationale of this weighting scheme, as indicated by Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) and Goodman-Bacon (2021), is to reflect a potential decreasing pattern in the strength of impact as the sequential introduction of DBNLs progressed. In other words, DBNLs in states that adopted them later were likely to have weaker effects than those adopted earlier because firms were already aware of previous DBNLs.<sup>22</sup> Due to the staggered nature of DBNL adoptions across different states, each change in the value of DBNL was triggered by the enactments of the data breach disclosure mandates and the magnitudes of the series of changes for a firm (which capture the diminishing marginal impacts of DBNLs over time). Regarding the descriptive statistics for DBNL, Table 2 shows that its mean value is 0.6235 and its standard deviation is 0.4258.

The year fixed effects controlled for general time trends in data breaches, security technologies, and regulations other than DBNLs, which likely had similar effects on all firms. We included firm fixed effects to control for differences in timeinvariant omitted factors among firms and to differentiate treatment firms in DBNL-affected states from benchmark firms in other states. According to Bertrand and Mullainathan (2003) and Jayaraman and Shivakumar (2013), in a DiD framework with staggered adoption events for a regulation, the benchmark control firms would not be earmarked as those never affected by the regulation; rather, they would be earmarked as firms unaffected by the regulation at a given time, even if they were affected in the past or will be affected in the future. With this specification, we can identify the effect of DBNLs by examining differences in the incidence of data breaches around the DBNL enactment year between treatment firms and control firms. A significantly negative coefficient on the variable DBNL represents a mitigating effect of DBNLs on the likelihood that data breaches would occur.

We included firm-level control variables, measured for firm i in year t, in Equation (1), specifically, firm size measured as the logarithm of total assets, Ln(Size)<sub>it</sub>; return on equity measured as income before tax scaled by total equity, ROE<sub>it</sub>; market-tobook ratio, MB<sub>it</sub>; leverage measured as the sum of current debt and long-term debt scaled by total assets, LEV<sub>it</sub>; the logarithm of net property, plant and equipment (PPE) scaled by total assets, Ln(PPE)<sub>it</sub>; the risk in firm operations measured as the standard deviation of yearly cash flow from operations divided by total assets over the previous five fiscal years, Operational Risk<sub>it</sub>; and intangible assets measured as one minus the ratio of net PPE to total assets, Intangible Assets<sub>it</sub>. These characteristics are likely to influence a firm’s exposure to data breach risks (Akey et al., 2021; Huang & Wang, 2021; Kamiya et al., 2021). Table 2 reports summary statistics for these control variables.

## Empirical Results

## Baseline Result for H1

Table 3 reports the result of our baseline regression in Equation (1) examining the general effect of DBNL enactment on the occurrence of data breaches. As shown, the coefficient on the key variable DBNL is -0.0211 and is significant at the 5% level (p-statistic = 0.021). The negative sign of the coefficient indicates that the likelihood of data breaches declined more among treatment firms than control firms from the pre-DBNL to the post-DBNL period. In other words, when states enacted DBNLs, the frequency of data breaches among firms located in those states tended to decrease. This effect is economically meaningful: given the standard deviations of 0.4258 and 0.0825 for DBNL and Data Breach (as shown in Table 2), respectively, our regression estimate indicates that a DBNL increase of one standard deviation translates to a 0.1089 standard deviation decline in a firm’s data breach likelihood.<sup>23</sup> In sum, these findings suggest that DBNLs lead to reduced incidence of data breaches, which provides supporting evidence for H1.

## Robustness of the Baseline Result

In Table 4, we present the results of additional tests to verify the robustness of our baseline findings. A critical assumption underlying the DiD model in Equation (1) is that the trends in the occurrence of data breaches would have been the same for firms without the implementation of DBNLs. That is, although data breaches could have a systematic change over time, the treatment and control groups would be expected to share the overall pattern of this change. Our first robustness check addressed whether this parallel-trend assumption is valid. If so, we expected to observe no difference in the trends of data breaches between the two groups in the period before the implementation of DBNLs. To this end, we conducted a multiperiod dynamic analysis of the DBNL effect on the incidence of data breaches. Specifically, following Christensen et al. (2017), Hombert and Matray (2017), and Dasgupta and Žaldokas (2019), we generated a set of indicator variables for various periods before and after the changes in DBNL. The variable Pre(1, 2) is an indicator variable that took a value of 1 for treatment firms in the one-to-two-year period before a DBNL was enacted, and 0 otherwise; we define Pre(3, 4) similarly, indicating the three-to-four-year period prior to DBNL enactment for treatment firms. In a similar vein, we defined Post(0, 1) as an indicator variable that equals 1 for the

DBNL enactment year and one year after, and 0 otherwise; we used Post(2, 3) to indicate the second and third years after DBNL enactment and use Post(4+) to indicate the years beyond. The unspecified years (i.e., five or more years before the enactment) served as the benchmark period. We used this set of indicator variables to replace DBNL in the baseline model in Equation (1) while continuing to control for firm and year fixed effects, in keeping with the DiD design. As Column 1 of Table 4 shows, coefficients on the indicator variables in the post-DBNL period (i.e., Post(0, 1), Post(2, 3), and Post(4+)) are all negative and significant. In contrast, the coefficients on the indicator variables in the pre-DBNL period (i.e., Pre(1, 2) and Pre(3, 4)) are not statistically significant. This result suggests that the difference in data breach trends between the treatment and control groups manifests only after DBNL enactment; no preexisting differing trends were observed. Thus, the parallel-trend assumption is not violated.

Another important feature of the multi-period dynamic analysis is that we set the benchmark period to be sufficiently distant— five or more years—from the DBNL shock year. Therefore, the enactments of DBNLs were unlikely to be influenced by the level of data breaches in the distant benchmark period.<sup>24</sup> Given this premise, we found no significant deviation in the incidence of data breaches from the benchmark in any other pre-DBNL year, suggesting that there was no unusual increase in data breaches before a DBNL is enacted. That is, there was no evidence that a heightened level of data breaches motivated the enactment of the notification law. This result corroborates the view that the institutional setting of DBNLs is exogenous to the individual firms.

Our second robustness test followed Abadie and Gardeazabal (2003), Abadie et al. (2010), Liu et al. (2024), and Mirenda et al. (2022) and adopted a generalized synthetic control method to create synthetic firms in such a way that the resulting control group had a behavior similar to that of the treated group before treatment. This method improved treatment-control fit in terms of pre-treatment dynamics, which ensures that the control used in the DiD analysis resembled the relevant features of DBNLaffected firms before the laws were implemented. More specifically, for the pre-treatment period, we constructed controls that were matched for treatment based on data breach predictors such as Ln(Size), ROE, MB, LEV, Ln(PPE), Operational Risk, and Intangible Assets in Equation (1). Using the synthetic control, we reestimated the baseline regression and found similar results (see Column 2 of Table 4); in particular, the coefficient on DBNL remained negative and significant at the 5% level.

Our third robustness test addressed the concern that the reduction in data breaches associated with DBNL enactment is a local rather than universal phenomenon driven by specific legislations in some states. For this purpose, we conducted a randomization test by randomly excluding five states from our sample and then reestimating the baseline regression.<sup>25</sup> We repeated the exercise 500 times and report the average coefficient on DBNL in Column 3 of Table 4. In this test, each state had the same likelihood of being excluded from (or included in) the analysis; put another way, because our sample included states selected at random, the result was not dominated by any one state. If the impact of DBNLs on data breaches was prevalent for states in general and not clustered in some of them, the baseline result should hold after this randomization exercise. As Column 3 shows, the average coefficient on DBNL remained negative and statistically significant at the 5% level, suggesting that our main finding was not likely driven by one or more dominant states.

For the fourth robustness test, we conducted a propensity score matching (PSM) analysis to filter out the potential influences of observed firm characteristics that may differ between the treatment and control groups. Specifically, we first regressed an indicator variable, which equals 1 for treatment firms and 0 otherwise, on the firm characteristic variables included in the baseline regression in Equation (1). Based on the estimated coefficients, we calculated the propensity scores for all sample firms. Then, for each treatment firm, we chose the control firm with the closest propensity score, limiting the difference to a 0.5% caliper. Because the matched sample featured similar firm characteristics, this approach highlights the impact of DBNLs. We re-estimated the baseline regression using the matched sample. As Column 4 of Table 4 shows, DBNL still has a significantly negative coefficient, suggesting that our main result was not subsumed by the difference in firm characteristics.

For the fifth robustness test, we adopted an instrumental variable (IV) to further address concerns about potential endogeneity in our analysis. Although the multi-period dynamic analysis described above helps exclude reverse causality (i.e., data breaches could lead to the disclosure legislation), it is still possible that some omitted factors may have affected the incidence of data breaches and the enactment of DBNLs simultaneously, in which case the association we document between them could be spurious. To alleviate this concern, we chose an IV that is likely to be related to a state’s implementation of DBNL but unlikely to be related to the incidence of data breaches in that state. Following the approach in prior studies of state-level legislations (e.g., Jha & Cox, 2015; Hossain et al, 2021), we used the DBNL of a neighboring state as the IV. This measurement is valid because, as explained in the discussion of the institutional background, data breach disclosure legislation in one state is influenced by similar legislation in neighboring states.<sup>26</sup> Employing the usual IV approach, we estimated the fitted value of the regression of the variable Data Breach as defined in Equation (1) on the IV in the first stage and then regressed Data Breach on the fitted value in the second stage. Column 5 of Table 4 reports the second-stage results, in which we continued to use DBNL to denote the fitted value. The result is a negative and highly significant coefficient on DBNL, which supports our main conclusion about the relation between DBNLs and the incidence of data breaches.

For the final robustness test, we adopted an alternative method to construct the treatment and control samples for the DiD analysis. Specifically, we considered a sample of banks included in the WRDS Commercial Banks database and exclusively focused on those with operations (and customers) confined within a single state so that each bank received only a single shock from the DBNL in the state where it was located. One merit of this approach is that it fits better with the data breach notification schemes targeting local customers. In addition, banks generally collect highly sensitive personal data and are thus likely to be responsive to DBNLs. To implement this alternative sampling strategy, we obtained detailed bank branch information, namely location, and identified banks for which all branches were within the territory of one state. To match the individual customers of banks more effectively, we further narrowed our focus to private banks with a customer base consisting mainly of individual depositors and borrowers.

These customers tend to demonstrate a strong propensity to deposit in and borrow from local banks because of the ease of communication and geographical convenience (Bharath et al., 2011; Dass & Massa, 2011; Amore et al., 2013). By focusing on private banks with operations confined to a single state, we were able to identify the residence of customers at the state level in a clean manner.

This alternative sampling strategy generated 83,856 bank-year observations during our sample period of 2005-2018. In this sample, we assigned DBNL a value of 1 for treatment banks (i.e., those affected by the implementation of a state’s DBNL) in the post-implementation period, and 0 otherwise. With firm (i.e., bank) and year fixed effects, we re-estimated the baseline DiD model and report the result in Column 6 of Table 4. As shown, DBNL has a significantly negative coefficient that is consistent with our main result, suggesting that our key finding of a reducing effect of DBNLs on data breaches continued to hold in this alternative analysis scheme.<sup>27</sup>

## Cross-Sectional Variation in the Effect of DBNLs on the Incidence of Data Breaches

After documenting supporting evidence for the general effect of DBNLs on data breach reduction, as outlined in H1, we sought to account for cross-firm heterogeneity in the effect, as specified in H2a-c. Our strategy was to add to the baseline regression the interaction terms between DBNL and the moderator variables corresponding to the cross-sectional hypotheses,<sup>28</sup> and then examine the coefficients on the interactions terms to obtain inferences regarding the hypothesized arguments.

## Result for H2a: Reliance on Sensitive Customer Data

For the empirical testing of H2a, we introduced a variable, Sensitive Customer Data, to measure the degree of firm reliance on sensitive data. Specifically, we assigned it a value of 1 for firms in industries likely to involve larger amounts of sensitive customer data, including healthcare, transportation, restaurants, hotels and motels, personal services, and other related industries, and 0 otherwise. As evidenced in various sources,<sup>29</sup> firms in these industries tend to have more household customers and their businesses are more reliant on detailed and sensitive personal information.

We conducted the test by interacting Sensitive Customer Data with our key DiD variable DBNL and included it in the baseline regression.<sup>30</sup> The results reported in Column 1 of Table 5 shows that DBNL × Sensitive Customer Data has a significantly negative coefficient, suggesting that after the implementation of DBNLs, data breaches decrease more for firms holding more sensitive data. We found similar results in Column 4, where we included DBNL × Sensitive Customer Data together with two other interaction terms related to other cross-sectional hypotheses. To the extent that the level of a firm’s access to sensitive customer data is positively associated with the level of external pressures imposed on it by the mandate to disclose data breaches, this finding supports the moderating role of sensitive data in DBNLs’ effect on the incidence of data breaches, as hypothesized in H2a.

## Result for H2b: Privacy Protection Environment

To test H2b, we first identified the strictness level of a firm’s privacy protection environment using the privacy protection scores assessed by Comparitech. These scores were constructed based on 20 key criteria identified in 2018, including social media privacy laws, ISPs barred from sharing information with third parties, laws to protect biometric data, and call recording consent.<sup>31</sup> By revealing a wide range of varying privacy protections, these criteria reflect a state’s overall protection of its residents’ privacy and thus the general data protection environment in which a firm operates. Comparitech evaluates each criterion using Yes = 1 or No = 0 to mark whether an applicable law exists and then sums the values for all criteria as a ratio of the total value. The ratio is then taken as the privacy protection score. We considered the states (and firms therein) with higher-than-median scores as those with strict privacy protection environments, for which we defined an indicator variable, Strict Privacy Protection Environment, as 1; for other states (firms) with scores lower than the median, we set Strict Privacy Protection Environment to 0.

## Result for H2c: Intangible Assets

Regarding the moderating role of intangible assets, as stipulated in H2c, Huang and Wang (2021) and Kamiya et al. (2021) pointed out that firms with less PPE are more likely to rely on intangible assets. Based on this notion, we used one minus net PPE scaled by total assets as a proxy for firms’ asset intangibility, i.e., Intangible Assets, as defined before. Kamiya et al. (2021) also used this method to examine the economic consequences of cyberattacks.

We included Intangible Assets and its interaction term with DBNL in the baseline regression. In Columns 3 and 4 of Table 5, we see that the coefficients on the interactive term DBNL × Intangible Assets are negative and significant, suggesting that firms with a higher level of asset intangibility tend to adapt better to a regulation involving the disclosure of data breaches. Stated another way, these firms tend to be more responsive to DBNLs and make more efforts to improve their privacy protection performance in the post-DBNL period, consistent with H2c.

## Potential Channels That May Facilitate DBNLs’ Curbing of Data Breaches

The results documented thus far provide robust evidence that DBNLs lead to a decrease in the incidence of data breaches. In the following discussion, we present further evidence showing how DBNLs induce firms to ameliorate their data security management and minimize data loss. In exploring the potential channels through which firms can better safeguard customer privacy in response to the enactment of DBNLs, we focus on two corporate-wide strategic actions that directly facilitate the use and management of data. First, we assess whether firms are more likely to appoint an IT-specialized executive (e.g., CIO or CDO) to serve on their top management team after DBNLs go into effect. As the individuals overseeing people, processes, and technologies relating to information systems within a firm, ITspecialized executives are responsible for control, policy development, and effective exploitation of firm resources,<sup>33</sup> all of which contribute directly to data security. Moreover, the establishment of information or data security-related top management positions signals a firm’s seriousness in dealing with its IT problems, especially potential pitfalls that could lead to privacy breaches and trigger customer notifications. Overall, the staffing of IT specialists in the top management team indicates a strategic movement towards information security that could have a long-lasting impact on a firm’s IT performance. This IT strategy can facilitate privacy safeguarding, thereby reducing the incidence of data breaches.

Second, we investigate firms’ remediation of IT-related material weaknesses in their internal controls. IT internal controls have long been a key concern of managers and regulators and are highlighted in the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 (Li et al., 2007). IT internal control weakness (ICW) relates to deficiencies in IT governance, IT infrastructure, data management, and system maintenance that could be detrimental to data security. Effectively addressing these material weaknesses is a useful way to improve privacy protection and mitigate the risk of data breaches.

Using data from ExecuComp, including information about top managers for the S&P 1500 firms, we identified firms’ appointment of IT executives. Among the 22,097 observations obtained from ExecuComp, we created an indicator variable, IT Executive, which equals 1 if the firm had IT-specialized executives (such as CIO, CDO, and managers carrying the titles related to information, data, IT, internet, cyber, and security), and 0 otherwise. To measure firms’ remediation of weaknesses in IT-related internal controls, we extracted data from AuditAnalytics to be matched with our main sample and defined an indicator variable, IT ICW Remediation, which equals 1 if the firm did not report material ICW relating to IT in a given year but had done so in prior years,<sup>34</sup> and 0 otherwise. The rationale is that ceasing to list previously reported material IT ICW indicates that the weaknesses have probably been remediated. Ashbaugh-Skaife et al. (2008), Kim et al. (2011), and Bauer (2016) provide evidence suggesting that this measurement can capture a firm’s success in addressing its IT ICW.

Using IT Executive and IT ICW Remediation as the dependent variable in the baseline DiD regression, we report the results in Columns 1 and 2 of Table 6, respectively. The key independent variable DBNL has significantly positive coefficients in both columns, suggesting that the enactment of DBNLs increases the likelihood of a firm to appoint an IT-specialized executive and the likelihood of firm managers to remediate prior weaknesses in IT-related internal controls. To the extent that both actions can improve data security, the results identify possible channels through which DBNLs improve firms’ protection of their customers’ private data.

## Effects of DBNLs on Internal and External Data Breaches

The unauthorized leak of personal information can be initiated both externally and internally. Although external cyberattacks have attracted tremendous attention from IT professionals, regulators, and academics, as shown in Panel B of Table 1, the majority of data breaches actually stem from firms’ internal mishandling of data.<sup>35</sup> Different types of information breaches may have distinct implications regarding firms’ data safeguarding behaviors.

Internal data protection behaviors occur, by definition, within firms where managers control behavioral change in response to external institutional pressures. In terms of preventing external cyberattacks targeting personal data, managers may have different incentives and ability to take actions that improve security. First, firms may be at a technical disadvantage when responding to external cyber threats, especially amid an escalating technological arms race between firms and hackers. Second, to build and strengthen their security infrastructure, firms must undertake protective measures that create positive externalities for others (Kunreuther & Heal, 2003). Meanwhile, firms may not fully internalize the costs caused by cyberattacks because they share these costs with peer firms as a result of extensive business networking (Varian, 2004). For these reasons, it would be interesting to examine whether DBNLinduced institutional pressures are large enough to compel firms to overcome such hurdles and achieve security improvements regarding cyberattacks. Although it is tempting to conjecture a stronger DBNL effect for internal breach incidents and a weaker effect for external cyberattacks, whether the effects manifest differently is ultimately an empirical question.

To address this question, we categorized data breaches into two broad types: external hacking-related cyberattacks and internal data breaches. We accordingly created two indicator variables, External Breach and Internal Breach. In our sample, External Breach referred to situations in which the system was hacked by an outside party or infected by external malware, and Internal Breach included other forms of data breach incidents caused by reasons internal to the firm. ## Discussion and Conclusion

Businesses and organizations have become more dependent on digital data, and there have been frequent high-profile data leaks in recent decades. In this study, we examined the effect of laws mandating firms to notify their customers of information leaks involving customers’ personal data. Guided by institutional theory, we focused on institutional pressures fueled by the disclosed breach information and hypothesized that disclosure mandates help reduce the incidence of such breaches. Our empirical approach exploited state-level data breach disclosure statutes in the U.S. (i.e., DBNLs), which require notifications to be sent to customers if an unauthorized party has acquired their private information. Using the staggered adoptions of these DBNLs across U.S. states, we identified exogenous shocks to firms’ data breach disclosure and analyzed the impact of the disclosure on their security safeguarding performance. For this purpose, we constructed a treatment sample of firms more affected by breach disclosure laws and a benchmark control sample of less-affected firms. We then estimated a DiD regression to capture the effect of DBNLs on subsequent firm-level data breach incidents.

Our results show that the enactment of DBNLs meaningfully reduced the likelihood of security breach incidents for treatment firms relative to control firms. We further found this effect to be stronger among firms that were more reliant on sensitive customer data, operated in more privacy concerning environments, or had more intangible assets. These conditions are associated with higher levels of disclosure-induced institutional pressures from outside stakeholders, which compel firms to strengthen their data security measures. Additional analyses revealed that the disclosure mandates also induced firms to include IT-focused positions in their top management teams and improve IT-related internal controls, both of which facilitate data protection. Lastly, we found that the implementation of DBNLs reduced the incidence of privacy impairments caused by both internal breaches and external hacking and malware-related attacks. Overall, our findings provide useful information about the regulatory consequences of data breach disclosure mandates.
