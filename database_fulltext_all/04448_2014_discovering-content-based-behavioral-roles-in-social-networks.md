## a b s t r a c t

Role analysis helps us characterize users' interactions in a social network. However, previously proposed methods are mainly based on structural analysis of social networks rather than content-based behavior analysis Therefore, we propose a method to use the content-based behavioral features extracted from user-generated content and behavior patterns to identify users' roles and to explore role change patterns in social networks. The proposed method allows a user to play multiple roles in a social network and can identify roles without using any pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned roles. Thus, it provides a more general and <sup>fl</sup>exible way to perform role analyses in social networks. The experimental results show that the proposed method can <sup>fi</sup>nd various roles in different social networks, additional roles that haven't been previously aware of, and some interesting role change patterns. The results may help us better understand the characteristics and trends of a social network, and formulat more effective management strategies.

## 1. Introduction

Social networks such as Facebook, Google+, and Twitter have made a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on how users share and exchange data. Users join these networks, share opinions, make comments, and interact with their friends. The interactions involve behavior associated with particular roles. Thus, role analysis helps us to characterize users' interactions in a social network.

The concept of a social role is considered as the behavioral expectations that are associated with identi<sup>fi</sup>able positions in a social structure. What role does an individual play in a group? A user may play her role as a leader, a participant, a commentator, a lurker, or etc. Fournier and Lee [15] pointed out that communities are strongest when everyone plays a role. Members stay involved and add value by playing a wide variety of roles. Akaka and Chandler [2] mentioned that social roles act as resources for change in value networks because they can lead to social norms and establish social positions. Since users may shift their positions and change their statuses, their roles in a social network may change over time. Gleave et al. [17] showed that by identifying roles, it would be more effective to monitor some proportions of roles. Also, a better search result can be obtained if the authors of content are considered. Role analysis has long been central to sociology [8,11,34,38], and become more and more valuable in many aspects. For example, many studies [1,20,27,28,42] focused on <sup>fi</sup>nding the in<sup>fl</sup>uential leaders in a social group.

Since the rise of Web 2.0 from early 2000s, content generated by users become increasingly popular and important. Velardi et al. [44] mentioned that communicative content attracts more and more interest from business, social and research community analysts. Naaman et al. [36] pointed out that interesting and informative content is the key to increase amount of readers. McCallum et al. [33] presented the

Author-Recipient-Topic (ART) model for social network analysis. ART combines the connectivity structure and probabilistic language model to capture topics, <sup>fi</sup>nd social roles, and summarize a large amount of data. Bao et al. [3] explored user's potential interests by analyzing the user-generated content in micro-blogging. Through analyzing the content created by users, we can better understand the characteristics of users and behavior between users.

Most previously proposed methods [27,28,41,42,45] focus on structural analysis of social networks rather than content-based behavior analysis. In general, structural analysis methods de<sup>fi</sup>ne some social roles in the social network by the relationship patterns among users and then assign each user to a single pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned role. Consequently, structural analysis is con<sup>fi</sup>ned to analyzing the pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned roles speci-<sup>fi</sup>ed by some relationship patterns. In addition to social relationships, behavior and content generated by users play an important role in characterizing users, and may reveal personal characteristics, such as personality and emotions. User-generated content and behavior in social networks may be affected by current jobs, emotions or interests. Thus, content-based behavioral features are more capable of giving the upto-date statuses, where each feature may be used to characterize users in different aspects. For example, the affectivity expressed in their posts may reveal their attitudes and the actions captured from their posts may show implicit interests or habits. In comparison to structure analysis, current social roles are presented more precisely in contentbased behavior analysis.

In addition, many existing methods classify a user into a single social role. However, a user may play multiple roles at the same time. For example, a user may be simultaneously an opinion leader and a commenter since the user may often make comments on others' posts and the articles posted by the user are frequently shared. Moreover, users may change their roles in a social network over time because of constant involvement, new political orientation, etc. The roles in different time periods can be represented as a role change pattern which may indicate how and what users have reacted in response to their involvement and statuses over time. The role change patterns may help politicians review their policies if most supporters shift their roles to dissenters or neutralists, or help the site administrator of a social network learn how users shift their roles from viewers to opinion leaders because of long-term involvement. Therefore, mining role change patterns may facilitate the understanding of a social community and provide valuable insights for site administrators, politicians, social analysts, etc.

To the best of our knowledge, there is no method using contentbased behavioral features extracted from user-generated content and behavior patterns to identify roles without using any pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned roles and to explore role change patterns in social networks. Therefore, in this paper, we propose a content-based behavioral method to analyze the roles and role changes in social networks. Unlike previous studies, we combine content-based and behavioral features to discover roles in social networks. The features provide us a better view to analyze users' roles. Our proposed method <sup>fi</sup>rst extracts the content-based behavioral features for each user, and then utilizes fuzzy c-means clustering method (FCM) [7] to identify different roles in a social group. Next, it transforms the results obtained from FCM into role change sequences, and applies Pre<sup>fi</sup>xSpan [39] to discovering role change patterns. The proposed method can <sup>fi</sup>nd various roles in different social networks without using any pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned roles and may discover additional roles that haven't been previously aware of. Using the concept of fuzzy set to record user' role memberships provides us a <sup>fl</sup>exible way to identify multiple roles played by users in a social network. Moreover, contentbased behavioral features provide valuable information of users and better understand users' behavior.

We analyze the roles on technology, political, and entertainment social networks (or groups). There are six roles in a technology group: expert, kicker, leader, viewer, participant and follower; three roles in a political group: supporter, dissenter and neutralist; and three roles in entertainment groups: creator, commentator and participant. We also discover some interesting role change patterns in different groups. In the technology group, users may shift their roles from viewer to leader since they learn more and more expertise from the fan group so that their recognition increases with time. In the political group, users shift their roles from supporters to dissenters or neutralists with higher negative affectivity. It may be a warning sign for the politician.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys the related work. Section 3 describes the problem de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and our proposed framework. Section 4 evaluates the proposed framework. Finally, the conclusions and future work are described in Section 5.

## 2. Related work

Role analysis has long been central to sociology. Callero [11] mentioned that roles are assumed to be real, objective, meaningful features of the social world. Many studies focused on using structural features to identify different roles. Golder [19] combined qualitative and quantitative methods to develop a typology of social roles, including ethnographic study and statistical analysis of participation to determine different roles. Turner et al. [43] categorized newsgroups, authors, and threads in terms of their activity patterns in Usenet. They presented a series of visualizations showing newsgroup hierarchies, newsgroup populations, authors, and conversation threads of Usenet newsgroups and user activities. They also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed some characteristic patterns from users' posting behavior and classi<sup>fi</sup>ed users into different roles such as answer person, questioner, troll, spammer, binary poster, <sup>fl</sup>ame warrior, and conversationalist. Fisher et al. [14] presented a method to <sup>fi</sup>nd second-degree ego-centric networks by the patterns of replies in Usenet Newsgroups. Welser et al. [46] used visualization methods including authorlines, neighborhood networks and degree distribution charts to reveal structural signatures used to describe the characteristics of a social role. Skopik et al. [41] proposed a method to determine trust relationships between users and performed structural analysis to infer social roles. Morzy [35] presented a model for social role discovery with statistical analysis and network analysis in Internet forums. Wu and Chen [48] developed a systematic framework to classify users into four types of users such as posting picture user, literary user, commenting user, and quickly-re user who always gives a quick response to other users.

For behavior-based analysis, IX survey [25] conducted a survey and classi<sup>fi</sup>ed social network members into six roles according to their involvement, namely, creator, discussant, commentator, sharer, participator and viewer. Jamali and Rangwala [26] used a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation and regression framework to study the behavioral characteristics of users. Benevenuto et al. [4,6] provided a clickstream model to characterize user activities in online social networks. Welser et al. [47] analyzed messages sending behavior in Q&A systems, and tried to <sup>fi</sup>nd the experts. Yang [50] proposed an approach to build user pro<sup>fi</sup>le capturing the strength of users' behavioral patterns that can be used to identify users. Zhang et al. [52] attempted to study bloggers' post-adoption behavior and analyze the roles of genders in switching behavior. Yang and Padmanabhan [51] analyzed the effect of observation time period on user identi<sup>fi</sup>cation based on online user behavior. Zhu et al. [53] presented a behavior-based clustering approach to identify roles in a telecommunication network.

By pre-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed roles, a few content-based methods have been proposed for role analysis. Garg et al. [16] combined lexical features and structural interaction features to automatically recognize different speaker roles, including project manager, marketing expert, user interface expert, and industrial designer. Bodendorf and Kaiser [9] presented a text mining approach to detect opinion leaders and opinion trends. Naaman et al. [36] analyzed the messages posted by Twitter users and developed a content-based examination for users' activities.

To analyze different roles in various social media, Fisher et al. [14] presented a method to <sup>fi</sup>nd second-degree ego-centric networks by the patterns of replies in Usenet Newsgroups. Benevenuto et al. [5] provided a characterization of social and content attributes to classify Youtube users into three roles as spammer, promoter, and legitimate. Maia et al. [31] proposed a method for characterizing user behavior in an online social network. They utilized speci<sup>fi</sup>c features of Youtube and interaction features to cluster similar behavioral patterns. Welser et al. [45] used structural signature methods to identify four key roles in Wikipedia, namely, substantive expert, technical editor, vandal <sup>fi</sup>ghter and social networker. Fazeen et al. [13] proposed two methods for classifying the roles in Twitter into leader, lurker, spammer and close associate. Gwebu and Wang [22] pointed out that social identi<sup>fi</sup>cations in the Open Source Software (OSS) community may in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals' decisions to adopt OSS applications where the social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is referred to the degree of belonging to the community.

An important issue in role analysis is to <sup>fi</sup>nd the in<sup>fl</sup>uential leaders in a social network. Kempe et al. [27] presented a provable approximation to choose a good in<sup>fl</sup>uential set of individuals to maximize the spread of in<sup>fl</sup>uence in a social network. Then, Kempe et al. [28] de<sup>fi</sup>ned a natural and general model of in<sup>fl</sup>uence propagation to solve the target set selection problem. Goyal et al. [20] introduced a frequent pattern mining approach to discover leaders and tribes in a social network. Trusov et al. [42] developed a Bayesian shrinkage approach to identify in<sup>fl</sup>uential nodes in a social network, and implemented a Poisson regression model to estimate the users' in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Agarwal et al. [1] presented a preliminary model to quantify in<sup>fl</sup>uential bloggers and built a robust model to <sup>fi</sup>nd various types of in<sup>fl</sup>uential bloggers. Xu et al. [49] used four different models (the degree of adopter friend model, the personal network model, the frequency model and the frequency-rating model) to measure the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence of a member in an online product review social network.

Unlike the previously proposed methods, we utilize content-based behavior features to identify user's roles without de<sup>fi</sup>ning role types in advance. In addition, by introducing the concept of fuzzy sets to the proposed method, we allow a user to play multiple roles in a social network, not limited to just one.

## 3. The proposed framework

In this Section, we describe the problem de<sup>fi</sup>nition and propose a framework to identify the roles in a social network and analyze role change patterns. The proposed framework contains two phases for role analysis. First, we modify the fuzzy c-means algorithm to cluster together users with similar characteristics and identify social roles in different social groups. Next, we propose an approach to <sup>fi</sup>nd the role change patterns and use them to analyze users' behavior.

## 3.1. Problem definition

A social network contains a number of users, where users may perform actions and create content. By analyzing the behavior and content generated by users in the social network, we can <sup>fi</sup>nd users' roles and role change patterns.

De<sup>fi</sup>nition 1. Let ${ \sf S R } = \{ r _ { 1 } , r _ { 2 } , . . . , r _ { n } \}$ be a collection of roles in the social network. Every role is distinct from each other and exhibits a particular behavior characteristic.

A user may play multiple roles in the social network. The membership MS to each role for the user can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into a <sup>fi</sup>nite number of degrees, where $0 \leq M S \leq 1$ . For example, the membership is classi-<sup>fi</sup>ed into three degrees by Eq. (1).

$$
\text { Degree } = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} L, & 0 <   M S <   0. 2 5 \\ M, & 0. 2 5 \leq M S <   0. 7 5 \\ H, & 0. 7 5 \leq M S \leq 1 \end{array} \right.\tag{1}
$$

De<sup>fi</sup>nition 2. Let a role-degree pair (RD pair), $( r _ { 1 } , d _ { 1 } )$ , denote that the degree of membership to role $r _ { 1 }$ is $d _ { 1 }$ . The roles played by a user in a certain period of time are denoted as a roleset, a set of RD pairs. The roles played by the user in several periods of time can be represented by a sequence of rolesets (roleset sequence). For example, the roles played by user A in the <sup>fi</sup>rst period of time can be denoted as $[ ( r _ { 1 } , M ) ( r _ { 2 } , L ) ( r _ { 3 } , L ) ]$ , where A plays $r _ { 1 }$ with degree M, $r _ { 2 }$ with degree $L ,$ and $r _ { 3 }$ with degree L. A sequence $\{ [ ( r _ { 1 } , M ) ( r _ { 2 } , L ) ( r _ { 3 } , L ) ] [ ( r _ { 1 } , H ) ( r _ { 2 } , L ) ] \}$ represents the roles played by A in the <sup>fi</sup>rst two periods.

De<sup>fi</sup>nition 3. A roleset contains another roleset if every RD pair in the latter can be found in the former.

De<sup>fi</sup>nition 4. A roleset sequence contains another roleset sequence if every roleset in the latter is contained by a roleset in the former, and those rolesets in the former appear in the same order as these in the latter.

De<sup>fi</sup>nition 5. A role change pattern (RC pattern) of length k is called an RC k-pattern, where the length of an RC pattern is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the number of RD pairs in it.

De<sup>fi</sup>nition 6. A RC pattern is frequent if its support is not less than the minimum support threshold, where the support is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the fraction of roleset sequences containing the pattern in the database.

For example, a pattern $\{ [ ( r _ { 1 } , H ) ] [ ( r _ { 1 } , M ) ( r _ { 2 } , M ) ] [ ( r _ { 1 } , L ) ( r _ { 2 } , H ) ] \}$ denotes that users play $r _ { 1 }$ with degree H, shift to play $r _ { 1 }$ with degree M and $r _ { 2 }$ with degree M, and then shift to play $r _ { 1 }$ with degree L and $r _ { 2 }$ with degree H.

The role appearing most frequently in a social group is called a main role. The main role can help us to understand the major composition of a group. The role appearing only once is called a special role. The special role can help us to detect some special events.

## 3.2. Proposed framework

The proposed framework is shown in Fig. 1. There are two phases in our framework. In phase 1, we <sup>fi</sup>rst partition the data stream into several periods (ex. one month per period). For each period of data, we employ the fuzzy c-means algorithm to group users into c clusters and identify the roles played by the users in each cluster. In phase 2, we transform the roles played by each user into a roleset sequence. Next, we apply the Pre<sup>fi</sup>xSpan algorithm to <sup>fi</sup>nd frequent role change patterns and analyze the patterns found.

## 3.3.2. User properties

To characterize users for role analysis on social groups in various social networks, we categorize the attributes used in the previous studies into four types, namely personality [6,21], behavior [6,17], action [6,21], and recognition [1,27]. Personality captures the basic information of an individual user. Behavior assembles user's behavior in a social group. Recognition denotes the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of an individual user. Action represents the mode of a post. To understand the post habits, we extend action into action sequence by capturing the sequence of post modes.

In addition, we observe that the attitudes of users may express their positions or opinions toward a certain topic. Sentiment analysis [32] has been widely used to present the subjective orientation of attitudes, opinions, feelings and experiences of users over the Web for analyzing social groups [18], online reviews [12,23,24,40], feature selection [12], business intelligence [30], etc. Thus, we introduce affectivity to measure users' attitudes implicitly re<sup>fl</sup>ected by the sentiments in their posts. Therefore, we use <sup>fi</sup>ve types of attributes, namely, personality (PE), behavior (BE), action sequence (AS), affectivity (AF), and recognition (RE), to describe user properties. Considering the privacy settings and the information that describes user properties must be accessible across multiple social groups, we collect the available data for these types of attributes. It is worth noting that our proposed method can be easily extended to use additional attributes of a targeting social network if they are available.

Analyzing the emotion in the articles generated by a user can discriminate user's implicit attitude. For example, a political group generally has supporters and dissenters. By analyzing the emotion in articles, we can detect users having similar behavior but standing on different positions. The affective norms for English words (ANEW) [10] have been developed to provide a set of normative emotional rating for a large number of English words. Nielsen [37] evaluated 2477 English words used in microblogs and rated them with a score between −5 and 5. We use these words to calculate user's affectivity. The calculation steps are listed as follows.

a. Sum up all positive and negative scores in a post.

b. Divide total positive and negative scores by the length of the post.

c. Normalize the scores by multiplying each score by the average length of posts.

Thus, an affectivity vector is denoted as AF = (positive score, negative score), which represents the average positive and negative affective scores in a post for each user. Next, we use the cosine distance to compute the distance between two affectivity vectors.

## (5) Recognition (RE)

Recognition in sociology is public acknowledgement of person's status or merits. By analyzing the users' recognition accumulated in a social group, we can <sup>fi</sup>nd the in<sup>fl</sup>uential users whose posts are more respected by or attracted to other users. We take three features into consideration. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is the number of comments obtained from other users, which shows the topicality or attraction of user's posts. The second is the number of posts shared by other users. The third is the number of likes obtained from other users, which implies the acceptance or usefulness of user's posts. It may reveal the value and in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the user. Thus, the recognition feature vector is denoted as $R E =$ (number of comments received from other users/number of posts, number of posts shared by other users/number of posts, number of likes received from other users/number of posts). We de<sup>fi</sup>ne the distance between two recognition feature vectors, $R E _ { 1 }$ and $R E _ { 2 } ,$ by CD(RE<sub>1</sub>, RE<sub>2</sub>) · ED(RE<sub>1</sub>,RE<sub>2</sub>). Then, the distance is normalized into [0,1].

Therefore, a content-based behavioral feature vector of each user is formed by concatenating together personality, behavior, action sequence, affectivity, and recognition feature vectors. The distance between two content-based behavioral feature vectors (or two users) is computed by $\alpha D _ { P E } + \beta D _ { B E } + \gamma D _ { A S } + \delta D _ { A F } + \eta D _ { R E } ,$ where $D _ { P E } , D _ { B E } , D _ { A S } , D _ { A F }$ and $D _ { R E }$ respectively denote the distances between the personality feature vectors, the behavior feature vectors, the action sequences, the affectivity feature vectors and the recognition feature vectors of the two users, and $\alpha + \beta + \gamma + \delta + \eta = 1$ . Similarly, the distance is normalized into [0,1].

3.3.4. Fuzzy c-means clustering

For example, in Android fans, users can post related information (news links, videos, photos, etc.), make comments to discuss with other users, and click “Like” button to follow leader's posts. ## 3.4. Discovering role change patterns

To mine role change patterns, we <sup>fi</sup>rst transform every membership in each membership vector into a degree as de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Eq. (1). For example, the membership vector (0.18,0.01,0.81) is transformed into $[ ( r _ { 1 } , L ) ( r _ { 2 } , L ) ( r _ { 3 } , H ) ]$ . The transformed degree vectors are collected into a database, where each transaction contains a sequence of rolesets. Next, we employ the Pre<sup>fi</sup>xSpan algorithm [39] to mine frequent RC patterns. The Pre<sup>fi</sup>xSpan algorithm <sup>fi</sup>rst scans the database and <sup>fi</sup>nds all frequent RC 1-patterns, where each RC 1-pattern is associated with a projected database. The projected database of an RC pattern contains the post<sup>fi</sup>xes of the roleset sequences containing the pattern, where the post<sup>fi</sup>x of a roleset sequence containing pattern P is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the rest sequence behind P. For each frequent RC pattern found Q, we recursively <sup>fi</sup>nd frequent 1-patterns in its projected database and use them to extend Q to longer frequent ones in a depth-<sup>fi</sup>rst search manner.

## 4. Experiment setup and results

## 4.1. Data collection

We collected the datasets from Facebook. Facebook is a social networking website launched in 2004. The population of users is over 800 million<sup>1</sup> and still increases. Facebook is not only the largest social network site in the world, but also grows at the fastest rate. People use Facebook to communicate with friends, play Web games, share information and form groups with users having the same hobbies. There are more and more fan groups formed on Facebook. Most users join more than one group. They can post articles, upload photos, share links, ask questions in the discussion areas, post ideas on the News Feed and interact with group members. The groups on Facebook comprise users with the same interest.

Facebook Pages<sup>2</sup> group people who have the same interest and give them a stage to share with others. People in Pages can post articles which can be a link, video, photo or status, make comments on others' posts, click “Like” button, and even share information to their friends not in the group. We used the Facebook Graph $\mathsf { A P I } ^ { 3 }$ to collect public data from different types of Pages, including member list, news feed, photos, videos, Pages information and other related data such as descriptive messages, comment text and like list. By the Graph API and $\mathrm { F Q L } ^ { 4 }$ provided by Facebook, we also collected public data from users in the member list and then extracted user features from the public data.

Fan pages of categories may exhibit different characteristics of usergenerated content and behavior, which may formulate various social roles. To verify if our method can identify diverse social roles by using the proposed user properties, we investigated three categories of fan pages, namely, politics, technology, and entertainment, which have salient user properties on affectivity, recognition and action sequences, respectively. We observed that most users in politics category tend to actively express their opinions with a large number of affective words while many users in entertainment category simply perform sharing actions, such as videos, photos or links. It is because entertaining informative materials uploaded to websites often provide links to be shared. On the other hand, users usually require expertise to discuss technology applications. Since there may exist some users more familiar with technical details in technology category, they are able to solve the problems encountered by other users and thus receive higher recognition. As a result, considering the popularity and publicity of examples in each category, we select Barrack Obama Page from politics, Android Page from technology, as well as Music and Movie Page from entertainment, respectively. Android is a widely-used operating system for mobile devices and has been extended in many additional applications, such as televisions and game consoles. Music and Movie are considered as two of the most common and prevailing entertainments around the globe.

The time spanned by the dataset is divided into several periods on a monthly basis. Thus, the Obama dataset is split to 18 periods, the Android dataset 27 periods, the Movie dataset 7 periods, and the Music dataset 7 periods.

## 5. Conclusions and future work

In this paper, we have proposed a content-based method to identify users' roles and <sup>fi</sup>nd the role change patterns in a social network. Our proposed method can <sup>fi</sup>nd various roles in different social networks without using any pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned roles and identify multiple roles played by users. It provides a more general and <sup>fl</sup>exible way to perform role analyses in a social network. Users' behavior and content generated by users play an important role in characterizing users. Thus, the affectivity and recognition features in our model lead us to <sup>fi</sup>nd more meaningful roles from different aspects. The recognition helps us to <sup>fi</sup>nd the in<sup>fl</sup>uential individuals. The affectivity expresses the attitude of users to the group. Moreover, by introducing the concept of fuzzy sets to the proposed method, we allow a user to play multiple roles in a social network, not limited to just one.

The experimental results show that the proposed method <sup>fi</sup>nds various roles in social networks without using any pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned roles and can discover additional roles that haven't been previously aware of. For example, kicker in Android is found unexpectedly. In addition, our method discovers the role distribution and role change patterns. It is helpful for us to identify different roles of users and implement different policies to manage them.

In addition, we discover some interesting frequent RC patterns in the Android and Obama datasets. In Android, some users are likely to shift their roles to leader since they frequently discuss technology issues about Android, and learn more and more expertise from the fan group. Thus, their recognition increases with time. In Obama, even there are some users staunchly support the politician, some users may shift their roles to the other roles with higher negative affectivity, which may be a warning sign for the politician.

## 5.1. Implications for research

This study contributes to three theoretical aspects. First, although many prior studies [4,6,25,49,51,52] explored different user behaviors to characterize a user, few have shed light on the users' attitudes and interests. In the proposed method, we introduce two new features, action sequence and affectivity, to analyze the content and behavior in social networks. The affectivity expressed in users' posts may reveal their attitudes or stands, and the actions sequence captured from their posts may show implicit interests or habits. Incorporating these two new features can derive more diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed social roles as shown in the experiments. Second, while classifying a user into a single role at a time is a research paradigm in this research domain, our study has advanced the literature by allowing users to play multiple roles and have a membership to each role. We believe that this extension can broaden the applications as well as enrich the research on positioning one's social roles. Finally, the research on role change pattern over time is barely explored. Examining such patterns may boost our understanding of the transition process of social roles in the social network.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings from implications for research inform practitioners on several managerial aspects. Many methods [27,28,42] of <sup>fi</sup>nding in<sup>fl</sup>uential leaders for viral marketing consider either social structure or information propagation structure. However, some in<sup>fl</sup>uential leaders may make positive comments and the others may make negative ones. By taking the affectivity of user-generated content into account, we can choose the supportive in<sup>fl</sup>uential leaders who often make positive comments as the initial targets for viral marketing. Besides, users with a straight “photo” post mode may have different interests or habits from users with a straight “link” post mode. By action sequences, the <sup>fi</sup>rm of a fan group can choose the adequate information or media to interact with users so that the users may aggressively share it with others. Next, our proposed method allows a user to play multiple roles and have a membership to each role. By targeting customers with high, medium and low supportive memberships, a <sup>fi</sup>rm can formulate different marketing strategies for them to maximize its pro<sup>fi</sup>t. Finally, exploring the role change patterns may offer an alternative way to gain valuable insights for politicians, site administrators, social analysts, etc. For example, in the political dataset, the role change patterns help politicians to review their policies. Or, in the technology dataset, the role change patterns show that the users having engaged in long-term involvement may learn more expertise and then become experts. Thus, the site administrators can adequately cultivate more role changes, such as, from participant to leader, or kicker to follower, so that the social group will lead to a value-creating environment, and in turns, attract more users to the fan group and make revenue from either showing ads to users (impressions) or being paid for each click made by users in response to an ad.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research directions

Although our method has been intensively conducted, there are still some limitations which can be done further in the future to enrich role analyses. First, we do not take the semantics of content into account in the proposed method. It is worth analyzing the semantics to improve the performance. Second, although our model is capable of analyzing roles in many social groups, maybe it is not yet robust enough to analyze a large scale of social networks. Thus, we may also consider extending the proposed method in the MapReduce framework.<sup>6</sup> Third, with the release to enterprise or government data, we may also broaden the scope from fan groups to enterprise or government groups and compare the roles found from different fan groups. Finally, confronting with the privacy issues and settings, we have dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in collecting complete information of users to better describe users due to massive missing values and incomplete data. If more information is available in the future, we may further expand the user properties by including more attributes and behavior as well as conduct more experiments on fan pages from a wider range of categories.
