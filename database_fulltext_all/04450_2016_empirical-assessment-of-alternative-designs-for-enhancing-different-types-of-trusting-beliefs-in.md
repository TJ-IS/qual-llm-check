# Empirical Assessment of Alternative Designs for Enhancing Different Types of Trusting Beliefs in Online Recommendation Agents

WEIQUAN WANG AND IZAK BENBASAT

WEIQUAN WANG (weiquan@cityu.edu.hk; corresponding author) is an associate professor in the College of Business at City University of Hong Kong. He received his Ph.D. in management information systems from the University of British Columbia. His research interests include online recommendation agents, human–computer interaction, social media, and online information privacy. His work has been published in Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and others. He has served as an associate editor of MIS Quarterly.

IZAK BENBASAT (izak.benbasat@ubc.ca) is a Fellow of the Royal Society of Canada, and Sauder Distinguished Professor of Information Systems at the Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia. He serves on the editorial boards of Journal of Management Information Systems and Information Systems Journal. He was editor in chief of Information Systems Research and editor of other journals. He received the LEO Award for Lifetime Exceptional Achievements in Information Systems from AIS and was conferred the title of Distinguished Fellow by the INFORMS Information Systems Society.

ABSTRACT: competence, integrity, and benevolence are the three key trusting beliefs that are widely acknowledged in the trust literature. Drawing on users’ different dispositional attribution of these trusting beliefs, we investigate the different influence of two sets of experiential reasons on the competence belief versus the benevolence and integrity beliefs in online recommendation agents (RAs). The two sets of experiential reasons encompass interactive reason, including three performance factors (namely, perceived cognitive effort, advice quality, and perceived strategy restrictiveness), and knowledge-based reason (i.e., perceived transparency of an RA). Data were collected through a laboratory experiment to test our hypotheses. Results demonstrate that the three performance factors affect only the competence belief, whereas perceived RA transparency influences all three trusting beliefs. In addition, the effects of perceived transparency on competence are partially mediated by perceived cognitive effort and advice quality. The research contributes to the trust literature by revealing the different antecedents of the three trusting beliefs and provides guidelines for designers to choose specific design elements to improve a particular trusting belief of the user toward an RA.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: advice quality, cognitive effort, decision support, recommendation agent, restrictiveness, transparency, trust, trusting beliefs.

Trust is widely conceptualized as a multidimensional construct that is composed of a set of trusting beliefs, namely, competence (ability of a trustee to effectively perform in a specific domain), integrity (adhering to principles generally accepted by a trustor, such as honesty and promise-keeping), and benevolence (caring and motivation to act in the trustor’s interests) [44, 46, 65, 67, 70, 79]. The discriminant validity of these three trusting beliefs is well-established in the literature (e.g., [46, 70, 79]). Each belief “contributes a unique perceptual perspective from which to consider the trustee” [44, p. 717], and the three beliefs are separable from, albeit correlated with, one another. For example, a benevolent mentor may not necessarily possess the expertise to supervise a doctoral student (i.e., may have low competence).

Recent studies in information systems (IS) have investigated the effects of various designs on users’ trust in technological artifacts [10, 65, 67, 70, 79], such as the effects of perceived system quality on trust in websites [65]. Surprisingly, limited research has investigated how users’ perceptions of various technological designs may lead to different trusting beliefs. Such investigation is important for practitioners to be able to influence different trust dimensions via the features selected and incorporated into information technology (IT) designs. Users’ concerns about different trusting beliefs may vary according to context in which the IT is applied. For example, users may care more about the competence issue regarding a national identity card system than its benevolence, whereas users of e-commerce recommendation systems may place equal importance on all three trusting beliefs.

This study aims to investigate the different antecedents of each of the three trusting beliefs in the context of web-based decision support technologies. It particularly focuses on users’ trust that arises from their interaction experiences with a technology (i.e., experiential trust). Previous research has suggested that trust in a technology is influenced by both interaction experiences and nonexperiential factors (e.g., dispositional factors) [45]. Experiential bases of trust are generally more robust and influential than nonexperiential bases because these experiences are more readily accessible in people’s memory and people consider behavioral experiences as highly reliable [47, 67, 75]. In addition, interaction experiences vary based on objects that are trusted (e.g., persons versus technologies), whereas nonexperiential factors are common to different trust objects [32]. Unlike the context of interacting with other people, users can freely test, manipulate, and gain control over a technology (as long as these actions are allowed), and these interactions subsequently shape their trust judgments. Therefore, studying the experiential bases of trust constitutes a distinctive aspect of research on trust in technologies. Nevertheless, little research has been conducted to investigate the experiential bases that can account for users’ different trusting beliefs toward technologies.

Wang and Benbasat [67] have identified a set of reasons and factors leading to trust in technologies; two of these reasons are based on experiences. One is an interactive reason, and the other is a knowledge-based reason. The interaction reason focuses on users’ performance assessments of a technology when they interact with it to perform decision tasks (i.e., assessments of what the technology does to assist users), whereas the knowledge-based reason refers to users’ understanding of a technology. However, whether or not users’ perceptions of technological designs related to performance assessments and understanding of technology can equally affect the three trusting beliefs remains unknown.

In the absence of a deep understanding of the effects of these designs, one cannot easily identify the most effective experiential base for increasing a specific trusting belief; moreover, understanding how different designs work synergistically to enhance users’ trusting beliefs in a technology becomes difficult. Yet this understanding is important given that it can inform designers of the relative value of various experiential bases in enhancing trust in a technology. In addition, it will enable them to choose certain design factors from these bases by considering both the design effort to be expended and the value to be achieved, including trust enhancement.

This study draws on Weiner’s (1986) attribution theory [73] to investigate the differential effects of various assessments made by users of technological designs on the three trusting beliefs toward a technology. In particular, we examine the differences in people’s stability attributions of the trusting beliefs. Stability refers to the degree to which the cause fluctuates (or remains constant) in the future [63]. Stability attributions are the primary driver of future expectations since these attributions indicate what to expect in the future under similar circumstances [73]. Stability attributions play a central role with respect to trust because users’ trust judgment is generally anchored in their expectation of trustees’ future behavior [44].

People generally make dispositional attributions of their morality-related beliefs about others (e.g., integrity and benevolence). By contrast, with respect to the competence belief, individuals can work to develop skills and expertise; thus, competence is regarded as less stable than integrity and benevolence. Integrity and benevolence beliefs cannot be easily changed by the satisfactory task performance of a given technology, whereas assessments of task performance may be effective in influencing users’ competence belief toward the technology. A key premise of this study is that the three trusting beliefs have different antecedents given their different attributions. Weiner’s (1986) attribution theory [73] lays out a theoretical ground to investigate the distinguishing effects of the two sets of experiential reasons identified in Wang and Benbasat [67] on the three trusting beliefs.

The primary contribution of this study is to provide a fine-grained understanding of trust formation at the trusting belief level. Although the prior literature widely acknowledges the three trusting beliefs to be conceptually different from one another, this study further and for the first time certifies their difference in terms of the various antecedents for enhancing competence as compared with those for benevolence and integrity. Wang and Benbasat [67] identify a set of experiential reasons for users’ trusting beliefs in a technology; however, they do not focus on the differences among these beliefs. We advance this line of research by applying Weiner’s [73] attribution theory to investigate the differences in the three trusting beliefs in decision support technologies.

## Background

## Recommendation Agents for e-Commerce

This study was conducted in the specific context of a web-based decision support technology, that is, product recommendation agents (RAs) for consumers’ online decision making (see Xiao and Benbasat [77, 78] for a comprehensive review). RAs are Internet-based softwares that carry out a set of operations on behalf of users to provide product advice based on user needs, profiles, and previous shopping activities [5, 42, 69]. These RAs help buyers reduce their information overload [36, 42] and improve the quality of their purchase decisions [21, 24].

Various RA technologies have been successfully implemented on websites. This study examines content-filtering-based RAs. This type of RA requires users to explicitly specify their needs and attribute preferences. The RA then integrates and uses these individual preferences by applying certain decision strategies [68, 69]. The focus on this type of technology is in line with the technology target in a series of recent IS studies [33, 59, 68, 69]. Many commercial systems are based on a content-filtering method (e.g., those offered by Shopping.com and Sears.com).

A central component of a content-filtering-based RA is the decision strategy that it applies to generate product recommendations. Additive compensatory (AC) and elimination models are two widely studied strategies [62] that are applicable to content-filtering-based RAs (e.g., [53, 68, 69]). The AC strategy involves the evaluation of every alternative (e.g., a camera model) based on all its attributes. Each attribute is assigned an importance weight by the user. A weighted total score for each alternative is then calculated by multiplying an attribute’s transformed (e.g., normalized) value for that alternative and its corresponding weight and then summing them up. Once these computations are complete for all alternatives, users are provided with a list of recommendations ordered by the weighted total score.

The AC strategy considers not only all of users’ attribute preferences but also the importance level of each preference. This characteristic allows for trade-offs between different preferences according to the relative importance of these preferences. The AC strategy applied by RAs is in line with the fact that people usually make tradeoffs in their decision-making processes [6, 15]. Such trade-offs are often necessary because “hardly ever is one lucky enough to find an alternative that is superior in every way to other alternatives” [15, p. 175].

By comparison, in the elimination strategy, each product alternative is evaluated by different attributes, and any alternative that violates any threshold value specified by the user for an attribute is eliminated. In contrast, in the AC strategy users preferences are not processed in a comprehensive manner by the elimination strategy (e.g., lower-valued attributes are not compensated for higher-valued attributes). As such, many product alternatives of satisfactory quality risk being prematurely eliminated. The AC and elimination strategies therefore represent the normative and less normative ends of the strategy continuum, respectively [69]. In general, users decision processes are better supported and less restricted by AC-based RAs than by elimination-based RAs [69].

This study focuses on nondeceptive RAs that are reasonably well-designed and are intended to help users find suitable products. These RAs faithfully assist users in making their product choices. Deceptive RAs also exist [16, 77], and these RAs do not exactly follow the AC or the elimination strategy. Users’ mistrust in deceptive RAs and poorly designed RAs is an interesting research topic [76], but it is beyond the scope of this study.

## Trusting Beliefs Toward RAs

Organizations increasingly provide web-based decision support technologies to consumers to improve their online experiences [12, 35, 68, 78]. However, the lack of consumer trust prevents the full utilization of these technologies [33, 50, 60, 65, 71]. RAs facilitate consumers’ decision making by assuming a portion of their decision tasks (e.g., narrowing down the consideration set to products that fit their preferences); as such, an agency relationship exists between an RA and its users. Trust plays an important role with respect to the acceptance of an RA because of this agency relationship [70]. When a user delegates certain decision tasks to an RA offered by an Internet vendor, the goal of the provider or designer of the RA might differ from that of the user. For example, an RA could be designed in such a way that it only aims to attain high profits for the Internet vendor and thus fails to consider the best interests of its users. When users recognize the incongruence of the RA’s goals, they may realize that the vendor is attempting to take advantage of them [20, 64, 70]. Unlike traditional offline decision support technologies owned by their users, RAs in online environments frequently induce trust concerns because of the lack of assurances that e-vendors or RA providers will not engage in opportunistic behaviors [14, 20].

The recent trust literature has discussed four general approaches to defining trust [14, 46]: (1) a belief or a collection of beliefs [7, 70]; (2) emotional feelings [33, 72]; (3) an intention [46]; and (4) a combination of these elements [46]. Several comprehensive literature reviews on trust have already been published (e.g., [14, 44, 46]). This study focuses on trusting beliefs because they mediate IT designs and trusting intentions [39, 46]. Multiple dimensions of trusting beliefs have been identified in the trust literature, and those concerning a decision support technology include users’ perceptions of the competence, benevolence, and integrity of the technology in question [67, 70, 79]. The discriminant validity of these trusting beliefs has been widely discussed in the literature (e.g., [46]), and the nomological validity of trusting beliefs toward RAs has been empirically confirmed by Wang and Benbasat [71] and Komiak and Benbasat [33]. Users form a certain level of trusting beliefs toward the RA (in this study, “trusting beliefs” and “trust” are used interchangeably), when interacting with it, which in turn influences their intentions to adopt the RA.

## Hypothesis Development

## Effects of Performance Factors on Competence

The interactive reason and the knowledge-based reason are the two key experiential reasons influencing users’ trusting beliefs toward RAs, according to a systematic review of the related literature and a coding of users’ written protocols during their interactions with an RA conducted by Wang and Benbasat [67]. The interactive reason centers on the performance and behavior of the RA with respect to the focal decision task. This study particularly applies the effort– accuracy–restrictiveness (EAR) framework proposed and investigated by Wang and Benbasat [69] to identify users’ key performance evaluations of an RA. This framework includes three key performance factors: (1) perceived cognitive effort of the decision task aided by an RA, (2) perceived strategy restrictiveness of the RA, and (3) perceived advice quality of the RA. This EAR framework is an extension of the effort–accuracy framework of cognition that has been applied in prior behavioral research on decision support systems to investigate their beneficial impacts on reducing the cognitive effort expended by users and increasing their decision quality (accuracy) [21, 24]. In addition to these two factors, Wang and Benbasat [69] confirmed that the level of constraints posed by an RA (perceived strategy restrictiveness, which refers to the extent to which users’ preferred decision processes are constrained by the functionalities and support provided by the technology) is also a primary performance factor as perceived by users. As such, this extended framework presents a comprehensive spectrum of users’ key performance assessments by covering both the benefits and the costs to RA users.

The effects of the performance assessments on users’ trusting beliefs in an RA are in line with an expectancy view of trust. A classic view of trust is to consider it as an expectancy held by a trustor [14, 44], because “individuals’ judgments about others’ trustworthiness are anchored, at least in part, on their a priori expectations about others” [34, p. 576]. Users posit two types of expectancy beliefs when interacting with a technology: outcome and process expectations [11]. In the literature on technological trust, outcome-focused performance is widely regarded as a key factor that influences users’ trust in the context of automation [37, 38]. This consideration is mainly because a decision to trust an entity inevitably involves risk taking (e.g., [44]). Operators will not trust the technology unless it is highly likely that the automation can reliably achieve their performance goals (i.e., high-quality outcomes); otherwise, relying on it will involve high risks that inhibit their trust in the automation.

The EAR framework covers evaluations of both the decision process and the outcome. In particular, perceived advice quality is related to the outcome, whereas perceived cognitive effort and perceived strategy restrictiveness are related to evaluations of the decision process.

We first examine the effects of the three factors of the EAR framework on users’ competence belief. Users form outcome expectations that are directed to the product advice provided by an RA. When the product advice matches their expectations, trust emerges; otherwise, trust might decline [28, 29]. In the presence of an agency relationship between an RA and its users, the acceptance of the product advice provided by the RA involves decisional risks, which generally arise from following unsatisfactory product advice [68]. This condition necessitates users’ evaluation of advice quality when judging the competence of an RA. Especially at an early stage of trust development, users have no previous experiences that they could rely on in judging the competence of an RA. Instead, users will make situational judgments. That is, to judge whether an RA possesses the necessary capabilities to act as their agent (i.e., competence belief), users will assess the quality of the product advice each time they receive it from the RA. Therefore, we posit:

Hypothesis 1: Advice quality will positively affect users’ competence belief in an RA.

Another benefit of using RAs for a decision task is that RAs can save the cognitive effort expended by users [36, 68], and this saving is essential to the process expectancy of users. As the “cognitive miser” model suggests, saving cognitive effort plays a more critical role in users’ decision-making process than the decision quality considerations when interacting with an RA [4]. Since websites presumably provide users with RAs to deal with information and choice overload in an online environment [36], users naturally expect an RA to reduce the effort they need to exert to find a suitable product [68]. When the use of an RA meets users’ expectation of a reduced cognitive effort for handling a decision task, their trust in the RA will increase.

The perceived cognitive effort involved in a decision task can also be a heuristic cue to judging the competence of an RA in serving as a user’s agent. When an RA can effectively reduce users’ perceived effort for a decision task, the users will consider the RA to be capable of carrying out decision-making activities for them; they will also believe that the RA designer possesses expertise in designing the RA. By contrast, users will lack confidence in the capability of the RA as their product agent (i.e., a heuristic cue) if the RA-aided decision task requires high cognitive effort. Therefore, we posit:

Hypothesis 2: Perceived cognitive effort will negatively affect users’ competence belief in an RA.

Perceptions of strategy restrictiveness will arise when users’ preferred decision processes are constrained by the strategy applied by an RA to process their attribute preferences [69]. The perceived strategy restrictiveness of an RA conveys direct cues about the competence of the RA, and its effect on trust is also related to the process expectancy of users. The central functionality of an RA is to employ a decision strategy to integrate users’ preferences and generate product advice for them. When users delegate certain decision tasks to an RA, they expect their preferred decision processes to be applied. Whether an RA is capable of processing users’ attribute preferences using users’ preferred decision strategy is a key consideration influencing the trust of users when they delegate decision tasks to an RA [69]. When an RA processes users’ requirements in the way they expect (i.e., an RA with low strategy restrictiveness by using users’ preferred decision strategy), users will believe the RA to have the ability to assist them in a decision task [67]. Otherwise, an RA with high perceived strategy restrictiveness will be viewed as incapable of serving as the users’ agent (i.e., low competence) [69]. Therefore, we posit:

Hypothesis 3: Perceived strategy restrictiveness will negatively affect users’ competence belief in an RA.

The three performance factors provide a solid basis on which users can judge whether a technology is capable of performing a decision task and achieving their goals, thereby affecting their competence beliefs in an RA. However, would these factors influence the benevolence and/or integrity beliefs as well?

Previous studies have suggested the existence of fundamental differences in individuals’ attributions of different trusting beliefs [30, 31, 63, 73]. People generally make dispositional attributions of morality-related beliefs, including integrity and benevolence beliefs. In particular, once perceptions of a trustee’s low integrity and benevolence are formed, people are likely to produce a dispositional attribution that is generally stable [63]. Positive performance cues are not effective in ensuring these two morality-related beliefs [30, 31, 55, 58]. With respect to integrity, for example, positive cues do not necessarily indicate high integrity because people with high integrity and those with low integrity may both behave honestly in certain situations (e.g., a dishonest person may behave honestly when subjected to high surveillance or when incentives for dishonest behaviors are lacking) [31]. In the context of RAs, low perceived cognitive effort does not necessarily indicate that an RA is truthful and considers all the users’ requirements when providing product advice (i.e., integrity and benevolence beliefs). This is because a deceptive RA (i.e., lack of integrity or benevolence beliefs) may also reduce users’ cognitive effort in a decision task by filtering many irrelevant product alternatives. For technologies designed nondeceptively and nonmalevolently, which are of interest in this study, users are not likely to encounter many negative cues that would indicate a trustee’s lack of benevolence or integrity. Therefore, we do not posit the effects of the three performance factors on users’ benevolence or integrity beliefs in an RA.

By contrast, with respect to competence, individuals can work to develop their skills and expertise; therefore, competence is not as stable as integrity and benevolence. A single piece of evidence of favorable performance can lead to a high competence belief because an incompetent entity could not demonstrate such highquality performance. Entities with high competence are “capable of exhibiting performance at many levels” depending on the task, whereas those with low competence “can only perform at levels that are commensurate with or lower than their level of competence” [31, p. 106]. This unstable attribution of competence provides a solid ground to hypothesize the effects of the three performance factors on competence as posited in H1 to H3.

## Effects of Perceived Transparency on Trusting Beliefs

Given the potential dispositional attribution of the integrity and benevolence beliefs, as well as the inability of the three performance cues in affecting such beliefs, we examine alternative designs that may drive users’ reattribution of low integrity and benevolence beliefs to nondispositional factors (i.e., not attributed to the technology itself), thereby improving these two beliefs. In particular, drawing on the knowledgebased trust perspective (i.e., the knowledge- based reason of trust) [13, 14, 40], we investigate the effects of the perceived transparency of an RA on users’ trusting beliefs. An RA is perceived to be transparent when users understand its inner workings for producing product advice, as well as its underlying motives and characteristics that drive its behaviors [40, 57, 69]. When users cannot use performance cues to effectively form their benevolence or integrity beliefs in an RA, they will shift their focus from performance cues to their understanding of the RA [38, 70]. According to a knowledge-based trust reason, trust generally develops when one gains knowledge and develops a better understanding of an RA [14, 19]. By interviewing users of various interactive systems, Hertzum et al. [23] found that the transparency of a system facilitates users’ conceptualization of that system, reduces their concerns about the system, and minimizes the incorrect attributions of low trusting beliefs. Therefore, in this study, we contend that the perceived transparency of an RA can complement the effects of the performance factors on trusting beliefs.

When users have low benevolence and integrity beliefs in an RA, they may not necessarily attribute these beliefs to the technology itself (i.e., a nondispositional attribution) if they can understand the inner workings of the technology. Given that this study focuses on nondeceptive, nonmalevolent RAs that are reasonably welldesigned and are intended to help users find suitable products, a transparent technology can rectify their original dispositional attribution of the low level of these two beliefs, thereby improving their trust in the technology.

The critical role of the perceived transparency of an RA in building trust can be further justified by the existence of an information asymmetry between an RA and its users. Such asymmetry presents a major obstacle to developing trust because of an agency relationship between the users and the RA [70, 74], which gives rise to the potential misattribution of low trusting beliefs in the RA. Unlike a tool that is owned by a user, an RA is offered by the website to facilitate users’ decision making; as a result, a “knowledge gap” exists between the RA and its users in that users normally lack information regarding the reasoning process of the RA [70, 72]. Therefore, users cannot verify the expertise and ability of the RA to provide sound recommendations, thereby possibly misattributing their low competence belief. By contrast, a transparent RA allows users to understand its inner workings (e.g., through the provision of explanations on how the RA generates product recommendations), thereby allowing users to build up their competence belief in the RA.

An “intention gap” between an RA and its users also hinders the trust-building process. An “intention gap” exists when users do not know why a website provides RA services. These users may be concerned that the RA is designed to “work” for the provider (e.g., the e-vendor or the manufacturer), and then question whether the RA puts their interests first when producing product recommendations (i.e., the “goal incongruence” concern). As such, users are likely to make a dispositional attribution of the perceived low benevolence of a nontransparent RA. By contrast, with a transparent RA, users can understand why the website provides them with the RA services. When the RA is indeed provided to help users make decisions, a transparent RA can mitigate users’ concerns about the goal incongruence. Consequently, users’ benevolence belief in the RA will increase.

Similarly, a transparent RA allows users to verify whether the product recommendations truly fit their requirements and are generated according to its inner workings [67]; thus, this verification process allows users to rectify any misattribution of their low integrity belief toward the RA and improve this belief. This process also reflects a distinctive aspect of human–computer interactions. Unlike in the context of interacting with a person, users can freely test, manipulate, and have control over an RA. Users can revise and update their requirements to an RA, and with a transparent RA, they can then check whether the product recommendations of the RA reliably reflect the changes in their requirements. Moreover, a transparent RA will be perceived as very forthcoming and having a low tendency to hide information [70]. As a result, the perceived transparency of an RA will enhance the users’ integrity belief in the RA.

In sum, we contend that perceived RA transparency will influence all three trusting beliefs toward an RA.

Hypothesis 4a: Perceived RA transparency will positively affect users’ competence belief in an RA.

Hypothesis 4b: Perceived RA transparency will positively affect users’ benevolence belief in an RA.

Hypothesis 4c: Perceived RA transparency will positively affect users’ integrity belief in an RA.

## Effects of Perceived Transparency on Performance Evaluations

When users can understand the inner workings of an RA, we contend that their perceived transparency of the RA can also positively influence users’ performance evaluations of the RA [17], which in turn are posited to affect competence (i.e., H1– H3).<sup>1</sup> Perceived RA transparence can also directly influence competence as posited in H4a. Therefore, the effects of perceived transparency on the competence belief will be partially mediated by users’ performance evaluations of an RA. Figure 1 presents the overall research model.

First, perceived RA transparency allows users to judge the advice quality of an RA. Users may not exclusively focus on the quality of the products recommended by an RA (e.g., the product attribute levels) when assessing the advice quality of the RA. Users may also need to understand how (the process by which) the RA recommends the products and to what extent the recommended products fit their needs and preferences [70]. Users of a transparent RA have a clear mental model of the RA and will be able to make these assessments [17]. As a result, a transparent RA can better convince the users about the quality of its advice.

Hypothesis 5a: Perceived RA transparency will positively affect users’ evaluations of advice quality.

Second, perceived RA transparency can reduce users’ effort in a decisional task aided by an RA. Users normally use an RA iteratively during a decision task to adjust their preferences through user–system interactions until they receive satisfactory product advice [68]. When users do not clearly understand the inner workings of an RA, they may have to use the RA iteratively to infer how the RA works and can be used effectively, thereby expending extra effort in the decision process. By contrast, with a transparent RA, users can save effort by avoiding excessive interactions with the RA. Although users need to expend effort in understanding the inner workings of a transparent RA, we expect that this effort is relatively smaller as compared to the effort saved through reduced iterative interactions with the RA. Therefore, we posit the following:

Hypothesis 5b: Perceived RA transparency will negatively affect perceived cognitive effort.

Third, perceived RA transparency may also reduce perceived strategy restrictiveness of an RA. According to the adaptive nature of users’ preferences and decision making [51], when a user knows that the decision strategy used by a transparent RA to generate recommendations differs from his/her own decision strategy, the user may try to cope with and circumvent the RA’s strategy. For example, if a user prefers to use an elimination strategy to process a given attribute preference when the RA adopts an AC strategy, the user can simply indicate the strongest level (or weight) of preference for that attribute to the RA. Conversely, if a user prefers to use an AC strategy to process his/her preferences when the RA adopts an elimination strategy, the user can begin by indicating the most important attribute preferences to the RA. The user may gradually indicate more attribute preferences to further narrow down the recommendations from the RA. In doing so, the user can deal with the strategy deviation of a transparent RA, though only to some extent, thereby lowing the perceived strategy restrictiveness of the RA. Therefore, we posit:

Hypothesis 5c: Perceived RA transparency will negatively affect perceived strategy restrictiveness.

## Control Variables

Apart from experiential factors, nonexperiential bases of trust have been widely studied in the literature. We controlled for these factors, including users’ propensity to trust, general attitudes toward computers, general web use experience, comfort with the Internet, and gender.

1. Propensity to trust is a personality trait that reflects an individual’s general predisposition to trust other parties [44]. This trait influences users’ trusting beliefs toward the technology as an individual difference factor [67].

2. General attitudes toward computers. Previous research has consistently identified general attitudes toward computers as a significant determinant of users’ overall evaluation of a computer-based system [61]. Given that RAs are computer-based systems, users’ attitudes toward computers may influence their trust assessments.

3. General web use experience. The previous web experience of users may generate knowledge about web-based applications that reinforces the users trusting beliefs toward an RA [25]. Therefore, this factor was also controlled for in this study.

4. Comfort with the Internet. Given that RAs are Internet-based applications, users’ comfort with the Internet affects their beliefs and evaluations of these applications [25, 33]. Thus, users’ comfort with the Internet was controlled for.

5. Gender was controlled for in this study because previous studies have revealed gender differences in users’ trust in online vendors [56].

Institution-based trust has been widely conceptualized to include structural assurance and situational normality (e.g., [9, 46]); however, we did not control for this factor for two reasons. First, actual purchases were not considered in our experimental context (refer to the research methodology section for details), which mainly involved decisional risks. Thus, the structural assurances for business transactions were not very relevant. Second, given that RAs are relatively new and emerging technologies, users might face difficulties in judging the situational normality of RAs. As such, institution-based trust was not controlled for in this study.

## Research Methodology

## Study Settings

The data were collected in an experimental setting. The participants performed online shopping tasks for digital cameras while being assisted by a fully functional RA designed specifically for this study. Digital cameras were deemed suitable for a multi-attribute, multi-alternative choice task, as they bear a variety of attributes and there are hundreds of different models. Many of the RAs that are currently available are for the marketing and sale of digital cameras.

Two experimental tasks were performed to ensure that the participants had sufficient interactions to evaluate the experimental RA [69]. One task was to choose a digital camera as a wedding gift for a close friend who enjoys photography very much, with the cost of the gift to be split with four other friends. The other task was to select a digital camera for a close family member who is an amateur photographer. These two tasks covered different types of digital cameras (i.e., one for an experienced photographer and the other for an amateur photographer), and we expected the participants to indicate different types of attribute preferences to the RA and receive different camera recommendations for each task. As such, these different tasks enriched the participants’ interactions with the RA. These tasks were also designed to enhance experimental realism. The participants could easily identify with a specific experimental scenario in which they chose a camera for others, and not for themselves. If the participants were to buy cameras for themselves, some of them might not show interest, either because they already had one or for other reasons.

The participants were informed that the tasks were flexible and that they could make as many assumptions as they wished. The participants evaluated the RA after finishing both tasks. To ensure that the participants would take the experiment seriously, they were informed that they could freely choose any digital camera but would later be asked to justify their choices.

## Experimental RA

In line with previous studies on RAs for e-commerce, the experimental RA in this research was a content-filtering-based product-brokering system that advises users on which digital cameras they should buy according to their specified needs and preferences. Current commercial applications of content-filtering-based RAs employ various choice strategies, among which the AC and elimination strategies are the two most suitable and widely used strategies [69]. We developed two types of RAs that employed the AC and elimination strategies, respectively, and expected that the participants’ perceived strategy restrictiveness would significantly differ across these two RAs. To elicit users’ preferences, a user–RA dialogue was implemented for the simulation of the dialogues presented in other studies and in commercial applications [33]. Appendix A presents screenshots of the two RAs.

Apart from the decision strategy difference, we manipulated the transparency levels of RAs by providing explanation facilities. Explanations are important components of decision support technologies, including RAs, because these explanations make the technologies transparent to their users [17, 23, 39, 57, 70].

Wang and Benbasat [70] have investigated the explanation facilities for an RA that can enhance its transparency. These explanations were provided by the RA to clarify the line of reasoning used by the RA based on the users’ preferences (i.e., “how” explanations), justify the purposes of the questions posed by the RA to the users (i.e., “why” explanations), and help users choose among and use different RA features (i.e., “guidance”). The effect mechanisms of these explanation facilities have been examined in previous studies, such as the works of Wang and Benbasat [70] and Arnold et al. [1]. We manipulated the RA transparency levels by either providing or not providing a set of explanation facilities.<sup>4</sup> Appendix B provides the definitions and examples of these explanations.

## Participants and Experimental Procedures

A total of 156 undergraduate and graduate students were recruited from a large North American university as participants. Among these participants, 52 were males and 104 were females. The participants had a mean age of 21.3 years, which matches the Internet user demographic data [27]. Approximately half of the participants surfed the Internet for more than 2 hours per day; 47 percent of them between 30 minutes and 2 hours per day, and 2 percent of them for less than 30 minutes per day. Most of them (96 percent) have used the Internet for more than three years.

The participants were randomly assigned to websites with different experimental RAs. The experimental procedures were as follows. Upon arrival, the participants completed a background questionnaire that collected their demographic information. A research assistant trained the participants on how to use and navigate the assigned web interface by playing recorded videos of a tutorial RA that had the same features as the experimental RA. Each participant was then asked to finish the two tasks. The order of the two tasks was counterbalanced. After each task, the participants were directed to an online form on which they were to write and justify their choices. The tasks had no time limit. The participants were required to use the experimental RA first, but they were eventually allowed to use a simple search query (search by brand name) available on the homepage of the experimental website if they were not satisfied with the RA. Finally, the participants were asked to complete an online questionnaire that included the measures of the variables investigated in this study.

The participation was voluntary, and all the participants were guaranteed monetary compensation (\$15) for their work. To further motivate these participants to view the experiment as a serious online shopping session and to enhance their involvement, they were informed before the experimental tasks that 25 percent of them would receive an additional award of \$5 to \$100 based on their performance. They were informed that their performance would be judged based on the persuasiveness of their justifications for their shopping choices.

## Measures

Whenever available, scales to measure the variables in the research model were adapted from existing measurement scales. In particular, trust in RAs (competence, benevolence, and integrity), perceived strategy restrictiveness, and perceived cognitive effort have well-established multi-item measures in the literature. The measures for trust in RAs were adapted from McKnight et al. [46] and Wang and Benbasat [67], and those for perceived cognitive effort and perceived strategy restrictiveness were acquired from Wang and Benbasat [69].

A direct measure of perceived advice quality is whether or not the participants follow the top recommendations provided by an RA [69].<sup>5</sup> After the user–RA dialogues, the participants received the top five recommendations, and they could navigate to the next page to obtain the next five recommendations, if available. We coded the perceived advice quality into three levels. If the participants chose one of the top five recommendations, the perceived advice quality was coded as “2.” If they chose recommendations that were not among the top five, it was coded as “1.” Otherwise, if the participants selected products on their own using the search query, we concluded that they perceived the RA recommendations as unsatisfactory; therefore, we coded the perceived advice quality as “0.”

Based on the work of Wang [66], we developed a new instrument for perceived RA transparency by following the guidelines provided in Moore and Benbasat [48]. Previous empirical and theoretical literature marked the starting point for scale development. However, we could not find well-developed multi-item measures; thus, we generated candidate items with the help of four participants (two graduate students and two assistant professors in management information systems [MIS]). To check the content validity, we submitted the instrument to a panel of graduate students majoring in MIS to obtain their views on the appropriateness of the candidate items. After following these rigorous development procedures, we generated seven items for measuring perceived system transparency. These measures were further validated in the main experiment. Appendix C shows the final measurement items of all the dependent variables.

The control variables were measured in the background questionnaire prior to the experimental tasks. These variables were measured using the existing measures in the literature (see Appendix C).

## Data Analysis and Results

We conducted manipulation checks to ensure that the experimental RAs created a certain level of variance in the independent variables. A multiple analysis of variance (MANOVA) was first conducted to check whether the participants perceived the various RAs differently in terms of the three performance factors and perceived RA transparency [18]. The significant main effects of the explanations $( F = 3 . 2 9 , p <$ 0.01) and decision strategies $( F = 7 . 9 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ were found. We then checked the success of the two manipulations specifically. Explanations significantly increased the perceived transparency of the RA $( F = 5 . 0 3 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . The perceived strategy restrictiveness of the AC-based RA was significantly lower than that of the elimination-based RA $( F = 1 4 . 3 9 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . These results indicate the success of our manipulations in generating variances in the antecedents of trust investigated in this study. Table 1 shows the descriptive statistics of the variables, including their means and standard deviations.

As a component-based estimation approach, PLS can assess the psychometric properties of all the measures and subsequently to examine the structural relationships among the constructs in our research model.

After performing extensive pretests in the instrument development process, we assessed the reliability (individual item reliability and internal consistency) and validity of the constructs by examining the item loadings, the composite reliability of the constructs, and the average variances extracted (AVEs). All reflective constructs displayed highly positive loadings (all significant at the 0.001 level), indicating a high individual item reliability. ## Test for Common-Method Variance

We assessed the common-method variance (CMV) because the measurements of all constructs in this study were collected simultaneously using the same instrument, except for advice quality, which was measured objectively by observing the participants’ choices. Two analyses were conducted to assess CMV. First, we applied Harman’s one-factor test on the experimental data. We performed an exploratory factor analysis on all the variables and found that no single factor accounted for the majority of the covariance in the variables.

Second, we adopted a marker variable technique, which has been proved to be a powerful and rigorous technique for identifying common-method bias [43]. Particularly, following the recommendations of Lindell and Whitney [41] and Malhotra et al. [43], we used a post hoc approach to estimate the CMV. A variable that is theoretically unrelated to one or more constructs of interest to this study should be used as a marker variable. CMV can be assessed based on the correlations $( r _ { M } )$ between this marker variable and the other constructs. The correlations between all constructs of this study were calculated, and the second-smallest correlation (the absolute value of the correlation between the product knowledge of digital cameras and the perceived cognitive effort, $r _ { M } = 0 . 0 3 8 )$ was selected as the estimate for the method variance to avoid capitalizing on chance factors (i.e., product knowledge of digital cameras was chosen as a marker variable) [43]. The corrected correlations among the constructs that partialed out the method variance were calculated using the formula of Malhotra et al. [43]. Table 3 presents the results.

The corrected correlations in Table 3 were still significant, and very small differences were observed between the uncorrected and corrected correlations $\left( \Delta r { < } 0 . 0 3 \right)$ . We also conducted a sensitivity analysis by assuming $r _ { M } = 0 . 1 0 , 0 . 1 5 , 0 . 2 0 ,$ and 0.30. The corrected correlation between perceived strategy restrictiveness and competence became insignificant only when $r _ { M } = 0 . 3 0 $ ; the corrected correlations remained significant in all other scenarios. Therefore, the results of both Harman’s one-factor test and the marker variable technique showed that common-method bias was not a serious concern in this study.

## Discussion

## Findings

This research examines the different experiential bases across the three trusting beliefs (i.e., competence, integrity, and benevolence) in the context of RAs. We particularly investigated the different effects of two sets of experiential factors (i.e., three performance factors related to the interactive reason of trust, and perceived transparency related to the knowledge-based reason). The three performance factors influenced only users’ competence belief in an RA, but not their benevolence belief or integrity belief; by contrast, perceived RA transparency influenced all three trusting beliefs. In addition, the effect of perceived transparency on competence was partially mediated by two performance factors, namely, advice quality and perceived cognitive effort.

The only unexpected result is the insignificant effect of perceived RA transparency on perceived strategy restrictiveness. This result indicates that although a user’s attribute preferences may be generally constructive and adaptive [51], circumventing or adjusting the strategy applied by the RA to implement another strategy that mimics the user’s preferred decision strategy for processing his/her preferences may not be easily feasible. Therefore, when a user is aware that the decision strategy used by an RA to generate recommendations differs from his/her preferred strategy, he/she may not be able to easily cope with the RA’s strategy. For multi-attribute, multi-alternative choice problems, consumers often assign different importance levels to various attribute preferences, and they need to make trade-offs among them [6, 59]. However, when a user wishes to use an AC strategy, an eliminationbased RA that ignores the relative importance levels of users’ preferences cannot be easily manipulated to implement the AC strategy. As such, even if a user is aware that his/her desired strategy is not being applied by an RA, such knowledge cannot help him/her deal with the strategy restrictiveness of the RA. Consequently, perceived RA transparency may not affect users’ perceived strategy restrictiveness of the RA.

## Contributions

This study offers significant contributions to both research and practice. As its primary contribution to research, this study provides a fine-grained understanding of trust formation at the trusting belief level. The multidimensional nature of trust has been widely acknowledged in the literature (e.g., [44, 46]), and the discriminant validity of the three trusting beliefs has been empirically supported (e.g., [46, 71]). However, the different formation mechanisms of these three trusting beliefs have not yet been identified in the literature. To firmly establish the differences among these trusting beliefs, we must ensure that, although they are correlated, they have distinguishable antecedents and consequences [8].

Based on the different dispositional attributions of competence versus moralityrelated beliefs (i.e., benevolence and integrity), this study theorized the different effects of two experiential reasons (i.e., performance factors and perceived transparency) on the three trusting beliefs. Given that people generally make stable, dispositional attributions of integrity and benevolence beliefs, the performance factors cannot easily affect these two beliefs. By contrast, competence is not as stable as integrity and benevolence, and this unstable attribution allows performance factors to affect competence belief. To our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the different antecedents across the three trusting beliefs. Although Wang and Benbasat [67] identify a set of reasons for users’ trusting beliefs in an RA, they do not focus on the differences among these beliefs.

Our empirical findings confirm that the interactive reason (i.e., performance factors) significantly influences users’ competence belief only but not their integrity or benevolence beliefs. By contrast, the knowledge-based reason (i.e., perceived transparency) can effectively influence all three trusting beliefs. The perceived transparency of an RA can possibly rectify any misattribution of the low integrity and benevolence of the RA, thereby positively affecting these two beliefs. This theoretical and empirical contribution significantly advances the extant research on trust by focusing on the differences among the three trusting beliefs. To make further progress, future research needs to explore the different consequences of the three trusting beliefs.

This study also offers two additional theoretical implications. First, the experiential bases identified in this study have a strong explanatory power for users’ trusting beliefs toward RAs. Figure 2 shows that the sets of experiential factors explained a satisfactory level of variances in users’ trusting beliefs toward RAs (66 percent, 44 percent, and 40 percent of variances of competence, benevolence, and integrity, respectively). Other nonexperiential factors did not have strong effects on trust in RAs, thereby confirming that experiential bases are the primary sources of trust in RAs (especially during the early development stages of trust, which is the focus of this study). Unlike interpersonal and organizational contexts, RAs, as technological artifacts, can be “freely” manipulated by users and involve fewer social constraints than interpersonal interactions. This feature provides users with a rich interaction experience with an RA with which to evaluate its trustworthiness [32]. Moreover, the strategy restrictiveness of RAs cannot be applied to an interpersonal context. Thus, by revealing the experiential bases of trust in RAs, this study advances the theory of trust in technology, which is most relevant to IS researchers.

Second, the results reveal the absolute and relative values of the different experiential bases of trust. These results highlight the importance of perceived transparency in building technological trust. Perceived RA transparency directly affects all three trusting beliefs, whereas the three performance factors only affect competence belief. Perceived transparency also indirectly influences competence by affecting advice quality and perceived cognitive effort. Therefore, perceived transparency exerts significant complementary effects on trust in an RA in addition to those of the three performance factors.

This study also offers significant implications for practitioners. Understanding the experiential bases of trust can inform designers on how individuals form trust beliefs on the basis of their interactions with an RA and can provide guidelines for designing trustworthy RAs. In particular, perceived RA transparency and perceived strategy restrictiveness have not attracted much attention from designers. In terms of the strategy restrictiveness of RAs, we compared the AC-based RA with the elimination-based RA and found that the former was less restrictive than the latter, as reported in our manipulation check results. However, our review of the extant RAs on commercial websites shows that many of these RAs are elimination-based [69]. Therefore, firms should consider providing AC-based RAs instead of eliminationbased RAs to enhance the trustworthiness of such technology.

Regarding RA transparency, previous studies have shown that the provision of explanation facilities can enhance RA transparency (e.g., [17]), which was manipulated in this research. However, by examining the existing RA applications, we found that many of these applications do not provide explanation facilities, and this finding presents an area for improving current RAs.

According to our findings on the relative effects of various experiential bases on trust, designers can emphasize certain design elements over others if they want to work on a specific trusting belief in a particular context. Designers must consider the design effort of such factor(s) in the focal context and the value that they can achieve in terms of trust enhancement, given that focusing on different experiential bases to enhance users’ trust may require different levels of design effort. In addition, not all trusting beliefs are equally important for a specific technology. For example, competence belief is more relevant than benevolence and integrity beliefs for a national identity system. Based on our findings, designers can focus on specific factors that lead to a particular desired trusting belief.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations. First, readers must exercise caution in generalizing the results to other demographic groups because the experiment participants were university students. A highly diverse sample may yield findings that can be generalized to various situations in which RAs are used. Further research is therefore suggested to address this limitation. Nevertheless, the experiment was conducted in the online shopping context, and our sample generally matched the Internet user demographic data [27].

Second, this research was conducted in the context of RAs for e-commerce, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings to other contexts. We focused on the RA context to identify a specific set of trust-building factors, given the context-specificity of the technological attributes of IT artifacts [49]. Although perceived transparency may be applied to other technological contexts, specific performance factors may differ across various contexts (e.g., perceived strategy restrictiveness may not apply in some contexts). These performance factors are related to the purposes of using a specific technology, and the identification of these factors is context specific. Future studies must identify the different performance factors that can be applied to different types of technology.

Moreover, the type of decision support technology investigated in this study was non deceptive, nonmalevolent, and generally reasonably designed. The perceived transparency of deceptive, malevolent, or poorly designed technology may not lead to users’ trust in such technology or their positive advice quality assessments. Users may also mistrust a deceptive, malevolent, or poorly designed technology. These issues of trust and mistrust in these technologies are beyond the scope of this study, but the issues may be investigated in future research [77].

## Conclusions

The success of a decision support technology highly depends on users’ trust. However, trust is a complex construct with multiple dimensions. This study investigated the different effects of two sets of experimental factors on three trusting beliefs (i.e., competence, integrity, and benevolence). These experiential factors included users’ assessments of the extent of decisional support (i.e., strategy restrictiveness), the cognitive effort of the decision task aided by an RA, the advice quality, and the transparency of the RA. Our theorizing of their different effects on trusting beliefs advances the extant literature on technological trust and helps practitioners identify and select concrete designs that can augment users’ trust. These designs can consequently enhance the effectiveness and acceptance of web-based decision support technologies. With enhanced trust in technologies, organizations can attract and retain more consumers by building trustworthy relationships.

## Appendix C: Measurement Items

All the multi-items for dependent variables used seven-point Likert scales, with the endpoints labeled as “Extremely disagree” and “Extremely agree.”

● Trust in RA–Competence Belief (CMP) [46, 70]

CMP1: The virtual adviser is competent and effective in providing digital camera recommendations.

CMP2: The virtual adviser performs its role of giving recommendations very well.

CMP3: Overall, the virtual adviser is a capable and proficient Internet digital camera recommendation provider.

CMP4: In general, the virtual adviser is very knowledgeable about digital cameras.

● Trust in RA–Benevolence Belief (BNV) [46, 70]

BNV1: I believe that the virtual adviser would act in my best interest.

BNV2: If I required help, the virtual adviser would do its best to help me.

BNV3: The virtual adviser is interested in my well-being, not just its own.

● Trust in RA–Integrity (INT) [46, 70]

INT1: The virtual adviser is truthful in its dealings with me.

INT2: I would characterize the virtual adviser as honest.

INT3: The virtual adviser would keep its commitments.

INT4: The virtual adviser is sincere and genuine.

● Perceived Strategy Restrictiveness (PSR) [69]

PSR1: The virtual adviser constrained my alternatives for possible approaches it can use to generate its recommendations.

PSR2: In the context of my preferred way of selecting a digital camera, the approach this virtual adviser uses to generate recommendations was rigid.

PSR3: In the context of my preferred way of selecting a digital camera, this virtual adviser’s reasoning processes for generating recommendations were restricted.

● Perceived Cognitive Effort (PCE) [53, 69]

PCE1: The task of selecting digital cameras using this virtual adviser took too much time.

PCE2: The task of selecting digital cameras using this virtual adviser was easy.

PCE3: Selecting digital cameras using this virtual adviser required too much effort.

PCE4: The task of selecting digital cameras using this virtual adviser was too complex.

PRT4: I could easily understand this virtual adviser’s reasoning process.

PRT5: It was easy for me to understand the inner workings of this virtual adviser.
