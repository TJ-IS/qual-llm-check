# The business value of information technology and inputs substitution: The productivity paradox revisited

Winston T. Lin <sup>a,\*</sup>, Benjamin B.M. Shao <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> State University of New York at Buffalo, School of Management, 325A Jacobs Management Center, Buffalo, NY 14260-4000, United States <sup>b</sup> Arizona State University, Main Campus, W.P. Carey School of Business, Department of Information Systems, P.O. Box 85287-4606, Tempe, AZ 85287-4606, United States

Received 25 August 2004; received in revised form 8 July 2005; accepted 29 October 2005 Available online 6 January 2006

## Abstract

The business value of information technology (IT) is an extremely important but highly controversial issue that has sparked a great deal of research during the past two decades. Closely related to the issue are the productivity paradox of information systems and the substitutability of IT stock for both traditional capital and labor. Numerous studies have been undertaken to either explain or dispel the paradox. This paper represents one significant extension to previous work and is a further effort to jointly investigate the business value issue, the paradox, and the potential of the substitution between IT capital and ordinary capital and labor, by estimating the IT business value in terms of the impact of IT on technical efficiency, based on the constant elasticity of substitution (known as CES) stochastic production frontier model, at three levels: firm, industry, and sector. The major findings include: the relationship between technical efficiency and IT investment is not robust with respect to the specifications of production frontiers; the productivity paradox is still existent, inconsistent with conventional wisdom, IT has substantial impacts on the five parameters associated with the CES production process; IT stock, traditional capital, and traditional labor are not pairwise substitutable; IT stock appears to be as important as capital, but it is not possible to use IT stock to replace the role of labor entirely; decreasing returns to scale are found irrespective of the levels of IT investments, and technical efficiency tends to decrease as IT investments increase; the industry-level analysis suggests that IT capital is more important for the services industries than for the manufacturing industries; and the sector analysis seems to indicate that the services sector is just slightly less technically efficient than the manufacturing sector. <sup>D</sup> 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information technology; IT investments; Productivity paradox; Technical efficiency; CES production function; Substitutability; Complementarity

## 1. Introduction

During the past two decades, information technology (IT) has become a major driver of economic change and restructured the landscape of a business organization. IT spendings have increased faster than expected in recent years. For example, the IT spendings in 2001 for the United States and Japan were \$546,681 and \$188,012 (in millions of US dollars, source: Digital Planet 2002: The Global Information Economy, p. 29), respectively. Therefore, IT has played an increasingly important role in a business organization and the business value of IT investments has become a crucial but controversial issue. Abundant research has been devoted to resolving this issue using different performance measures. Notable examples include the studies of Cron and Sobol [15], Bender [7], Dos Santos et al. [18], Strassman [37], and Hitt and Brynjolfsson [21], using the measures of productivity, consumer surplus, and profitability; the work by Weill [39], Dewan and Min [17], Dewan and Kraemer [16], and others, by means of productivity; the research by Bresnahan [10] and Hitt and Brynjolfsson [21] relying on consumer surplus, by Mukhopadhyay et al. [28] using quality, and by Banker et al. [6] utilizing operational efficiency; and the paper by Bharadwaj et al. [9] based on Tobin’s q.

In essence, the empirical results of the studies addressing the business value of IT are inconclusive and conflicting (Mukhopadhyay et al. [28]). In particular, closely related to the issue of IT business value is the phenomenon known as the productivity paradox of information systems (IS) (Bailey and Gordon [4] and Roach [32]) that was found to exist in the 1980s but was shown to have disappeared in the early 1990s (Brynjolfsson [11], Brynjolfsson and Hitt [12], and Hitt and Brynjolfsson [21]). Based on a set of country-level data, Dewan and Kraemer [16] have concluded that the productivity paradox is absent from the developed countries but does exist in the developing countries in their sample.

Recently, Lin and Shao [25] have adopted a different approach to re-examine the issue of IT business value, using a performance measure called technical (or productive) efficiency (also see Shao and Lin [34–36]). Technical efficiency is a useful economic performance measure (Lin and Shao [25]) different from profitability, productivity, consumer surplus, quality, operational efficiency, and Tobin’s q. Under a variety of specifications of the stochastic production frontier, including the generalized Cobb–Douglas function (Lin and Shao [25] and Shao and Lin [34,35]), the Box–Cox and Box–Tidwell transformations (Lin and Shao [25]), the translog frontier (Shao and Lin [34,35]), and data envelopment analysis and the Tobit regression (Shao and Lin [36]), and employing the same data set as used in a number of studies such as Hitt and Brynjolfsson [21] and Dewan and Min [17], Lin and Shao [25] have concluded that the disappearance of the productivity paradox is supported by the efficiency enhancement of IT investments.

The positive effect of IT investments on technical efficiency is a good news in favor of the argument of the productivity paradox disappearance. However, we question if such a pleasing conclusion is robust with respect to the specification of stochastic production frontiers. A major finding of Lin and Shao [25] is that there is a robust and consistent relationship between IT investment and efficiency, irrespective of the production frontier model employed. Thus, the debates go on. We argue that the question of whether the productivity paradox disappears or exists depends on the specifications of the stochastic production frontier.

Another important issue that is also closely related to the business value of IT is the substitutability of IT for both traditional capital and labor. Although the IS literature seems to abound in research dedicated to either explain or dispel the productivity paradox of IS, it is the scarcity of research that makes the substitutability of IT for ordinary capital and labor a subject for serious inquiry along with the business value of IT. The only exception is the study of Dewan and Min [17] which estimates IT substitutability by relating it to the marginal products of inputs, using the partial elasticity of substitution of Allen and Hicks [2]. Since the ability to secure the benefits of IT is partially determined by the potential of substituting IT for capital and labor, the inputs substitution issue is equally important and deserves our efforts.

This paper offers a joint study on the relationship between IT and technical efficiency, the existence of the productivity paradox, and the possibility of the substitution of IT capital for both traditional capital and labor, within the framework of the constant elasticity of substitution (known in the econometrics literature as CES) stochastic production frontier model. More specifically, the primary objective of this research is three-fold: one, to assess how IT investments affect technical efficiency; two, to revisit the productivity paradox; and three, to explore the importance of IT in comparison with, and the substitutability (or complementarity) between IT and, the traditional production factors (capital and labor). A secondary objective is to analyze how IT impacts the five important parameters associated with the CES production process, namely, the efficiency parameter, the distribution parameters, the returns-to-scale parameter, and the substitution parameter. Especially, the question of the substitution between IT and capital and between IT and labor is dealt with based on the substitution parameter. Since the CES stochastic production frontier models have never been applied in the IS literature as a methodological basis, the empirical results are both interesting and challenging.

It should be noted that, methodologically, there is a choice between a parametric approach (e.g., the translog cost function used in Menton and Lee [27] and the stochastic production frontier approach as discussed in Jondrow et al. [22] and used in Lin and Shao [25] for the first time in the IS literature, Shao and Lin [34,35], and this study) and a non-parametric approach (e.g., data development analysis known as DEA used, e.g., in Pendharkar and Rodger [30], Serrano-Cinca et al. [33], and Shao and Lin [36]). The latter technique is deterministic and probability distribution-free, while the former approach is stochastic and, hence, is not probability distribution-free, and requires the specification of production functional forms. Lin and Shao [25] have provided some compelling reasons for the application of the parametric stochastic production frontier approach and the economic measure of technical efficiency inherent with it. This approach is related to at least two important areas of economic theory, namely, production economics and organizational performance, as reviewed in Bakos and Kemerer [5].

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. The theoretical foundation of the CES stochastic frontier models is introduced in Section 2. The empirical results of the whole sample and subsamples as well as the impact of firm size at the firm level, and those at the sector and industry levels, are presented and discussed in Section 3. Finally, some remarks and conclusions are summarized in Section 4.

## 2. Theoretical foundation: the CES stochastic frontier

This research builds on the CES production function and, hence, a brief description of this function is necessary and helpful. To begin with, let $K _ { i t }$ (capital, i.e., non-IT capital) and $L _ { i t }$ (labor) be used as production factors in firm i at time t. Then, the constant elasticity of substitution (CES) production function can be described by (cf. Arrow et al. [3], Chiang [14], and Kmenta [23])

$$
\begin{array}{l} Y _ {i t} = \gamma [ \delta K _ {i t} ^ {- \rho} + (1 - \delta) L _ {i t} ^ {- \rho} ] ^ {- \alpha / \rho} e ^ {V _ {i t}}, \quad \gamma > 0, \quad 1 > \delta > 0, \\ \alpha > 0, \quad \rho \geq - 1 \qquad i = 1, \dots , n, \quad \text { and } t = 1, \dots , N, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $Y _ { i t }$ is the actual output produced by firm i at time $t , \gamma$ is called the efficiency parameter, $\dot { \delta }$ and $1 - \delta$ are the distribution parameters for capital and labor, respectively, a is the returns-to-scale parameter, and $\rho$ is the substitution parameter. Most studies based on CES, e.g., Berndt [8] and Dewan and Min [17], assume a constant returns-to-scale by setting a equal to 1. This study does not make such a restrictive assumption. It could be any value as long as it is positive (integer or fractional).

The CES production function has gained its popularity because it includes other more specialized production functions, such as the standard Cobb–Douglas (as $\rho = 0 )$ and the fixed proportions (as $\rho \longrightarrow _ { \infty } )$ , as special cases. It is an intrinsically nonlinear model. However, by taking logarithms of both sides of the CES function, it can be estimated by the maximum likelihood method, an alternative simple method of estimating the five parameters is available. This is achieved by using the Taylor’s series expansion (Kmenta [23]). Doing this algebraic expansion operation leads to a stochastic approximation given by

$$
\begin{array}{c} \ln Y _ {i t} = \ln \gamma + \alpha \delta \ln K _ {i t} + \alpha (1 - \delta) \ln L _ {i t} \\ - 1 / 2 \rho \alpha \delta (1 - \delta) (\ln K _ {i t} - \ln L _ {i t}) ^ {2} + v _ {i t}, \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

where $\nu _ { i t }$ in Eq. (2), as in Eq. (1), is the traditional random error distributed according to $N ( 0 , \sigma _ { \mathrm { v } } ^ { 2 } )$ . The right-hand side of Eq. (2) is composed of two main parts, one corresponding to the Cobb–Douglas production function and the other representing a correction (or an adjustment) due to the departure of $\rho$ from zero. Thus, if $\rho = 0$ , the correction factor would disappear and the CES function collapses to the standard Cobb–Douglas function.

The estimation of the parameters of the restricted Eq. (2) is the same as the estimation of the following unrestricted version with nonlinear restrictions under exact identification:

$$
\ln Y _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \ln K _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \ln L _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} (\ln K _ {i t} - \ln L _ {i t}) ^ {2} + v _ {i t}.\tag{3}
$$

Consequently, the unknown parameters of the restricted Eq. (2) are related to the coefficients of the unrestricted version (3) as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \gamma = \text { anti } \ln \beta_ {0} = \exp (\beta_ {0}), \quad \delta = \beta_ {1} / (\beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2}), \\ 1 - \delta = \beta_ {2} / (\beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2}), \quad \alpha = \beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2}, \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

and

$$
\rho = \left[ - 2 \beta_ {3} (\beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2}) \right] / \beta_ {1} \beta_ {2}\tag{5}
$$

Eq. (5) is the criterion on which the estimates of the substitutability (or complementarity) between two inputs are based.

Furthermore, using the unrestricted version of the CES approximation, we are able to consider three stochastic production frontier models of special relevance to this research. If only capital $( K _ { i t } )$ and labor $( L _ { i t } )$ are employed in the CES production process, then we have the first stochastic production frontier model (called Model l):

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {   Model   1   :   } & \ln Y _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \ln K _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \ln L _ {i t} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {3} (\ln K _ {i t} - \ln L _ {i t}) ^ {2} + v _ {i t} - u _ {i t}, \end{array}
$$

where $\nu _ { i t }$ is as defined above, and $u _ { i t } \left( \geq 0 \right)$ represents the technical inefficiency and is a one-sided normally distributed random error, that is, $u _ { i t }$ is distributed according to $| N ( 0 , \sigma _ { u } ^ { 2 } ) |$ (see Lovell [26], Lin and Shao [25], and Shao and Lin [35,36] for more details).

It follows that replacing $L _ { i t }$ in Model l by IT capital of firm i at time $t \ ( I _ { i t } )$ yields the second stochastic production frontier model (to be referred to as Model 2):

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {   Model   2:   } \ln Y _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \ln K _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \ln I _ {i t} \\ \qquad \qquad \qquad + \beta_ {3} (\ln K _ {i t} - \ln I _ {i t}) ^ {2} + v _ {i t} - u _ {i t}. \end{array}
$$

Finally, if $K _ { i t }$ in Model 1 is substituted by $I _ { i t } ,$ , we have the third stochastic production frontier model (called Model 3):

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {   Model   3:   } & \ln Y _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \ln L _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \ln I _ {i t} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {3} (\ln L _ {i t} - \ln I _ {i t}) ^ {2} + v _ {i t} - u _ {i t}. \end{array}
$$

The impact of IT on technical (productive) efficiencies and the paradox of productivity have been successfully investigated. The efforts of prior work are reflected by the studies of Lin and Shao [25], Lin and Chen [24], and Shao and Lin [34–36]. However, two points deserve our special attention. First of all, since previous work has relied on the generalized Cobb– Douglas function, the Box–Cox and Box–Tidwell transformations, and the translog function, the application of the CES stochastic frontiers models (Models l– 3) helps to determine whether the conclusions reached in previous research are robust with respect to the choice of stochastic production frontiers.

In the second place, the CES stochastic frontier models provide an appropriate method of analysis, via formula (5) embodied in the CES models, to resolve the issue of inputs substitution which is relative to both the technical efficiency and the productivity paradox but has been neglected by previous studies using technical efficiency as a performance measure of the IT value. Formula (5) measures the possibilities of the substitution (or complement) between capital and labor (employing Model l), between capital and IT capital (based on Model 2), and between labor and IT capital (using Model 3).

Unlike Berndt [8], Dewan and Min [17], and others who have used the Allen partial elasticity of substitution between two inputs, $\rho$ defined by formula (5) gives the magnitude of the parameter of substitution between two factors of production and $\rho \varepsilon [ - 1$ , l]. In general, the substitution parameter $( \rho )$ , defined by formula (5) based on the CES production function, corresponds to the so-called elasticity of substitution (r, Allen [1]) between the factors of production at the combinations of factors considered in some interesting aspects. Table 1 summarizes some general and limiting cases of their correspondences. For example, when the production function is a straight line (fixed proportions), both $\rho$ and $\sigma$ are infinite; and when $\rho = 0 ,$ the CES production function reduces to the standard Cobb–Douglas production function which is homogenous of the first degree (constant returns to scale, i.e., $\alpha = 1 )$ with $\sigma = 1$ . As such, $\sigma { = } 0$ for any two factors implies that these two factors are entirely incapable of substitution, but $\rho = 0$ implies $\sigma = 1$ (rather than $\sigma = 0 )$ and $\mathsf { \Phi } \mathsf { z } = 1$ . To discuss the empirical results that follow and to put $\rho$ in an understandable perspective, we therefore attempt to interpret the economic meaning of $\rho$ as follows.

$\rho = 0$ means r = 1; if $\rho { > } 0$ , two inputs are substitutable and the larger the magnitude of $\rho _ { ; }$ , the higher the possibility of substitution between any two inputs, i.e., the higher potential of substitutability; and it follows that $\mathrm { i f } \mathrm { \mathrm { ~ - ~ } } 1 { \le } \rho { < } 0$ , the two factors of production may be said to be complementary and the larger the magnitude of $\rho$ in absolute value, the higher potential of complementarity.

## 3. Results

3.1. Estimation method, technical efficiency, and IT substitutability

The estimation of Models l to 3 is done in a two-step procedure as described in Lin and Shao ([25], p. 17), using the Limit Dependent (LIMDEP) statistical package software. Note that if the exception condition, i.e., wrong (positive) skewness (w.s.) exists, the estimation process would stop and no results are available (Waldman [38]). If the estimation process succeeds, then the firm’s technical efficiency at time t is measured by exp( $- \boldsymbol { u } _ { i t } )$ (Lovell [26], p. 20) which must lie between 0 and 1, implying that a higher value means a higher efficiency. Overall, the average technical efficiency (ATE) is computed via $\Sigma _ { i , t } \mathrm { e x p } ( - u _ { i t } ) / n N$ (Lin and Shao [25], p. 13). As stated earlier, formula (5) for the substitution parameter $( \rho )$ is the basis of exploring the possibility of the substitution of IT capital for ordinary capital and labor (see Table 1).

## 3.2. Data at the firm level

The data used in this study are the same as those previously used in the work by Hitt and Brynjolfsson [21], Dewan and Min [17], Lin and Shao [25], Shao and Lin [34–36], and others. Two compelling reasons why the same data set is used are that it facilitates a meaningful comparison of the results with those reported in previous research and that the data set is the most comprehensive one available at the firm level for evaluating the IT business value at the present time.

As described in the above-mentioned studies using the same data set, the source of the IT-related data is the International Data Group (IDG)/Computerworld surveys of IS spending by large U.S. firms, conducted annually over the period from 1988 to 1992. The surveys focus primarily on large Fortune500 firms. About two-thirds of these firms belong to the manufacturing sector and the rest is from the service sector.

The data set consists of 1115 observations over the time period under consideration for 376 firms. The key variables are measured in 1990 dollars, including K = non-computer capital, L = non-IS labor, computer capital, IS labor, and Y = the firm’s value added. The production factor for IT investments is represented by I (= IT stock) and is calculated via computer capital + 3 	 IS labor (Brynjolfsson [11], Brynjolffson and Hitt [12], Hitt and Brynjolfsson [21], Dewan and Min [17], Lin and Shao [25], and Shao and Lin [34–36]), where the multiplier $m = 3$ of IS labor used in calculating I is the assumed service life of the asset created by IS labor. Lin and Shao [25] have conducted a sensitivity analysis by allowing the multiplier (m) of the IS labor to change from 1 to 7 and arrived at the same conclusion.

Table 2 reports estimated results based on the whole data set with m = 3. Also included in Table 2 are the estimates of the five parameters associated with the CES stochastic frontier, ATE, and $R ^ { 2 }$ (the coefficient of determination). The empirical results presented in Table 2 suggest three interesting points as explained below.

First of all, the coefficient estimates suggest that the productivity paradox is existent in view of the average technical efficiency (ATE), contrary to the conclusions reached by Hitt and Brynjolfsson [21], Lin and Shao [25], and Shao and Lin [25,26]. For example, when the ordinary labor factor (L) in Model l is replaced by the IT stock input (I), resulting in Model 2, with the ordinary capital input (K) being kept unchanged, the estimate ( .0706) of the coefficient of I is not statistically significant (t =  1.63) with a wrong (negative) sign, the ATE decreases from .8577 to .4709, and $R ^ { 2 }$ decreases from .9829 to .7918. On the other hand, when the capital factor (K) in Model l is replaced by I, with L being kept unchanged, leading to Model 3, the estimate (.0480) of the coefficient of I is briskly significant (t = 2.87) with an anticipated sign and both ATE and $R ^ { 2 }$ decline only slightly (from .8577 to .8539 and from .9829 to .9816, respectively).

To state alternatively, the statistical insignificance, wrong sign, and the decline in the ATE based on Model 2 imply that when ordinary capital (K) is paired with IT stock (I), the overall impact of the IT investment on the technical efficiency declines and the IT investment factor is much less important than the labor input. In contrast, when L is paired with I in Model 3, the results indicate that I is as important as K. These results provide an important managerial/organizational implication that IT capital (I) and non-IT capital (K) are equally important in the production process but, technically, IT capital cannot be used to substitute for labor (L) totally, although IT capital may be used to replace K in the presence of L.

Next, we consider the issue of inputs substitution empirically. On the one hand, the estimates of input substitutability between capital and labor $( \rho _ { K L } = . 5 4 9 4 )$ differs from that between labor and IT capital $( \rho _ { I L } = . 3 2 4 2 )$ in magnitude, both being positive and much greater than zero, suggesting that the possibility of non-IT capital–labor substitutability is greater than that of IT capital–labor substitutability and that both the elasticities of substitution between traditional capital and labor $( \sigma _ { K L } )$ and between IT capital and labor $( \sigma _ { I L } )$ are positive. On the other, since the input substitutability between K and I is estimated to be .4731 $( = \rho _ { I K } )$ , a negative value far from zero, non-IT capital and IT capital are complementary rather than substitutable $( \sigma _ { I K } { < } 0 )$ . These results suggest another important managerial/organizational implication that the three factors of production considered are not pairwise substitutable (K and L and I and L are substitutable while K and I are complementary in the production process).

Our results lead to these findings. First, the elasticities of substitution between K and $L ~ \left( \sigma _ { K L } \right)$ and between I and L $( \sigma _ { I L } )$ are positive (and possibly greater than unity) because $\rho _ { K L }$ and $\rho _ { I L }$ are much greater than zero. Second, as mentioned above, two pairs $( K , L )$ and $( I , L )$ are substitutable, but one pair $( K , I )$ is not, i.e., K and I are complementary. Third, to maintain a given level of output or to keep a certain level of technical efficiency unchanged, it is necessary to realize that the elasticity of substitution between K and $L ~ \left( \sigma _ { K L } \right)$ is greater than that between I and L $( \sigma _ { I L } )$ because $\rho _ { K L } > \rho _ { I L }$ . These findings are highly relevant for IT and production managers in making IS spending and production decisions.

Our findings do not concur with the existing literature on production and IT economics which has obtained empirical evidence suggesting unity elasticities of input substitution (e.g., Berndt [8], Dewan and Min [17], and others), although varying models and data were used. We believe the unity elasticity of substitution is caused by the assumption of constant returns to scale imposed by these studies because, in theory, the scale of constant returns corresponds to $\rho = 0$ (in the CES function) and $\sigma = 1$ (for a linearly homogeneous function). For example, using the same set of data as used in this study, a variant of the CES and translog functions (suggested by Pollak et al. [31]) in which the constant returns-to-scale assumption (a = 1) is imposed, and a Monte Carlo simulation, Dewan and Min have reached two major conclusions: all three inputs (K, L, and I) are pairwise substitutable and all three input elasticities $( \sigma _ { K L } , \ \sigma _ { I L }$ , and $\sigma _ { I K } )$ are very close to unity. The constancy assumption regarding returns to scale is rejected by the same data as discussed below.

Third and finally, we turn to other parameter estimates including the estimate of the returns-to-scale parameter (a). The pair of K and L in Model l, the pair of K and I in Model 2, and the pair of L and I in

Model 3 in the CES production frontier result in substantially different estimates of the efficiency parameter (1.9305 vs. 17.030 vs. 2.210), the distribution parameter (.1662 or about 17% from Model l vs. 1.1018 or about 110% from Model 2 to K; .8338 or 83% from Model l vs. .9511 or about 95% from Model 3 to L; and .1018 or about 10% from Model 2 vs. .0489 or 5% from Model 3 to I), and the returns-to-scale parameter (.9694 from Model l vs. .6934 from Model 2 vs. .9822 from Model 3). In particular, our special attention must be paid to the estimates of a (the returns-to-scale parameter) and d and 1  d (the distribution parameters). All three models clearly show decreasing, rather than constant, returns to scale, which is also implied by the non-zero estimates of the q parameter. The most obvious one is Model 2, followed by Model l. Furthermore, we note that when L is substituted by I (Model 2); that is, when I is paired with K, the output distributed to I is 10.18% while the share of output distributed to K is 110.18% (i.e., exceeding 100%). This raises an unresolved question: if factors get more than total output, from whom do they get it? Neoclassic economic theory is unable to answer the question (Lin and Shao [25]). When I is paired with L (Model 3), the share of output going to I is relatively small (0.0489 or just less than 5%) comparing with the percentage proportion going to L (0.9511 or more than 95%). This raises a serious concern about the income (output) distribution problem, a long-debated issue in the economics literature.

In sum, comparing with Model l, we find that the substitution of I for L (Model 2) considerably decreases the average technical efficiency, raises the distribution of output for K, and lowers the returns to scale, and that the replacement of K by I (Model 3) only slightly decreases the average technical efficiency, increases the distribution of output to both L and I, and also increases the returns-to-scale. More importantly, we note that technical efficiency and productivity are two related concepts but different performance measures (Lin and Shao [25] and Shao and Lin [25,26]). Although productivity growth and technical efficiency do not always vary in the same direction, the evidence provided by Caves and Barton [13], Fecher and Perelman [19], Lin and Shao [25], Lin and Chen [24], and Shao and Lin [34–36] suggests that they normally do. In line of this empirical argument, the decline in the average technical efficiency caused by the substitution of I for L (Model 2) and for K (Model 3) may well suggest the existence of the productivity paradox, a highly controversial issue we now turn to for more details.

## 3.7. A two-equation model vs. a simultaneous equations system

The two-equation model mentioned above falls short of a simultaneous-equations system and has nothing to do with the statistical identification problem. Intuitively, there are important interrelationships among key determinants in our single equation models. If the hypothesized interrelationships are present, simultaneous equations should be more appropriate than the single equation models proposed and applied in the preceding sections.

A simultaneous-equations system may be constructed. For example, incorporating with Model 2, the system may be described by

$$
\begin{array}{c} \ln Y _ {i t} = \ln \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \ln K _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \ln I _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} (\ln K _ {i t} - \ln I _ {i t}) ^ {2} \\ + \beta_ {4} \ln S _ {i t} + v _ {1 i t} - u _ {i t} \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

$$
\ln I _ {i t} = \ln \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \ln P _ {i t} + \alpha_ {2} \ln Y _ {i t} + \alpha_ {3} \ln E _ {i t} + v _ {2 i t}\tag{7}
$$

$$
\ln P _ {i t} = \ln \gamma_ {0} + \gamma_ {1} \ln I _ {i t} + \gamma_ {2} \ln Y _ {i t} + v _ {3 i t},\tag{8}
$$

where $S _ { i t }$ is the variable of firm size, $E _ { i t }$ is the e-business variable, and $P _ { i t }$ is the price of $\operatorname { T } _ { i t } .$ Eq. (7) is the demand for IT capital equation [20] and Eq. (8) is the supply function of IT capital. The system implies that firm size affects the observed output and, hence, the technical (in)efficiency (see Section 3.6) and that e-business has a positive impact on the demand for IT capital. Therefore, it is expected that $\beta _ { 4 } { > } 0 , \alpha _ { 1 } { < } 0 , \alpha _ { 2 } { > } 0$ , and $\gamma _ { 1 } > 0$ . The system means that the observed output, the demand for IT capital, and the supply of IT capital are jointly determined and are exogenously influenced by ordinary capital, ordinary labor, firm size, and e-commerce. Data on these variables involved are available.

Furthermore, it can be shown that Eqs. (6) and (7) are just-identified, while Eq. (8) is over-identified, meaning that the entire system is identified. However, we face the difficulty in estimating the system. Since Eq. (6) is non-linear and, more importantly, the system involves a half-normally (or one-sided normally) distributed stochastic error $\left( { { u } _ { i t } } \right)$ , in addition to the traditional random errors $( \nu _ { j i t } , \ j = 1 , 2 , 3 )$ , the well-known two-stage least-squares (2SLS) and three-stage leastsquares (3SLS) methods are no longer valid. At the present time, legitimate methods of estimation are not readily available for estimating a simultaneous-equations model like the one consisting of Eqs. (6)–(8). Nevertheless, we can speculate that the technical inefficiency $\left( { { u } _ { i t } } \right)$ and the technical efficiency $( \exp ( - u _ { i t } ) )$ calculated from the simultaneous-equations model differ from their counterparts based on the single equation model (Model 2).

Thus, the development of simultaneous equations would nicely facilitate a comprehensive and useful comparison between single equation models (such as Models 1–3) and simultaneous equations systems (such as the one proposed above). It also serves to identify the contributors (such as $S _ { i t }$ and $E _ { i t } )$ that directly and indirectly influence technical (in)efficiencies.

## 3.8. A sector-specific and an industry-level analysis

Reviewing the literature on IT studies reveals that a vast amount of research is confined to a firm-level analysis using a set of cross-sectional data, i.e. a sample of firms selected from a specific industry (e.g., Pendharkay and Rodger [30] who have used a set of crosssectional data from 188 hospitals and Serrano-Cinca et al. [33] who have used a set of cross-sectional data from 40 dot com firms), or a set of panel data from a special industry (e.g., the DSS paper by Menon and Lee [27] used panel data from all hospitals in the healthcare industry in the State of Washington during the period from 1976 to 1994), or a set of panel data from various industries (the long list includes, e.g., Brynjolfsson and Hitt [12], Dewan and Min [17], Hitt and Brynjolfsson [21], Lin and Shao [25], Shao and Lin [34–36], and the present study). The DSS article of Gurbaxani [20] has studied the demand for IT capital at both the aggregate (economy) and industry (sector) levels, but suggested pursuing an analysis at a more disaggregate industry (sector) level. We present a sector- and an industryspecific analysis in this subsection.

The sector analysis splits the data set into two groups (sectors): manufacturing (n = 760) and services (n = 355). The estimation of the models for the services sector is less successful than that for the manufacturing sector. The sector specific analysis is somewhat limited on the grounds that, due to w.s., no results from Model 3 in the manufacturing sector and from Models 2 and 3 in the services sector are obtainable. We believe it is caused by the fact that the sample size of the services sector is much smaller than that of the manufacturing sector; the estimation method of the LIMDEP software is highly sensitive to the size of the sample and the distribution of the data used.

We are ready to mention the following points of interest. (i) Overall, the manufacturing sector is only slightly more technically efficient than the services sector (ATE = .8785 for manufacturing vs. ATE = .8751 for services based on Model 1). (ii) Ordinary K and ordinary L are complementary in both the manufacturing and services sectors and the degree of complementarity is stronger in the manufacturing sector than in the services sector, which are implied by $\rho _ { K L } = - . 5 3 9 3$ for manufacturing vs.  .2447 for service. (iv) Moreover, in the manufacturing sector, the replacement of L by I decreases the ATE from .8785 to .7677 and, because $\rho _ { I K } = 2 . 2 8 0 9$ , K and I are substitutable instead of complementary. The implication is clearly that the input substitution between L and I affects the business value of IT as measured by the technical efficiency and enforces the productivity paradox!

As for industry-specific analyses, since most industries in the data set used are constituted by small numbers of firms (i.e., n is small), we can only consider the few industries that have enough observations to generate meaningful results, meaning that in view of the sensitive nature of the LIMDEP program, if n is too small, estimates may not be obtainable. Keeping this criterion and limitation in mind, we examine only two representative (in terms of n) industries in the manufacturing sector, namely, food and kindred products (FKP) (SIC = 2000, n = 59) and chemicals and allied products (CAP) (SIC = 2800, n = 109). We also analyze two representative services industries: electricity, gas, and sanitary services (EGSS) (SIC=4900, n =87) and general merchandise stores (GMS) (SIC = 5300, n = 38). Due to w.s., the estimates of Model 3 for the two manufacturing industries and those of Model 2 for the two services industries are lacking.

There are similarities and differences between the industries in the manufacturing sector, between the industries in the services sector, and among the industries across different sectors. ## 4. Concluding remarks

In this paper, equipped with the constant elasticity of substitution (CES) stochastic production frontier model, we are able to jointly and critically examine the business value of information technology (IT) investments, the productivity paradox, and inputs substitution, using the performance measure called technical (or productive) efficiency at three levels: firm, sector, and industry. The analyses at the three differing levels offer virtually the same conclusions concerning the three interrelated issues: the value of IT, inputs substitution and complement, and the paradox of productivity. Specifically, we have found evidence suggesting some important points.

Using IT capital to replace ordinary capital or ordinary labor or spending more on IT investments does not necessarily mean more technically (productively) efficient. The relationship between productivity and technical efficiency is not robust with respect to the stochastic production frontier model employed. The productivity paradox is still existent, contrary to the wisdom of previous studies employing the same data set; but it was found that the general merchandise stores industry seems to have escaped the paradox.

Non-IT capital (K), IT capital (I), and labor (L) are not pairwise substitutable; especially, K and I are complementary at the high level of IT investments while they are substitutable at both the low and medium levels of IT investments. The relationship of substitutability or complementarity between the inputs varies from one pair of the three inputs considered to another, depending on the extent to which the amount of the IT investments is treated as a low, medium, or high level in the organization. From a positive point of view, the inputs substitution or complement is a contributing factor that may determine whether the output level and technical efficiency can be maintained or improved.

IT stock is as important as ordinary capital in the production process. It is unlikely that IT stock may be used to substitute for labor entirely; in other words, the total substitution of IT stock for labor is not technically possible or feasible. Accordingly, at a certain level of IT investments, IT stock may better pair with labor than with ordinary capital. Also, decreasing returns to scale were found; however, a larger relative size of IT investments would lead to higher returns to scale. An increase in the multiplier of the IS labor variable results in a persistent decline in the returns to scale. Finally, the sector analysis seems to suggest that the manufacturing sector is only slightly more technically efficient than the services sector; and the industry-level analysis indicates that IT capital is more important for the services industries than for the manufacturing industries.

Compared with the literature on the subject based on the same data set as we have employed here, we have documented new results with respect to inputs substitution, returns to scale, the productivity paradox, and technical efficiency. Capital (non-IT capital), labor, and IT capital are not pairwise substitutable, contrary to Dewan and Min [17] who found that capital, labor, and IT capital are pairwise substitutable; we have found that capital and labor are substitutable and so are labor and IT capital, but capital and IT capital are complementary at the high level of IT investments. The returns to scale are found to be decreasing rather than constant as concluded by Berndt [8] and Dewan and Min [17], among others representing conventional wisdom; in fact, the constant returns-to-scale (i.e., the returns-toscale parameter a = 1) is one of the assumptions underlying the CES-translog model (Pollak et al. [31]) used by Dewan and Min. The phenomenon of the productivity paradox does not disappear, which is at variance with popular belief that it has disappeared (Brynjolfsson [11], Brynjolfsson and Hitt [12], Hitt and Brynjolfsson [21], Lin and Shao [25], and Shao and Lin [34–36]). Our empirical work suggests that the business value of IT measured by technical efficiency tends to decline as IT investments increase, which is in conflict with the conclusion reached by Lin and Shao [25] and Shao and Lin [34–36] using the same performance measure.

We emphasize that using the same source of data as used in a number of studies mentioned above has led to some conflicting conclusions concerning the issues of IT value and inputs substitution and complement and the phenomenon of the productivity paradox. According to our empirical results, the conflicting conclusions include lack of a robust relationship between technical efficiency and IS investment, absence of a robust relationship between the factors of production which is affected by the relative magnitude of IT investments, and existence of the productivity paradox. One obvious source of the contradictory conclusions is the econometric specification of the stochastic production frontier model employed and the econometric method used. Another source is that the three questions posed here must be resolved jointly, yet all previous research using the same set of firm-level data from the United States private sector treated them separately and individually.

Previous research on IT-related issues has examined the problem of IT impact at the macro-level (economy or specific industries), at the firm level, or at the application level (e.g., Mukhopadhyay et al. [29] who examined the impact of IT on labor productivity at the application level). However, Bakos and Kemerer’s [5] argument that presently the national significance of IT investments has been the subject of speculation indicates the need for rigorous research using national-level data in an attempt to quantify the business value and productivity impacts of IT investments. This need is also evidenced in the work by Gurbaxani [20] who, as mentioned before, studied the demand for IT capital at both the aggregate and industry levels. Therefore, one extension to the present and previous studies is that the three issues are put in a global perspective, thereby requiring a multinational comparison based on country-level data. In particular, we believe inputs substitutability or complementarity in IS management is a complex matter which needs more research taking a global perspective.

As a final remark, we comment on the relationship of IT investments to decision support systems (DSS) per se and the relevance of this research to DSS. The three levels of analysis undertaken in this research have strengthened the link of our paper to the themes of DSS; and the issues addressed in this study are consistent with the topics of IT investments and IS economics relevant to DSS. While we recognize that it is somewhat difficult to separate the aggregate impact of IT payoff from a specific one on DSS, based on the data set used in this study, the findings presented have a cascading effect on DSS usage and payoff. This is because DSS has always been, and still is, an important IT application, used extensively in businesses to support managers in their decision making processes. The expenditures made in developing, maintaining, and upgrading such DDS constitute an essential part of the overall IT investments. Thus, the aggregate measure of IT payoff may very likely have a significant impact on the DSS usage and payoff, meaning that the DSS usage and payoff are closely related to the aggregate measures of IT payoff and usage, and vice versa.
