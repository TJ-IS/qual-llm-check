## ABSTRACT

The wide popularity and rapid growth of social commerce have opened a new arena for business and grand opportunities for Information Systems (IS) research. However, there is only a limited theoretical understanding of and even less substantive empirical grounding on how social media technologies, providers, and customers interact to achieve social commerce initiatives and opportunities. This study integrates the service-dominant (S-D) logic and customer-dominant (C-D) logic perspectives and both streams of service marketing and IS usage literature, and proposes a unified framework outlining key qualities and dimensions of social commerce and their interactions. We tested the framework with a large set of field survey data of 1,250 social commerce customers. The results show that each of the proposed dimensions captures a unique prominent aspect of social commerce; the multi-dimensions combine to define the underlying structural nature and process of social commerce. Furthermore, the testing of the model validated the hypothesized interactive relationships of key social commerce qualities. The unified view contributes to IS research in general and social commerce innovations, and provides managerial implications for understanding the overall interactions of social commerce.

Keywords: Social commerce, service, experience, relationship, co-created value, service-dominant logic, customerdominant logic, unified view.

## 1.1 Social Commerce on the Rise

The integration of social media technologies and applications in e-commerce is often referred to as social commerce. Social commerce involves customers using social media technologies to facilitate transactions, recommendation, referral, and communications that enable electronic commerce (e-commerce) activities such as marketing, buying, and selling of products and/or services. In recent years, social media (e.g., Facebook, YouTube, Twitter, Instagram, Pinterest, and others) has been viewed as the most exciting interactive platform on the Internet. By January 2015, 1.97 billion individuals were using social media around the world [116]. Nielsen reported that 46% of online customers use social media to make purchase decisions. The widespread use of social media by customers has galvanized organizations to embrace social media technologies to reengineer business structure and processes [122], and has opened grand opportunities for novel e-commerce business models.

Presently, social commerce is of global interest driving much bigger increase in retail traffic than any other online channel. For example, between the first quarters of 2014 and 2015 in the U.S., social media has increased its share of e-commerce referrals by nearly 200% [115]. Gartner projected that by 2016, the social commerce revenue will have reached \$34 billion. Firms and businesses are setting new records in their expansion, and stock prices increase because of the social commerce innovations. Many companies have recognized the unprecedented impact and significance of social commerce within their business model. For example, 80% of Fortune 500 companies are holding active accounts on Facebook, Twitter, and YouTube and conducting various social commerce–based transactions [9]; more than 63% of managers assert that social commerce is important for their business [68]].

## 1.2 Research in Social Commerce

Social commerce, as the integration of social media and electronic commerce, has piqued increasing interest in Information Systems (IS) research because it provides a fresh direction for e-commerce and affects individuals and organizations in many ways [145]. IS research has long called for the investigations into the prominent interactions of key social commerce actors (e.g., [78],[140],[146],[145]]).

In this regard, recent studies (e.g.,[2],[51]) have suggested that customer reviews can serve as important information sources for decision-making in the context of social commerce. Choudhury and Harrigan [25] examined the interactive relationships from the customer relationship management (CRM) perspective and contributed to our understanding of the communications between the service provider and customer. Liang et al. [79] reported that social support from online connections may drive customers to actively participate in social commerce interactions. Zhang et al. [145] developed a model to theorize how social media website characteristics improve customers’ usage experience and subsequently affect their intention to participate in social commerce. Some of the interesting theoretical perspectives used in the studies of social commerce include the social support theory [79], commitment–trust theory and trust transfer theory [21], information quality and e-commerce design principles [146], and switching behavior and Push-Pull-Mooring framework [77].

Although each of these theoretical perspectives provides critical insights into social commerce, Wang and Zhang ([133], pp. 119) state that “based on what we learn from social commerce evolution, there are diverse views and positions in social commerce that may call for additional theoretical perspectives…(and) IS researchers are similarly encouraged to use diverse perspectives, assumptions, and approaches to enrich our theoretical understanding.” This is similar to Luo and Lai [83] recommendation that IS researchers investigate social commerce “…using novel theories and/or methods as well as research contexts” (pp.1) to develop a more comprehensive understanding of this important emerging phenomenon. It is therefore critical that IS researchers begin to investigate social commerce phenomenon to further our insights into the context of academic research and managerial practice.

Moreover, according to Li and Ku [77] (pp. 1), “compared with traditional product-centric online marketplaces with firm-provided information, social commerce focuses on social-centered and consumer-driven online marketplaces”. Echoing this differential perspective existing between the more well-understood e-commerce phenomenon and lessdeveloped or emerging nature of social commerce, Wang and Zhang [133] identified “Conceptual Ambiguity” (pp. 120) in social commerce as part of challenges being faced by IS scholars, thus pointing to future research directions and opportunities related to “conceptualizing and theorizing” (pp. 120) to advance our understanding of social

commerce for both research and practice. Similarly, Yadav et al., [140] (pp. 2) concludes that “taking a collective look at extant work that seeks to characterize the domain of social commerce, it is evident that there is much confusion about what the term social commerce means.” They further argue “that there is both a consumer-side and a firm-side to activities that comprise the domain of social commerce. Thus, activities that consumers engage in before, during, and after a transaction, along with corresponding firms' initiatives to facilitate those activities, are included in the domain of social commerce” (pp.2).

## 1.3 Our Approach

In this study, we argued that, although social commerce providers offer various mechanisms and services for customers to communicate and share experience, at the end it is the individual customer who realizes, appreciates, and determines the value of any service including social commerce. Hence, following [76],[37],[56],[105], we contend that the central focus of social commerce remains on the individual customer and her/his perceptions about service quality (SQ), service consumption experience, and resultant relationship quality (RQ). Although extant studies in IS have focused on customer behaviors, website design, social processes, adoption strategy, business models, security and privacy policy, and firm performance of social commerce [4],[78],[146],[145],[8],[25],[44], research related to social commerce in this direction is largely fragmented [140]. Our study contributes in this context by responding to calls for research [83],[78] to further our understanding of social commerce.

Unfortunately, extant conceptualization and theoretical perspectives in the IS literature are underdeveloped about these consumer and firm activities, in the context of social commerce, primarily due to lack of a unified theoretical framework and foundation that encompasses such consumer and firm activities. Interestingly, Yadav et al. (2013) propose that “social commerce refers to exchange-related activities…” and Vargo and Lusch [127] states that the central tenet of S-D logic (Service-dominant logic) is, service is the fundamental basis of exchange. Taking an integrative perspective on the current conceptualization in the extant literature [140],[133],[127],[77],[79][145][137] [83]], social commerce can be conceptualized as a collection of service, mediated by information technology (IT), that forms the basis of exchange in the context of social relations among service consumers and providers. In this research, we address the identified gap related to “Conceptual Ambiguity” [133] and the lack of a unified theoretical perspective that integrates consumer and provider activities in social commerce by proposing a unified view of the servicedominant (S-D) logic and the customer-dominant (C-D) logic that seeks to delineate customer activities and service provider activities in the context of exchange and value creation in social commerce.

In this study, we argue that social commerce providers integrate social media technologies and invest resources to build the Internet-enabled service platform that customers use to conduct commercial transactions in the context of social relations – a typical process of service co-creation, delivery, and performance in a collaborative manner [137]. The S-D logic establishes significant role that IT plays – as an operand and operant resource – in enhancing the opportunities for service innovation [84]. The S-D logic emphasizes service innovation as a collaborative relationship occurring in a network of key service actors that integrate specialized competences and resources for co-created value of involved parties [127][128][131]. Thus, the S-D logic provides a valuable perspective that supports the notion that key actors and entities of social commerce interact with each other and co-create service value [59][84].

Although the S-D logic has increased our understanding and insight into services marketing, the view remains very much production and interaction focused, i.e., service provider-dominant rather than customer-dominant. Service research of this stream tends to focus on analyzing an individual service system from the company’s point of view (service blueprinting, see Bitner et al., [10]) or on customer-provider interactions that are played out during time [104]. Service is then typically conceptualized as co-creation dominated by and from the perspective of the service provider and not from the perspective of the customer. However, when scholars argue that the ultimate goal of service should be to create value for the customer [48], the traditional approaches will inevitably lead to an incomplete and confusing conceptualization of what the customer does with the service [53]

The perspective of customer being prime and central in determining service value is well established in the service marketing literature. For example, Levitt [76] argued that companies are very focused on their own production processes and not sufficiently oriented toward customer satisfaction. Drucker ([37], p. 61) argued that the business purpose is to create a customer: “It is the customer who determines what a business is.” He saw marketing as the

#

business as perceived by a customer: “It is the whole business seen from the point of view of its final result, that is, from the customer’s point of view” (p. 63). Recent debates in the service literature have focused on the role played by the active customer who co-creates value. However, what needs to be addressed is, how value emerges for customers and especially through their episodic co-creative experience of value of a service provider’s participation in their activities and tasks. This argument is supported by Gro¨nroos[48], Holbrook[56], Pen˜aloza and Venkatesh[105], and Schembri [113] who emphasize the need to consider a customer’s perspective in service research. The central focus within the C-D logic is the understanding of the mechanisms related to value-in-use in the customer’s own context, life-wants, and the customer experience with the service. We argue that the customer’s value in-use evaluation is based on the customer’s service experience which is embedded in the customer’s context and customer activities. The customers’ context should then become a central part of service creation and delivery. This shift in focus then requires novel conceptualization about the role of the service provider in the customers’ context. From the customer’s point of view, service contains three types of elements: value proposals and outcomes of the service providers’ internal activities, co-creation processes and their outcome elements with customers, and experiential processes and outcomes of the customer’s own activities. Co-creation is seen here as an element of service co-created with the customer experience by integrating service within customer activities. This is true for social commerce services offered by service providers through social media or when they incorporate social media technology as part of their platform (websites) for service creation and delivery.

Recent conceptualization of the C-D logic [53][48][119][55] places customers at the center rather than service or service delivery mechanism. Although we agree with the literature that tends to conceptualize the S-D logic and C-D logic as distinct [55][53], we argue that in the context of business, both S-D logic and C-D logic are parts of a continuum. That is, service remains a central concern of service providers following the S-D logic, but customers become a central concern when viewed from the service consumption and experience perspective and the role the service and service provider play in the customers’ context and activities. Therefore, we argue that it is logical to consider the S-D logic and C-D logic as being part of the same continuum. Various theoretical perspectives in IS research tend to provide description, explanation, prediction, and in some cases prescription about the phenomenon under investigation [46]. Central to any theory or theoretical integration is the phenomenon under investigation and the unit of analysis [69]. That is, if two theoretical perspectives are to be integrated, then these two theories need to have concern about the same central phenomenon and they also need to have a similar unit of analysis [94][97]. In the context of this research, the phenomenon under investigation is the individual customer’s perception of value cocreation in the context of IT-mediated services that incorporates elements of social interaction or social commerce. Therefore, in this research, we have considered two theoretical perspectives – the S-D logic [127][128][131] and the C-D logic [53][48][119][55].

## 1.4 Research Questions

As afore argued, in this study we propose a unified comprehensive perspective in terms of key social commerce qualities and dimensions [12][27][31][40][32], especially integrating S-D logic and C-D logic, to develop deeper insights and contribute to business and managerial practice [8][25][44][140][53]. To the end, this study attempts to address the following three research questions.

1. What are the qualities and dimensions of social commerce from the unified view of the S-D and C-D logics?

2. How can social commerce qualities and dimensions be integrated to co-create value from the unified view?

3. How do social commerce qualities and dimensions and value co-creation affect resultant relationship quality of the phenomenon?

We address the research questions by integrating the S-D logic and C-D logics and both streams of IS usage and service marketing literature (e.g., [12][27],[31],[40][32]). We proposed a theoretical model to describe key quality dimensions of social commerce and their interactions. We empirically validated the model using a large set of field survey data of 1,250 social commerce customers. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The next section provides an extensive literature review of social commerce, S-D logic, and C-D logic, thus serving as the research foundations of this study. Then, a unified research framework and hypotheses are developed outlining major qualities and dimensions of social commerce, and their nomological relationships in shaping the structural process of social commerce. The subsequent section reports data collection, instrument development, and data analysis, demonstrating

## 2. Research Background

It is generally believed that both S-D and C-D logics provide valuable perspectives that necessitate the re-evaluation of the conventional literature of service innovations [84][114][53][54]. In this section, we present an overview of social commerce and summarize the major points of both S-D and C-D logics. We show in the next section that the S-D and C-D logic perspectives are theoretically relevant to our study in enriching the understanding of the nature of social commerce and its key qualities and dimensions.

## 2.1 Overview of Social Commerce

Social commerce integrates social media technologies that support social interactions and user-generated contents to enable online marketing, buying, and selling of products and services. Collaborative designs and features of social commerce allow customers to take advantage of online social networking resources to share transactional experience, exchange goods/service information and recommendations, increase trust and commitment among peers and service providers, and ultimately influence their decision to participate in social commerce.

Three types of social commerce have been identified [78],[146]. The first one is the social networking service websites. In this type, social-oriented transactions are performed, such as in Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn, and YouTube. The second is the one in which traditional e-commerce websites (e.g., Amazon, eBay) incorporate social media technologies (e.g., recommendations, reviews, social networking, etc.) to facilitate transactional activities. The third one is the group shopping websites in which customers form online social groups based on similar interests and needs, and make purchases for price advantages.

Social commerce can be viewed as a newly born baby of the marriage between social media and traditional exchangebased electronic commerce (E-Commerce) such as business-to-business (B2B), business-to-customer (B2C), and customer-to-customer (C2C). In this sense, social commerce is commonly considered as the evolution and expansion of traditional E-Commerce [5][21][51][77][78][80][117][133][140][146][148]. Compared to the traditional E-Commerce, however, social commerce has demonstrated significant design advances and features of its own, thus representing a unique opportunity for IS research and business practice. In social commerce, the main goal of the service provider is to convert online players into customers and brand advocates, whereas the goal of the customers is to get advices and recommendations from trusted individuals, find goods/services from trusted providers, and make informed transaction decisions [59]. Both sets of goals can be established, when (1) the social media technologies are configured with desirable quality and function and support online collaborative interactions, (2) the goods/services offered are in suitable quality to meet customers’ benefit needs, (3) the customer achieves desirable service experiences of certain dimensions, and (4) the customer shares the transactional experience, co-creates the service value, and is willing to continue the relationship with the provider and peers. These exchange-based co-created activities, transactions, and experiences form the underlying qualities, dimensions, and structural process of social commerce [59],[58]. In this context, the S-D logic and C-D logics provide a strong theoretical foundation to conceptualize social commerce and its underlying qualities and dimensions.

## 2.2 Service-Dominant Logic and Customer-Dominant Logic

The S-D logic defines service as “the application of specialized competences (skills and knowledge) through deeds, processes, and performances for the benefit of another entity or the entity itself” [127] (p. 2). Here, service is viewed as a series of managerial/marketing processes and activities through which customers and providers collaborate through the exchange of specialized competences and resources, and co-create the service for unique needs and values.

The process renders an interactive relationship among customers and service providers and generates value for involved parties.

A fundamental premise of the S-D logic is that the customer is always a co-creator of value together with the service provider [128], [129]. It is argued that the service provider cannot unilaterally create value and can only offer value propositions and thus potentially co-create service and the resulting customer value: “In S-D logic, the roles of producers and consumers are not distinct, meaning that value is always co-created, jointly and reciprocally, in interactions among providers and beneficiaries through the integration of resources and application of competences [129] (p. 146).” The co-creation process revolves around customer needs and use. In this context, customer assumes a significant role as correctly conceptualized in the C-D logic literature. When the customer is absent, there is no cocreation of value. Service value has to be co-created with active participation of the customer. The process of value co-creation is intimately integrated with customer experience. Thus, conceptualizing co-creation using both S-D and C-D logic provides a consistent insight into the structural process of social commerce service creation, delivery, and consumption.

The C-D logic provides an understanding of the role the customer plays in transactional activities, practices, experiences, and contexts [53][119]. From the C-D logic perspective, the terms “co-production” and “co-creation” indicate a service provider–oriented view in the sense that it focuses on an offering or service rather than on the role this offering plays for the customer and the activities carried out by the customer in the use of the service. Hence, there is a need to contrast the established provider-oriented view of involving the customer in service co-creation with a more radical customer-oriented view of involving the service provider in the customer’s life. In this sense, the C-D logic argues for more consideration of customer activities and how the service might benefit the customer in carrying out his/her activities on a regular basis. In this context, customers’ perception of service and service value becomes central to understanding the process of service delivery and value creation. Thus, it is essential to consider and understand customers’ lives, including their contexts, activities, and experiences performing different transactional activities. It is even more critical to understand how the service supports customers’ life – thus the overall customer experience formed during time.

The S-D logic contends that services have a certain utilitarian value for “improvement in system well-being” [130]. In a specific context, service value is realized as effectively utilizable benefits for involved entities including hedonic, utilitarian, and interactional values. Particularly, for customers, service value is perceived deliverable “wants” for higher order needs (Value-in-Use) [49]; for the provider, service value is properly reflected in business behaviors and performance that the provider can seek to maximize the lifetime benefits (Value-in-Use); it is learned and improved from the interactive relationship in terms of trust, participation, and commitment [130].

## 2.3 Integrating S-D and C-D logics toward a Unified View

Although the perspectives of S-D and C-D logics differ significantly in their own right, both exhibit central concern related to service value-co-creation and the significant role and perceptions of the individual customer within the broader service consumption and value co-creation process. We recognize clearly that both theories conceptualize and arrive at the phenomenon from diametrically opposite directions – one from the service provider’s perspective (S-D logic) and the other from the customer’s perspective (C-D logic). However, both theoretical perspectives meet at the point of integration, that is, the customer and how the customer perceives value co-creation in the context of service consumption experience. Both theories recognize that value cannot be created alone by either party. These theoretical perspectives obviously differ in how value is proposed, perceived, and co-created and what role the customer or service provider plays in the value co-creation process. Our approach to conceptualizing the S-D and C-D logics as part of the same continuum is based on [54](pp. 534) theorization that “... reflects how a service, from the customer’s perspective, is not only consumed or used, but potentially integrated into the customer’s ongoing experience and activity structures beyond the service process.” They further “… argue that value emerges when the service provided by service company X and used by the customer becomes embedded in the customer’s context, activities, practices and experiences together with the service company’s activities.” (pp. 537) This line of reasoning clearly demonstrates as per [54] that the S-D logic and C-D logics do intersect at the central point – the customer. Our central argument following the S-D and C-D logics is that if the customer is the central point of intersection for both theories – one is likely to develop a more comprehensive understanding and insights about customer activities, value co-creation, and proactive role of the service provider to be more fully involved in customer life wants and needs – if one considers the S-D and C-D logics as a continuum.

#

Moreover, the S-D logic considers social relations and society as central as Greer et al. [45] argue “…that society (and what holds it together), is service-for-service exchange among entities in society. In brief, service exchange and society are inseparable.” (pp. 29) Similarly, Heinonen et al. [54], in the process of extending the S-D logic concept of valuein-context, suggest that “the context is dynamic and dependent on the customer’s role, position and interaction within a social structure, which subsequently forms the basis for both the co-creation of service and value-in-use assessment… value must also be understood as part of a collective and intersubjective context.” (pp. 540) These arguments from both the S-D and C-D logics provide a strong theoretical foundation for social commerce, as both theories explicitly consider society, social structure (social relations), and social reality as well as collective and intersubjective context as central to value creation. Thus, considering the cumulative arguments and theoretical premises and logic outlined above, we propose through our unified view that a much richer understanding of the individual customer’s perception of the value co-creation, episodic experience, and resultant RQ in the context of social commerce can be developed if both S-D and C-D logic theoretical perspectives are integrated – fully recognizing that they approach the same phenomenon from diametrically opposite directions but both centrally focusing on the customer and service consumption experience. This integrated conceptualization is beneficial to develop a holistic understanding of the role of customer in social commerce services including service activities of both providers and customers’ [140].

When viewed from the C-D logic perspective, we argue that co-creation is not always a straightforward activity orchestrated by the service provider. In the course, while the provider is seen as the active party, the customer does not merely reacts to the provider proposed activities in relation to the provided service. Although the customers’ role as co-producers of services has been accepted in the literature, the interaction has largely been considered from the provider’s perspective with the view that the provider initiates and controls the interaction [39],[47]. The service marketing literature has applied the same perspective in examining the company–customer relationships. Increasingly, however, customers’ memory and experiences from one interaction, use occasion, or even relationship have been acknowledged as an influence on customers’ perception of the next service and value-in-use [111]]. Vargo and Lusch [[129] even suggest that value is always “determined by the beneficiary” or, in other words, the customer, and thus the role of customers should be largely recognized and highlighted. Gro¨nroos [48] (p. 9) suggests that the control of value creation varies and that the providers can take the role of value facilitators where they can potentially influence the customer: The customers create the value for themselves, but in the interactive course, the service provider gets opportunities to change the process of value creation, so that the customers get more value than otherwise would be possible. The C-D logic contends that the service providers pay more attention to what type of opportunities are presented to them so that they can begin to play a value-creating and value-added role in customers’ activities and customer life wants and needs beginning with the intersection between the core customer activities that intersect with the provided service [54].

In summary, a unified view of both S-D and C-D logic perspectives contends that service provision and use reflect the collaborative interactions of customers and providers through certain operant/operand (e.g., physical, financial, and technology) resources. A service is co-created jointly and reciprocally through the interactions and integration of competences and resources of both parties (Resources-in-Exchange). At the inception, the provider applies its competences and resources to propose and make service offerings. The customers then continue the service creation through the experiential use and maintenance of the service (Value-in-Use). In the process, the customer and the provider interact to co-create the service and make reciprocal contributions to service marketing, delivery, and performance (Value Co-Creation).

## 3. Theoretical Development: A Unified View of Social Commerce

As discussed above, the S-D logic reveals the important role that IT plays – as an operand and operant resource – in enhancing the opportunities for service innovation. The S-D logic emphasizes service innovation as a collaborative process occurring in a network of key service actors that integrates specialized competences and resources for benefits of involved parties. Additionally, the C-D logic places customer and customer perspectives as central to conceptualizing service consumption experience. It is our contention that both S-D logic and C-D logics are part of the same continuum and are both important to understand the design of social media platforms that facilitate service delivery, consumption experience, and value co-creation process of social commerce. Thus, the unified view of the S-

D and C-D logics provides valuable points of view that support the notion that key actors of social commerce interact with each other and co-create service value. In this section, we combine S-D logic and C-D logics and findings of both IS usage and service marketing literatures to develop a conceptual framework, outlining major qualities and dimensions of social commerce and their interactions that jointly constitute the structural process and business opportunities of social commerce.

## 3.1 Key Actors and Interactions of Social Commerce

Building upon the unified view, this study maintains that key qualities and dimensions of social commerce and their interactions reflect the underlying structure and process of social commerce that engages both customers and providers to co-create service value in a collaborative conversation over the social media platform.

Through the lens of the S-D logic, social commerce consists of three key actors – the provider, the customer, and the social media platform, each of which can be characterized with certain defining dimensions. On one hand, social commerce reflects the overall interactions of the three key actors in terms of service provision mechanisms, value cocreation, and associated value co-creation performance. On the one hand, the provider integrates social media technologies as operant/operand resources to build the Internet-enabled service platform for online buying and selling of products and services. All exchange-based activities of social commerce occur on the intermediate social media platform, which embeds various technological designs and characteristics. The technological designs and features impact customers’ perception of technology quality (TQ) as it relates to social commerce.

The C-D logic provides additional insight into customer life cycle and how such a service plays a role in customer value co-creation process. Customers bring knowledge, competence, relationship, life wants and needs, and episodic experiences as they engage in social media in the process of service consumption and value co-creation. The C-D logic shifts the focus from the service or relationship to the customer and her/his personal, multi-contextual reality. In this context, both service quality (SQ), as offered through social commerce, and perceived experience quality (EQ) together impact customers’ perception of value co-creation in social commerce. Additionally, value co-creation perception affects how customers perceive relationship quality (RQ) with the provider.

Unifying both perspectives provides researchers and practitioners a much more valuable insight than is possible with only one perspective. Thus, in this paper, we contribute to social commerce literature by integrating the S-D logic with C-D logic and developing a unified view that both perspectives for all practical purposes are part of the same continuum for a business.

The exchange-based activities of social commerce are built and delivered within the ongoing interactive relationship between the customer and the provider through the exchange of specialized resources of both parties (Resources-in-Exchange). In the process, the provider proposes and creates service value of social commerce over the social media platform. Meanwhile, the customer contributes the resources and experience of social networking in terms of relationship developing, information sharing, and commercial transactions, and further maintains and co-creates service value. In the set of the two-way dialogs, service value of social commerce is proposed and delivered by and provides benefits for both the provider and customer (Value Co-Creation). In the long run, the provider and customer connect and communicate with each other through social media. This makes service value continue to be co-created over the social media platform by both parties. Moreover, service value of social commerce is used and appreciated by the customer for unique life “wants” and social needs (Value-in-Use). For the provider, the service value is realized and maximized in the business processes and organizational performance (Value-in-Use). As such, social commerce embodies and hence is a specific case of service value co-creation and exchange in the organizational setting over the social media platform.

## 3.2 Qualities and Dimensions of Social Commerce

In the structural process of social commerce, key actors (i.e., customers and providers) play a dual role as the service co-creator and the designer/user of IT artifacts [44],[134]. Therefore, the investigations of the structural process of social commerce should integrate both streams of service marketing and IS usage literature and extend the S-D and C-D logics specifically into the social commercial context. This represents a unique opportunity for the refinement of

## IS usage theory.

## Social Media Platform: Technology Quality

As a unique type of IS composed of a bundle of IT artifacts, the social commerce platform encompasses external technological characteristics and features. In this study, we conceptualize the collective set of technological features of the service platform as TQ of social commerce. According to the S-D logic, the advances and collaborative properties of social media technologies have made the platform a technical facilitator of CRM for social commerce. It is on and through the intermediate technological appliance that providers connect and communicate with customers, whilst from the C-D logic perspective customers achieve and enjoy the exchange-related activities and experience as they incorporate and co-create services through the platform.

This study integrates major constructs derived from both streams of IS usage and service marketing literature (e.g., [32][33][90][107],[123][135][139] to conceptualize TQ of social commerce along four dimensions such as (1) attractiveness defined as the degree to which customers believe that the social commerce website is visually attractive; (2) reliability as the degree to which customers believe that the operation of the social commerce website is reliable; (3) accessibility as the degree to which customers believe that the social commerce website is accessible; and (4) flexibility as the degree to which customers believe that the social commerce website is flexible to use. These conceptualizations are in line with the C-D logic in that the customer’s perceptions, experience, and expectations for service value play a central role in determining the TQ. Social commerce service providers can invest in and develop with their resources the most compelling social commerce platform, but, finally, it is the customer who decides on the ultimate value of the platforms. Thus, integrating the perspectives of both S-D logic and C-D logics, we develop a unified view that can significantly enhance our insights into the social commerce platform.

## Social Commerce Provider: Service Quality

According to the unified view, the provider proposes and creates service value in terms of translatable benefits and advantages such as self-fulfillment, happiness, convenience, and accomplishment. Specifically, in social commerce, the provider delivers customer value though effective and efficient business processes and organizational performance. This is the story of value co-creation told from both sides of the service provider and customer, which this study conceptualizes as SQ – the extent to which social commerce providers initiate, perform, and facilitate quality exchange-based activities for customers at service encounters. Per the unified view, SQ captures customers’ subjective assessment of their interactions with the service provider and manifests the extent to which customer value expectations have been satisfied in the course [30],[100].

SQ is a well-established concept in both service marketing and IS usage literature (e.g., [12][27][31][32][40][78][79][101][123][138][142][143]). This study unifies the conceptualization from both streams of literature, and characterizes SQ of social commerce along four dimensions such as, (1) service content as the extent to which complete, accurate, and timely service content of social commerce is available to customers; (2) service convenience as customer perceptions of time and effort in relation to the exchange-based activities of social commerce; (3) service delivery as the manner in which social commerce functionalities are accessible to customers; and (4) customer value as customers’ overall assessment of social commerce services “based on a perception of what is received and what is given” [143](p. 14).

## Social Commerce Customer: Experience Quality

According to the unified view, the use of and participation in social commerce is emotional and experiential in nature. In their social commerce activities, customers develop social connections and share information with other customers. In the process, customers build emotional feelings and sensations that are so quite exciting, simultaneous, and live that they are fully immersed in. These observations are rather in line with the C-D logic perspective in that customer experience with service consumption is at the core of service creation and delivery [53],[119]. This study thus conceptualizes customers’ emotional experiential feelings and sensations of such kind with social commerce as EQ –

#

The social commerce customer perceptions related to the fully emotional experience and immersion arising from exchange-based activities of social commerce.

Synthesizing the extant literature of service marketing and IS usage (e.g., [1][19],[71][92][134]), this study views “EQ” as a multi-dimensional construct, and identifies two dimensions of EQ of social commerce such as, (1) emotion defined as customers’ emotional experiential feelings and sensations with exchange-based activities of social commerce, and (2) immersion as customers’ fully engaging experience with exchange-based activities of social commerce.

## Social Commerce Customer: Co-Created Value

According to the unified view, service value of social commerce is co-created in two phases occurring with the application and exchange of specialized knowledge and resources of both customers and providers [22]. At the first phase, the provider proposes service value through the configuration of the social commerce platform in terms of translatable benefits (i.e., dimensions of SQ). At this stage, the social commerce provider plays leading roles in delivering service value through associated business processes and organizational performance. At the second phase, the customer uses the social commerce platform to make transactions, develop social connections, share information, and build fun entertaining experience. At this phase, the user’s autonomous initiatives play an active role creating service value based on their episodic experience with social commerce.

In the social media context, the customer co-created value (CCV) may take a great variety ranging from altruistic, to materialistic, to social, and to extrinsic and intrinsic [109],[150]. For instance, Nambisan and Nambisan [89] developed a conceptual framework suggesting four experience dimensions of co-created value: hedonic, pragmatic, sociability, and usability. Kohler et al. [70] explored co-creation experience and developed three value components (i.e., hedonic, pragmatic, and sociality). Hu et al. [58] developed an aggregate construct of online social value, whereby customers evaluate utilitarian and hedonic benefits of social networking services. Zhang et al. [145] proposed three value dimensions – hedonic, pragmatic, and sociability – serving as the components of co-creation experience in the social media use.

Synthesizing the existing IS studies [22],[65][72][78][79][80][90][99][139],[142] on value co-creation in various contexts, we conceptualize the co-created value of social commerce along three dimensions: (1) utilitarian value defined as the service value that customers use social commerce platform to build in enhancing their transactional performance and goal achievement; (2) hedonic value as the service value that customers use social commerce platform to build for online play, entertainment, and fun; and (3) interactional value as the service value that that customers use social commerce platform to build for online social interactions.

## Social Commerce Interactions: Relationship Quality

According to the unified view, social commerce customers and providers maintain an interactive relationship on the basis of exchange of specialized resources. The customers’ collaborative role in co-creating the service makes them primarily an operant resource for the provider. The provider learns and gains service value from the relationship in terms of customers’ commitment, participation in transactions, recommendations, and trust, which can ultimately be realized and maximized as the improvement in overall corporate performance, marketing extension, and long-term survival opportunities [4],[146]. Thus, this study conceptualizes the interactive relationship of social commerce as RQ – the extent to which the social commerce customer is willing to maintain interactive relationship with the provider in the long term.

Further synthesizing the extant literature of both service marketing and IS usage (e.g., [12],[42][65],[72][78][79][81][90][99][139][142], this study identifies four dimensions of RQ of social commerce such as, (1) commitment defined as the extent to which the social commerce customer believes that an ongoing relationship with the provider is quite important as to warrant maximum efforts at maintaining it; (2) participation as the extent to which the social commerce customer is willing to actively participate in transactional activities with the provider; (3) recommendation as the extent to which the customer is willing to recommend the provider’s goods/services; and (4) trust as the extent to which the customer believes the provider is honest and provides unbiased goods/services. ## 4.1 Research Context

The field survey is employed to assess the proposed research framework. We identified three types of social commerce websites to conduct the survey. The first one is the social networking service websites such as Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn, and YouTube on which exchange-based transactions of social commerce are implemented. The second is the one on which traditional e-commerce websites (e.g., Amazon, eBay, walmart.com, and bestbuy.com) incorporate

social media features to facilitate commercial activities. The third is the group shopping websites (e.g., livingsocial.com, groupon.com, and scoutmob.com) on which customers form groups based on similar interests and needs to perform commercial transactions.

The field survey consists of questions with multi-item scales to capture the respondents’ perceptions and opinions of their buying and/or selling experience on the social commerce websites. The survey provided contextual information to ensure respondents complete it while thinking about the one social commerce website they have used most often in the last six months. Given the survey was conducted at the individual level, demographic data such as age, gender, education, and income level were collected. In addition to the regular goods/service buying and selling, some examples of reported social commerce activities include music buying, car insurance purchasing, and online gaming and education. ## 4.4 Data Analysis

We used the component-based partial least squares (PLS) structural equation modeling (SEM) approach to analyze the field survey data. The SEM-based PLS modeling is quite popular in IS research owing to its minimal restrictions on measurement scales, sample size, and residual distributions [23].

## 4.4.1 Assessing Potential Common Method Bias

Before assessing the measurement model, we conducted detailed tests to examine potential common method bias with the dataset<sup>2</sup>. First, we followed the suggestions by Tourangeau et al. [124] on item construction to avoid comprehension ambiguity, and performed several expert reviews before the survey was distributed. The respondents of the survey were assured that there were no “right” or “wrong” answers in responding to the survey questions; and the online questionnaire was divided to provide temporal separation between the measurements of the first-order reflective constructs.

Second, according to Pavlou et al. [103], the common method bias would exist if the correlations between the firstorder reflective constructs are higher than 0.90. In our study, as Table 5-1 shows, the highest correlation coefficient is 0.49, which suggests that common method bias is not an issue in this study.

Third, following the suggestions of Lindell and Whitney [82] and Malhotra et al. [85], we conducted the post hoc marker variable test using the second-smallest positive correlation among the first-order reflective constructs as a proxy for the estimation of common method variance. Using the second-smallest positive correlation, 0.16, between Interactional Value and Recommendation in Table 5-1, a new common method variance (CMV)-adjusted correlation matrix for the constructs was developed. In the new matrix, all correlations remained significant at the significance level, $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ . Moreover, the differences between the original and CMV-adjusted correlations were all lower than 0.05 and insignificant. We thus concluded that the common method bias did not significantly affect the results of the study.

## 4.4.2 Assessing Reflective Measurement Model

Before assessing the reflective measurement model, we followed the guidelines of Gefen et al.[43] to test the goodness-of-fit (GoF) of the model. We relied on the commonly used GoF indices In the estimation: the chi-square normalized by degrees of freedom $( \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d . f . } )$ , goodness-of-fit index (GFI), comparative fit index (CFI), adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI), root mean square residual (RMR), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). A measurement model is deemed appropriate when its χ<sup>2</sup>/d.f. < 3 [13][18]; GFI, AGFI, and $\mathrm { C F I } > 0 . 9 0 \ddagger$ and RMR and RMSEA fall between 0.05 and 0.08 [6]. Our analysis shows that all the GoF indices of the measurement model were acceptable at the significance level, p < 0.01, χ<sup>2</sup>/d.f. = 2.25, GFI = 0.92, AGFI = 0.95, CFI = 0.99, RMR = 0.06, and RMSEA = 0.07.

To assess psychometric properties of the first-order reflective dimension constructs, we first assessed the content validity of the reflective constructs through the extensive literature review of the previously validated scales, the pilot test of measurement scales, and the series of discussions with IS researchers and social commerce customers.

Second, we performed a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using the SEM-based PLS approach to validate convergent and discriminant validity of the reflective measures of the constructs [23][93]. As shown in Table 5-1, Table 5-2, and Appendix, respectively, we reported the CFA results including the values of composite reliability (CR), the square root of average variance extracted (AVE), and factor loadings, with t-values reported in the Appendix of the first-order reflective dimension constructs at the significance level, $\mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 1$ . Table 5-1 reports the value of CR and AVE of the first-order reflective dimension constructs. The estimates show that each of the dimension construct is sufficiently different from others $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . For each of the reflective dimension constructs, $\mathrm { C R } > = 0 . 7 6$ and the square root of $\mathrm { A V E } > = 0 . 7 3$ , suggesting the construct reliability exceeds the threshold criterion of $\mathrm { C R } > = 0 . 7 0 $ , and the square root of $\mathrm { A V E } > = 0 . 7 0 ~ ( p < 0 . 0 0 1 ) [ 4 1 ] [ 9 3 ]$ . Additionally, the square roots of all AVEs are above 0.70 in Table 5-1 $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , higher than all cross-correlations. Furthermore, the CFA tests the convergent validity and discriminant validity of the reflective dimension constructs. ## 4.4.3 Assessing Second-Order Formative Constructs

We employed the SEM-based approach to assess the second-order formative constructs focusing on multicollinearity among measures, and issues regarding the content, construct, and nomological validity. We reported the analysis results following the guidelines outlined in Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer [35], Jarvis et al. [64], and Petter et al.[107]. First, for this study, the content validity of the principal formative constructs was adequately assessed through the extensive literature review of the previously validated scales, the pilot tests of the measurement scales, and the series of discussions with IS researchers and social commerce customers.

Second, we examined the bivariate correlations among the first-order-reflective sub-construct dimension indicators of each second-order formative construct (i.e., TQ, SQ, EQ, CCV, and RQ). We found that the proposed sub-construct dimension indicators are significantly related to the hypothesized second-order formative constructs, respectively; the bivariate correlations among them range from 0.41 to 0.57, which are lower than the threshold of 0.64[20], and the Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) range from 1.37 to 3.13, which are lower than the threshold of 3.33[35]. The estimates indicate no multicollinearity with the formative model. We thus concluded that there were no significant conceptual overlaps among the sub-construct dimension indicators, and each of the dimensions contributes to a specific unique facet of a particular second-order formative construct of social commerce qualities.

Third, we assessed the construct validity of the formative constructs by examining item weights of the sub-construct dimension indicators. As shown in Figure 2, the weight coefficients of all the first-order sub-construct indicators are significant on the hypothesized formative second-order constructs, respectively $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , suggesting that all the first-order reflective sub-constructs capture various dimensions of the second-order formative constructs, and the proposed dimensions contribute to the content domain of these formative constructs, respectively.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1 Findings

Based on both S-D and C-D logic perspectives and integrating streams of IS usage and service marketing literature, this study proposed a unified framework outlining key qualities and dimensions of social commerce and their interactions to describe the structural process of social commerce. The hypothesized model was tested with a large set of field survey data of social commerce customers. The findings address the research questions raised at the beginning of the paper.

First, the results show that the weights of the proposed dimensions are significant, respectively, on the hypothesized qualities (i.e., TQ, SQ, EQ, CCV, and RQ) and suggest that each of the dimensions captures a unique prominent aspect

#

of social commerce. That is, TQ of social commerce can be defined and characterized along four dimensions – accessibility, attractiveness, flexibility, and reliability; SQ along four dimensions – service content, convenience, delivery, and customer value; EQ along two dimensions – emotion and immersion; CCV along three dimensions – utilitarian, hedonic, and interactional value; and RQ along four dimensions – commitment, participation, recommendation, and trust.

Second, this study demonstrates that the multi-dimensions at the lower level for each of social commerce qualities at the higher level are conceptually and empirically interdependent and interrelated and, as a group, jointly determine one underlying nature/quality of social commerce. Moreover, according to the construction notion of formative constructs, because the dimensions are conceptually and empirically distinct from each other, the interchangeability of the indicators for each quality is not expected, suggesting that dropping any of the dimensions would change the overall domain of social commerce qualities.

Third, the proposed model paints a holistic picture of the structural process of social commerce, indicating that (1) the technological designs, features, and characteristics of social commerce (TQ) significantly influence customer’s immersing emotional episodic experience (EQ) of social commerce consumption, and enhance customers’ interactive relationships (RQ) with service providers and customers in terms of service commitment, participation, recommendation, and trust; (2) SQ with a wide variety of measures such as service content, convenience, delivery, and customer value plays a significant role in enhancing customer EQ and RQ; (3) Customers’ episodic experience enhances their value co-creation behaviors in transactional performance, playful fun pursuing, and social interactions; and, (4) CCV has significant impact in shaping RQ with service providers and customers. Overall, the effects of both SQ and CCV are greater than those of TQ on EQ and RQ, respectively, suggesting that SQ and CCV are more salient in shaping the structural process and interactions of social commerce.

## 5.2 Theoretical Implications

The study reported here responds to the research calls and provides a unified, theory-driven, empirically validated framework and a comprehensive understanding of key qualities and dimensions of social commerce and their interactions describing the structural process of social commerce. Our study holds important implications for IS research in general and social commerce innovations.

Firstly, to our best knowledge, this study represents the very first attempt in IS research adopting and empirically assessing a unified view of both S-D logic and C-D logic perspectives in the social commerce context. The unified framework identifies and validates key constructs, variables, and relationships of social commerce surrounding social exchange and application of specialized resources and competence, customer episodic experience, and co-creation and delivery of social commerce services. The contextualized perspective helps frame the important questions and agendas of social commerce research such as how the social media platform is technically co-produced and implemented along with what dimensions (TQ), how key social commerce actors (i.e., customers, providers, and IT artifacts) are involved socially producing what experiential feelings and sensations for customer participation (EQ) to co-create value for benefit needs of customers and providers (SQ and CCV), and how the elements of the service process (e.g., SQ) impact customer episodic experience of service consumption and value co-creation behaviors in what aspects (i.e., EQ, CCV, and RQ).

Investigations of these issues from the unified perspective in the ever-ubiquitous context of social commerce should facilitate deeper insights into the phenomena of interest [59][84]. The findings shall help re-evaluate key social commerce phenomena and elaborate the cumulative tradition of IS usage research in general. Specifically, the unified view affords us with the shift in focus from the visible service and its interface, atmosphere, and environment to the invisible reality of the customer and customer activities that incorporate intimately and integrally the services through social commerce platforms [54]. Although the customer would physically be alone in the customer experience, the experience is always influenced by the customer’s internal and external context and his/her life experiences. Value is extended beyond the individual and his/her subjectivity recognizing value to be co-created as part of a collective social context [38].

#

Secondly, our study developed a formative model that conceptualizes social commerce qualities as a set of secondorder formative constructs with reflective first-order multi-dimensions. The model demonstrates strong content, construct, and nomological validity. For IS research, the formative model enhances the theoretical parsimony and representativeness and assumes greater explanatory power over the reflective alternatives [36], and should benefit empirical investigations of social commerce in an aggregate manner. While the multi-dimensions and qualities of social commerce were discussed disjointedly in prior studies [146], very few have systematically synthesized them together. Our study has the set of constructs and relationships conceptualized, operationalized, and validated in a single nomological network. The interactive components encompassed can be further conceptualized as measurable variables, and the model can serve as an overarching foundational framework to be contextualized for investigating the emerging phenomena of social commerce. This shall help IS researchers develop a full domain and context-specific measures of social commerce while minimizing measurement errors and model misspecification [64]. We hope that the framework can contribute to the evolving findings about the relationships of social commerce qualities and dimensions.

## 5.3 Practical Implications

The findings of this study hold important managerial implications for justifying social commerce initiatives and opportunities, especially for gaining more definitive insights on the overall interactions of social commerce technologies, service providers, and customers. The well-developed framework and its validation offer a comprehensive set of analytical metrics/tactics to facilitate mangers to conduct benchmark investigations of the interactive dimensions and qualities of social commerce. The findings articulate an interesting customer-centric perspective, which highlights the critical role of customers as a primary corporate resource in the co-creation, delivery, and conduct of social commerce. The findings therefore establish a solid base for social commerce managers, designers, and providers to manage customers and achieve the long-term marketing strategies and customer relationships.

Furthermore, the unified view extends the notion from corporate-centric conceptualization to customer-centric conceptualization, thus allowing managers to consider not only corporate perspectives but also customer perspectives. That is, placing customers and their value behaviors and activities as central to conceptualizing customer value cocreation process and then applying both S-D and C-D logics to design, provide, and deliver compelling services that create value first for the customer and then for the provider. Recently, Apple decided to provide an affordable battery replacement option for older iPhones that are slowing down because of operating system upgrade. Initially, using S-D logic, where device performance was a central concern, Apple missed the entire reason as to why customers buy iPhones in the first place to obtain the bundle of service that creates value for the customer using iPhone or similar devices. Service bundles create value, not just the device. Slowing down the device to satisfy Apple’s perspective on value is not in line with customers’ perspective of value. Applying C-D logic clarifies customer value as central and not that of the provider. This does not mean that provider value is not important – all we contend in this research is that both need to be considered simultaneously for better service design, delivery, and consumption and for better value creation in a collaborative way.

As such, this study delineates EQ, CCV, and RQ as the most prominent dependent variables, of which EQ and CCV also serve as the mediator facilitating the relationships of TQ and SQ on RQ. The findings provide actionable mechanisms for social commerce practitioners to improve high priority on EQ and CCV at the customer side and RQ for better interactions between the service provider and customer, such as (1) establishing long-term interactive relationships (RQ) with customers through engaging customers’ episodic service experience (EQ) and their value cocreation motivations (CCV); (2) enhancing service quality in various aspects (SQ); (3) extensively exploiting the technological design features of the social media platform (TQ) to stimulate the technical innovations of social commerce for the social CRM (RQ); (4) orchestrating the attractive measures of business models (SQ) to build the value-added experience (EQ) and behaviors (CCV) and sustain desirable customer relationships (RQ); and (5) monitoring and responding to customer social media experience (EQ) to encourage value co-creation (CCV) for customer retention and marketing extension

Additionally, the findings reveal design implications in and for the ubiquitous social networking environment where

social commerce recommendation systems are built as one of the most important components. In these regards, our recommendations include, (1) configuring the effectiveness of the social media platform (TQ) so that service providers and customers can take full advantage of the SQ for exchange of resources and experience (EQ) and value co-creation (CCV); (2) cultivating a transparent service innovation ecosystem that comprises multi-dimensions and qualities of social commerce, so that both service providers and customers are effectively engaged to collaborate and co-create service value.

## 5.4 Limitations and Future Research

This study has limitations in some respects, which should be addressed in future research. Firstly, we drew upon the theoretical and empirical findings from both streams of IS usage and service marketing literature to develop a holistic typology of social commerce dimensions and qualities. Although this is consistent with the guidelines for developing formative and reflective constructs, a deeper understanding of the multi-dimensional nature of social commerce may benefit for further theoretical and literature refinement. This shall ensure that the full formative domain of the associated constructs is covered. For example, the salience and meaningfulness of incorporating customers’ concerns about online security and privacy into SQ and EQ may be considered in future research [4][90]. With this regard, we urge researchers to consult theories and literature to achieve an integrated understanding of social commerce qualities and dimensions.

Secondly, this study develops and operationalizes the relationships of social commerce solely in a one-way direction (i.e., TQ to EQ and RQ; SQ to EQ and RQ; and CCV to RQ). The structure of the nomological network is consistent with the IS research practice for the purpose of model parsimony. Future research should fully recognize the two-way multi-directional nature of social commerce interactions [44][8]. To the end, the structuration theory of technology [34][96] can serve as the theoretical foundation to inform the development and operationalization of the refined framework of social commerce interactions [84].

Thirdly, there is the possibility of lack of generalizability, as the surveyed sample is a large group of young people (92.2%), and more than 90% of the respondents have at least some college experience. The demographics of the samples are very familiar with social commerce and have a higher technology readiness. Prior studies indicated that this group of population is more open to the kind of innovations found in the e-commerce environment and is the first to adopt such innovations [67]. This stream of studies, however, can be enhanced by including more demographic variables to validate the segmentation regarding perceptions of interactive qualities and dimensions of social commerce.

## Conclusion

IS is embodied in the way that users collaborate and create value with information and relationships. The wide popularity and rapid growth of social commerce have opened a new arena for business and grand opportunities for IS research. However, there is only a limited theoretical understanding of and even less substantive empirical grounding on how social media technologies, service providers, and customers interact to achieve social commerce initiatives and opportunities. In this study, we integrated the S-D logic and C-D logic perspectives and both streams of service marketing and IS usage literature and proposed a unified framework outlining key qualities and dimensions of social commerce and their interactions. We tested the framework with a large set of field survey data of 1,250 social commerce customers. The results show that each of the proposed dimensions captures a unique prominent aspect of social commerce; the multi-dimensions combine to define the underlying nature of social commerce. Furthermore, the testing of the model validated the hypothesized interactive relationships of key qualities in shaping the structural process of social commerce. The findings contribute to IS research in general and social commerce innovations, and provide managerial implications for understanding the overall interactions of social commerce. We believe, as the most significant impact and implications of social media are yet to come, research in social commerce helps us understand and re-evaluate the sociotechnical context of IS value creation.
