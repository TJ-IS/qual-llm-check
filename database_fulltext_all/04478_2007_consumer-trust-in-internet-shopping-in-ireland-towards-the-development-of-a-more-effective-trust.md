# Consumer trust in Internet shopping in Ireland: towards the development of a more effective trust measurement instrument

## Abstract

Trust is a complex phenomenon that pervades human relations. It is essential for the success of business-to-consumer electronic commerce, where many of the tools that can be used in its absence (contracts, advance payments, insurance, etc.) may not be available. However, research as to how consumer trust can be built in an online environment is limited and varies considerably in terms of the dimensions of the problem that are examined. Consequently, much of our understanding of the antecedents of trust in online shopping context remains fragmented. This study uses a previously validated measurement instrument to investigate, in an Irish context, the existence and importance of specific perceptions and factors that are thought to predict the generation of consumer trust in Internet shopping. The research results provide evidence that Irish consumers’ perception of vendor trustworthiness is the result of specific factors that it is possible for vendors to manage. A modified model that addresses the key dimensions of consumer trust in Internet shopping in Ireland is proposed.

## Introduction

Background: the role and importance of trust

rust, its causes, nature and consequences, is recognised as a significant concept by academics from across a wide spectrum of intellectual disciplines. Sociologists (Gambetta, 1988), psychologists (Deutsch, 1962), organisational behaviour scientists (Sitkin and Roth, 1993; Mayer et al., 1995; Kramer and Tyler, 1996; Mishra, 1996; Kramer, 1999), as well as economists (Williamson, 1974; Zucker, 1986), anthropologists (Ekeh, 1974) and political scientists (Barber, 1983) have contributed to the wide body of work that exists on this topic. In the information systems (IS) field, researchers have shown an increasing awareness of how trust contributes towards the success of many types of virtual environments (e.g. Lee and Turban, 2001; Belanger et al., 2002; Koufaris and Hampton-Sosa, 2002; Shankar et al., 2002; Corritore et al., 2003; Gefen and Straub, 2003; Huang et al., 2003; Gefen et al., 2003a; Briggs et al., 2004; Sillence et al., 2004; Cyr et al., 2005). It is considered to have critical importance for the success of online consumer purchasing (Jarvenpaa and Tractinsky, 1999; Reichheld and Schefter, 2000).

Although IS researchers’ interest in this topic has increased, to date studies on the trust construct provide a limited view of the phenomenon. In part, this stems from the conceptual and operational confusion that surrounds the construct. For example, Grabner-Krauter and Kaluscha (2003) observe that research in this area is limited by differing conceptualisations of the construct and a blurring of the distinction between trust and its antecedents. Moreover, there is no consensus as to how the trust construct should be operationalised (Bhattacherjee, 2002). Compounding the problem is the fact that many of the scales used in trust studies in an online context are neither theoretically grounded nor authenticated. This confusion has manifested in studies that examine isolated dimensions of the construct such as privacy (Hoffman et al., 1999), structural assurances (Belanger et al., 2002) or specific technical antecedents such as those proposed in the Technology Acceptance Model (in which Davis (1989) proposes that intention to use a technology is predicted by beliefs regarding the perceived ease of use and the perceived usefulness of the technology, see Pavlou (2003)). Moreover, the unique context of some studies, such as online banking (e.g. Tan and Teo, 2000), means that the insights provided by these studies are frequently not generalisable to consumer trust in online shopping.

Practitioners also recognise the importance that consumers accord to being able to trust online vendors. A Cap Gemini Ernst & Young (2000) study of over 6000 consumers in nine European countries revealed that online consumers rank being able to trust the online vendor as more important than low prices. The results showed that online shoppers also value honesty, respect and reliability from online retailers. Interestingly, online consumers in the United States rated these same factors as among the top five factors that they valued. Nonetheless, a Consumer Web-Watch study (2002) of 1500 Internet users in the United States found that online shoppers continue to have little trust in electronic-commerce (e-commerce) sites. It revealed that only 29% of the respondents trusted websites that sell products and services, while 64% of the respondents had little or no trust in online vendors.

Web vendors’ concern about consumers’ lack of trust in online retailing has an obvious commercial motivation. The consumer online market has huge potential. According to an ActiveMedia research survey (Hermes, 2000), online sales for the business-to-consumer e-commerce market will be worth in the order of \$1.1 trillion by 2010 (although forecasts of this type should always be treated with some scepticism). Online vendors are aware that the websites that consumers trust are among the most successful. For example, a recent study (Ponemon Institute and TRUSTe study, 2004) of 6300 online consumers in the United States found that the 10 most trusted companies were: online auction company eBay, American Express, Procter & Gamble, Amazon.com, Hewlett-Packard, the United States Postal Service, IBM, EarthLink, Citibank and Dell. The fact that consumers view these companies as trustworthy has contributed in no small measure to their success.

Although there has not been direct research into trust and online shopping in Ireland, a study by Ama´rach Consulting (2002) indicates that the number of Irish Internet users buying online, having risen each quarter for several years, fell for the first time in that year. Further studies in 2003 found that although nearly half of the Irish population had Internet access, only 38% of Irish Internet users had made an online purchase and the majority of those were in the 35–49 age category and based in Dublin. It appears that, although there has been a significant increase in the number of Irish Internet users over the past decade, only a modest fraction of those users have made purchases over the Internet. It is conceivable that the reason for consumers’ hesitancy to purchase online is related to their lack of trust in online vendors.

## Objectives of this research

The objectives of this research were first to examine critically the model of the antecedents of trusting behaviour (such as perceptions of vendor trustworthiness) proposed by Cheung and Lee (2000a). In their paper, they propose 13 hypotheses (reproduced in Appendix A), which they test, arriving at a quite complex model of trust behaviour in Internet shopping. Cheung and Lee acknowledged the homogeneity of the underlying population from which their sample was drawn and suggested that their model needed to be examined in other cultures and different social and economic groups. Based on a wide ranging review of the literature, it was conjectured that a detailed study of the dimensions of consumer trust in Ireland, that is, of the antecedents of trust tested by Cheung and Lee, using Cheung and Lee’s own instrument, would suggest that a number of these 13 hypotheses are either not generalisable or can be consolidated into a single dimension. If this could be shown, a more parsimonious model could be developed which, apart from being more elegant and robust, would also provide vendors with clearer and more effective guidelines on how to build consumer trust.

A second objective was to examine whether patterns of trust behaviour exhibited by people working in the information and communications technology (ICT) industry were different from their peers who, while business and computer literate, were working in non-ICT specific jobs. To test this, a form of stratification was used with two different frames. The first frame comprised people with a high level of ICT technical knowledge. For convenience, this group is referred to as the ‘technical’ sample. The second frame comprised people from the wider graduate business community. This is referred to as the ‘non-technical sample. This distinction is not intended to imply that the latter group are not familiar with computers and the web, rather that members of the former group will, due to the nature of their qualifications and work, have a much greater knowledge of the mechanics of important technical factors and, in particular, of computer security and the risks involved in online shopping. To increase the likelihood that respondents to both samples, but particularly the ‘nontechnical’ sample, would have experience of online shopping, it was decided to impose upper age limits on respondents in both of the sampling frame (see below).

## Structure of this paper

This paper is organised as follows. First, the literature on the antecedents of trust is reviewed. This literature is summarised under a number of broad headings and the current state of knowledge is assessed. Next, the measurement instrument used in the research is briefly described including Cheung and Lee’s conceptual model. In the fourth section the research methodology is set out and in the fifth section the principal findings are presented. The sixth section contains a discussion of the findings and proposes an improved and more parsimonious model, which is then discussed in the next section. The last section concludes the paper with a discussion of the limitations of the research and suggests further lines of enquiry.

## Trust antecedents identified in the literature

It has been demonstrated that trusting beliefs positively influence online consumers’ purchase intentions (Jarvenpaa et al., 2000; Lim et al., 2001; McKnight et al., 2002; Verhagen et al., 2004, 2006). Consequently, understanding the factors that influence the generation of trusting beliefs in an online environment is of considerable interest to researchers (e.g. Lee and Turban, 2001; Koufaris and Hampton-Sosa, 2002; McKnight et al., 2002; Shankar et al., 2002; Chen and Dhillon, 2003; Corritore et al., 2003; Gefen and Straub, 2003; Gefen et al., 2003a; Briggs et al., 2004; Wang and Emurian, 2005). The literature provides considerable evidence that a number of factors have strong predictive importance and are therefore deserving of consideration in any examination of the construct. These factors include the characteristics of the online vendor, situational factors, third-party certification, the individual’s propensity to trust and the influence of perceived risk. These factors and their inter-relationships are now discussed in more detail.

## Characteristics of the vendor

Researchers who consider trust to be a dependent variable posit that a perception of trustworthiness results from the perception of a number of characteristics (Barber, 1983; Covello, 1992; Lee and Turban, 2001). Many attempts have been made to identify these characteristics (e.g. Strickland, 1958; Gabarro, 1978; Butler and Cantrell, 1984; Butler, 1991; Mayer et al., 1995; Peters et al., 1997). However, as of yet, there is no agreement in the literature as to which or how many characteristics influence the generation of a trust response. Despite this fact, certain factors repeatedly surface – albeit under differing titles. For example, Lieberman (1981) contends that trust in fiduciary relationships is based on beliefs that derive from two characteristics – the trustee’s perceived competence (i.e. ability) and perceived integrity. For Barber (1983) and Covello (1992), perceptions of knowledge and expertise, openness and honesty, and concern and care constitute the key perceptions influencing the formation of trusting beliefs. Later research by Sitkin (1995) emphasises competency, benevolence and value-based trust. Mishra (1996) maintains that trust is the result of attributes defined as competence, concern, openness and reliability. A study of the determinants of trust and credibility in the area of risk communication (Peters et al., 1997) indicates that perceptions of trust and credibility are dependent on three factors: perceptions of knowledge and expertise; perceptions of openness and honesty; and perceptions of concern and care. These three factors correspond closely with the characteristics of ability, integrity and benevolence (Mayer et al., 1995).

Researchers who study trust in an online environment consider that these characteristics apply equally to web vendors and thus influence the formation of online consumers’ trust beliefs. For example, Chen and Dhillon’s (2003) extensive review of the trust literature identifies competence, integrity and benevolence as key dimensions of trust in an Internet vendor. Similarly, Bhattacherjee’s (2002: 219) in-depth review of the literature concludes that ability, integrity and benevolence are conceptually distinct and indicate ‘different elements of cognitive and affective abstractions of trust.’Support for this view is also provided by McKnight et al. (1998, 2002) who define trusting beliefs in terms of the customer’s perception of the vendor’s competence, benevolence and integrity, and by Lee and Turban (2001: 77) who maintain that ‘three attributes arguably constitute the main elements of trustworthiness: ability, benevolence and integrity.’

In summary, the literature indicates that the three characteristics that most influence a perception of vendor trustworthiness are competence (ability), benevolence and integrity. While each characteristic is important, if an individual is perceived to have all three characteristics, they are likely to be perceived as highly trustworthy (Mayer et al., 1995). (1995: 718) ability is ‘that group of skills, competencies, and characteristics that enable a party to have influence within some specific domain.’ In an online purchase, the technical characteristics of the website that convey competence include website design, ease of use, the reliability of the website, the speed of the transaction, the delivery of the correct product within the agreed time frame (i.e. fulfilment of the transaction) and the presence of security features (Cheskin Research, 2000; Tamimi et al., 2000; Belanger et al., 2002; Cho and Park, 2002; Madu and Madu, 2002).

The second characteristic, benevolence, is widely recognised as influencing trustworthiness (Strickland, 1958; Larzelere and Huston, 1980; Barber, 1983; Ganesan, 1994; Doney and Cannon, 1997; Lee and Turban, 2001; Chen and Dhillon, 2003; McKnight et al., 2002). Benevolence implies a perception of positive intent and good motives. Chen and Dhillon (2003) describe benevolence as the ability of a company to hold consumer interests ahead of its own selfinterest and the indication of sincere concern for the welfare of the customers, while Lee and Turban (2001: 78) define benevolence as ‘the extent to which the trusting party believes that the trusted party wants to do good things rather than just maximise profit.’ Mayer et al. (1995: 718) also consider a perception of benevolence to be a key indicator of trustworthiness, and define it as ‘the extent to which a trustee is believed to want to do good to the trustor, aside from an egocentric profit motive.’

Authors such as Johnson and Grayson (2005) suggest that benevolence encompasses the reputation of the vendor and argue that a firm’s reputation for benevolent behaviour can elicit an emotional bond of trust in the customer. This bond of trust allows the psychological barrier of risk created by electronic transactions to be overcome (Di Biaggi, 2003). Support for the influence of firm reputation on consumer trust beliefs is provided by Jarvenpaa et al. (2000), who describe reputation as the extent to which buyers believe that a selling organisation is honest and concerned about its customers. Studies by other researchers (e.g. Wetsch and Cunningham, 2000; McKnight and Chervany, 2001) also indicate a positive relationship between reputation and trusting beliefs.

The third characteristic, integrity, has also been discussed as an antecedent to trust by multiple researchers (e.g. Lieberman, 1981; Butler and Cantrell, 1984; Butler, 1991; Lee and Turban, 2001; Bhattacherjee, 2002; McKnight et al., 2002; Chen and Dhillon, 2003). For example, early research (Gabarro, 1978) indicates one of the determinants of trust to be ‘character’, of which integrity is a key component. Butler and Cantrell (1984) created a model that identifies the five characteristics of trust as: integrity, competence, consistency, loyalty and openness. Later research by Butler (1991) suggests further characteristics associated with trustworthiness: consistency, discreteness, fairness, integrity and promise fulfilment.

Chen and Dhillon (2003) define integrity as acting in a consistent, reliable and honest manner, while Lee and Turban (2001: 78) describe integrity as ‘the trusting party’s perception that the trusted party will be honest and adhere to an acceptable set of principles’. The latter note that in the literature the term ‘reputation’ sometimes encompasses competence and integrity (e.g. Doney and Cannon, 1997) and thus suggest that the three trustworthiness attributes include the concept of reputation. This view is supported by Jarvenpaa et al. (2000), who describe reputation as the extent to which buyers believe that a selling organisation is honest and concerned about its customers. Support is also provided by studies that indicate a positive relationship between reputation and trusting beliefs (e.g. Jarvenpaa et al., 2000; Wetsch and Cunningham, 2000; McKnight and Chervany, 2001).

## Situational factors

Researchers (Ring and Van de Ven, 1992; Lewicki and Bunker, 1995; Sitkin, 1995; Bigley and Pearce, 1998; Hagen and Choe, 1998) acknowledge the causal role of situational factors in the formation of trust beliefs. They suggest that institutional factors can encourage risk taking and consequent trusting behaviour. Thus, Zucker (1986) stresses the need for institutional arrangements, social structures, processes and norms, which have been termed the ‘guardians of trust’ (Shapiro, 1987: 635). These structures function as contextual cues and, by making a transactional environment appear normal, they engender trusting beliefs. Structural assurance beliefs, as defined by Shapiro (1987: 204), signify structural protections or governance mechanisms. Collectively they facilitate the formation of trusting beliefs and risk-taking behaviour. Furthermore, at a societal level, these guarantees, safety nets and other supporting structures (Shapiro, 1987) are evident through legal measures that protect the individual’s rights and property (Fukuyama, 1995) and thus facilitate trust (Zucker, 1986).

There is evidence to suggest that cultural differences influence the formation of consumer trust beliefs (Hagen and Choe, 1998) and the degree to which consumers value institutional factors. For example, e-commerce researchers such as Shankar et al. (2002) note that studies of online trust across international contexts such as Cheskin Research (2000) have shown significant differences between online consumers in different countries in terms of their confidence in the ability of their governments to control identity and other forms of risk associated with online shopping.

## Third-party certification

In an online purchase context, third-party certification bodies are also considered to influence the consumer’s trust response. For example, McKnight and Chervany (2001) and Hoffman et al. (1999) argue that some third-party seals have the ability to influence the formation of trusting beliefs. Research by Jarvenpaa and Grazioli (1999) emphasises the importance of a good reputation and endorsements from independent third parties in influencing the formation of consumer trust beliefs. However, Kim and Benbasat (2003) extend a note of caution pointing out that, although studies by Kovar et al. (2000a, b), Cheskin Research (2000) and Cheskin Research Studio Archetype Sapient (1999) indicate the logos of security guaranteeing firms to have a positive effect on trusting beliefs and intentions, these results are influenced by consumers’ familiarity with these seals and the amount of attention they pay to them.

## Propensity to trust

Chopra and Wallace (2003) note that a frequently proposed definition of trust is as a personality characteristic of the individual that influences that person’s interactions with the world at large. However, the literature indicates a lack of consensus as to whether, and to what degree, personal dispositional characteristics such as the individual’s propensity to trust influence his or her trust response. For example, personality-based trust researchers posit that trust is a personality construct developed during childhood as a child asks for and receives help from a benevolent caregiver ( Rotter, 1967, 1971, 1980; Erikson, 1968; Bowlby, 1982). As a consequence of these early-life experiences, the child develops a stable tendency to trust in a broad range of situations (Rotter, 1967). These researchers suggest that it is this dispositional propensity to trust that determines the amount and level of trust that a person has for another party in the absence of available or experiential information on which to base a judgment (Rotter, 1971, 1980; Hofstede, 1980). Organisational psychologists, on the other hand, tend to view the propensity to trust characteristic with caution and show little interest in exploring the effect of dispositional trust on the individual’s trust response (Kramer, 1999). They consider that situational factors exert a greater influence on the trust response than does the individual’s tendency to trust (Burt and Knez, 1995; Mishra, 1996; Wicks et al., 1999).

This raises the question as to whether an examination of consumer trust in online shopping should include a measure of dispositional trust as a control variable. While the view of trust as purely a psychological attribute has been criticised as reductionist as it fails to consider the social context in which the trust takes place (Chopra and Wallace, 2003); nonetheless, the effect of the propensity to trust characteristic on the individual’s trust response should not be disregarded as there is sufficient evidence to suggest that individuals differ greatly in their tendency to trust others (Gurtman 1992; Ambrose and Johnson, 1998; Gefen, 2000; Kim and Prabhakar, 2000; Lee and Turban, 2001) and the individual’s propensity to trust is frequently proposed as one of several key determinants of trust in an electronic channel (e.g. McKnight et al., 1998; Egger and de Groot, 2000; Lee and Turban, 2001; McKnight and Chervany, 2001; Gefen, 2000; Kim and Prabhakar, 2004).

## Perceived risk

The literature indicates that the individual’s need to trust is directly related to the risk inherent in a given situation (Mayer et al., 1995; Rousseau et al., 1998; Snijders and Keren, 1999; Hardin, 2001; Grabner-Krauter and Kaluscha, 2003). The concept of risk involves both uncertainty (Lewis and Weigert, 1985) and vulnerability (Barney and Hansen, 1994). In the business-to-consumer e-commerce environment, the degree of risk is greater than in the traditional commercial environment (Grabner-Krauter and Kaluscha, 2003). Consequently, the need for trust is greater. For example, purchasing on the Internet holds risks that are unique to that context. These include the temporal difference between the exchange of money and receipt of the purchase, conflicting marketing messages, the requirement to provide sensitive information and the uncertainty of what the retailer will do with the consumer’s personal information (Grabner-Krauter and Kaluscha, 2003). In fact, it has been shown that awareness of their lack of control over personal data can lead to consumers withholding information from companies and resisting the adoption of online purchasing (Goldsmith and Bridges 2000). Owing to this lack of control and uncertainty, many consumers do not trust web providers enough to engage in relationship exchanges with them (Hoffman et al., 1999).

It has been argued (Verhagen et al., 2004, 2006) that purchasing in an online environment is subject to two forms of risk, institutional risk and party risk. The former relates to the failure of institutional mechanisms employed by the intermediary and the latter relates to the relational risks resulting from the trading partner, that is, the uncertainties that arise because online sellers can behave opportunistically (Pavlou, 2003; Verhagen et al., 2004, 2006). However, research on perceived risk in online environments tends to treat it as a unidimensional construct (e.g. Jarvenpaa et al., 2000; Van der Heijden et al., 2003; Pavlou and Gefen, 2004) despite the fact that a large body of literature indicates it to be a complex, multidimensional construct (Gefen et al., 2003a). Moreover, there does not appear to be any agreement in the literature as to how the relationship between trust and risk should be articulated, for example, whether risk is an antecedent to trust or a consequent to trust (Gefen et al., 2003b). These problems stem in part from the fact that research into both constructs and their relationship in an online purchase situation is still in a developmental stage and further research is required to progress our understanding.

Nonetheless, the importance of these constructs and their impact on the online consumer’s behaviour is indicated in the literature. For example, Jarvenpaa et al. (2000) found that both trust and perceived risk influence individuals’ willingness to purchase online, while Van der Heijden et al. (2003) found that perceived risk strongly influences intention to purchase online and that trust influences attitude indirectly through its influence on perceived risk. Recent empirical work by Verhagen et al. (2006) confirms that both trust and risk influence consumers’ attitudes towards online purchasing, thus providing support for the findings of Jarvenpaa et al. (2000). The work of other researchers (Egger and de Groot, 2000; Cheung and Lee, 2000a; Pavlou, 2003; Pavlou and Gefen, 2004) also draws attention to the relationship between perceived risk and consumer trust in an online purchase environment.

In summary, the literature indicates that a number of factors have strong predictive importance in relation to consumer trust in online shopping. Consequently, it is essential that the influence of these factors – the characteristics of the online vendor, situational factors, third-party certification, the individual’s propensity to trust and the influence of perceived risk on the consumer’s trust response – is examined in this study of consumer trust in online shopping in Ireland. In choosing a model to conduct the study, it was therefore of particular importance that each of these constructs would be adequately represented.

A comprehensive review of all-trust studies and all-trust models was undertaken. A number of models were found to address some of the issues of concern in this study (e.g. Kini and Choobineh, 1998; Tan and Thoen, 2000–2001; Egger and de Groot, 2000). However, the model proposed by Cheung and Lee (2000a) was deemed to be the most suitable for the purposes of this study as it captures the most significant set of trust antecedents, derived from different lines of previous research, and presents them as an integrated entity that can provide direction for empirical testing. It also tests them in the narrowly focused way necessary for this research. For example, the measurement instrument contains 30 items measuring trust antecedents such as perceived security controls (Gauzente, 2004), perceived privacy controls (Belanger et al., 2002), the vendors perceived integrity (Chen and Dhillon, 2003), the vendors perceived competence (McKnight et al., 2002), personality (Kim and Prabhakar, 2004), cultural environment (Hagen and Choe, 1998; Shankar et al., 2002), experience (Kong and Hung, 2006), third-party recognition (McKnight and Chervany, 2001), legal framework, (Fukuyama, 1995; Bigley and Pearce, 1998) and perceived risk (Jarvenpaa et al., 2000; Van der Heijden et al., 2003; Verhagen et al., 2006).

Cheung and Lee’s measurement instrument has been widely published (2000a, b; 2001; 2002; 2003) and cited by authors in international peer-reviewed journals such as the Journal of Global Information Management (Corner et al., 2005), the Journal of E-Commerce in Organisations (Cheung et al., 2005), Electronic Markets (Wingreen and Baglione, 2005), the International Journal of Management (Parnell et al., 2003) and the International Journal of Electronic Commerce (Hampton-Sosa and Koufaris, 2005). The instrument has been previously applied in the United States (Borchers, 2001). In addition, researchers (Lee and Turban, 2001) have used it as the basis for their own trust model, drawing particular attention to the applicability of the instrument development and testing methodology employed by Cheung and Lee.

## Measurement instrument

In their model (Figure 1), Cheung and Lee show that consumer trust in online shopping is predicted by two sets of antecedents – factors that create a sense of vendor trustworthiness and factors related to the external environment. The former relate to the vendor’s perceived integrity and competence and the vendor’s security and privacy controls. The latter (external environment) encompass third-party recognition (e.g. seals of approval) and the legal framework. The model shows that the effect of both sets of factors on the consumer’s trust beliefs is moderated by the consumer’s propensity to trust. It also acknowledges the relationship between perceived risk and the online consumer’s trust response. The 13 hypotheses regarding the proposed trust antecedents, the influence of propensity to trust on the online consumer’s trust response and the relationship between perceived risk and the online consumer’s trust response are shown in Appendix A. The 30-item measurement instrument developed and validated by Cheung and Lee can be seen in Appendix B (source: Cheung and Lee, 2000a).

## Methodology

The measurement instrument proposed by Cheung and Lee was extended to include demographic information and was applied to two samples, one set of respondents with nontechnical backgrounds and the other set with highly technical backgrounds. The combined sample consisted of 840 subjects.

The first sample chosen for this study was obtained from the Irish Master of Business Administration (MBA) Association. It consisted of 620 individuals who had completed an MBA degree in the preceding 10 years. Their participation in the study was requested via post and they received the questionnaire and accompanying documentation via post. The following week, follow-up letters were sent to all subjects who had not yet replied to remind them to complete the questionnaire. Previous studies of consumer trust in online shopping have been based on current undergraduate or postgraduate students from one university (Borchers, 2001; Belanger et al., 2002). In this study, the participants were graduates of six universities in Ireland. Two hundred of the participants had completed their MBA degree at Dublin City University, while the other 420 participants represented the five other universities in roughly equal measures.

The majority of the respondents from this sample had an undergraduate degree with a non-technical focus. For example, more than 40% of the respondents had a business undergraduate degree, more than 11% had a humanities undergraduate degree and 4% had an undergraduate degree that was neither business, technical nor science related. Of the remainder, 15% had a science undergraduate degree, 12% had a degree that combined business and technology and 17% of the respondents had an undergraduate degree in either engineering or information technology/computer science. Over 71% of the respondents were employed in positions that they described as either senior or middle management.

The second sample was obtained from the Irish Computer Society (ICS) and consisted of 218 consumers with highly technical backgrounds. To become a professional member of the ICS necessitates having a degree in a technical discipline along with a minimum of 3 years work experience in a technical or technical-related position. These requirements increased the likelihood that the respondents would have adequate disposable income and technical competency to engage in online shopping. However, it was reasoned that an individual could have recently joined the ICS, but that their technical knowledge could be considerably outdated (which would reduce their knowledge and experience of online shopping). To overcome this potential limitation, an age limit of 45 years of age was imposed on the participants selected. The participation of this second sample in the study was requested via email. They also received and returned the questionnaire via email.

A previous study, which used this measurement instrument, consisted of a sample of 118 students in the United States (Borchers, 2001). In concluding his study, Borchers stated that future studies using this measurement instrument should have larger samples than were used in his research and ideally should be derived from one culture only. Therefore, both samples for this research are substantially larger than the sample used in Borcher’s study and consist predominantly of Irish subjects. In total, 233 respondents from the MBA association and 38 respondents from the ICS group completed and returned the questionnaire. Thus, the overall response rate to the survey worked out at 38% of the effective sample from the MBA association and 20% of the effective sample from the ICS.

The data collected was input into SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences). Checks for the internal reliability of each construct were made using Cronbach’s alpha values and factor analysis. The factor analysis results indicated a strong association between certain items and constructs other than had been indicated in the original measurement instrument. Checks for the internal reliability of these newly formed constructs were made using Cronbach’s alpha values. The ‘perceived security’, ‘perceived privacy’ and ‘cultural environment’ constructs did not work well with this sample. As the ‘perceived security’ construct is composed of only two items, this is likely to contribute to the low reliability measure for that construct. The measure for the ‘cultural environment’ construct (which consists of only two items) was also low at 0.2628. In a previous test of this measurement instrument, Borchers (2001) also reported a low measure (0.5307) for the ‘Cultural Environment’ construct. He concluded that his use of a sample consisting of multiple ethnicities might have confounded his results. However, the sample used in this research was composed of predominantly Irish respondents. Conflicting interpretation of items due to the respondents’ multi-ethnicity does not, therefore, explain the lack of a good measure for this construct. It may be that the high alpha result that Cheung and Lee (2000a) obtained for the ‘cultural environment’ construct is particular to the Chinese environment (Cheung and Lee’s measurement instrument was developed in Hong Kong) and that the respondents in their study were influenced by elements unique to that environment (Table 2).

As three of the constructs have scores less than 0.65, a principal components analysis was conducted. The principal components analysis resulted in a number of significant changes to item associations for three of the constructs (‘perceived integrity’, ‘personality’ and ‘trust in Internet shopping’). Checks for the reliability of three constructs that contained new item associations showed that they worked particularly well with this sample (Table 3).

Correlation, partial correlation and regression analysis techniques were used to test the 13 hypotheses proposed by Cheung and Lee. In addition, an independent t-test, an analysis of variance test and frequency statistics were used to compare both samples.

## Findings

The study findings provide interesting insights into the factors that predict consumer trust in online shopping. They show that the independent variables explain 40% of the variation in consumer trust in online shopping. More specifically, the results (direct correlation tests, partial correlation tests and regression analysis) provide evidence that two variables – a perception of vendor integrity and the consumer’s previous experience – exert the strongest effect on the dependent variable and thus are the variables with the strongest explanatory power. The next most significant variable is a perception of vendor competence. While the findings show that the vendor’s perceived security controls have some predictive significance, this is far less influential than a perception of vendor integrity, a perception of vendor competence or the consumer’s previous experience.

Findings from the independent t-test (which assessed whether the means of the business and technical groups were statistically different from each other) show differences existed between the business and technical samples in terms of their perceptions of (i) the online vendor’s competence and (ii) the online vendor’s security controls (Appendix E). Both of these constructs were significant at 0.05 level. The results indicate that consumers with a technical background have greater confidence in vendor security controls and do not seek evidence of the presence of these controls to the same extent that consumers with a business background do. The results also show that consumers with a technical background have greater confidence in vendor competence, while consumers with a business background have greater uncertainty in this regard. It therefore appears that the individual’s level of technical efficacy influences his or her perception of web vendor trustworthiness.

The results indicate that the relationship between the legal framework and consumers’ trust response is weak. Similarly, the relationship between assurances provided by recognised third parties and consumer trust in online shopping is weak. Neither the individual’s dispositional tendency to trust nor their online experiences can provide reasonable explanations for the weakness of these relationships. The weakness of the latter relationship contradicts the emphasis placed by consultancy bodies such as Cheskin Research Studio Archetype Sapient (1999) and Cheskin Research (2000) on the effectiveness of third-party recognition in generating consumer trust.

As noted previously, this study examined the variables that composed the ‘propensity to trust’ construct (personality and experience) independently, and as a result of this analysis, marked differences were observed. (aside: In Cheung and Lee’s model, ‘propensity to trust’ is a composite of personality, experience and cultural environment. However, factor analysis indicated that the two items that composed the cultural environment variable should be combined with the personality variable. Hence, in this study propensity to trust is viewed as a composite of personality and experience.) For example, direct correlation results showed ‘experience’ to have a notably stronger relationship with trust that did ‘personality’. Partial correlation tests confirmed that experience exerted a greater impact on trust in online shopping than did personality. When regression analysis techniques were applied, the coefficient results showed personality to have insignificant predictive value. On the other hand, experience had the second highest predictive value of all of the variables. In fact, the predictive value of experience was second only to perceived integrity. This means that previous experiences exerts a strong effect on online consumers’ trust beliefs, stronger than perceptions of vendor competence or the vendor’s perceived security controls.

In marked contrast to Cheung and Lee’s model, the propensity to trust construct (personality variable) did not moderate the relationships between the independent variables and the individual’s trust response. Borchers (2001: USA) also found that the propensity to trust construct did not have any impact on the relationships between the independent variables and trust. He assumed that testing with a larger sample drawn from a single nationality would show stronger results. This research supports Borchers’ original conclusion. However, this research provides strong evidence that the online consumer’s trust response is influenced by their previous positive or negative online experiences.

In summary, the results provide only partial support for the Cheung and Lee model. For example, there is strong support for the hypothesis that certain characteristics convey a perception of vendor trustworthiness, and thus are significant antecedents of trust beliefs. The two vendorrelated characteristics that show the strongest predictive relationship with trust beliefs are a perception of vendor integrity and a perception of vendor competence, with the former being the more effective characteristic. Although there was some association between perceived security controls and the formation of trust beliefs, it was to a far lesser degree than was the case for either of these variables. Relationships between the ‘external environment’ factors and consumer trust in online shopping were insignificant or negative. The results do not support the hypothesis that the propensity to trust characteristic influences consumer trust in online shopping. However, there is strong evidence that experience influences the online consumer’s trust response.

In light of the results above, it can be concluded that:

The subset of Cheung and Lee’s hypotheses that propose that a positive relationship exists between the factors that convey vendor trustworthiness and consumer trust in Internet shopping are accepted. Perceived integrity and perceived competence are strong predictors of consumer trust. Perceived security also has a positive relationship with trust, but to a lesser degree.

The subset of their hypotheses that propose that the individual’s propensity to trust moderates the relationship between the independent variables and consumer trust in Internet shopping are rejected.

## Discussion

This study provides evidence that Irish consumers’ trust in Internet shopping is the result of specific factors, the first of which relates to the vendor’s perceived integrity, and the second of which relates to the vendor’s perceived competence. The former encompasses social antecedents of trust, while the latter encompasses the technical antecedents of trust. Both of these factors are within the control of the online vendor. For example, it is possible for the online vendor to convey a perception of integrity by providing clearly defined terms and conditions regarding the transaction and by providing customers with guarantees that their rights are protected. Similarly, by providing a web interface that is easy to use is reliable and secure, and that anticipates customers’ information needs, it is possible for the online vendor to convey a perception of competence.

The finding that the perceived integrity of the online vendor has the strongest predictive value in terms of the formation of Irish consumers’ trust in online shopping is consistent with the view of researchers who consider a perception of integrity to be a critical antecedent to trust in a traditional environment (e.g. Lieberman, 1981; Butler and Cantrell, 1984; Butler, 1991). Similarly, the finding that a perception of vendor competence is also an important predictor of consumer trust beliefs confirms the view of trust researchers such as Mayer et al. (1995) and Hovland et al. (1953), who emphasise the influence of perceived ability and perceived expertise on the formation of trust beliefs. Moreover, by providing evidence that the antecedents of trust beliefs in an online context are both technical and social in nature, the research findings add to recent attempts to combine elements from the trust and diffusion literatures and to define trust both in terms of trust in the vendor and trust in the technical interface (e.g. Gefen et al., 2003a; Gefen and Straub, 2003).

One of the interesting insights that emerged from this research relates to the influence of technical efficacy on consumers perception of online vendors’ trustworthiness. In the literature, technical efficacy is conceptualised as a control belief, that is, the individual’s perception that they have the skills, resources and opportunities to engage in the behaviour. For example, social psychology models such as the Theory of Reasoned Action (Ajzen and Fishbein, 1980), the Theory of Planned Behaviour (Ajzen, 1985), Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura, 1986) and psychology models specific to IT usage such as the Decomposed Theory of Planned Behaviour (Taylor and Todd, 1995) all recognise the influence of control beliefs on the individual’s behaviour. The results show that respondents with highly technical (in the sense defined in section ‘Objectives of this research’) backgrounds differ from those with nontechnical backgrounds in terms of the factors that influence their trust in online shopping. Those with highly technical backgrounds have greater confidence in website security controls and web vendor competence than do those with non-technical backgrounds. This finding is not surprising. For example, individuals with technical knowledge are more likely to understand the significance of the security features that are evident on a website and therefore to have greater confidence in the adequacy of those controls. Similarly, the fact that the respondents in the technical sample showed greater confidence in the competence of online vendors than did respondents in the business sample may also be an indication of greater confidence in their ability to evaluate websites in terms of features such as technological sophistication, navigation and presentation. On the other hand, consumers who do not have a high degree of technical knowledge may be more prone to attribute difficulties experienced when using website to their own lack of expertise than to poor website design.

The finding that the propensity to trust characteristic does not moderate consumer trust in online shopping is consistent with the view of trust researchers who consider that the individual’s trust response is more influenced by situational factors than by a dispositional tendency to trust (Burt and Knez, 1995; Mishra, 1996; Kramer, 1999; Wicks et al., 1999). However, experience was found to strongly moderate the consumer’s perceptions of the online vendor’s competence, security and integrity. It had greater predictive (see Appendix C) effect on the generation of trust beliefs than did perceptions of vendor competence. In fact, the only variable that had greater predictive importance was ‘perceived vendor integrity’. This means that not only is experience a strong predictor of consumer trust beliefs, but it is more influential than perceived vendor competence and evidence of vendor security controls. The fact that experience exerts more influence on the customer’s trust beliefs is not surprising. The online purchase environment is fraught with risk and even a single bad online transaction experience will almost invariably influence subsequent beliefs regarding that environment.

The predictive importance of experience (to consumer trust beliefs) indicates the need to ensure that customers’ transaction experiences are satisfactory and that they view their website interaction experiences positively. However, as it is not possible to anticipate the concerns of every customer, it is essential to have measures in place to alleviate customers’ concerns and increase their satisfaction with web interaction outcomes. Enabling the consumer to communicate with the vendor’s customer service representatives is one means of addressing the consumer’s concerns, reducing perceived risk and ensuring a positive outcome.

Based on the research findings, a refined model (Figure 2) of consumer trust in online shopping is proposed. The new model proposed has significant modifications to the model proposed by Cheung and Lee (2000a). These modifications result in a more compact set of trust antecedents and greater accuracy as to the factors that moderate those beliefs.

This model shows that consumer trust in Internet shopping is predicted by a number of vendor characteristics that convey trustworthiness, by the individual’s control beliefs, specifically their level of technical skill and their previous experience of Internet shopping. The model’s emphasis on the influence of vendor characteristics on the consumers’ trust response is supported by the study findings, which demonstrated the vendor’s perceived integrity to be the dimension that proves most valuable in terms of its power to explain variation in Irish online consumers’ trust response, that is, of all of the independent variables examined, integrity has the strongest predictive value. The results also show perceived competence and perceived security to be important, but to a lesser degree. Therefore, in this model the characteristics of the vendor that convey trustworthiness, perceived integrity, perceived competence and perceived security controls are emphasised. In line with the study findings, the model recognises the influence of control beliefs, specifically level of technical efficacy, on the consumer’s trust response. The results show that consumers with a highly technical background exhibited greater confidence in vendor competence, while consumers with a less technical background exhibited greater uncertainty in this regard. Second, customers with a highly technical background had greater confidence regarding security controls, than did the less technical sample. They had more confidence that security controls are adequate and did not prioritise evidence of their presence to the same degree that respondents in the less technical sample did. This model therefore incorporates the influence of consumer control beliefs on the importance attributed to vendor characteristics, which in turn influences the consumer’s trust response. The study finding that experience exerts a significant moderating effect on the relationship between consumers’ perceptions of vendor characteristics and trust is recognised in this model. The influence of experience on consumers’ trust response is strongly supported by the study results, which show experience to have the second highest predictive value of all of the variables. In fact, the predictive value of experience was second only to perceived integrity and was stronger than either perceived competence or perceived security controls. This model is now discussed in more detail.

## Discussion of revised model

The revised model of consumer trust in online shopping that emerges from this research draws on the strong theoretical foundations of multiple trust, risk, technology acceptance and psychology-based conceptualisations. Based on the survey findings, it proposes a number of modifications to the Cheung and Lee trust model.

The first major modification relates to antecedents of trust beliefs. In their model, Cheung and Lee (2000a) proposed two sets of antecedents. The first set consisted of four factors related to the vendor’s perceived trustworthiness and the second set consisted of two factors related to the online environment. The findings of this study have resulted in the four factors related to the vendor’s perceived trustworthiness reducing to two factors – perceived integrity and perceived competence – both of which are influenced by the consumer’s control beliefs. While perceived security was an antecedent of trust beliefs, it did not have as strong an effect as either of these variables. Similarly, perceived privacy was strongly associated with integrity. These associations are clear from the model.

Furthermore, the external factors (third-party recognition and legal environment) proposed by Cheung and Lee were shown to have no significant influence on the generation of consumer trust beliefs. Consequently, this model proposes a far more parsimonious set of antecedents that predict consumer trust beliefs.

A second modification relates to the individual’s level of technical knowledge. The research found that individual’s level of technical knowledge influences their perception of both the online vendor’s competence and security controls. It found that customers with a technical background had more confidence that security controls were adequate and did not seek evidence of the presence of these controls to the same degree that customers with a business background did. Similarly, customers with a technical background exhibited greater confidence in vendor competence, while customers with a business background exhibited greater uncertainty in this regard. In the model, this distinction is reflected by the inclusion of control beliefs as a factor that influences the consumer’s perceptions of vendor trustworthiness and perception of risk. A control belief is the individual’s belief that they have the necessary skills, resources and opportunities to engage in a behaviour. The literature recognises the influence of control beliefs on behaviour across many settings. For example, the Theory of Planned Behaviour (Ajzen and Fishbein, 1980) proposes that the intention to engage in behaviour is predicted by three factors, one of which is control beliefs. The Decomposed Theory of Planned Behaviour (Taylor and Todd, 1995) specifically refers to self-efficacy as a control belief.

The third modification relates to the factors that moderate the consumer trust beliefs. In Cheung and Lee’s model, the individual’s trust beliefs were moderated by their propensity to trust. However, the survey findings show that, at least in an Irish context, the individual’s propensity to trust has little influence on their trust beliefs. On the other hand, previous experience was shown to exert a strong influence on consumer trust in online shopping.

This model also provides a more parsimonious set of trust antecedents than those proposed by Cheung and Lee. It provides insight into the factors that shape Irish consumers’ trust in online shopping. Moreover, it draws attention to the significant influence of factors such as the level of technical skill and experience on the consumer’s trust response, factors that typically receive insufficient attention from trust researchers.

One question that remains is whether the results obtained in this study indicate that Cheung and Lee’s model is intrinsically incorrect or whether Irish online consumers differ from Hong Kong/Chinese consumers in terms of the factors that predict their trust response in an online purchase context? The instrument used encompasses a comprehensive set of antecedents, all of which are strongly supported by the literature. Moreover, when Cheung and Lee tested their model, they obtained a very strong set of results. However, their test was limited to a Hong Kong context. In our paper, we have observed that it is difficult to determine whether the results obtained in this study indicate that Cheung and Lee’s model is intrinsically incorrect or whether Irish online consumers differ significantly from Hong Kong/Chinese consumers in terms of the factors that predict their trust response in an online purchase context. The only other test of Cheung and Lee’s model was that conducted by Borchers (2001) in the USA. Borchers considers that the multi-ethnic composition of his sample may have contaminated his results. He therefore suggests that further testing with a larger sample drawn form a single nationality would provide stronger results.

The research described in this paper does just that using a large sample drawn from a single nationality. It found that many of the relationships proposed by the Cheung and Lee model were weak in an Irish context. An important contribution of this research is therefore that it provides evidence that the Cheung and Lee model is not culture independent and that a number of different trust antecedents apply in an Irish context. What is claimed is that the new model that emerges from the present research is valid in an Irish context. Moreover, we have noted that while this model advances our knowledge of the factors that predict Irish consumers’ trust response in relation to online purchasing, further testing of this model is required in other countries in order to establish whether this proposed model is culture independent. Future research is also needed to progress towards a full understanding of the factors that influence consumer trust in online shopping at a general level. Other researchers may, therefore, like to use this model as a basis for investigating the factors that predict consumer trust beliefs in online shopping in other countries.

## Conclusions

The revised model of trust proposed in this paper has both practical and theoretical value. It provides increased insight into the nature of the trust construct as observed in the behaviour of users and potential users of online shopping. By providing a more refined understanding of the predictors and moderators of trust, it makes a valuable contribution not only to IS research but also to the overall body of marketing, trust and diffusion research.

This study significantly extends our understanding of the phenomena along a number of dimensions. It is the first large-scale empirical study of its kind in Ireland. It therefore provides insight into the contrasting antecedents of consumer trust in online shopping from a European perspective. It clearly distinguishes between trust and trust antecedents – an issue that has plagued trust research to date. It builds on conceptualisations of trust accepted by researchers and applied a validated measurement instrument that has previously been used by a trust researcher in another country. Thus, the test results were used to refine an existing model and increase our knowledge of the factors that predict the online consumer’s trust response.

There is, nonetheless, a number of limitations inherent to the design of the study that suggest further research opportunities. Firstly, although this model advances our knowledge of the factors that predict Irish consumers’ trust response in relation to online purchasing, further testing of this revised model is required in other countries in order to establish whether it is culture independent. Future research is also needed to progress towards a full understanding of the factors that influence consumer trust in online shopping at a general level. Other researchers may, therefore, like to use this model as a basis for investigating the factors that predict consumer trust beliefs in online shopping in other countries.

Secondly, as a result of the factor analysis, the items that had composed the perceived risk construct were combined with items that composed the trust in Internet shopping construct and consequently it was not possible to use Cheung and Lee’s measurement instrument to examine the relationship between the perceived risk variable and other constructs. It is not suggested that the findings obtained necessarily invalidate the role of perceived risk in relation to the formation of trust beliefs, but further research using a more representative set of items than those proposed in Cheung and Lee’s instrument is necessary to measure perceived risk as an independent construct and to strengthen the argument for the positioning of this measure.

A third limitation relates to the MBA sample. Although the majority of the respondents in that sample had nontechnical undergraduate degrees, 15% had a science undergraduate degree and it is conceivable that their degree may have had a technical component. This may have introduced some bias into the results particularly when combined with the 17% of the respondents who did have a technical undergraduate degree. Another consideration relates to the gender balance of the sample. Membership of the MBA association is predominantly male and to seek a 50:50 gender balance would have meant reducing the sample size dramatically as far fewer females in Ireland have completed an MBA degree. A gender balance with the ICS sample was not achievable a similar reason. Despite the fact that the t-test showed that there were no statistically significant gender-based differences in relation to the antecedents of trust in online shopping, this does not prove that the research findings are gender neutral. It is not possible to say that gender differences do not influence the online consumer’s trust response, but rather that the survey did not provide evidence of gender-based differences. Finally, there may be other antecedents of trust beliefs and other moderating factors that have not been identified by this study of by other work to date. For example, in this study the majority of the respondents purchased online at least once every 2–3 months. It is possible that customers with different online purchase frequencies and differing online experience would differ in terms of the factors that predict their trust response.

As noted at the outset, it is estimated that the business to consumer online market will be worth in the order of \$1.1 trillion by 2010. In such a vast market, an improved purchasing conversion rate of a modest fraction of a percentage point will translate into many millions of dollars in additional sales. For this reason alone, understanding the factors that influence consumer trust in online shopping is of enormous importance not just to researchers, but also to online vendors everywhere. This research has sought to advance that understanding by providing specific insights into the factors that influence consumer trust in online shopping in Ireland. Those insights will enable knowledgeable vendors to understand what it is their potential customers seek in return for their trust and their willingness to buy.

## Table A1

Proposition 1. The perceived security control of an Internet vendor is positively related to (CTIS). Perceived security control refers to the Internet users’ perception of the Internet vendors’ ability to fulfil security requirements, such as authentication, integrity, encryption, and non-repudiation.<sup>a</sup>

Proposition 2. The perceived privacy control of an Internet vendor is positively related to CTIS. Perceived privacy control is conceived as the Internet users’ perception of the Internet vendor’s ability to protect consumers personal information collected during electronic transactions from unauthorized use or disclosure.

Proposition 3. The perceived integrity of an Internet vendor is positively related to CTIS. Perceived integrity refers to the Internet user’s perception of on the vendor’s honesty. For instance, whether it has consistent actions, whether its actions are congruent with its own words, and whether its transactions with its consumers are fair.

Proposition 4. The perceived competence of an Internet vendor is positively related to CTIS. Perceived competency is defined as the Internet users’ perception of the skills, abilities and expertise of Internet vendors.
