# Understanding Users’ Dynamic Behavior in a Free Trial of IT Services: A Three-Stage Model

## Weiling Jiao

School of Economics and Management Yancheng Institute of Technology No. 1, Xiwang Dadao Middle Road Yancheng, Jiangsu, 224051 China jiaoweiling@foxmail.com

School of Business Qingdao University No. 38, Ningxia Road Qingdao, Shandong, 266071 China ch9569@qdu.edu.cn \* Corresponding Author

## Yufei Yuan

DeGroote School of Business McMaster University 1280 Main St. W. Hamilton, Ontario, L8S 4M4 Canada yuanyuf@mcmaster.ca

Abstract. A free trial of an information technology service (ITS) provides users an opportunity to obtain direct usage experience without purchase commitment, which can significantly reduce their uncertainty about the utility and quality of the ITS and promote their intention of final purchasing. Previous studies of user behavior in a free trial of ITS have mainly focused on either the adoption intention in the pre-trial stage or purchase intention in the post-trial stage. There is a lack of study investigating the trial stage that facilitates the transition and connection between these two separately studied stages. In this study, we view free trial as a motivated learning process and propose a Three-Stage Model (TSM) based on the learning motivation theory and reference-dependent theory to investigate users’ free trial behavior dynamics in moving from motivation to trial, making efforts to gain trial experience, and finally making a further purchase decision after the trial. We collected 377 users’ free trial experience to test our TSM using partial least squares-based structural equation modeling. Our results indicate that the perceived trial utility experience and flow experience gained through trial effort leads to a willingness to accept and ultimately affect user’s willingness to pay through the mediation effect of expected additional value and price justification. Our study contributes to the theory that explains the dynamics of user decision-making behavior in the context of a free trial of ITS.

## 1 Introduction

A free trial of the information technology service (ITS) refers to the practice in which IT companies offer users free access to experience their services, such as offering a free “demo” version with limited functionality, or a fully functional version with a limited trial period, or the combination of the function-limited version and time-locked version. The free trial helps to stimulate and promote users’ initial use and eventual purchase intention (Cheng et al. 2015). As ITSs are increasingly being offered to individual end users “from free to fee”, free trial has become widely adopted in the IT market and has been now a crucial promotion strategy and a common practice for IT companies (Cheng and Tang 2010；Foubert and Gijsbrechts 2016; Li and Yi 2017；Yang et al. 2018). Free trials are offered by many companies such as Netflix (netflix.com) for online movies and TV shows, King’s Candy Crush Saga (king.com) for online games, and Kindle Unlimited (Amazon.com) for eBook.

Free trial is traditionally a behavioral influence strategy in marketing, guided by the assumption that direct and personal experience constitutes an important basis for subsequent purchase action (Scott 1976). Offering a free trial has long been a potent marketing stimuli strategy, especially for selling experience products (Anderson 2009). associated with great quality uncertainty unless they are experienced or consumed by users (Wang et al. 2013; Cheng et al. 2015). Such uncertainty usually means that users are unable to form an accurate and reliable assessment before usage (Lee and Tan 2013), which may reduce their willingness to adopt or purchase (Cheng and Liu 2012; Li and Yi 2017). By providing the opportunity to experience the service without payment, a free trial can ease users’ uncertainty about the utility and quality of the chargeable ITS (Cheng and Tang 2010), which may help them to gain better perception of the ITS functionality (Li and Yi 2017) thus to make more informed purchase decisions (Faugère and Tayi 2007; Foubert and Gijsbrechts 2016).

Many studies have shown that free trial is an important way to increase exposure and awareness of new ITSs and attract new users (Lee and Tan 2013; Foubert and Gijsbrechts 2016; Li and Yi 2017). It plays a powerful role in the formation of favorable quality judgments and consequently enhances users’ propensity to adopt or purchase (Zhu and Chang 2014). However, there are two gaps in the literature on the free trial of ITS from the perspective of users adoption and purchasing decision-making.

First, studies to date have mainly been based on extended versions of traditional IT adoption and post-adoption models, which do not fully reflect the characteristics of free trials. For instance, in the pre-trial adoption stage, the factors proposed by the traditional technology acceptance model (TAM) (Davis 1989) such as perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use of ITS are considered, but the perceived benefits and risks of a free trial are not included. The factors proposed by the traditional expectation confirmation theory (ECT) (Oliver 1980), such as expectation and satisfaction; and the factors proposed by value-based adoption model (VAM) (Kim et al. 2007), such as quality-based benefits and money-based sacrifices, have been used to study continuance intentions (Lin et al. 2012), but the added value and price justification (PJ) for the full version from the consumer perspective have not been included.

Second, studies have focused on user behavior in two isolated stages of free trial of

ITS, either adoption intention in the pre-trial stage (Zhu and Chang 2014) or purchase intention in the post-trial stage (Lin et al. 2012; Wang et al. 2013). Few studies have focused on user behavior in the transition or connection between these two stages. In fact, experiencing free trial is the central stage that connects these two stages in the whole trial process. Free trial of an ITS is a three-stage learning process, during which users are motivated to learn, implement their self-learning, and conduct an evaluation after learning (Foubert and Gijsbrechts 2016; Hamari et al. 2017). Through free trial, the user’s learning effect makes a user familiar with the service and helps improve the user’s utility, which further influence the user’s purchase decisions (Hua et al. 2019). A free trial of an ITS, therefore, may trigger specific decision dynamics. For example, the trial experience may change users’ perceptions, attitudes, and needs, so their post-trial behavioral intentions may not be the same as the intentions that led to their initial adoption (Bhattacherjee and Premkumar 2004), causing them to give up due to the negative experience even though they had a strong adoption intention (Kempf and Smith 1998). Another common example is that many users of temporary trials explicitly opt out of continuing using only because they prefer not to adopt the paid service; that is, these users may be easily attracted but be difficultly retained and upgraded (Foubert and Gijsbrechts 2016; Lee and Tan 2013). Furthermore, when users become acquainted with the ITS during the trial, they may be satisfied with the basic free service and become less willing to pay to use the paid full ITS (Foubert and Gijsbrechts 2016; Pauwels and Weiss 2008). Thus, it is important to understand how users’ motivation and learning affect their decision-making during the dynamic pre-, during, and post-trial stages.

To fill these two research gaps, the objectives of this study are: (1) to create a three-stage conceptual framework to capture the motivated learning and dynamic decision-making process of the free trial of ITS; and (2) to develop and verify a Three-Stage Model (TSM) to study user’s behavior in a free trial and identify the key motivational factors and consequent decisions and actions at each stage that truly reflect the nature of the free trial of ITS.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present the literature review and introduce the theoretical background. In Section 3, we focus on building the three-stage conceptual framework. In Section 4, we build the TSM to identify the motivational factors at each stage and the conditions for stage transition and develop the corresponding hypotheses. In Section 5, we report the data collection and experiment design. After testing the research model using the partial least squares technique, we report the data analysis results in Section 6. Finally, we discuss the key findings, academic and managerial contributions, limitations, and future studies.

## 2 Literature Review and Theoretical Background

## 2.1 Existing Research on the Free Trial of ITS

A few studies have investigated the adoption and post-adoption of free trial of ITS.

The well-known TAM has been used to study the adoption of free trial of ITS. For example, Zhu and Chang (2014) indicated that the perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, perceived risk, and social influence (SI) were important determinants of users attitudes and intentions toward the adoption of a free trial of an ITS. However, rather than reflecting the specific characteristics of the free trial, their study only considered the same factors used for the adoption of traditional unpaid-for ITS. For the initial adoption of a (Pauwels and Weiss 2008), no or low commitment (Su 2009), uncertainty reduction (Faugère and Tayi 2007), option premium (Sriram and Manchanda 2015), and feeling like a smart shopper (Bicen and Madhavaram 2013). However, the TAM only reflects the perceived usefulness and ease of use from IT innovation; it does not explicitly consider the benefits of a free trial that might influence and shape user’s belief and behavior, nor does it consider the cost and risk involved in a free trial.

Other extant research studied user’s inference of using a trialed ITS from the perspective of its economic value for vendors (Cheng and Tang 2010; Cheng and Liu 2012; Li and Yi 2017). For example, Li and Yi (2017) indicated that the size and content of the trial version and the distribution of user’s prior belief would affect an ITS trial’s ability to convey information so that it makes a difference on user’s valuation on trial. However, these factors are chosen to reflect their influence on the profit margin benefit so that it can help the vendors decide whether or not to release a free trial; they do not explicitly consider the influence on user’s trial adoption intention.

When IT users turn into consumers of the commercialized ITS, they have to bear the costs themselves, so the perceived net value (the result of a thorough comparison between benefits and sacrifices) becomes an important consideration in paid-for IT adoption. The VAM has been broadly applied to reflect the nature of the evaluation of benefits and sacrifices and to explain the user’s rational behavioral intention for the adoption of paid ITS without a free trial. Perceived value is a principal determinant of the intention to adopt IT innovations, which is influenced by the benefits and sacrifices (Kim et al. 2007). However, as the VAM assumes that users have to pay to use the IT innovation, thus the model cannot be directly applied to the adoption of a free trial of an innovative ITS because users can use (try) the IT innovation free in this context. Most studies of IT adoption based on the VAM have focused on quality-oriented benefits and monetary-based sacrifices (Lin et al. 2012). However, before the actual use of the ITS, users have little knowledge about the quality of the ITS. Moreover, research shows that users also have to bear certain nonmonetary-based sacrifices such as privacy and security risks (Vinod et al. 2009; Chellappa and Shivendu 2010; Cheng et al. 2015). In other words, the antecedents of trial ITS adoption may differ from those of chargeable ITS adoption based on the VAM.

The ECT has been widely used to study the post-adoption intention of continuous usage. In the context of a free trial of an ITS, the ECT has been used to examine post-adoption behavior in combination with the VAM. For example, Lin et al. (2012) and Wang et al. (2013) suggested that the trial experience, represented by confirmation and satisfaction in the model, also had a strong impact on user’s continuous usage or purchase intentions toward the paid ITS. However, the added value that users expect in the future should also be considered (Meuter et al. 2005; Wagner et al. 2014).

Yang et al. (2017) examined the possible coping strategies that are likely to be adopted by a user when facing negative disconfirmation on trial restrictions, which may influence her propensity to procure the full version. They found that negative disconfirmation on time/functionality restriction positively influenced the adoption of rational thinking strategy, which, in turn, positively influence the level of action coping. As a result, the degree to which rational thinking and action coping strategy was exploited positively influenced a user’s willingness to pay (WTP) for the full version. However, this study assumes that the user is facing negative disconfirmation on trial restrictions and it did not reflect the situation that the user is facing positive disconfirmation on trial restrictions and the situation that performance match expectation (confirmation). Also, the study did not reflect the value (i.e., utility and flow) that the user may gain from experience.

In summary, although the TAM, VAM, ECT and the coping theory originated in traditional IT adoption and post-adoption studies, they cannot fully reflect the nature and full adoption and post-adoption process of the free trial of ITSs.

According to our understanding, a free trial of an ITS involves an experience-centered three-stage learning process: first, the user forms the initial adoption intention in the pre-trial stage; second, the user experiences the quality of the ITS through learning by doing during the experience stage; and third, the user evaluates the ITS to decide whether to purchase the full service or not in the post-trial stage. These three stages are closely related. People may not necessarily be interested in trying an ITS even it is “free”. Only when users are motivated to adopt the free trial in stage one will they move to stage two and spend time and energy trying the ITS. In this stage, users’ accumulated experience and learning may facilitate their informed assessment of the utility and quality of the ITS (Foubert and Gijsbrechts 2016). Only if the experience is satisfactory will users move to stage three and make a post-adoption purchase decision based on their informed assessment. The success of free trial depends on the success of

each previous stage. However, studies have mainly focused on the first and third stage, that is, the pre- and post-trial stages, whereas few have explored the critical second stage of trial experience that connects stages one and three. The failure to consider the three stages as a whole means that the dynamic of user’s decision-making remains unaddressed and many questions remain unanswered. In particular, why, when, and on what condition users are motivated to transfer from each stage to the next in this dynamic process are unanswered.

## 2.2 Learning Motivation Theory

The learning motivation model proposed by Dörnyei (2000) provides a viable theoretical framework that can account for the dynamics of learner’s motivational and behavioral changes in different stages of a self-learning process. The model proposes that learner’s behavioral intentions are ongoing and interact with each other and that different motivational influences can result in a certain action sequence. For instance, the experience of uncontrollable failure or unrealistic instructions may incline users intentions toward their actions (Kuhl 1987). There are two main dimensions in this model: action sequence and motivational influences. The action sequence is divided into three phases. The first phase is the pre-actional phase, defined as the process of goal setting, intention formation, and initiation of intention enactment, during which the motivated behavioral process begins in earnest. Because an operationalized intention is the immediate antecedent of action, the following phase is the actional phase, which is the process of action. The model suggests that the availability of the necessary means and resources and the starting condition should be two necessary conditions for issuing the “action-launching impulse” (Dörnyei and Ottó 1998). The onset of action is a major step in the whole motivational process, resulting in significant qualitative changes because the person intends to continue learning, appraising, and self-regulating during this stage. Based on learning by doing, the ongoing action will lead to some kind of actional outcome: the actor either achieve her wish or is not satisfied. The last phase is the post-actional phase, which begins after the action has ended, and usually entails the actor evaluating the outcome of her action and contemplating the possible inferences to be drawn for future actions.

A free trial of an ITS can also be defined as a three-stage learning process, in which a user’s decision-making also changes dynamically. The pre-trial, experience, and post-trial stages reflect the pre-actional, actional, and post-actional phases in the learning motivation model. Various phenomena, such as users’ decisions and actions at each stage being motivated by internal or external factors, the transition through stages being conditional, and the important role of experience in the process, are consistent with those of the learning motivation model. As one of the major purposes of this study is to understand better the dynamics of the user’s decision-making in a free trial of an ITS, in the next section we will build a three-stage theoretical framework based on the learning motivation model.

## 2.3 The Reference-Dependent Theory

The Reference-dependent theory was originally developed by Kahneman and Tversky (1979). It is a generalization of subjective expected utility theory. Contrary to the conventional assumptions of consumer theory and decision theory, individuals’ preferences are reference dependent, that is, preferences over the final outcomes vary according to the reference point from which those outcomes are viewed (Sugden 2003). In a multi-step decision-making process, people may consider different references under different situations and make a decision to act depending on their preferences. With the situations changed, people’s reference points also changed. Thus people have to consider new references to make a further reference act until the target act of consequences is achieved.

This theory has been adopted as a theoretical basis to explain consumption acts in different business contexts (De Borger and Fosgerau 2008; Köszegi and Rabin 2009; Viscusi and Joel Huber 2012; Wang et al. 2017). The trade-off analysis between expected utilities and expected loss also integrated into the reference-dependent theory to help make choices of preference judgment. For example, De Borger and Fosgerau (2008) suggested that consumers’ willingness to accept (WTA) will arouse their WTP on the premise of the trade-off of equivalent gain and loss. Similarly, Viscusi and Joel Huber (2012) also explained WTA-WTP gap from a reference risk effect perspective to explore the negative effect of cost and risks between WTA to WTP. Since the free trial is a multi-stage decision process, the reference-dependent theory can be used to explain how users are making decisions at different reference points in ITS free trial process towards final WTP of the ITS.

## 3.1Three Stages of Users’ Decisions and Actions

In this three-stage conceptual framework, we define the following three stages based on the learning motivation theory.

The pre-trial stage is the phase in which users form and enact the adoption intention, during which the motivated behavioral process begins in earnest. Generally, the benefits of a free trial and SI may motivate users to adopt. However, potential risks may inhibit users’ adoption intention (Sarin et al. 2003). In this stage, a user must consider carefully “Why should I adopt the free trial?” and “Does the free trial worth to adopt?” so that the user can make a reasonable free trial adoption decision.

The experience stage is the following actional phase, during which users expend effort on the free trial experience (i.e., spend time and energy) to learn about the utility and quality of the ITS. This action is necessary to assess the performance and value of the ITS and thus for the user to consider “What can I gain from the free trial?” and “Does the trial experience live up to my expectation?” so that the user can make choice on acceptance.

The post-trial stage begins after the action has been terminated, during which users usually evaluate and contemplate the possible inferences to be drawn for future purchase actions. A user must carefully consider “Is it necessary to continue to use the services?”, “Are the charged services worth to purchase?”, and “Is the price and commitment acceptable?” so that the user can make a reasonable post-trial purchase decision.

## 3.2 Reference-dependent Decisions for Stage Transition

The learning motivation theory is helpful for us to divide the free trial processes into three closely related stages; while the reference-dependent theory provides a compelling explanation to understand how ITS users make decisions in the transition of the three stages (as three reference points). Based on the reference-dependent theory, we argue that willingness to trial (WTT), WTA, and WTP are the decisions at three reference points for stage transition or termination.

At the first stage of pre-trial, ITS users face the uncertainty of no hands-on experience and the opportunity of a free trial. They need to decide trial or not-trial. Willingness to trial (WTT) is the likelihood or possibility that users are willing to adopt the free trial of the ITS. It is the essential prerequisite for transitioning from the pre-trial to the experience stage. Users in the pre-trial stage may have the preferences of trial or not-trial; they should make an assessment based on the trade-off of expected trial benefits and sacrifices (trial risk) to support or change their preferences, and then take a reference act of trial. If a user’s intention to adopt the free trial is low, she may refuse to put efforts to take a free trial; on the opposite, if a user’s intention to adopt the free trial is high, and is capable of doing so, she will take action and enter the experience stage.

In the second stage of experience, users make efforts and balance the expected benefits and cost of experience to make a reference-dependent choice to accept (satisfaction) or not-accept (dissatisfaction) the ITS. Willingness to accept (WTA) is the likelihood or possibility that users accept the trialed ITS, which is determined by the degree to which the trial experience meets or surpasses a user’s expectations. It is the requirement for transitioning from the experience stage to the post-trial stage. WTA is naturally related to satisfaction. Previous studies confirmed that satisfaction could predict ITS user’s continuous use intention, indicating that ITS user’s satisfaction is a precondition or measurement of WTA (Christian et al. 2005; Bernard et al. 2005). Users in the experience stage have the preferences of satisfaction or dissatisfaction; they take a trade-off of expected utilities and costs (i.e. trial efforts, TE) to evaluate their preferences and then decide whether to accept the ITS or not. Prior study has confirmed that the unsatisfied users do not move forward to further consider the possibility of purchasing (Kuhl 1987). On the contrary, only when users are satisfied with the trial experience will they further consider other factors to decide whether to buy the full paid-for ITS. Satisfaction represents a positive signal of the quality or utility of ITS, which increase the users’ confidence to believe their purchase of an ITS is worth.

When users are willing to accept the trialed ITS after the experience, they come to the last stage of post-trial. They will compare the expected benefits and expected costs of the full vision of ITS to make the final decision of payment or not-payment. Willingness to pay (WTP) is the likelihood or possibility that users intend to purchase the full ITS. It is the requirement for transitioning from the post-trial stage to the purchase action stage. Users in the post-trial stage may have the preferences of payment or not payment; they should compare expected benefits (added value) and losses (price) to evaluate their references, and then achieve WTP. The intention is the precondition of action (Ajzen 1985). If the users have a low level or lack of WTP, they will refuse to pay for the IT service. However, the intention may not necessarily guarantee the action happen unless the individuals are, in fact, capable of carrying out their newly formed intentions. The actual transition may be affected by many factors. For instance, although a customer is willing to pay, she may not have enough money to pay at the moment. Therefore, users future needs and their affordability will intrigue them take action largely.

We conceptualize these factors and propose our hypotheses explicitly in this section.

## [Figure 2]

## 4.1 Antecedent Factors for WTT

In the first stage of pre-trial, ITS users are under an uncertain situation. They lack necessary firsthand information or knowledge to make a rational judgment on the ITSs. Free trial is an effective way to reduce uncertainty and get to know more about the ITS. Based on the reference-dependent theory, we focused on the analysis of potential benefits and cost of the trial, and capture the antecedents of WTT based on such analysis. Besides, SI is also an important indirect channel to receive information about ITS. The direct information of benefit-cost analysis of the ITS can be complemented by other people’s views, helping users to develop an initial understanding of ITS before trial. Therefore, in the first stage of pre-trial, we extended the benefit-cost analysis by integrating the SI to understand how users’ WTT is generated.

## 4.1.1 Perceived Trial Benefit (PTB)

Perceived trial benefit refers to the advantages brought by a free trial of an ITS. Based on the literature and the ITS context, we identified the following trial benefits that may appeal to users who otherwise would not adopt. In addition to perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use widely used in TAM for IT adoption (Davis 1989), we also include other benefits specifically related to free trials. (1) Free of use, which means that users do not need to pay for using the trial version of ITS; this is one of the main factors that attract users to a free trial (Foubert and Gijsbrechts 2016). It may also trigger the user’s gratification, such as the feeling of being a smart shopper, which is likely to advance user’s adoption intentions (Bicen and Madhavaram 2013). (2) No/low commitment, which means that users can engage in a loose and free relationship with the ITS supplier (Dwyer et al. 1987). If users decide not to continue to use, they can terminate the relationship with the ITS supplier any time without penalty, which is often called an option premium (Su 2009). The no/low commitment can reduce the barrier to adopt a free trial of an ITS (Sriram and Manchanda 2015). (3) Uncertainty reduction, which means that the degree of user’s uncertainty toward the ITS can be reduced. A free trial can significantly ease user’s uncertainty because the first-hand information gained through personal experience learned from the trial is more reliable, stable, and persuasive than second-hand information, especially when the quality of the paid ITS is uncertain (Cheng et al. 2015).

## 4.1.2 Perceived Trial Risk (PTR)

Perceived trial risk refers to the possible threat or damage that may occur from the free trial of an ITS. We consider the following potential risks that users may face in a free trial of an ITS: (1) Privacy concern, which refers to the possibility that users’ personal identity, preference, financial, or other types of private information could be intentionally collected and disclosed by the ITS supplier during the free trial ( Ozdemir and Trott 2009; Cheng et al. 2015; Hamari et al. 2017), which may lead to possible privacy violations (Chellappa and Shivendu 2010); (2) Perceived security threat, which refers to the possible risk of malicious software attacks when users download, install, and use the software of the ITS (Vinod et al. 2009).

According to the utility theory in economics, individuals always try to achieve maximum utility; given their resource limitations, users will make a trade-off between perceived benefits and costs. Cazier et al. (2008) also stressed the importance of examining both positive and negative utility for technology acceptance. Perceived benefits and perceived risks can have opposite impacts on user’s online intention (Kim et al. 2007). Thus, perceived benefits may entice and perceived risks may deter users from adopting the free trial of ITS. In line with these theories, we propose the following hypotheses.

H1. PTB is positively associated with WTT.

H2. PTR is negatively associated with WTT.

## 4.1.3 Social Influence

Social influence is defined as the degree to which the user perceives that other important people believe or recommend he or she should adopt the technology. It is an important antecedent of behavioral intention toward information technology according to the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) (Venkatesh et al. 2003).

With the wide-spreading of online social media, users can be easily influenced by other people through social evaluations. Social evaluation of an ITS represented by consumer reviews in social media is an important source of reference for users to judge the quality of ITS. For example, a product ranking based on users vote can generate a high level of awareness and interest among potential users (Liu et al. 2014). Accordingly, social evaluation can act as a strong quality signal or influential reference, which can directly affect users’ judgments of the quality of the ITS (Lee and Tan 2013).

As ITS is a typical experience product, users often imitate their peers’ behavior in addition to social evaluation (Sun 2013). This kind of peer influence sometimes will meet the users’ social needs to a large extent because users can participate social network to climb the so-called “ladder of community participation” to complete their social learning (Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013), and their utility will increase when more people become users of the same ITS due to positive network effect (Cheng and Tang 2010).

The impact of SI on users’ adoption and purchase intention has been studied extensively in a variety of markets and the results generally support the idea that social evaluations are a primary reference for non-users, and user-generated information plays a significant role not only in users’ adoption and purchase decisions but also in users’ perceptions (Ma et al. 2014; Oh et al. 2016). Because many studies have found that SI exerts a positive influence on users’ attitudes and intentions toward the adoption of high-technology innovation (Prins et al. 2009; Zhu and Chang 2014), we accordingly propose the following hypothesis.

H3. SI is positively associated with WTT.

## 4.2 Transition Decisions from the Pre-trial Stage to the Experience Stage

As proposed in the learning motivation model (Dörnyei 2000), the operationalized intention is the immediate antecedent of action. Accordingly, users will usually move to take action (i.e. make TE) when they have a high level of WTT. Evidence based on the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen 1985) has shown a clear connection between intention and action in the framework of the theory of rational action. When behavior poses no serious problems of control, it can be predicted from the intention with considerable accuracy (Ajzen 1985). In our case, a free trial of an ITS can be fully controlled by the user. The stronger they have intention to trial the ITS, the more they will input time and energy to act and experience. Here, we define Trial effort as the time and energy that users invest to gain personal experience during the trial. Based on the above-mentioned analysis, we propose the following hypothesis.

H4. WTT is positively associated with TE.

## 4.3 Antecedent Factors for WTA

Experience is a necessary way to change the uncertainty situation of ITS to certainty. In the experience stage, the effect of external information from SI is reduced. Learning from personal trial usage can generate more favorable quality beliefs than those based on external quality signals alone. Users can learn about the ITS service quality through their own experience (Foubert and Gijsbrechts 2016), which may reduce the cost of uncertainty and improve their perceived service benefit. At this reference point, a core goal of experience for a user is to make a judgment on the confirmation between the initial impression built in the pre-trial stage and their real experience. If such confirmation is reached, the user will be motivated to generate and enhance the willingness to accept the ITS. Accordingly, in the experience stage, the ECT model is outcomes. In this section, we examine the impact of a user’s TE on perceived ITS quality performance. Moreover, as consumers of ITS, users will expect to receive both utilitarian and hedonic benefits from usage. Prior research on satisfaction has proposed both utilitarian and hedonic benefits can motivate user’s satisfaction in ITS usage context (Zhou et al. 2014). Therefore, we suggest that performance experience could be utility experience (UE) and flow experience (FE) to reflect the user’s purposes of utilization (such as fulfillment or problem-solving) and entertainment (or other positive emotions or feelings). We extended the ECT model by integrating the utilitarian and hedonic benefits and set them as the main indicators to satisfaction or acceptance confirmation.

## 4.3.1 Trial Effort

The trial is a learning process. Users’ TE to input time and energy have a great impact on their experiences as a trial just involves a fixed consumption period, such that the experience accumulation and learning effect totally depends on users’ usage intensity (Foubert and Gijsbrechts 2016). Current research has shown that frequent usage will remind users about the value of the ITS constantly (Datta et al. 2015); the increased usage rate during the trial period can improve users’ beliefs about the quality of the ultimate paid ITS, while the lower usage rate only trigger the opposite effect (Foubert and Gijsbrechts 2016), so that usage intensity can be an important driver of continuous usage (Bolton and Lemon 1999), and usage level is the antecedent of users retention in post-adoption (Prins et al. 2009; Datta et al. 2015). Our study takes utility experience (UE) into consideration and defines it as the perceived utilitarian outcomes that users gain from a trial experience for utilization purposes, such as efficiency, effectiveness, and good technological and service quality, which can positively predict user’s intention to continue using (Chiu and Wang 2008). We also infer that the more effort they put during the trial, the more likely they would get better experiences and generate more favorable quality beliefs, and the more likely they would move to the post-trial stage.

Different with previous studies that only focused on the utility-based considerations (Chiu and Wang 2008), we also take hedonic-related experience into consideration in our ITS trial context because study has shown that hedonic experience is also very important for users, and gain increasing importance in IT service (Falk et al. 2010). Flow experience (FE) in our study is defined as the perceived hedonic outcomes that users gain from a trial experience for entertainment purposes, such as users’ immersive and enjoyable feelings generated during the trial. In extensive prior studies, hedonic benefits have been identified as one of the drivers for user participation in IT innovations (Dholakia et al. 2009; Nambisan and Baron 2009). Hedonic benefits of IT services experiences (Nambisan and Baron 2009), and feelings of focused immersion or heightened enjoyment (Ghani and Deshpande 1994).

Research has shown that confirmed users tend to rate ITS as being more useful and valuable during the usage process (Oliver and Desarbo 1988; Bhattacherjee 2001). That is, confirmed users tend to evaluate their experienced utility and flow highly, while a feeling of dis-confirmation will reduce these perceptions. Studies also found that focused attention or cognitive absorption would arouse the FE (Hoffman and Novak 1997). Thus, the TE is the precondition to get hedonic-related performance. Overall, in line with previous studies, we proposed the following hypothesis.

H5. TE is positively associated with UE.

H6. TE is positively associated with FE.

## 4.3.2 Utility Experience

The utility value that users experience and perceive in this stage is only temporary and partial because the trialed ITS is limited in terms of time or functionality. The original authors of ECT theorized about the relationships between perception and satisfaction (Oliver and Desarbo 1988). Many empirical studies have also found that the level of user’s perceived performance determines their satisfaction, which is, in turn, influenced by the confirmation (Bhattacherjee 2001; Lai 2004). Therefore, the more utility value people experience, the more likely they are satisfied and willing to accept the ITS. We propose the following hypothesis.

H7. UE is positively associated with WTA.

## 4.3.3 Flow Experience

Many researchers have pointed out that flow is a state of consciousness experienced when people are deeply involved in an enjoyable activity (Hsu and Lu 2004). Users perceive flow as an experience in its own right, apart from any performance consequences that may be anticipated (Heijden 2004). As such, FE sometimes may be particularly important for individuals because the FE is just as or even more important than the final instrumental result in the online setting (Novak et al. 2003). Moreover, research has shown that flow is a sensation that occurs as a result of significant cognitive involvement, which determines a user’s satisfaction and future behavior (Yang et al. 2018). In this study, we argue that FE is another important factor that may drive WTA, and propose the following hypothesis.

H8. FE is positively associated with WTA.

## 4.4 Transition Decision from the Experience Stage to the Post-trial Stage

The learning motivation theory points out that people usually evaluate the outcomes of their accomplished actions from a broader perspective for future behavior (Dörnyei 2000). WTA may lead the experienced users in the experience stage to make the transition to a potential consumer in the post-trial stage. Many empirical studies have verified that continuance intention is determined primarily by users’ satisfaction with prior IT usage (Oliver 1980), where satisfaction is determined by the extent to which users find the IT innovations to be useful, high in quality, or playful (Bhattacherjee 2001). As an outcome, satisfied users possess higher purchase intentions than those who are dissatisfied (Oliver and Desarbo 1988). Users who accept such ITS may have a high level of satisfaction with their trial experience, such that they will more likely move to stage three and have more intention to continue use by paying such ITS. Studies in other business settings have shown that WTA is positively associated with WTP (De Borger and Fosgerau, 2008). Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis.

## H9. WTA is positively associated with WTP.

Because a trial version has functional or time limitations, acceptance or satisfaction with the trial may stimulate new demand for the additional functions in the chargeable version or additional time for long-term use with a paid subscription. When users are satisfied with the trial, they will be more likely to expect added value and judge the price as acceptable. Expected additional value (EAV) refers to the extended usage time available through subscription or the additional advanced functionality or features that users may expect to receive from the paid ITS compared with those in the free trial. Price justification (PJ) or Price fairness is defined as the degree of rationality, acceptability, and fairness that users perceived for the price of the paid ITS. Satisfaction and WTA

based on user’s own experience during the experience stage creates a new information base that influences the assessment of the additional ITS value and the judgment of the price for the purchasing decision at the post-trial stage (Li and Yi 2017). After completing the free trial, satisfied users will become paying adopters because the positive experience gained through the trial usage may generate more favorable beliefs about quality, which will outweigh the negative price effects on users’ informed assessment of the ITS and their further intention (Foubert and Gijsbrechts 2016). Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses.

H10. WTA is positively associated with an EAV. H11. WTA is positively associated with PJ.

## 4.5 Antecedent Factors for WTP

In the post-trial stage, we also use the benefits and costs analysis to capture the antecedent factors that motivate users to pay for the ITS. The reasons for this are as follows. In marketing studies, purchase intention or WTP is based on an assessment of the perceived quality and price (Zeithaml 1988). Different from the objective quality, which describes the actual technical superiority or excellence of the products from the manager’s view, perceived quality is more important as it represents consumer’s judgment about the attributes of the products. Users tend to pay for the products when the quality they perceived is high. Thus, they can get more benefit from such products. Besides, consumers do not always know or remember actual prices of products, since sometimes they encode the price as expensive or cheap, indicating that the perceived price may have more significance than the objective price when making decisions to purchase. From a rational perspective, users may generate the WTP when the perceived price is low; therefore, they bear a low level of cost. In the context of a free trial of an ITS, the perceived quality and price are shown as the EAV and PJ. Many empirical studies have found that value differences between the trial version and the full version play a crucial role on users’ purchase decisions after they are informed about the attributes and functions of the ITS (Meuter et al. 2005). Therefore, the benefits assessment is a motivation factor for users to pay. When users turn into consumers, their perception of the price will be more meaningful than a given monetary price, and the more they are satisfied with the perceived price, the more transactions they will make (Danaher 2002). Thus, given the user’s EAV, the PJ will be another substantial motivational factor for a user’s continuing usage and purchase intentions after trial from the cost assessment consideration.

## 4.5.1 Expected Additional Value

In the post-trial stage, users evaluate whether the paid ITS offers significant added value they will need in the future before making their purchase decision. Empirical studies have also found that users are generally more easily convinced to purchase when they further evaluate and recognize the added value of the full version of the ITS (Meuter et al. 2005). The more facets of the full version users can evaluate, the more likely they will purchase the full service (Wagner et al. 2014). Thus, we propose the following hypothesis.

H12. EAV is positively associated with WTP.

## 4.5.2 Price Justification

Xia et al. (2004) defined price fairness as a consumer’s assessment and associated emotions of whether the difference (or lack of difference) between a seller’s price and the price of a comparative other party is reasonable, acceptable, or justifiable. It is obvious that users or consumers have to judge the price for continuing to use the ITS or getting more functionality after trial. Consumers usually judge the price based on their own standard or selected references. For example, Bolton et al. (2003) emphasized that consumer perceptions of price fairness were derived from past prices, competitor’s prices, and vendor costs, which elicited perceptions of a product’s current price and represented an individual’s assessment of whether the price of a product or service was reasonable or acceptable. In the context of a paid ITS, users’ PJ is also determined by the value of the monetary sacrifice, as they have had some knowledge about the quality of the ITS (Kim et al. 2007). The more rewarding users find the paid ITS in comparison with the monetary sacrifices, the more they are willing to pay. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis.

H13. PJ is positively associated with WTP.

## 5 Research Methodology

## 5.1 Experiment Design

To test our TSM, we designed an experiment in which the subjects went through all three stages of an ITS free trial, and we then surveyed their opinions and actions. Two representative real-world ITS cases with free trial offers were selected for the experiment. The first one is Kindle Unlimited (https://www.amazon.cn), an electronic book service that allows users a 7-day (i.e., time-locked) free trial (users only pay a symbolic US\$1 cent). During the trial, users can download any number of electronic books they wish to read without any functional limitations. The second one is CamScanner (https://www.camscanner.com), a mobile scanner service that allows users to scan and share images using a smartphone or tablet. Users can use its limited basic function for free of charge with no time limitation. If users want to use its more advanced features, they have to pay.

## 5.2 Measurement Development

The measurement scales used in this study were mainly adopted from previously validated research. All of the constructs were measured using 5-point Likert scales anchored from totally disagree to agree. The scales were checked by knowledgeable IS professors and Ph.D. students who were familiar with survey methods to ensure they were clear and easy to understand. As all of the scale items developed or adapted from existing validated scales were originally in English, a double-translation procedure (Brislin 1993) was used to translate the survey into Chinese. A pilot study was used to verify the validity of the measurement and make the necessary adjustment. The final measurement scale items and sources are listed in the Appendix.

## 5.3 Participants and Procedures

A full survey was conducted to verify the TSM. University students and young faculty members were invited to participate in the experiment. They were selected because young users represent the largest segment of ITS users and are more likely to be innovators and early adopters because of their sensitivity to technological innovation (Bagozzi et al. 1991). Thus, the samples in this study are representative of the target population.

To eliminate the potential impact of teacher-student and colleague relationships, all of the experiment participants were recruited from different disciplines in a school voluntarily. Also, no incentives were used to encourage participation in this study or the trial experience process of the selected ITSs. Thus, participants’ motivation was purely related to their true intentions and behavior. All of the participants were the first time to try both of the offered ITSs. Thus, their decisions relied on the present trial experiences rather than the past experiences.

The study was conducted as follows. Participants were invited to the multimedia laboratory and asked to provide their email address as the identity code for participating in the experiment. After a brief introduction of the purpose and process of the experiment by the researchers, all of the participants were asked to watch a short video about the two types of ITSs offered in this experiment. The contents of the video include the general introduction, basic functions, and operation usage of the trial-version ITSs. Then,

participants were asked to select one ITS (Kindle Unlimited or CamScanner) that they prefer as a trial object. Also, participants were instructed to download the ITS on their mobile devices. During the following one-month trial period, participants received the remind emails of the free trial once a week. At the end of the trial period, they received an online survey via email to assess their trial decision and experience process. Users reported their perceptions at each stage retrospectively. In the survey, we also provided introductions of the paid-version ITSs, including price, payment information, and additional functions. Then, participants were asked to report their cognitions about the paid-version ITSs accordingly, as well as their WTP for such ITSs.

It is worth to notice that participants can withdraw at any time during the experiment. Generally, those participants who withdrew the experiment would not complete the survey, thus were not included in the data pool. We received 500 responses from the 550 invited participants after the whole experiment. Of these, 97 responses were discarded because of missing data and data inconsistencies. The Z-score method was used to detect outliers (Tabachnick and Fidell 2007), and 26 outliers were consequently dropped from the data pool. Finally, 377 usable responses were included in the data analysis. Of these, 235 subjects (62.3%) chose the Kindle Unlimited trial with a time limitation and 142 subjects (37.7%) chose the CamScanner trial with a functional limitation. ## 6. Data Analysis, Results, and Discussion

We used partial least squares (PLS) and SmartPLS 3.0 to analyze the measurement quality and the path model for hypothesis testing. PLS was selected for two reasons. First, PLS is more suitable when the purpose of the model is to explore, rather than to test established theory (Gefen et al. 2011), as such in this study. Second, PLS is suitable to test complex models with many variables and constructs (Fornell and Bookstein 1982).

## 6.1 Assessment of Measurement Validation

The following criteria suggested by Hair et al. (2011) and Gefen and Straub (2005) were adapted to measure construct validity and reliability in a reflective measurement model: (1) Internal consistency reliability: Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability (CR) should be higher than 0.70; (2) indicator reliability: indicator loadings should be higher than 0.70; (3) convergent validity: the average variance extracted (AVE) should be higher than 0.50; (4) discriminant validity: a) the AVE of each latent construct should be higher than the construct’s highest squared correlation with any other latent construct and b) an indicator’s loadings should be higher than all of its cross-loadings (i.e., loadings should be at least 0.10 greater than cross-loadings).

According to these criteria, all indicator loadings were higher than 0.75 (see Table 3). Also, the measurement model was tested and results showed that the lowest CR value was 0.869 and lowest Cronbach’s alpha was 0.777 (see Table 2), indicating acceptable reliability.

As shown in Table 2, all of the AVE values were higher than 0.50, implying reasonable convergent validity. The square roots of the AVEs were higher than their shared variances, and each indicator’s loadings were higher than all of its cross-loadings and were at least 0.10 less on their loadings than on other constructs, thereby establishing the discriminant validity of the constructs. ## 7 Conclusions and Future Research

## 7.1 Key Findings

At the pre-trial stage, the results indicated that the PTB and SI had significant effects on the user’s intention to trial ITS; thus, H1 and H3 were supported. However, PTR had no significant impact on WTT; thus, H2 was not supported. One possible reason is that we only used two specific cases, in which potential risks such as information security and privacy concern might not have been the main concern. However, security-related considerations may be significant in other contexts of IT services when aggressive personal information is collected. Also, we found a significant difference in information collection rules for ITS users in China and Western countries. For example, Netflix asks users to submit credit card information before trial, which may raise user’s privacy concern and may thus lead to the users to reject to trial, while the majority Chinese ITSs ask their users to provide some basic information like email and phone number. This difference calls for a future test of our TSM in other countries to understand the trial risks.

We found that WTT had a positive impact on the user’s efforts to trial; thus, H4 was supported. WTT was the precondition for the transition from the pre-trial stage to the experience stage.

At the experience stage, the TE increased the perception of satisfaction through UE and FE, and therefore, motivated users to accept the ITS, implying support for H5-H8. Our results suggested that user’s TE were important for the free trial of ITS. Studies have found that user’s satisfaction with ITS depended on its usefulness, quality, and playfulness (Karahanna et al. 1999; Bhattacherjee 2001). Our study confirmed that user’s satisfaction could arouse their strong willingness to accept (WTA) such ITS, and the WTA was directly and significantly influenced by their experience of the utilitarian and hedonic value.

WTA is the desired output of the experience stage and a precondition for the transition from the experience stage to the post-trial stage.

At the post-trial stage, WTA increased the expectation of additional value and cognitive PJ; therefore, H10 and H11 were supported. WTA increased their expectation of the additional value before payment and cognition of PJ, which indirectly increased their intention to pay for the paid ITS. EAV and PJ were also found to significantly increase WTP; thus, H12 and H13 were supported. Different from our expectation, the direct impact of WTA on purchasing intention was insignificant; thus, H9 was not supported. This finding is different from previous studies in which satisfaction was directly linked to the user’s purchase intention (Oliver and Desarbo 1988) or continuation intention (Lin et al. 2012). The possible explanation is that in their study, there were no differences between the experienced and paid products, but in our study, the WTA was measured towards free trial version only, not the paid full version. A user who satisfied with the trial version may not necessarily be willing to pay the full service without the consideration of the added value and PJ.

We draw the following conclusions. (1) A free trial of an ITS is an integrated learning process from a user decision-making perspective, with three closely connected stages, the pre-trial, experience, and post-trial stages. WTT, WTA, and WTP are the three very important conditions for stage transition or termination. (2) PTB and SI are intrinsic WTA and EAV and PJ are strong predictors of WTP. (3) TE plays a very important role in the whole process, and the more time and energy users invest, the more satisfaction they gain from the mediation of experience, and motivate their strong willingness to accept such ITS. (4) WTA obtained through a free trial has a significant effect on the following assessment of value and price, which plays an important role in purchasing intention in the post-trial stage. However, WTA does not automatically or directly lead to a WTP. In summary, a free trial facilitates the transition from a non-user to the user, and finally to the convinced consumer.

## 7.2 Theoretical Contributions

Our study makes theoretical contributions to the comprehensive understanding of the dynamics and mechanisms of user’s decision-making in the context of a free trial process of an ITS. It fills the gap regarding the free trial of ITS in the IT adoption, post-adoption, and purchase literature.

First, we created a three-stage conceptual framework to capture the complete learning and decision-making process during a free trial of an ITS. To the best of our knowledge, no other study has presented such a framework before. Previous studies treated the pre-trial stage and the post-trial stage as two separate stages so that we cannot know the users’ behavior clearly in the transition or connection between these two stages. Also, there was a lack of study on the role of trial experience on the user’s purchase decision so that we cannot assessment the effect of the free trial strategy well. In our study, the pre-trial, experience, and post-trial stages were treated as well-connected units through trial experience stage in the learning process rather than as several isolated independent stages. Our conceptual framework demonstrated the relationship between the three stages, especially the role of experience in connecting the user’s pre-trial and post-adoption behavior. It helps IS researchers to better understand and explain the integrated process and dynamics of decision-making in a free trial of an ITS.

Second, we developed the TSM to study user’s behavior in a free trial of ITS and identified the key motivational factors and consequent decisions and actions at each stage that truly reflect the nature of a free trial of an ITS. Our TSM in each stage is different from existing TAM, VAM, and ECT for ITS adoption or post-adoption for continue use. For example, in the pre-trial stage, we considered the trial benefit and risk that might have enticed or deterred users from making an adoption decision, which was different from the traditional TAM and VAM used for IS adoption. TAM is used to model user adoption behavior for the public ITS service paid through advertisement or other sources, not the user；or for the organizational ITS paid by the organization, not the employee. It benefits of the IT innovation itself, not the benefit and limitation of a free trial. VAM is used to model customer adoption behavior for the purchasing and use of commercial ITS which must be paid for use. It considered the quality-based perceived benefits and the money-based perceived sacrifices; both do not reflect the specific learning benefits and costs of the free trial offer. In this case, our model contributes to offering specific factors that may intrigue the user’s WTT in the context of ITS free trial. In the experience stage, we investigated how TE affected the UE and FE and finally led to WTA, which had never been studied in the free trial literature. In the post-trial stage, we studied how WTA affected the assessment of EAV and PJ, and finally the intention to purchase from a marketing perspective, which was significantly different from the ECT and VAM-based ITS adoption model. ECT validated the strong and significant coefficient between satisfaction and continuous usage and purchase intention. However, our study found that WTA will not automatically or directly lead to WTP in a free trial context. Our contribution is to emphasize the role of the user’s actual trial experience on WTA and the role of WTA on the transition to the evaluation in the post-trial stage. VAM mainly take actual price as the cost into consideration, while we considered the user’s perception of price, which we think can be better reflected the individual assessment on price fairness. Also, we consider the user’s future needs for additional value. We contribute to finding a new important decisive factor of user’s WTP in the post-adoption stage. In this case, our TSM is the most comprehensive behavioral model of ITS free trial.

Finally, we investigated the motivation factors for the transition through consecutive stages based on the reference-dependent theory, thus extending previous studies that only examined adoption or post-adoption independently without considering the connection between them. Our study certifies that user’s WTT, which may be intrigued by user’s PTB and SI, is the condition for potential users to move from the pre-trial stage to the trial stage and thus become actual users. WTA, which may be affected by the user’s actual UE and FE, is the condition for users to move from the experience stage to the

post-trial stage to assess whether to purchase. WTP, which may be determined by users’ EAV and PJ, is the final output of the post-trial stage and is the condition for users to become committed consumers to purchase and use the full version of the ITS. We point out that the ultimate success of a free trial of an ITS depends on the success of these three transitions. However, it is worth to note that such transition motivation factors are reasonable but not absolute and sufficient. Other factors may motivate or prohibit users from moving to the next stage. For instance, a user may like to try but do not have time to try, or a user may be happy with the trial experience but is not ready to make payment decisions. Our study calls for further examination of other factors that may affect the transition between different stages.

## 7.3 Practical Implication

Our study also provides important managerial insights that can help ITS companies to view the whole picture of the user’s behavior during the ITS free trial process and the main motivating factors that may influence their behavior. Thus, the findings may guide ITS companies in augmenting their offerings through free trial promotions. Our findings suggest that it is necessary for ITS companies to pay attention to each stage as each may determine the success of the free trial strategy and the commercialized ITS innovations.

In the pre-trial stage, as PTB is the intrinsic motivational factor of WTT, ITS companies should use strategies to promote the free trial of ITS to users, such as taking advantage of online social media to diffuse information about the free trial campaign, using direct marketing to introduce and communicate the benefits of the trial ITS and so on. Meanwhile, ITS companies should explain their privacy policies to ease users’ concerns about privacy and security risks. Moreover, it is advisable for ITS companies to welcome positive user’s comments to improve the SI of their services and to improve the attractiveness of their free trial offerings.

ITS companies should pay more attention to the experience stage because the user’s ongoing behavioral intentions and action sequences are more likely to be influenced by experience. In this case, ITS companies could (1) stimulate user’s usage effort during the trial to ensure they fully experience the functionality and enjoyment of the service and (2) take measures to improve the service quality of the trial ITS to improve user satisfaction and avoid negative experiences. It is important to note that the design of services should reflect the user’s demands for entertainment, as the experience of flow is also very important as the experience of utility in an ITS trial context. Furthermore, the impact of experience depends on the dual mechanism of attracting and convincing users. A positive trial experience may reduce user’s uncertainty and have a positive effect on user’s appraisals of the ITS, which may generate new consumers among the trial users;

conversely, negative trial experiences such as poor service and technical malfunctions may only alienate potential consumers.

Our findings show that WTA cannot directly guarantee users WTP. It only provides a precondition to move from the experience stage to the post-trial purchase assessment stage and to create the basis for user’s assessments. EAV and PJ determine user’s purchase decisions. Thus, ITS companies need to develop appropriate product offering strategies and highlight the difference in quality and value between the trial and the chargeable ITS, thus motivating trial users to turn into paid users because of the attractive additional value gained from the ultimate service quality. Moreover, as PJ is another strong predictor of WTP, ITS companies should develop appropriate pricing strategies, such as offering different functionality and price packages and charging methods to cover user’s diversified needs, promote the feeling of price fairness, and advance their intention to purchase.

## 7.4 Limitations and Suggestions for Future Studies

Although our study provides meaningful findings, there are some limitations that may inspire future studies.

First, we verified our model using college students and young faculty members in China, which may limit its generalizability. To check whether our findings are more generalizable, the model needs to be further verified with general consumers in different countries. Besides, our study was mainly based on users reported subjective opinions in an experimental situation, not their real-world behavior. Future studies could focus on actual behavior by analyzing real customer data collected from the web. For instance, using web mining, we could investigate how many and under what situations users are attracted by free trials and move to become trial users; how many and under what situations users are satisfied with their experiences; and how many and under what situations users transfer to consumers after the free trial. Identifying these factors from real data would enrich our model and provide a better explanation of users’ behavior in free trials of ITSs.

Second, we used only two specific cases to investigate a user’s behavior in a free trial of an ITS. Although we found the trial types as a control variable does not affect our results, but our conclusion may not be generalizable for different ITSs without further investigation. For instance, the PTR may have a significant impact on WTT if an ITS request a privilege to obtain much sensitive personal information such as personal identity, financial payment information, or social network contacts, etc. Besides, the unique features of ITS trial may have a different impact on a user’s trial behavior in different stages. For example, for the time-locked free trial ITSs, very short free trial time may decrease user’s trial experience cognitions, and long free trial time may lead to less urgency to experience or make purchase decision, while for the function-limited free trial ITSs, if the trial functions are good enough to meet many users’ basic needs, those users may have no reason to pay for the full version, and if the trial functions are very limited, users may not be able to gain a good experience to judge the potential benefit of the full ITS. Overall, the frequency, intensity, and the connectedness of using ITS for accomplishing consumer’s tasks during the trial period would make a salient impact on the consumer’s decision to purchase the service. Future studies should identify more influential factors, especially the factors reflecting the impact of the unique features of the ITS trail.

Third, construct measurements could be further improved to avoid simplicity and other weaknesses. For instance, FE could be measured more specifically to reflect the feature of specific ITS trial; SI could be measured by not only the importance of social relationships but also the direct recommendations received. Moreover, some potential control variables could also be checked in future research, such as the user’s past similar experience. With the booming of information technology, the ITSs are becoming much more abundant than before. It becomes increasingly difficult for users to find brand new ITS. Thus, users are often faced with the comparison of details of similar ITS provisions. The decision model of an experienced user is different from that of an inexperienced user. Thus, it is helpful to take into account the user’s past experience with similar ITS provisions in the model, experiment, and survey to make the results more generalizable.

Fourth, how different company strategies may affect user behavior must be considered to further extend our model. For instance, in the pre-trial stage, we could investigate how ITS companies’ direct marketing and advertising strategies affect the PTB, how security and privacy protection policies affect the PTR, and how the use of social media for customer reviews affects SI. For the experience stage, we could investigate how the length of the trial and functional limitations affect usage intensity, and how the quality of free trial services affects user’s quality beliefs and overall satisfaction. The optimal length or appropriate functionality of a free trial of an ITS may help ITS companies to attract users and ensure their experiences are positive. As for the post-trial stage, packages with different value and price combinations could be tested to ensure more attractive additional value and better PJ. We believe that these extensions can be used to explore the connection between company strategy and user behavior, thus helping ITS companies to make better decisions and improve the success of free trial strategies in attracting committed consumers.

Finally, we may further improve our research methodologies. Although we conducted a user behavior study for the entire trial process, we collected the survey only

##

at the end after users went through the entire trial process. Due to the difficulties of monitoring individual trial progress, we did not collect the data at each stage, thus missed data collection for those who did not go through all the stages. This limitation may affect the validity and reliability of our findings. To overcome the limitation of our data collection, we recollected a new data set from a group of college students recruited online by incorporating users who terminate the process in the first stage. The new data set shows good reliability, discriminatory validity, and convergent validity. The results of structural model testing indicate that the PTB $( \beta = 0 . 3 0 4 , p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and SI $( \beta = 0 . 2 5 4 , p <$ 0.01) are two motivators of WTT, while PTR $( \beta { = } { \cdot } 0 . 1 0 7 , p { > } 0 . 0 5 )$ has no significan influence on WTT. The results are similar to our TSM findings with our original data set, implying the validation and test results of our TSM was not affected by our data collection limitations. Future studies could collect data using a stage-by-stage approach to retest the TSM. Besides, a longitudinal study method may be conducted to investigate the user perception such as perceived usefulness change over time from the pre-trial stage to the experience stage and to the post-trial stage. At the pre-trial stage, the perceived usefulness may be formed based on the web description or other people’s review comments; at the experience stage, the perceived usefulness may be formed based on users’ own experience; and at the post-trial stage, the additional functionality may be taken into consideration to assess expected usefulness.

## Appendix: Measurement Scale Items and Sources

Perceived Trial Benefit (PTB) adapted from Davis (1989) and Hsu and Lu (2004)

PTB1: I expect the adoption of the ITS on trial would be useful and easy to use.

PTB2: A free trial allows me to use the ITS without payment.

PTB3: It is easy for me to terminate the free trial at any time and without penalty.

PTB4: A free trial is helpful for me to understand the quality of the ITS and reduce my uncertainty.

Perceived Trial Risk (PTR) newly developed based on Chellappa and Shivendu (2010) and Vinod et al. (2009)

PTS1: I am concerned about the exposure of my private information with the use of ITS trial\*

PTS2: I am concerned about the security risks of my electronic devices and system after with the installation of the ITS software\*

Social Influence (SI) adapted from Venkatesh et al. (2003)

SI1：I always take social influence into consideration before I choose to trial an ITS.

SI2：Social evaluation of an ITS is an important reference for me to trial it.

SI3：I like to imitate my peers’ behavior because they are objective and authentic.

Willingness to Trial (WTT) adapted from Kim et al. (2007)

WTT: I intend to adopt a free trial of the ITS.

Trial Efforts (TE) newly developed for this study

TE1: I spend time and energy on experiencing the ITS.\*

TE2: I spend time and energy on exploring the functions of the ITS.\*

Utility Experience (UE) adapted from Chiu and Wang (2008)

UE1: I think the ITS could improve the quality of my life.

UE2: I think the ITS could enhance my efficiency.

Flow Experience (FE) adapted from Yang et al. (2018) and Hsu and Lu (2004)

FE1: I think it is interesting, enjoyable, or exciting to use the ITS.

FE2: I find that I am in flow during most of the time when I am using the ITS.

Willingness to Accept (WTA) adapted from Bhattacherjee (2001)

WTA: I am satisfied with the experience of using ITS trial.

Expected Additional Value (EAV) self-developed based on Yang et al. (2018)

EAV1: The paid ITS allows me to continue using the ITS without time limitation.\* EAV2: The paid ITS allows me to use more advanced functions and privilege of membership.\*

Price Justification (PJ) adapted from Habel et al. (2016)

PJ1: I think the price of the paid ITS is reasonable, fair, and acceptable.

PJ2: I think the pricing method of the paid ITS is flexible and has several suitable options to choose, such as pay monthly, quarterly or yearly.\*

Willingness to Pay (WTP) adapted from Yang et al. (2018)

WTP: I am willing to pay for the ITS that I have tried.
