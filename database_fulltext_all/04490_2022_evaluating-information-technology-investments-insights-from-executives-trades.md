# EVALUATING INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY INVESTMENTS: INSIGHTS FROM EXECUTIVES’ TRADES<sup>1</sup>

Taha Havakhor Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, PA 19122 U.S.A. {taha.havakhor@temple.edu}

Sanjiv Sabherwal College of Business, University of Texas at Arlington, Arlington, TX 76019 U.S.A. {sabherwal@uta.edu}

Rajiv Sabherwal Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, AR 72701 U.S.A. {rsabherw@uark.edu}

Zachary R. Steelman Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, AR 72701 U.S.A. {zsteelma@uark.edu}

Performance impacts of investments in information technologies (ITs) are difficult to evaluate. External investors are further constrained by their lack of visibility into the firm’s intangible, complementary actions and capabilities, creating an information asymmetry between them and the firm’s executives. Building on signaling theory and the research on senior executives’ trades in a firm’s stock, this paper addresses the following question: How are the stock trades by a firm’s senior executives before a major IT investment by the firm associated with the future value to the firm from that IT investment? The results based on data on 2,898 publicly announced IT investments from 926 firms during 2002–2016 suggest that (1) the purchasing of a firm’s stock by its senior executives before a firm’s IT investment is associated with the investment’s long term effect on firm value; (2) such stock purchases by a firm’s senior executives are associated with a stronger positive (negative) relationship between the IT’s newness and the long-term abnormal returns to firms emphasizing a revenue enhancement (cost reduction) IT strategy; (3) for firms pursuing a hybrid strategy, purchases by CIOs but not purchases by CEOs or the newness of IT are associated with firm value, and (4) purchases made by CIOs provide greater information about the IT investment’s impact on firm value than purchases made by CEOs. We further improve our predictive model’s accuracy from 75% for a model including the fit between IT newness and IT strategy to 80% and 91% when considering purchases by CEOs or CIOs, respectively, and 92% when considering purchases by both executives.

Keywords: Information technology investment, information asymmetry, senior executive, executives’ trades in a firm’s stock, IT newness, IT strategy, chief information officer

## Introduction

Individuals looking to purchase a firm’s stock evaluate how its major investments would help create value. But this is a challenging task, especially for information technology (IT) investments as IT artifacts become increasingly complex and multifaceted, with far-reaching impacts and dynamic connections among individuals within and across organizations. The literature on the business value of IT (BVIT) suggests that an IT investment may have a positive, negative, or insignificant effect on firm performance and provides rich insights into how the value generated by an IT investment depends on the key attributes of the ITs and the adopting firm (for example, the IT’s novelty and its strategic fit with the firm; e.g., Bardhan et al., 2013; Mithas & Rust, 2016; Mithas et al., 2012;Sabherwal et al., 2019). Such insights can enable analysts and tech bloggers to better assess the long-term value of an IT investment by a firm.

Despite the insights from the BVIT literature and the literature on stock trading, external investors have a limited ability to appraise a firm’s IT investments, especially relative to its executives. Prior literature has examined such information asymmetry between executives and external investors in non-IT contexts (e.g., Akerlof, 1970; Diamond & Verrecchia, 1991; Merton, 1987; Miller & Rock, 1985). So, investors could benefit from understanding how information asymmetry regarding IT investments could be reduced.

Prior research suggests that senior executives make informed trades of their firm’s securities before the firm makes major investments (e.g., Aboody & Lev, 2000; Ahuja et al., 2005), and such trades can serve as signals that reduce information asymmetry. Drawing upon insights from this literature, signaling theory (e.g., Connelly et al., 2011; Spence, 1973), and the BVIT literature (e.g., Bardhan et al., 2013; Mithas & Rust, 2016), we argue that since a firm’s senior executives know about potentially important but nonpublic aspects of IT investments (e.g., the firm’s capabilities and concurrent personnel changes that might help leverage the investment), their purchase of a firm’s stock before the firm makes an IT investment might be a valuable signal regarding the potential impact of that investment. However, with attention being a scarce cognitive resource (Kahneman, 1973), investors might not recognize signals that could be tacit in executives’ purchases prior to IT investments. This is consistent with the prior findings that limited attention can cause investors to ignore useful information in disclosures and associated potential signals (e.g., Hirshleifer et al., 2011; Karlsson et al., 2009). Accordingly, to understand the signaling value of executives’ trades prior to major IT investments, we address the following research question: How are the stock trades by a firm’s senior executives before a major IT investment by the firm associated with the future value to the firm from that IT investment?

In addressing the above question, we make two departures from prior literature and thereby contribute to the information systems (IS) literature as well as the broader literature on executive trading. First, the prior literature on executive trading around capital expenditures (e.g., Aboody & Lev, 2000) does not examine the synergy between the informativeness of executives’ trades and insights from the strategic theories of managing capital expenditures. By contrast, we draw upon the insights from the literature on BVIT (e.g., Mithas et al., 2012; Steelman et al., 2019) and strategic fit (e.g., Sabherwal et al., 2019; Saldanha et al., 2020) to examine how the strength of the relationship between the executives’ stock purchases before an IT investment and abnormal returns after the investment varies with the nature of the IT investment (specifically, the newness of the IT) and the firm’s strategy (specifically, the firm’s IT strategy). Second, prior literature suggests that executives may differ in the informativeness of their trades (e.g., Ozkan & Trzeciakiewicz, 2014; Wang et al., 2012). However, such prior literature has focused on trades: (1) about broader actions by the firm, such as acquisitions, but not on trades around a capital expenditure by the firm; and (2) by the chief executive officer (CEO) or chief financial officer (CFO) but not by executives who are operationally proximal to the investment. By contrast, we examine the differences between the direct as well as indirect associations of stock purchases by the chief information officer (CIO) and the CEO before major IT investments and abnormal returns.

We develop a research model addressing the above question by using insights from signaling theory (e.g., Connelly et al., 2011; Spence, 1973) and the strategic-fit view from the BVIT literature. In testing the model, our identification is based on the assumption that investors may not immediately parse executives’ purchases preceding an IT investment as a signal indicating the value of that investment. Instead, it would be over time that the stock prices would reveal the real value of information embedded in executives’ trades. Therefore, our focus is on long-term returns as the outcome variable of interest.<sup>2</sup>

We use data on 2,898 publicly announced IT investments to test the model. The results indicate that (1) the purchase of a firm’s stock by its senior executives before the firm makes an IT investment is positively associated with the long-term abnormal returns from that investment, and (2) this association is stronger when a firm pursuing a revenue-emphasis (cost-emphasis) strategy invests in an IT with a high (low) level of newness. Moreover, both these effects are stronger in the case of stock purchase by CIOs than CEOs.

In light of the results for the research model, we test several predictive models to examine the improvement in the ability to predict abnormal returns over prior insights. The results indicate that the purchase of a firm’s stock by its senior executives before the firm makes an IT investment enhances the prediction of the investment’s long-term effect on the firm’s value. Specifically, including information about executives’ purchases into a strategic-fit model of BVIT (that considers the firm’s IT strategy and the newness of the IT being invested in), raises the predictive accuracy by about 5 percent (from 75% to 80%) when also considering stock purchases by CEOs but not CIOs, about 16 percent when also considering stock purchases by CIOs but not CEOs (to 91%), about 13 percent (to 88%) when considering stock purchases by both CIOs and CEOs but not recognizing the differences between them, and about 17 percent (to 92%) when considering stock purchases by both and recognizing the differences between them.

## Theoretical Development

## Information Asymmetry and Signaling Theory

Individuals decide based on public information, which is freely available, and private information, which is available to only a subset of the public. Due to some information being private, information asymmetries arise between those who have that information and those who do not. Signaling theory addresses information asymmetry by focusing on how signals by one party can be interpreted by another party with different information (Connelly et al., 2011; Spence, 1973). Spence (1974, p. 2) defines signals as “activities or attributes of individuals in a market which, by design or accident, alter the beliefs of, or convey information to, other individuals in the market.” As indicated by the phrase “design or accident,” signals do not need to be intended; instead, a party can interpret actions or remarks by another party that did not seek to convey a signal (Daily et al., 2005; Janney & Folta, 2003). Along similar lines, Connelly et al. (2011) argue that parties may send a signal without even realizing they are signaling.

Corporate announcements have been viewed as signals that reduce asymmetry between the firm’s executives and outsiders, thereby affecting perceptions about future firm performance (e.g., Connelly et al., 2011; Reuer et al., 2012). This is also true for announcements about capital investments (e.g., John & Mishra, 1990) and specifically IT investments (e.g., Shea et al., 2019). Some BVIT studies view the announcement of an IT investment as a signal to investors about change in the firm’s future cash flow (e.g., Shea et al., 2019). Additionally, prior research finds that the public announcements of firms can provide important signals for investors to determine a firm’s business strategy, IT strategy, and strategic alignment (Sabherwal et al., 2019). Therefore, we have some evidence of the signals provided by a firm’s IT investment announcements but limited information about potential signals in executives’ stock trades before such investments.

## Stock Purchases by Senior Executives as Signals

Purchase of a firm’s stock by its senior executives can also serve as an important signal of their confidence in the firm’s future (e.g., Goranova et al., 2007; Piotroski & Roulstone, 2005). For example, Piotroski and Roulstone (2005) find the trading by the firm’s registered insiders (directors and top executives) to predict the firm’s future earnings, with the predictive ability increasing with information asymmetry between insiders and external investor. Similarly, in a study focusing on stock trades by CEOs of diversified firms, Goranova et al. (2007) argue that CEOs who increase their ownership of their firm’s stock may convey a signal to the market that they are optimistic about the effects of the firm’s diversification strategy. Several other studies find that executives benefit from access to internal information and earn profits from their stock purchasing in varying contexts and over time (e.g., Ahern, 2017; Finnerty, 1976; Jaffe, 1974; Lakonishok & Lee, 2001; Lorie & Niederhoffer, 1968). Recent evidence (e.g., Amir et al., 2019) shows that major IT-related incidents such as cyberattacks also trigger trading by insiders.

## Senior Executives’ Stock Purchase before IT Investments

Signaling theory emphasizes that multiple signals increase signal recipients’ confidence in their resulting judgments (Spence, 1973). The above sections have discussed that both (1) corporate announcements about capital investments and (2) trading of a firm’s stock by its senior executives can serve as signals that reduce information asymmetry between a firm’s executives and outsiders.This study focuses on these signals, which have not been examined together.

Prior literature (e.g., Mithas & and Rust, 2016; Mithas et al., 2012) has examined the potential value realization from an IT investment through aspects such as improved capabilities, cost efficiency, and profits. A firm’s public announcement of an IT investment can be viewed as an explicit signal. By contrast, when executives trade their own firm’s stock prior to an IT investment by the firm (potentially influenced by their anticipation and prior knowledge of it) and disclose the trade publicly, the market may view the trade as a tacit signal regardless of whether or not they intended it as a signal (Connelly et al., 2011).<sup>3</sup> Considering the tacit signals in executives’ trading the firm’s stock before the IT investment along with the potential value-realization of IT over time leads us to our first hypothesis.

Specifically, a firm’s senior executives, especially its CIO and CEO, participate in IT investment decisions and are likely to know about their expected impacts. Prior research suggests that IT expenditures succeed when effectively combined with other internal resources and capabilities in a firm (e.g., Bard han et al., 2013). Compared to outsiders, a firm’s executives better understand its existing resources and capabilities that enable successful implementation and use of IT. Executives’ knowledge about an anticipated IT investment and concurrent activities surrounding it, when combined with their knowledge about the firm’s complementary resources and capabilities (both disclosed and nondisclosed to the public), may enable them to better trade the firm’s stock. If executives have the financial and technological acumen to make such trading decisions, their trades prior to a major IT investment can be an effective indicator of the firm’s future success. So, we posit H1, which is also shown in the research model (Figure 1):

H1: The purchase of a firm’s stock by its senior executives before a major IT investment by the firm is positively associated with abnormal returns to the firm.

## BVIT and Strategic Fit

BVIT theories can inform investors in evaluating a firm’s IT investments. Some of these theories focus on the populationaverage value of IT investments across a broad range of firms (e.g., Bharadwaj et al., 1999). Other theories, explaining the contingent value of IT, explain firm heterogeneities in value creation by focusing on the attributes of the investment (e.g., Steelman et al., 2019) or the investing firm (e.g., Mithas & Rust, 2016). Finally, the strategic-fit view provides a contingent framework that offers a systematic way of understanding value heterogeneities by considering attributes of both the IT and the focal firm and thereby generating insights beyond the population-average value of the investment. As such, this view better fits the task of understanding the value that a particular firm would reap from the specific IT investment.

Newness of IT is a key IT attribute that is both readily observable to the firm’s investors and scrutinized in the BVIT literature (e.g., Steelman et al., 2019). On the one hand, IT markets are punctuated by frequent innovations that provide new opportunities to create value (Dos Santos et al., 2012). Firms investing in newer ITs can lead in the IT arena and accrue its prime benefits due to unchallenged access until others catch up to the opportunities that the new IT creates. On the other hand, empirical evidence suggests that IT innovations become more beneficial as related knowledge develops over time (e.g., Wang & Ramiller, 2009). Thus, firms adopting older ITs can benefit from other firms’ knowledge and thereby better utilize such ITs.

Building on the above arguments, the strategic-fit view suggests that the newness of the IT in which the firm invests, alone, does not provide much information about its potential benefits to the firm. Rather, the firm’s existing resources and capabilities determine the extent to which it would benefit from a new IT (Wade & Hulland, 2004). Firms investing in new ITs need to identify and implement promising new ITs and deal with the risks associated with newness. Such firms would benefit from flexible structures that enable learning and quick adjustments to new ITs (Benaroch et al., 2006). By contrast, investing in older ITs requires a context that can use existing internal and external knowledge about which existing ITs the firm should invest in, why it should do so, and how it can best leverage those ITs. Thus, firms investing in older ITs would benefit from stable IT structures that help assimilate the ITs (e.g., Rothaermel, 2001) and support continuous improvement (e.g., He & Wong, 2004; Steelman et al., 2019).

Strategy literature suggests that a firm’s strategy can be viewed in terms of its key abilities that represent the realized nature of the strategy and impact firm performance (e.g., DeSarbo et al., 2005). Similarly, the firm’s IT strategy can help assess the value of its IT investments (e.g., Steelman et al., 2019). The typology of revenue-, cost-, and dualemphasis IT strategies, proposed by Mithas and Rust (2016, p. 223), provides key relevant insights:

there are three strategic paths from IT to firm performance: IT can be used to (1) reduce costs by improving productivity and efficiency; (2) increase revenues by fully exploiting opportunities through existing customers, channels, and products/services and by finding or creating new customers, channels, and products/services; or (3) reduce costs and increase revenues simultaneously.

Mithas and Rust further argue that the firm’s strategic posture shapes the way in which it governs IT and manages IT projects, as well as the types of ITs it leverages. Firms with a revenue-emphasis strategy are accustomed to using IT to develop new products and services. So, they would benefit more from investing in new ITs as they offer new ways to generate revenue. This is consistent with the recent findings that firms that explore new opportunities benefit more from new ITs (Steelman et al., 2019). By contrast, firms with a cost-emphasis IT strategy use IT to improve efficiency and reduce costs. Such firms avoid the high costs of learning about new ITs and would benefit more from investing in older ITs that are already widely adopted and can help improve operational efficiency. This is consistent with the recent findings that firms that focus on efficiently exploiting their existing product markets benefit more from existing ITs (Steelman et al., 2019). We outline the conditions of strategic-fit between ITs that are high (low) in newness and a revenue-emphasis (cost-emphasis) IT strategy in Table 1, but do not posit the effect of this strategic fit because of its prior support in the literature (e.g., Steelman et al., 2019).

In contrast to the two extreme strategies, a dual-emphasis IT strategy, which Mithas and Rust (2016, p. 223) label as “IT strategic ambidexterity,” shows a mix of traits. Based on insights from studies that consider other hybrid strategic archetypes (e.g., Moore, 2005; Steelman et al., 2019), firms with a dual-emphasis strategy can benefit from investments in either new or mature IT depending on conditions. Therefore, unlike external investors in firms with the two extreme IT strategies, investors in dual-emphasis firms may find it difficult to assess whether investments in ITs that are high or low in newness would be more beneficial to the firm. The firm’s senior executives, who possess rich knowledge of the firm’s abilities and potential complementary investments, would thus have a greater advantage over external investors in terms of their ability to assess the IT investment’s long-term impact. Hence, for dual-emphasis firms, we expect only the executives’ purchases before the investment—and not the fit between IT newness and IT strategy—to be associated with the investment’s impact (as per H1).

## Senior Executives’ Stock Purchase: Complementary Insights to Strategic Fit

Despite the insights gained based on a firm’s IT strategy and the newness of the IT in which it invests, external investors face information asymmetries when gauging the future impact of IT investments. They can discern the firm’s realized IT strategy from public information (e.g., the firm’s language in explaining its IT initiatives in 10-K reports), but lack access to the other firm-specific factors (e.g., the firm’s hiring of human talent to leverage the IT investment and the technical acumen of engineers assigned to projects associated with the IT investment) that can potentially affect the firm’s ability to benefit from the IT investment (Nevo & Wade, 2010).

Moreover, outsiders face different information asymmetries when contemplating buying stock of revenue- or costemphasis firms that are making IT investments. For revenueemphasis firms investing in new ITs, the outsiders face questions about an unknown IT and how it will fit within the firm, given the limited span of the time that the firm has to bebecome ready to assimilate it. For cost-emphasis firms investing in less new ITs, the outsiders face questions about the firm’s ability to repurpose a well-known IT and reap efficiency gains from it. Despite this difference in the nature of the questions faced by outsiders, the resulting information asymmetry can be reduced through insiders’ insights about internal activities and capabilities. For outsiders appraising the value of major IT investments by firms with a dualemphasis IT strategy (i.e., those who combine behaviors of firms pursuing the extreme strategies of revenue- and costemphasis), it would not be clear whether or not such firms would benefit from investing in new ITs as prior strategic-fit literature offers little insight into such hybrid firms.

In sum, the firm’s unobserved concurrent actions and latent capabilities related to major IT investments combine with the firm’s potential strategic ambiguity (in dual-emphasis firms) to increase the information asymmetry between the firm and outsiders. In order to assess the expected impacts of an IT investment, we need to address such information asymmetry, and as discussed above, executives’ trading can help do so (John & Lang, 1991; Piotroski & Roulstone, 2005). Executive trades that are congruent with the fit between the IT strategy and the newness of the IT investment (i.e., when there is a fit, and the executives purchase the firm’s stock to a greater extent prior to the IT investment) indicate that the executives see the strategic fit as beneficial. Conversely, executives purchasing the firm’s stock to a lower extent before the IT investment when there is a fit between the IT strategy and the newness of the IT indicate that they either do not view the fit as beneficial or have some reservations. Thus, the stock purchasing by a firm’s senior executives before an IT investment announcement is not only directly associated with the subsequent abnormal returns to the firm, but also has a moderating association with the relationship between the strategic fit and abnormal returns. So, we posit:

H2: The purchase of the stock of a firm with a revenue (cost) emphasis by its senior executives before a major IT investment is associated with a positive (negative) relationship between the IT’s newness and the abnormal returns to the firm.

## Differences across Executives

As mentioned earlier, this study addresses the following research question: How are the stock trades by a firm’s senior executives before a major IT investment by the firm associated with the future value to the firm from that IT investment? In addressing this research question, considering the specific role of the specific senior executive (especially the CIO and the CEO in the case of IT investments) may be important, because depending on their role, some executives may make more informative trades. Although firm’s executives are sometimes viewed as homogeneous in their information asymmetry relative to investors when considering non-IT expenditures (e.g., investment in research and development (R&D); Aboody & Lev, 2000), when trading in their firm’s stock before an investment by the firm, executives also rely on managerial foresight about the investment, which they develop idiosyncratically based on their own background and experiences (Ahuja et al., 2005). Along similar lines, the finance literature highlights personal variations in confidence across executives, when exposed to the same companyspecific risks, and unravels the executives’ idiosyncratic backgrounds and experiences as factors explaining this variation (e.g., Malmendier & Tate, 2005). The idiosyncratically developed foresight is also not easily shared due to issues such as hoarding and greater confidence in the knowledge that is acquired through their own experience rather than vicariously from others (Kearns & Sabherwal, 2007; Kim & Mauborgne, 1998).

Executives’ varying perspectives relative to a key decision, such as their limited field of vision, selectively processing some of the phenomena in that limited field, and interpreting those limited observations in a personally idiosyncratic cognitive base, add to the heterogeneity among them in their foresight related to IT investments. Due to this heterogeneity, the participants in major IT investment decisions differ in their opinions on the same investment and expectations regarding its value to the firm (e.g., Enns et al., 2003). Overall, there are reasons to expect heterogeneity among managers in their foresight and perceptions regarding the same IT investment. Therefore, executives may differ in terms of how informative their trades in the firm’s stock before major IT investments might be to external investors. Moreover, signaling theory argues that when interpreting signals, individuals rely on information about both the signal and its source to interpret them (Schoemaker & Day, 2009).

Prior IT strategy literature indicates that we can draw contrasting conclusions regarding the specific nature of this heterogeneity. On the one hand, CIOs, who have a unique perspective regarding IT investments as the leaders of their firms’ IT function, are likely to have greater technological acumen than their peers (Chatterjee et al., 2001; Preston & Karahanna, 2009). This may enable them to better understand the IT trends and intangibles related to an IT investment as well as the firm’s concurrent actions that can complement the specific investment. In the same vein, CIOs can better understand how a current IT investment may fit in the broader plans of the firm (Preston & Karahanna, 2009). The notions of “foreknowledge,” or the “ability to successfully predict” and “discovery,” or the “correct recognition of something that possibly already exists, though hidden from view,” as discussed by Hirshleifer (1971, p. 2), are relevant to these arguments. Thus, CIOs may understand a broader set of ITrelated aspects than other senior executives. They may have greater managerial discretion in IT investments and might be the ones who directly approve them and subsequently lead their effective assimilation (Ravichandran et al., 2009). This gives CIOs greater access to minutiae of the investment that may get lost in the formal discussions with CEOs. Thus, the personal trades by the CIOs in the firm’s stock before major IT investments might be more informative to external investors than those by CEOs.

On the other hand, the business success of IT investments also depends on other non-IT strategic resources, such as R&D (e.g., Bardhan et al., 2013), and the broader financial and business trends, about which CEOs might have greater general knowledge and a broader field of vision than CIOs (Kearns & Sabherwal, 2007). CIOs are more likely to have followed a promotion path from operational IT positions and are less likely than CEOs to have gained experiential knowledge about the broader business trends and market dynamics. So, CIOs might have lower business and financial acumen than CEOs. Also, CEOs have greater managerial discretion than CIOs concerning the firm’s other investments, in general, and may also have greater control over the IT budget. These considerations point to the opposite conclusion than the above: personal trades by the CIOs in the firm’s stock before major IT investments might be less informative to external investors than trades by CEOs. Thus, there is a lack of clarity if trades by CIOs or CEOs are more indicative of the returns on major IT investments. So, we propose the following competing hypotheses to glean further insights from the stock trades by different senior executives of a firm before a major IT investment:

H3a: The purchase of a firm’s stock by its CIO before a major IT investment has a stronger association with the abnormal returns to the firm than the stock purchase by its CEO.

H3b: The purchase of a firm’s stock by its CEO before a major IT investment has a stronger association with the abnormal returns to the firm than the stock purchase by its CIO.

As mentioned above, this paper focuses on the relationship between the stock trades by a firm’s senior executives before a major IT investment by the firm and that investment’s future value to the firm. The above differences between CIOs and CEOs in terms of their insights for the firm’s abnormal returns would also extend to the moderating effects of their stock purchasing on the relationship between the strategic fit and abnormal returns (as posited in H2).

Prior literature provides evidence about the contested and sometimes political nature of IT investments (e.g., Enns et al., 2003; Menon & Siponen, 2020). Thus, certain IT investments may be made without a CIO’s or CEO’s strong support. Given their different levels of foresight related to the imminent IT investments (as discussed before H3a and H3b) and their different positions in the negotiation process leading to the investment decision, executives may also differ in the extent to which they can appraise the value of strategically fit investments. In the light of the above arguments, we posit H4a and H4b as contrasting hypotheses (building on H2 by using H3a and H3b) regarding the difference between CIOs and CEOs in terms of the moderating association of their trades on the effect of IT newness under different IT strategies:

H4a: The purchase of the stock of a firm with a revenue (cost) emphasis by its CIO before a major IT investment is associated with a stronger positive (negative) relationship between the IT’s newness and the abnormal returns to the firm to a greater extent than stock purchase by the firm’s CEO.

## H4b:

The purchase of the stock of a firm with a revenue (cost) emphasis by its CEO before a major IT investment is associated with a stronger positive (negative) relationship between the IT’s newness and the abnormal returns to the firm to a greater extent than stock purchase by the firm’s CIO.

## Empirical Methods

## Sample

We build the study sample by focusing on public announcements about investments in 14 types of ITs.<sup>5</sup> We focus on these ITs because their market value has been examined in prior event studies (e.g., Bose & Leung, 2013; Hendricks et al., 2007; Jeong & Stylianou, 2010), indicating that investments in them receive public coverage.

We search Lexis-Nexis and Factiva databases for public announcements over a span of 15 years (2002–2016) to create an initial set of 3,208 public announcements.<sup>6</sup> We only include announcements that are (1) by public firms traded on NYSE, AMEX, or NASDAQ; (2) by firms that made at least one other public announcement in another year (for withinsubject comparison in a fixed-effects estimation); and (3) at least two years apart from an earlier announcement by the firm that was also retained in the sample.<sup>7</sup> Also, following prior literature (Bessembinder & Zhang, 2013; Gay, 2015; Lansford, 2005), we drop 78 observations with confounding announcements (e.g., earnings, seasoned equity offerings, mergers and acquisitions, and initialization of dividend payment) in a 14-trading-day window around the IT investment announcement (–7d, +7d). Additionally, 232 observations were later dropped when estimating the regression models after removal of fixed-effects, resulting in 2,898 observations. We use complementary data on stock trading by the firm’s senior executives (specifically, C-level executives, e.g., CEOs, CFOs, COOs, CIOs, CTOs, as listed in the BoardEx database) subject to the disclosure requirements of section 16(2) of the Securities and Exchange Act of 1932 from the Thomson Reuters database for years 2002–2016.

## Measures

To measure the long-term returns for firms investing in IT, we use the two-year buy-and-hold abnormal returns (BHAR) from the day on which the IT investment is publicly announced. Short-term market reaction measures, such as abnormal returns cumulated over a window of a few days after the announcement, are limited in their ability to see results from a major IT investment. The longer time period used for BHAR addresses this issue. Additionally, BHAR accounts for the uncontrolled impact of other confounding factors by finding matched firms with no investment and adjusting the focal firm’s market performance by removing the gains accrued to a similar no-investment firm during the same period. To estimate BHAR, we follow Bessembinder and Zhang (2013) and first find a matching firm for each event observation (i.e., a firm investing in IT) by considering the closest book-to-market ratio (BM) among firms with a market capitalization of 70% to 130% of the focal firm, based on the values of BM and size (market capitalization) in the latest December before the announcement. Further, we ensured the matching firm has the same two-digit SIC code as the focal firm.<sup>8</sup> After finding a matching firm, we estimate the abnormal return as the difference between the daily compounded returns of the sample firm and its match over a two-year period.

We follow Aboody and Lev (2000) and first calculate the senior executives’ purchase of firm shares in the 30 days before the investment. Specifically, we calculate the extent of an executives’ purchase of firm shares by counting the number of shares purchased in the 30 days prior to investment. Second, to ensure that the trades we consider are not routine trades that happen to occur in the month prior to the investment, we follow Cohen et al. (2012) and consider executives with at least three consecutive years of trading in the same 30- day window, prior to the event year, as routine traders.<sup>9</sup> We set the value of purchasing by routine traders to zero in this step while keeping the value of purchasing for those without the designation, that is, the opportunistic traders, the same as that estimated in the first step. Based on this purchasing measure for each executive in the firm in the month before the investment announcement, we form variables corresponding to purchasing by all executives (Exec\_Purchase), only CIOs (CIO\_Purchase), and only CEOs (CEO\_Purchase).

To identify IT strategies—revenue-emphasis, cost-emphasis, and dual-emphasis—we rely on the definitions provided by Mithas and Rust (2016), who, as noted earlier, focus on whether IT can be used for “generating new revenue,” “reduce costs,” or “reduce costs and increase revenues simultaneously.” Their view of IT strategy as the ability to use IT to accomplish certain goals is consistent with others viewing IT strategy in terms of abilities (e.g., Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Wu et al., 2015). Surveys are commonly used to measure such abilities and thereby identify IT strategy, including in the above IS studies. However, survey data is often not available in panel data studies over several years, as is the case here. So, we rely on a stochastic frontier function approach, which has previously been used to measure the abilities of a firm in turning its inputs to outputs (e.g., Ayabakan et al., 2017; Dutta et al., 1999, 2005). We similarly use it to first estimate how well a firm utilizes (efficiency rate) its IT budget expenditure to maximize its revenue and minimize its operational costs. Then, we compare the normalized values of a firm’s efficiency rates in turning its IT budget expenditure into an increase in revenue and a decrease in operational costs. Firms with revenue-increasing and costcutting efficiency rates that differ less than one standard deviation are categorized as dual-emphasis, whereas firms with efficiency rates that differ more than one standard deviation are categorized into revenue-emphasis or cost-emphasis depending on which efficiency rate is higher. The Appendix further explains this approach. Our results are robust when using alternative strategy measures, as mentioned later.

We measure the newness of publicly announced IT investments (IT\_Newness) as the inverse value of the number of years (plus one, to avoid a zero denominator) elapsed from the first mention of the specific IT in ABI/INFORM Global<sup>TM</sup> indexed business and IT publications. Since our study covers a long time span of 15 years, the same IT can undergo significant changes in nature across this time period. For example, when an IT resurfaced within those publications, such as Business Intelligence and Analytics in 2005 and 2010, we compute the time elapsed based on the last year of resurfacing (1 / (number of years since the resurfacing year + 1)). The Appendix provides further details about how a resurfacing year is identified, as well as details about the measurement of IT newness. Bessembinder and Zhang (2013) show that estimates of BHAR based solely on values of size and BM prior to the event can be biased. Therefore, we regress BHAR on the independent variables along with the abnormal values (the value of the variable for sample firm minus the value of the variable for the matching firm) of several control variables shown in Table 2, which provides an overview of each variable, its measure, and the source used. Table 3 provides information about the industry breakdown of the firm-year observations (Panel A), attributes of sample firms (Panel B), and summary statistics of executive trading (Panel C). Table 4 provides the correlation matrix.

## Hypotheses Tests

We start our empirical estimation with a baseline model that only adds IT\_Newness to the controls. Since IT strategy is time-invariant in our sample, including IT\_Newness tests if it can explain any additional within-firm variations in BHAR. Therefore, the baseline equation is:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \left(\text { Baseline }\right) \mathrm{BHAR} _ {1 t} & = \alpha_ {0} + \beta_ {i} \times \left(\mathrm{IT} _ {-} \text { Newness } _ {i t}\right) + \\ & \beta_ {\text { control }} \times (\text { Controls }) + c _ {i} + \text { YEAR } + \text { TYPE } + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}
$$

where subscripts i and t indicate the firm and the observation year, respectively, “Controls” is the vector of controls mentioned earlier, $\mathrm { c } _ { i }$ is the firm-specific time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity, YEAR and TYPE are time and technology dummies, respectively, and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is the idiosyncratic error. We then test H1 by adding Exec\_Purchas $\mathrm { e } _ { i t }$ to the baseline equation:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{BHAR} _ {i t} & = \alpha_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \times (\text { IT\_Newness } _ {i t}) + \\ \beta_ {\text { control }} & \times (\text { Controls }) + c _ {i} + \text { YEAR } + \text { TYPE } + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{Eq 1}
$$

To test H3a and H3b, we replace Exec\_Purchase with the purchase indices for CIOs and CEOs as separate covariates:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & \mathrm{BHAR} _ {i t} = \alpha_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \times (\mathrm{IT} _ {-} \text {Newness} _ {i t}) + \\ & (\text {Eq2}) \beta_ {2} \times (\mathrm{CIO} _ {-} \text {Purchase} _ {i t}) + \beta_ {3} \times (\mathrm{CEO} _ {-} \text {Purchase} _ {i t}) \\ & + \beta_ {\text {control}} \times (\text {Controls}) + c _ {i} + \text {YEAR} + \text {TYPE} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}
$$

## Tests of Underlying Assumptions

In our main analyses, we have focused on long-term BHAR to assess the association between executives’ trading before an IT investment and returns that investing firms receive relative to peers that do not make such an investment. In these analyses, based on the evidence from prior literature, we assumed that investors have limited attention, which hinders them from evaluating the impact of IT investments in a short period. We examine the short-term impact by testing models with abnormal returns estimated in shorter (1–7 days) windows around the IT investment announcement. This analysis, coupled with our main analysis of long-term returns, can also shed light on the speed at which financial markets process information (Fornell et al., 2006) about announced IT investment. Table 7 provides the results.

We find that consistent with our arguments, the association between executives’ trading preceding the IT investment and short-term market reaction is not significant. This shows that the information in executives’ trades is not evident to the financial markets in a short time period surrounding the announcement. Over time, more information about the impact of investment becomes available to the markets (e.g., superior productivity, improved sales, etc.), resulting in a significant association between executives’ trades made before the announcement and the abnormal returns two years after the announcement.

Next, we note that in gauging the relationship between stock purchases by executives’ before an IT investment and the IT investment’s long-term impact on firm value, we assume that the association is driven by the signaling relevance of those trades to the focal IT investment. If this assumption is correct, the association should be weaker (stronger) as the signaling (predictive) relevance of the trades to the focal investment becomes weaker (stronger).

One way to test this assumption (i.e., changing the signaling relevance of the trades to the focal investment) is by considering the length of the window during which executives trades are accumulated. If our assumption is correct, as this window widens, trades with purposes irrelevant to the event are more likely to be captured when measuring the executives’ net purchases before the announcement. In that case, the overall relevance of Exec\_Purchase, CIO\_Purchase, and CEO\_Purchase to the announcement should subside, causing a drop in the observed coefficients of those variables. In other words, widening (restricting) the window used to estimate Exec\_Purchase, CIO\_Purchase, and CEO\_Purchase should be associated with weaker (stronger) signaling relevance. So, we run our models with Exec\_Purchase, CIO\_Purchase, and CEO\_Purchase estimated based on trades made in a 1-, 2-, 3-, 5-, 6-, and 7-week windows before the event (our main measure is based on trades made in the four weeks before the event).

Table 8 reports the results of the above analysis and shows that while the strength of IT\_Newness remains relatively unchanged across the models, the effect sizes of Exec\_Purchase, CIO\_Purchase, and CEO\_Purchase are larger (smaller) with a decrease (increase) in the length of the window during which these purchases are estimated. This indicates that executives’ trading prior to a major IT investment, rather than other potential factors, is the more likely explanation of the impact of the IT investment on the long-term value of the firm.

Subsequently, we examine whether the observed association of the stock purchasing by a firm’s senior executives with BHAR is due to reasons other than the information asymmetry between them and external investors. If the observed association is due to explanations other than information asymmetry, we will not find a difference between the associations of stock purchasing and BHAR in firms with higher and lower information asymmetry. One way to test this is by comparing firms covered by fewer analysts with firms covered by more analysts. Since analysts operate as informational bridges (e.g., Luo et al., 2015), firms with lower analyst coverage have greater information asymmetries with their investors. But a firm’s analyst coverage can change over time. So, we seek an external event that reduces the number of analysts covering a firm without the effect of the firm’s IT investments. Specifically, we use a set of exogenous shocks, identified by Derrien and Kecskés (2013), and use broker closures and broker mergers as events that can reduce the level of analyst coverage exogenously.

We identify broker closures and broker mergers by examining press releases related to brokers in Lexis-Nexis/Factiva and Yearbooks released by Securities Industry Association. Then, each broker’s portfolio of firm coverage is identified through records in the Institutional Brokers Estimates System (IBES) database. We select a subsample of firm-year observations (Sample A') where the firm loses some analysts due to closures or mergers in the 12 months before the IT investment. To make a fair comparison between this subsample with increased information asymmetry and a subsample where information asymmetry is unchanged, we form a matching subsample of firm-year observations (Sample B'). The matching subsample includes firms that belong to the same two-digit SIC code and the same IT strategic category as a focal observation in subsample A', and also have analyst coverage within 30 percent of the coverage for the focal firm in sample A' before the closure or merger of its brokers. We could find only 156 observations with a broker closure or merger prior to the IT investment and at least one matched firm. So, we estimate the association between Exec\_Purchase with BHAR in the entire sample and not the three IT strategies. The results show that the coefficient of Exec\_Purchase (2.50) is significantly (p < 0.05) higher in the model estimated based on sample A' than its coefficient (1.26) in the model estimated based on sample B'. Thus, the trades made by executives of firms with increased information asymmetry (i.e., reduced analyst coverage) are more informative. This suggests that information asymmetry, rather than other potential reasons, is the likely explanation of the observed association of the senior executives’ stock purchase with BHAR.

Next, we examine whether two reasons other than senior executives’ foreknowledge and insights prior to the upcoming IT investment explain the observed association of the stock trading by a firm’s senior executives with BHAR. Specifically, we first investigate if intentionally withheld material information by senior executives can explain our results. We do this by searching SEC’s Litigation Releases Archive for any possible litigations pursued by the SEC about the trades in our sample. We do not find any instance of a trade included in our study being litigated. The no-litigation status of the trades in the study indicates that those trades were not made using time-sensitive information withheld from the public.

We then investigate the potential effect of the senior executives’ activities after the major IT investment. To do so, we examine our models in a subsample of firms with investing executives leaving the firm in a short period after the investment $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ when the investment is not made operational yet). Major IT investments are believed to undergo implementation periods of at least one year (e.g., Hendricks et al., 2007); so, the impact of the investment on long-term firm value would be less likely to depend on the engagement of an executive who leaves the firm prior to full implementation. Because CIOs are operationally close to investments and responsible for the implementation, we conduct this subsample analysis around CIO departures. We select a subsample of firm-year observations (Sample $\mathbf { A } " )$ where the CIO departed the firm within six months after the investment. This reduces our sample to only 231 observations. So, we focus on the estimation of the direct associations of the CIO versus CEO trading and do not analyze the three strategy subsamples. We construct a matching subsample of CEOs not departing the firm within six months (Sample $\mathbf { B ^ { \prime \prime } } )$ . Each observation in subsample $\mathbf { B } "$ belongs to the same two-digit SIC code and the same strategic category as a focal observation in subsample $\mathbf { A } "$ The estimates from these two models reveal no significant difference $( p > 0 . 1 0 )$ between the coefficient estimates of CIO\_Purchase in samples with departing CIOs (1.63 in Sample $\mathbf { A } " )$ and without departing CIOs (1.90 in Sample $\mathbf { B ^ { \prime \prime } } )$ and between the coefficient estimates of CEO\_Purchase in samples with departing CIOs (1.12 in Sample A') and without departing CIOs (1.00 in Sample B"). The relative equivalence of coefficient estimates of CIO\_Purchase in samples with and without departing CIOs shows that the senior executives’ activities after the investment do not explain the information asymmetry between senior executives and external investors. $\mathrm { I f C I O s } ^ { \prime }$ activities subsequent to the investment were the main driver of long-term market returns for the investment, the subsample of firms undergoing no change in the investing CIO would obtain significantly higher payoffs than the firms in which the investing CIO departs.

Finally, we investigate whether the higher association of CIO\_Purchase and BHAR compared to that of CEO\_Purchase and BHAR is due to a generally higher financial acumen of CIOs rather than their operational proximity to the investment. To do so, we examine the association of CIO\_Purchase and CEO\_Purchase with BHAR while considering a well-studied major non-IT investment, that is, corporate acquisition $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ Gaur et al., 2013) of a non-IT firm as the event of interest. If the alternative explanation (i.e., CIOs having a greater financial acumen) are true, CIO\_Purchase would have a stronger association with BHAR of the acquisition compared to CEO\_Purchase. However, CEO\_Purchase has a positive and significant association $( 2 . 6 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ with acquisition BHAR, but the association of CIO\_Purchase is nonsignificant $( 0 . 1 0 , p > 0 . 1 0 )$ . This finding is inconsistent with the alternative explanation (greater financial acumen of CIOs) and consistent with the explanation offered in this paper, which is based on $\mathrm { C I O s } '$ operational proximity to IT investment, richer insights about general IT trends, and higher foreknowledge about how a certain IT investment fits in the firm.

## Further Issues Related to Identification and Endogeneity

An alternative explanation to our findings is that the observed BHAR associated with the Exec\_Purchase is not due to the investment itself and is instead due to investors’ positive reaction to executives’ purchasing of firms’ stock and lowering the firm’s cost of accessing capital in the form of equity. To test this explanation, we append our observations with counterfactuals that are no-investment (we find a BHAR match firm for those counterfactuals), and test to see whether the observed positive association between Exec\_Purchase and BHAR is only limited to investment observations or if noinvestment observations also see a larger BHAR. We find a counterfactual for each observation similar to how we find a BHAR match by choosing the second closest firm (the first closest firm is used to form BHAR match) in terms of the book-to-market ratio (BM) among firms with a market capitalization between 70% and 130% of an IT investment firm. Then, we find a BHAR match for the counterfactual firm by choosing the firm closest to it in terms of BM among the no-investment firms with a market capitalization between 70% and 130% of the counterfactual. We successfully match 1,865 observations to a no-investment counterfactual. BHAR for the counterfactual is estimated based on the date of investment for its matched investment firm. Then, we estimate the following equation:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & \mathrm{BHAR} _ {i t} = \alpha_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \times (\text { Exec\_Purchase } _ {i t}) + \\ & \beta_ {2} \times (\text { IT\_Invest } _ {i t}) + \beta_ {3} \times (\text { Exec\_Purchase } _ {i t}) \times \\ & (\text { Eq   5 }) \left(\text { IT\_Invest } _ {i t}\right) + \beta_ {\text { control }} \times (\text { Controls }) + c _ {i} + \\ & \text { YEAR } + \text { TYPE } + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}
$$

where IT\_Invest is a dummy that equals 1 if the observation is a firm that invested in IT at the time that BHAR is estimated and 0 for counterfactual observations. If the effect of $\beta _ { 1 }$ is independent of IT\_Inves $\mathfrak { t } _ { i t } ( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \beta _ { 3 }$ is not significant), then one can conclude that regardless of investment in IT, Exec\_Purchase can enhance the firm’s BHAR. Estimation of the model without the interaction term (Exec\_Purchase \* IT\_Invest) shows that neither IT\_Invest (0.25) nor

Exec\_Purchase (0.46) significantly $( p \ > \ 0 . 1 0 )$ explains variations in BHAR. However, the estimation of the model with the interaction term (i.e., EQ 5) shows that the significance of Exec\_Purchase is contingent on whether or not the firm has invested in IT, with the interaction between Exec\_Purchase and IT\_Invest being significant and positive $( 1 . 0 3 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . This further shows that Exec\_Purchase alone does not lead to a higher BHAR.

Another estimation challenge is the possibility that executives who can recognize the fit between IT\_Newness and IT strategy increase their purchases based on the strategic fit. When left untreated, the model assumes equal coefficients for firm executives who are familiar with strategic fit consequences and those who are not. To better model this selection behavior by executives, we adopt a control function approach that allows modeling the executives’ endogenous choice of purchasing based on strategic fit in the first stage and accounting for the bias they cause in the second stage. We follow Garen’s (1984) procedure to estimate regressions with continuous endogenous variables (i.e., CIO\_Purchase and CEO\_Purchase in this study) controlled by the level of another variable (i.e., IT\_Newness). Garen’s procedure is similar to Heckman’s (1977) except that the dependent variable(s) can be continuously measured in Garen’s first step. Since Garen’s approach assumes the exogeneity of the explanatory variables that are present in both stages, we instrument IT\_Newness in both stages. Specifically, relying on the previous literature that suggests industry and geography influence investments in IT (e.g., Mitra, 2005), we consider the level and lagged values (1 year) of the average IT\_Newness of investments made by firms with the same twodigit SIC code as well as the average IT\_Newness of investments made by firms headquartered in the same metro area as instruments of IT\_Newness for a focal observation. This approach is consistent with existing examples of IT-related instruments (e.g., Mithas & Rust, 2016). These Hausmantype instruments are also common in similar studies (e.g., Aral et al., 2017; Cachon et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2017).

This set of instruments shows strong statistical relevance to the IT\_Newness. Specifically, the Cragg–Donald Wald $F _ { - }$ statistic exceeds 5% maximal bias of the IV estimator relative to OLS in both equations (Stock & Yogo, 2010), where Stock and Yogo’s critical value for one endogenous variable and four instruments is 16.85, rejecting the null hypothesis of the equations being weakly identified. Moreover, Sargan’s (1958) test of over-identification fails to reject the null hypothesis that the instruments used in the models are exogenous to error terms. To ensure that the instruments do not violate the exclusion restrictions, we include industry-time and location-time fixed-effects in all our models.

We model the first stages of Garen’s control function approach as the following:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { CIO\_Purchase } _ {i t} = B _ {1} \times \left(I T \_ N e w n e s s _ {i t}\right) + C o n t r o l s \\ + \eta_ {i t} + c _ {i} ^ {\prime} + \text { Industry - Year } + \text { Area - Year } \end{array} \tag {Eq6}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { CIO\_Purchase } _ {i t} = B _ {1} \times (I T \_ N e w n e s s _ {i t}) + \text { Controls } \\ + \eta_ {i t} ^ {\prime} + c _ {i} ^ {\prime \prime} + \text { Industry - Year } + \text { Area - Year } \end{array} \tag {Eq7}
$$

where controls in both equations are BM, shares and options granted (SOG), and shares and options exercised (SOE), and CIO and CEO purchases are abnormal net values. This specification follows Piotroski and Roulstone’s (2005) model of factors influencing executives’ purchase of firm securities. Considering EQs 6 and 7, along with EQ 4, and based on Garen’s procedure, we estimate the following equation to render unbiased coefficient estimates:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathrm{BHAR} _ {i t} = \alpha_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \times (\text {IT\_Newness} _ {i t}) \\ + \beta_ {2} \times (\text {CIO\_Purchase} _ {i t}) + \beta_ {3} \times (\text {CEO\_Purchase} _ {i t}) \\ + \beta_ {4} \times (\text {IT\_Newness} _ {i t}) \times (\text {CIO\_Purchase} _ {i t}) \\ + \beta_ {5} \times (\text {IT\_Newness} _ {i t}) \times (\text {CEO\_Purchase} _ {i t}) \\ + \beta_ {6} \times (\text {CIO\_Purchase} _ {i t}) \times (\text {CEO\_Purchase} _ {i t}) \\ + \beta_ {7} \times \eta_ {i t} + \beta_ {8} \times \eta_ {i t} \times (\text {CIO\_Purchase} _ {i t}) \\ + \beta_ {9} \times \eta_ {i t} ^ {\prime} + \beta_ {1 0} \times \eta_ {i t} ^ {\prime} \times (\text {CEO\_Purchase} _ {i t}) + \\ \beta_ {\text {control}} \times (\text {Controls}) + c _ {i} + \text {Industry - Year} + \\ \text {Area - Year} + \text {TYPE} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array} \tag {Eq8}
$$

where $\eta _ { i t }$ and $\eta _ { i t } ^ { \prime }$ are the residuals from equations (6) and (7), respectively, and were interpreted as excess or above expectations CIO\_Purchase and CEO\_Purchase, respectively. Thus, coefficients of terms including $\eta _ { i t }$ and $\eta _ { i t } ^ { \prime }$ can be interpreted as the extent of BHARs related to the portions of CIO\_Purchase and CEO\_Purchase that are not driven by IT\_Newness and other known influencers (therefore, they are more likely to be influenced by internal factors interacting with the investment). ## The Predictive Power of Considering Executives’ Trades

As discussed above, our estimations of the association between executives’ purchasing the firm’s stock and BHAR provide supports for associative hypotheses 1, 2, 3a, and 3b, and are robust with respect to numerous additional tests. Nonetheless, these econometric estimations are limited in gauging the predictive power of executives’ trades for investors.

Next, we examine the predictive implications of the associative model developed above by evaluating the performance of some simple predictive models and comparing the accuracy of these models with and without the information about executives’ trades. In conducting these analyses, we benefit from prior recommendations (e.g., Shmueli & Koppius, 2011).

We first dichotomize BHAR values in the pooled sample into those with nonnegative and negative values. This dichotomization has a predictive appeal as it turns the prediction task into a classification problem where an investor uses an array of public information about the firm, its investments, and the financial trades of the firm’s executives to correctly predict if the firm’s investment in IT leads to a no-loss (nonnegative BHAR) scenario for the investor. We use a 60/40 split to divide our pool of observations into training and testing subsamples. Then, we run a series of simple logistics regression models on the training subsample and use the model parameters learned in the training set to score the classification problem in the test subsample.

We compare the predictive accuracies of the following logistics regression models: only control variables (controls-only model); controls plus IT strategy dummies (IT-strategy-added model), controls plus IT\_Newness (IT\_Newness-added model); controls plus IT strategy, IT\_Newness, and their interaction (strategic-fit model); controls plus Exec\_Purchase controls plus (trading model); Exec\_Purchase added to the strategic-fit model without any interactions (additive model); Exec\_Purchase added to the strategic-fit model along with interactions among Exec\_Purchase, IT strategy, and IT\_Newness (complementary model); CIO\_Purchase and CEO\_Purchase added to the strategic-fit model along with interactions among CIO\_Purchase, CEO\_Purchase, IT strategy, and IT\_Newness (differentiated complementary model).

Table 10 presents the details of the factors and the prediction indices in each model. Against the benchmark of 50 percent accuracy in a fully random prediction (i.e., when the investor flips a coin to decide about whether or not investing in the stock of a particular firm results in a no-loss scenario), the control-only model shows a 66 percent accuracy rate, whereas the additions of IT\_Newness (IT\_Newness-added model) and IT strategy dummies (IT-strategy-added model) raise the prediction to 69 percent and 70 percent, respectively. When strategic fit is considered (strategic-fit-only model), the accuracy of prediction rises to 75 percent. This means that considering insights from the strategic-fit theories of BVIT help raise an investors’ classification accuracy by about 9 percent. A model that replaces strategic-fit factors with executives’ purchasing (trading-only model) renders about the same rate of accuracy, that is, 75 percent.

When insights from both executives’ purchasing and strategicfit factors are included (the additive model), the accuracy rises about 10 percent and reaches 85 percent. If executives purchasing and strategic-fit factors carried the same predictive insights, an additive model would not have increased by such magnitude. This further highlights the non-overlapping insights that considering executives’ purchasing brings to the mix. Further, a complementary model that considers the interactions between Exec\_Purchase, IT strategy, and IT\_Newness renders an accuracy rate of 88 percent, providing evidence that information about executives’ purchasing also complements the insights gained from the strategic-fit factors. If we consider a similar complementary model but only include the CIO’s stock purchases, accuracy increases to 91 percent, whereas if we only include the CEO’s stock purchases, accuracy drops to 80 percent, further showing the greater insights from CIO’s stock purchases than CEO’s. Finally, the highest rate of accuracy, that is, 92 percent, is achieved in a complementary model that includes information about both CIO’s and CEO’s purchases but differentiates between them (differentiated complementary model). We test the predictive model using four alternative techniques (naïve Bayesian, random forest, decision tree, and neural network) instead of logistic regression and find the results to be consistent and highly predictive.

## Discussion

Overall, the study unravels four major findings. First, it provides evidence that the purchase of a firm’s stock by its senior executives, especially CIOs, is associated with, and can help predict, abnormal returns from the firm’s subsequent major investments in IT. This implies that the benefit external investors obtain from purchasing a firm’s stock after it announces a major IT investment increases with an increase in the firm’s senior executives’ purchase of its stock before that announcement. Second, the paper shows that the executives’ purchase of their firm’s stock is associated with a stronger positive (negative) relationship between the newness of the IT in which the firm invests and abnormal returns from that investment in firms pursuing a revenue-emphasis (costemphasis) IT strategy. Third, executives’ stock purchasing predicts returns even in situations (such as dual-emphasis IT strategy) where the potential value of an IT investment is unclear when based on the IT that is being invested in and the firm’s IT strategy. Together, the second and third findings indicate that executives’ purchase of firm shares complements (for revenue- and cost-emphasis firms), or fills the void in (for dual-emphasis firms), the information that external investors can otherwise obtain. Finally, CIOs’ stock purchases are more informative than purchases by CEOs. This is seen in the results for the direct association of executives’ trades with BHAR (i.e., the first finding above), their moderating association with the association between strategically-fit IT investments and BHAR (i.e., the second finding above), and the hybrid strategy (i.e., dual-emphasis firms), where trades by CIOs have a significant predictive impact but trades by the other senior executives do not (this relates to the third finding above).

The paper’s results should be viewed in light of its limitations. First, the sample is based on publicly-announced IT investments. However, unannounced IT investments are also a source of information asymmetry, and insider trades can reduce such asymmetry as well. Data limitations preclude us from examining the effects of such unannounced IT investments, although the results remain robust when we control for instances where a firm increases its IT budget expenditure. Second, the sample is built by focusing on investments in a set of 14 major ITs that are publicly announced. Focusing on a finite set of ITs was necessary to obtain the keywords that help to identify announcements, but our set of observed investments may not cover other important types of IT investments by firms. Third, as mentioned earlier, the signaling literature notes that signals can be deliberate or unintentional. We are unable to assess the reasons due to which the senior executives purchased their firm’s stock (e.g., whether it was to make personal gains, send a message to external investors, or manipulate the market). Relatedly, while we have viewed senior executives’ stock trades as signals (consistent with prior literature, e.g., John & Lang, 1991; John & Mishra, 1990), we do not present them as intended signals. Finally, we had to drop observations with confounding investments within two years of a focal investment to ensure that the estimated BHAR is due to the focal investment event. Thus, our main sample excludes situations where firms make frequent IT investments and announce them publicly, although our estimations based on 1-year BHAR include those originally-dropped observations.

Despite the above limitations, this paper offers several theoretical contributions to the literature on the business value of IT (BVIT), CIOs, and insider trading.

Contributions to the BVIT literature: There is extensive prior literature on BVIT (e.g., Bardhan et al., 2013; Lee & Menon, 2000; Mithas & Rust, 2016), and especially on IT strategic fit (e.g., Preston & Karahanna, 2009; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). The strategic-fit view suggests that the firm’s strategy and the visible aspect of the IT investment (e.g., its newness) interact to explain impacts on business value (e.g., Steelman et al., 2019). This study shows that considering the purchase of the firm’s stock by its senior executives, especially before its major IT investments, increases the ability to predict the impact of the IT investments. Sabherwal et al., (2019) recently highlighted the moderating impact of external conditions on the strategic fit-performance link. Our findings unravel executives’ purchasing as a signal of internal conditions, such as the firm’s capabilities, intended investments in complementary resources, and hiring of new personnel who may help leverage the IT. Thus, the study contributes to the BVIT literature by highlighting the signaling implications of the executives’ trading of firm’s stock.

Moreover, the predictive ability of the BVIT theories subsides when unobservable factors change the dynamics of value creation or when the observable elements of strategy and the IT investment lie in the blind spots of such theories. For example, one blind spot of the strategic-fit view in BVIT is when firms pursuing a hybrid strategy (i.e., dual-emphasis firms) make IT investments (Sabherwal et al., 2019). This is a significant gap because of the importance of such hybrid strategies. For example, Mithas and Rust (2016, p. 233) find that “at higher levels of IT investments, dual-emphasis firms can significantly outperform revenue- and cost-emphasis firms.” Consistent with Sabherwal et al., we find that, in the case of firms pursuing a dual-emphasis IT strategy, factors identified by existing theories, such as IT newness, might not explain the business value of IT. More importantly, we find that this gap can be addressed by considering the trades by executives, which can be a reliable predictor of the value of IT investments in firms pursuing strategic ambidexterity.

Contributions to the literature on CIOs: Heeding the recent calls to unfold the role of top management in IS research (e.g., Angst et al., 2017; Salge et al., 2015), and building on the finance literature on executive trading around a firm’s capital expenditure (e.g., Chen et al., 2017) and the developing insights on stock trading from the IS literature (Amir et al., 2019), this study highlights the role of executives’ trading as a signal to external investors. This signal adds to, interacts with, and complements the observable aspects of the firm’s IT strategy and the specific IT investment to provide information about the value from IT investments.

We believe that this study might be the first to apply a hybrid of associative and predictive lenses to understand how trades by executives enhances the insights from formal theories of strategic management that explain the value a firm obtains from its capital expenditures. It might also be the first to show that, when considering trades that a firm’s senior executives make around the time of major IT investments by the firm, the trades by its CIOs can better predict the change in firm value than the trades by its other senior executives. Although understanding CIOs and their behavior is a part of the corporate research in IS, the CIOs’ personal actions, including their stock trades, have received little prior attention, in contrast to trades by CEOs and CFOs. Our results highlight the benefits of scrutinizing personal actions by CIOs to predict the impacts of major IT investments by their firms.

Contributions to the broader research on insider trading: We believe this study makes two contributions to the broader literature on insider trading. First, prior research on insider trading around a firm’s capital expenditures remains silent on how insights from such trades interact with insights from strategic theories that also attempt to explain the success of capital expenditures. This paper highlights the benefits of integrating a prevalent strategic theory lens explaining gains from capital expenditures (i.e., the strategic-fit theory) with the insider trading literature. Specifically, we show how insider trades add significant predictive power to the insights from the existing theories regarding the causal effects of firm expenditures. This study might be the first to propose, and empirically demonstrate, such use of the predictive power of insider trading to augment existing causal insights from other theories.

Moreover, although the literature occasionally studies differences in the informativeness of trades by various executives— mostly focused on the two most powerful executives, CEOs and CFOs (Ozkan and Trzeciakiewicz, 2014; Wang et al., 2012)—such comparative research is done in isolation from capital expenditures and instead focuses on general trades. Thus, this literature does not examine whether the difference among executives in the informativeness of their trades is due to general differences among them or can be attributed to specific firm events, especially major capital expenditures. Focusing on major IT investments, an increasingly prevalent category of capital expenditure in an operational area, this study shows that the trades by executives proximate to that area (in the case of IT investment, CIOs, whose stock purchases have not been previously examined in the insider trading literature) might have greater predictive power than those by CEOs and CFOs, despite these operational executives (specifically, CIOs in this study) having a relatively lower financial acumen. Furthermore, the sharp contrast between the informativeness of CIOs’ trades in operationally proximate areas (see Model 3 in Table 5 and Models 4–6 in Table 6) and the lack thereof in an operationally distant context (specifically, non-IT acquisitions; as discussed in the section under “Tests of Underlying Assumptions”) highlights how a generic understanding about informativeness of trades by certain executives can be misleading, and instead needs to be viewed while considering their proximity to the firm’s capital expenditures around that time.

In addition to these theoretical contributions, this study offers three implications for practice. First, it provides external investors and financial analysts with insights into how they might benefit from knowing about the purchase of a firm’s stock by its senior executives before the firm’s major investments in IT. These insights are especially valuable for investors considering dual-emphasis firms, where it is difficult to assess an IT’s fit with business strategy.

Second, the results suggest that focusing on stock purchases by operationally proximate executives (i.e., CIOs in the case of IT investments) can help outsiders to make purchases that produce financial benefits, not only for the extreme IT strategies (i.e., revenue- and cost-emphasis IT strategies) but also for the hybrid (i.e., dual-emphasis) strategy. Stock purchases by CEOs are also insightful but only in the case of the two extreme IT strategies, and even then they are less beneficial than purchases by the operationally proximate executives.

Finally, we find that CIO’s purchasing a firm’s stock is associated with enhancements to the firm’s payoffs, in terms of the more traditional measures of operational performance, in addition to the buy-and-hold benefits that provide direct financial benefits to executives purchasing the stock. This potentially implies that the CIOs’ stock purchases provide at least correlational (if not predictive) insights into the potential benefits of IT investments to the firm.

## Measure of IT Newness

A challenge in measuring IT newness is that some ITs may decline and plateau after their first appearance and then resurface. Therefore, IT newness is measured as the reciprocal of years since last surfacing (instead of years since the first introduction) plus one (to avoid division by zero). For technologies that emerge once and then do not decline and resurface, years since the last surfacing simply refers to the years since the first introduction.

The logic of identifying a resurfacing year is based on the typical IT life-cycle, an initial sharp increase in attention and interest in IT when it first surfaces (Gartner’s hype cycles call this phase the “innovation trigger phase”) is sometimes followed by a sharp decrease in interest (Gartner’s hype cycles call this phase the “trough of disillusionment”), and then, as the IT matures further, the level of interest in that IT plateaus. This identification of resurfacing based on community discussions is consistent with the existing studies that suggest heightened discussion in the primary stages of know-what and know-how generating (e.g., Wang & Ramiller 2009) and a decline in later stages.

To identify the resurfacing year, we follow a process that ensures that (1) there is a high enough jump in mentions about the technology and (2) this significant increase follows a previously stable period that represents the last cycle’s plateau. If the significant jump in mentions does not follow a previous stable trend, it would not be clear if this jump is due to the beginning of a new cycle or if it indicates a transitioning of that technology’s mentions within a cycle (e.g., a transition from the disillusionment phase to the plateau phase). Specifically, a resurfacing year (t) is a year that satisfies two conditions: (1) there is at least an 80% growth in the mentions of the IT on ABI/INFORMS in that year (t) compared to the previous year (t-1), and (2) the previous year (t-1) has a steady change (i.e., growth or decline of at most 10%) compared to the previous year (t-2). Our results are robust with respect to the growth rates that define both these aspects. The results hold when the minimum increase in mentions required for the year (t) is set at 70%, 75%, 85%, or 90%. Also, the results do not change qualitatively if the condition of stability for the year t-1 is set at a change of at most 5%.

For instance, we find the first mentions of business intelligence and analytics in ABI/INFORMS in 1996. Throughout the years covered in our study (2002–2016), we see two resurfacing years: 2005 and 2010. In the years leading to 2005 (the first resurfacing), the number of mentions had seen a steady downward trend. Then, in 2005, the mentions grow by 96%. The rate of change in the preceding year (2004) is only -8%.
