# Different but Equal? A Field Experiment on the Impact of Recommendation Systems on Mobile and Personal Computer Channels in Retail

Abstract. The benefits of recommendation systems in online retail contexts have received much attention in prior work. Much of this work has been conducted in personal computer (PC)–based settings, although mobile devices are becoming increasingly central to the online shopping experience. It remains to be examined if the effects of recommendation systems in retail differ across these two channels, in terms of customer-level decision outcomes. In this paper, we examine these differences in some detail, studying how product views and sales attributed to a recommendation system are different across mobile and PC-based channels. Further, we examine how the effect of a recommendation system across channels influences sales diversity, an important outcome in the retail industry. We conduct our analysis using a randomized field experiment, conducted in partnership with an online retailing firm in South Korea, where the experimental treatment is access to a recommendation system. Our results show that the use of recommendation systems enhances customer-level outcomes, such as views and sales of recommended products, clickthrough rate, and conversion. More importantly, the marginal impacts of the rec ommendation system are significantly higher for mobile users, indicating that the higher search costs imposed through mobile devices are more effectively reduced through recommendation systems. With respect to sales diversity, we observe that although the mobile channel leads to more diverse sales, we see no interaction effects of the recom mendation system and mobile use on sales diversity. These results provide boundary conditions for the efficacy of recommendation systems in retail contexts where online sales occur across both PC-based and mobile channels. We discuss the managerial implications of these results for online retailers and conclude with opportunities for further research.

## 1. Introduction

Recommendation systems that return a list of specifically selected items for a given user by analyzing the relationship between the user’s interests and items’ characteristics are pervasive in online retailing and web services (Linden et al. 2003). As a tool for nudging, by creating a personalized and contextaware shopping experience for each user, recommendation systems offer retailers an effective strategy for targeted marketing. Prior research shows that recommendation systems can significantly enhance customers’ shopping experience by automatically identifying personalized items and displaying the optimized offers (i.e., a set of recommended items) to each individual, thereby allowing customers to discover relevant products that they may not have sought out otherwise (De et al. 2010, Schrage 2017) These systems not only influence how customers utilize their prior product knowledge, in terms of their prior purchase behavior, but also alter how they search for product information, based on data from other users. Not surprisingly, 62% of U.S. online consumers find their presence on retail websites very useful during the purchasing process (Mulpuru et al. 2010). Correspondingly, 76% of retail websites list personalized product recommendations as a priority for their online sales strategy (IBM 2014) while also attesting to their positive effects on revenues (Accenture 2014). Indeed, online retailers indicate that recommendation systems drive between 2% and 20% of their revenue (Mulpuru et al. 2010). It is thus accepted in practice that recommendation systems improve conversion rates, increase average order value by helping customers find the products they are looking for, and help customers discover new products (IBM 2014, Schrage 2017).

The academic literature studying the impacts of recommendation systems on sales and sales diversity has also documented the positive effects of such systems on a variety of outcome measures in online retail contexts (Fleder and Hosanagar 2009, De et al. 2010, Pathak et al. 2010, Brynjolfsson et al. 2011, Oestreicher-Singer and Sundararajan 2012a, Oestreicher-Singer and Sundararajan 2012b, Hosanagar et al. 2014, Lee and Hosanagar 2019). However, although this research is well developed, much of this work on studying the efficacy of recommendation systems, as well as the practitionerbased reports discussed above, are based on contexts where customers are on desktops, whereas the retail world is increasingly going “mobile”. Mobile devices are increasingly central to the online shopping experience, given the growing number of smartphone shoppers and the growing proportion of mobile-based retail sales (Einav et al. 2014, Caine 2017). According to Comscore (2018), mobile commerce has annual growth of 40% in 2017, far outpacing the ecommerce growth rate of 15% in the United States. Though mobile represents 64% of time spent shopping online, the mobile channel only accounts for 24% of all retail dollars spent (Comscore 2018). Given these trends, it is not directly obvious that existing insights from research from PC-based contexts on the relationship between recommendation systems and sales translate to the mobile channel. In this paper, we address this question of potential channel-based differences, if any, specifically.

The literature on mobile-based commerce provides several factors that clearly differentiate the mobile ecosystem from traditional personal computer (PC)–based environments. Physically, mobile devices have smaller screens and lower resolutions than do PCs (Ghose et al. 2013). Customer preferences and purchase behavior on mobile devices show different patterns, compared with online contexts (Daurer et al. 2015). Mobile devices build on the properties of ubiquity, convenience, and ease of use in how they influence consumer behavior in their particular context (Fong et al. 2015). Additionally, customers’ search costs on mobile devices, particularly in the retail context, tend to be structurally different from those in PC-based contexts (Ghose et al. 2013). Given these characteristics of the mobile channel, and the increasing relevance of the mobile device in the context of retail, it is important to understand how consumer behavior may vary across channels. More to the point, it is particularly necessary to examine how these channel-specific differences may manifest when exposed to widely used technologies like recommendation systems. It is this broader open question in the literature—examining differences across channels in consumer behavior—that we address in this paper.

Specifically, our work here addresses two research questions pertaining to the efficacy of recommendation systems within the retail sector. First, we study the effect of recommendation systems on individuallevel decision outcomes (product sales and views) across the mobile and PC channels within retail. Building on existing work (Haubl and Trifts ¨ 2000, Fleder and Hosanagar 2009, De et al. 2010, Pathak et al. 2010, Jannach and Adomavicius 2017, Lee and Hosanagar 2019), we examine whether the presence of a recommendation system leads to differences in customer purchase and browsing behavior between mobile and PC channels. A related question pertains to the effects of recommendation systems on sales and view diversity, a market-level outcome of particular interest (Brynjolfsson et al. 2011, Hosanagar et al. 2014, Lee and Hosanagar 2019). Our second research question pertains to studying this relationship vis-a-vis mobile versus PC-based channels. The\` literature is ambiguous about how recommendation systems affect sales diversity. On the one hand, these systems help to homogenize the market, whereas others have shown how they increase sales diversity (Fleder and Hosanagar 2009, Hosanagar et al. 2014, Li and Karahanna 2015, Lee and Hosanagar 2019). We empirically examine these effects across the mobile and PC channels.

We conduct our empirical analyses in collaboration with a South Korean business analytics firm that implements recommendation systems on mobile and PC channels for online fashion retailers. Despite the increasing relevance of mobile in online retailing (Ghose et al. 2013, Einav et al. 2014), empirical research in this sector is sparse because of the un availability of suitable empirical data. We are able to overcome this hurdle by partnering with the business analytics firm in conducting randomized field experiments on the use of recommendation systems across the mobile and PC channels. We report on one such field experiment in this paper, whereas an earlier pilot field experiment, conducted for a different client, is described in the online appendix.<sup>1</sup> In the field experiment described here, the treatment is the availability of a recommendation system, which is randomly assigned to individual users across both the PC and mobile channels. We subsequently examine various aspects of their purchase behavior, such as their views and purchases of recommended products, their clickthrough and conversion rates, as well as the resulting sales and views diversity, across the two channels.

Although we are able to randomize the access to a recommendation service within our experiments, we were not able to randomize assignment to the mobile or PC channel in our experiments, leading to a potential channel selection issue. In other words, it is possible that mobile users are fundamentally “different” from PC users, and therefore any results from the experiment could be attributable to these unobserved differences. Unfortunately, this can only be addressed through randomization of channel use, which is largely infeasible in field studies with industry partners, as has been acknowledged in recent work studying channel differences (Jung et al. 2019). This remains a limitation in our work. However, from a managerial perspective, understanding such differences may be relatively unimportant. If mobile and PC users are truly intrinsically different in terms of how they respond to recommendation systems, acting upon these differences in terms of the design and deployment of recommendation systems remains the main insight for managers. That said, we conduct several additional tests across both experiments to check the robustness of our results to this limitation, as described later in the paper.

Our results from the field experiment show that as a baseline, the use of recommendation systems increase sales and sales quantities at the customer level, consistent with prior work (De et al. 2010, Pathak et al. 2010). We find that the effect of recommendation systems is significant mostly for mobile users, suggesting that higher search costs inherent with the mobile channel may be more effectively reduced through such systems, whereas the ubiquity of the mobile channel further helps enhance their effectiveness. Finally, we see no direct effect of the recommendation system on sales diversity per se. However, we do observe that diversity of product sales and views are higher on the mobile channel compared with the PC channel. Thus, our results suggest a contingent and nuanced effect of these systems on marketlevel outcomes, compared with prior work showing a direct homogenizing or heterogenizing effect of recommendation systems on the market (Li and Karahanna 2015).

These results highlight the two primary contributions of our work to the literature. First, we extend the study of recommendation systems to the mobile context, whereby we theorize about the specific features of mobile platforms and how they may moderate the traditional wisdom regarding the value of such systems. Although our field experiments cannot establish which specific mechanisms are responsible for the observed results, we are able to conjecture about potential causal mechanisms using a series of ex post tests, building on recent work using such an approach (Lee and Hosanagar 2019). Second, we provide evidence of the impact of recommendation systems on sales diversity, thereby adding more evidence to the ongoing debate on this question in the literature. More broadly, our work incrementally extends the literature on the efficacy of recommendation systems in retail contexts, focusing attention on the differences that manifest across the PC-based and mobile channels.

## 2. Literature Review

Our work sits at the intersection of three streams of research—recent work on differences between the mobile and PC channels, the role of recommendation systems in retail, and the implications of these systems for sales diversity at the level of the market. We review the literature on differences between mobile and PC-based contexts first and the literature pertaining to sales diversity subsequently.

## 2.1. PC vs. Mobile Channels—How Are They Different in Retail?

As mobile devices have become increasingly mainstream, the manner in which they are used can differ markedly from what was observed in traditional PC channels. These differences, if any, can have significant implications for how firms and users interact with markets and products across the PC and mobile channels. The literature describing these differences, or lack thereof, between mobile and PC channels is shown in Table A1 in the online appendix; we base our literature review on this work. Prior work contrasting mobile devices and PCs argues that there are structural differences between the two channels, because of inherent differences between the fixed and the mobile internet (Bang et al. 2013, Ghose et al. 2013, Xu et al. 2017). In terms of accessing content on the internet, the mobile and PC channels share similar capabilities (such as access to the same product assortment, for instance). However, the two channels are differentiated by access and search capabilities because of differences in the extent to which each channel is usable and provides ubiquitous access (Andrews et al. 2015, Bang et al. 2013, Burtch and Hong 2014, Chae and Kim 2003, Huang et al. 2016, Jung et al. 2019, Xu et al. 2017). These differences in terms of usability and ubiquity play a key role in driving user behavior on these channels and correspondingly how firms interact with users.

More specifically, the mobile channel provides higher and significantly easier access to online content than the PC channel (Chae and Kim 2003, Bang et al. 2013, Wang et al. 2015, Huang et al. 2016, Xu et al. 2017, Jung et al. 2019). Mobile devices are viewed as personal devices that remain attached to the user, transcending their role as a purely technological device to access the internet or telephony. This ubiquity enables the user to access content on the internet anywhere and anytime. In other words, the mobile channel applies fewer constraints on the user in terms of time and space. Prior work argues that this increased convenience leads to greater engagement with mobile platforms as well as the internet, relative to the traditional PC channel (Xu et al. 2014, Jung et al. 2019). When viewed through the lens of retail, this increased availability of content through the ubiquity of mobile is instrumental in increasing the views of products online as well as the increase in sales, relative to the constraints imposed by the PC channel (Wang et al. 2015).

Although the mobile channel provides ubiquity and thereby enhances access to content online per se, relative to the PC, its effects with respect to search capabilities are more nuanced. For search involving low information intensity, such as for simple products or for habitual purchases that do not require much incremental search or cognition, empirical work shows that the mobile channel works well compared with the PC (Chae and Kim 2003, Maity and Dass 2014, Wang et al. 2015). However, as tasks get more information intensive and search costs increase, mobile devices are limited because of physical constraints, such as small screen sizes, low usability levels, and problems with wireless network access (Bang et al. 2013). Importantly, small screen size and information structure of a mobile device negatively influence the efficiency of a user’s navigation activities (Chae and Kim 2003). These constraints lead to increased costs in search activities associated with interaction, navigation, reading, viewing, and evaluation tasks. The small screens also impose an obstacle to usability on the mobile browser interface (Venkatesh et al. 2003), increasing the costs of product and feature searches while reducing the extent to which accurate recall is possible in such environments.

These increased costs imply that although mobile commerce adoption is associated with an immediate and sustained increase in total retailing sales, mobile purchases are skewed toward commodity products rather than idiosyncratic products that require careful inspection ex ante, as shown by Einav et al. (2014). Mobile devices are also associated with specific effects that can be attributed to higher search costs, such as the ranking effects documented by Ghose et al. (2013). Practitioners have also noted that a significant gap between time spent on mobile devices by users versus their actual spending on the mobile channel, largely attributed to difficulties in navigation and product comparisons (Fulgoni and Lipsman 2016). Indeed, one of the goals of recommendation systems in such settings is to reduce search costs and the associated cognitive effort, inherent in the channel. We address this literature next.

## 2.2. The Effects of Recommendation Systems in Retail

Recommendation systems are defined as “software agents that elicit the interests or preferences of individual customers for products and make recommendations accordingly” (Xiao and Benbasat 2007, pp. 137–138). Online retailers offer a large number of product choices, incorporating vast amounts of productrelated information to customers. However, customers often find it difficult to efficiently identify products that meet their needs, given the cognitive constraints of human information processing (Xiao and Benbasat 2007). If this cognitive load can be eased, the purchasing process is likely to be positively influenced, leading to a better purchasing outcome for both seller and customer. Recommendation systems are designed to assist customers in product search and selection while simultaneously aiding product customization (Li and Karahanna 2015) and are offered by most online retailers across all channels. They influence customer decision making through three specific mechanisms—lowering search costs, triggering the recall and retrieval process, and a signaling effect (see Table A2 in the online appendix for a summary of research in this area). We briefly describe these mechanisms below.

Information processing theory contends that individuals satisfice in processing information to make decisions because of limited cognitive capacity (Simon 1955). Individuals do so in making purchase decisions by reducing the set of alternative products and evaluating products in the consideration set (Payne et al. 1992). By accessing the customer’s shopping history or choices made by other customers with similar profiles, recommendations systems can improve the quality of decisions customers make when searching and selecting among myriad products (Resnick and Varian 1997). Specifically, they assist customers in the initial screening of alternatives and facilitate in-depth comparisons among selected alternatives (Resnick and Varian 1997, Haubl and Trifts¨ 2000, Xiao and Benbasat 2007), thereby reducing information overload and information search costs (Haubl and Trifts¨ 2000, Haubl and Murray¨ 2006, Kumar and Benbasat 2006, Lee and Benbasat 2010 Dellaert and Haubl¨ 2012).

Beyond reduced search costs, recommendation systems trigger the recall and retrieval process (Bettman 1979, Lynch and Srull 1982) by presenting customers with a focal product (what you viewed last time) and related recommended products (people who viewed this product also viewed these other products) (Linden et al. 2003). Customers can use the focal product as a retrieval cue. The visual salience provided by recommendation systems increases a customer’s ability to retrieve focal product information from memory, based on the customer’s last viewing or purchasing behavior. Thus, recall of prior search behavior and memory retrieval facilitated by the recommendation system can influence purchase decisions positively (Bettman 1979, Lynch and Srull 1982).

Finally, recommendation systems generate a list of recommended products for the customer based on prior views or purchases by other similar customers, using a collaborative filtering algorithm, which also serves as an indirect signal of quality (Linden et al. 2003). Because recommendation systems provide prominent landing page real estate to a set of recommended products, these act as decentralized signals of quality and potential demand, thereby enhancing the appeal of such products (Pathak et al. 2010). Such exposure reduces uncertainty in the specific product while also helping the customer “discover” a larger number of desirable products.

Although the specific mechanisms are not always identifiable empirically, prior work has shown that recommendation systems have a significant effect on retail sales. De et al. (2010) show that the use of a recommendation system has a positive effect on the sales of both promoted as well as nonpromoted products, compared with search engines. Similarly, Pathak et al. (2010) find that recommendation systems lead to higher sales of online books. Oestreicher-Singer and Sundararajan (2012b) show that the visibility accorded by recommendation system increases the shared purchasing of complimentary books. In more recent work, Lee and Hosanagar (2019) report that purchase-based collaborative filtering algorithms significantly increase views and sales of online movie titles. In a different context, Adamopoulos and Tuzhilin (2015) show that recommendation systems in a mobile urban guide app positively impacts restaurant demand. In general, there is consensus in the literature that recommendation systems increase demand of online retailers.

This brings us to the question of how the effectiveness of recommendation systems in retail may differ across the mobile and PC channels, our primary research question. We argue that there are inherent tensions in how these systems operate within the mobile context that make this an open empirical question. On the one hand, the literature on recommendation systems within the retail sector, mostly based on the PC channel, argues that they reduce search costs for consumers, while enhancing recall through visual salience. By virtue of signaling, they are able to directly influence the focal customer positively. However, the literature on mobile devices shows that detailed and in-depth search is typically hard on such devices, assuming that technical issues such as broadband availability and software capabilities are managed. Mobile devices impose higher search costs and cognitive loads on customers as they move through the purchase process. Therefore, mobile-based customers view less content on their main page than PC customers do and consume less product information in the same time compared with their PC counterparts. Even the provision of a recommendation system on smaller screens, arguably designed to reduce search costs, may not fully address the structural limitations of mobile devices. These arguments would indicate that recommendation systems may not be as effective on the mobile channel relative to the PC channel.

However, mobile devices benefit from ubiquity and ease of access for the average consumer. Ubiquity and unfettered access to online content, even under physical conditions that may impose higher search costs, can allow for quick decision making, quick recall, and the presence of significant learning through customization. To the extent that the mobile device is viewed as a personal artifact and generates tremendous engagement, the recommendation system may actually enhance the consumer’s ability to locate other relevant products, higher search costs notwithstanding. These arguments suggest that the efficacy of a recommendation system on mobile devices may be higher, relative to the PC that may not engender the same level of engagement, ubiquity, or use.

In summary, there remains a theoretical tension in terms of how recommendation systems work on mobile devices, relative to the PC channel, which can be resolved through empirical analyses. Although nailing down specific mechanisms is hard, given the various explanations provided in the literature, we believe that it is still possible to generate empirical results that show how recommendation systems operate across channels, with the hope that more specific mechanisms can be tested in future work. Therefore, in lieu of formal hypotheses, we allow the empirical analysis to provide us guidance here.

## 2.3. The Effects of Recommendation Systems on Sales Diversity

Beyond the impact of recommendation systems on customer decision making, researchers have also ex plored how these systems influence sales diversity, a market-level outcome of interest that complement consumer-level analysis (Park and Han 2013). The relationship between recommendation systems and sales diversity, summarized in Table A3 of the online appendix, interestingly remains ambiguous in the literature, specifically on whether these systems homogenize or heterogenize the sales distribution (Li and Karahanna 2015).

One line of research argues that recommendation systems tend to increase sales diversity, a desirable outcome. Increased sales diversity allows consumers to obtain a more ideal product mix while also ensuring that most products on the platform retain some probability of purchase, rather than concentrating sales around a few select popular items. By increasing awareness of niche products and allowing the exploration of products not directly in the consumer’s consideration set, recommendation systems can shift demand toward the “long tail” (Pathak et al. 2010). Indeed, Brynjolfsson et al. (2011) show that recommendation systems in e-commerce contexts are associated with increases in the sales of niche products, thereby enhancing sales diversity. In a different context, Oestreicher-Singer and Sundararajan (2012a) also show that recommendation systems shift demand from popular items to niche items, thereby increasing aggregate sales diversity.

However, an alternative viewpoint suggests exactly the opposite effect in online contexts. Fleder and Hosanagar (2009) argue that recommendation systems can increase individual-level diversity but decrease aggregate diversity because they tend to push each customer toward new products while in aggregate, recommending the same products to similar users (Fleder and Hosanagar 2009). Consistent with Fleder and Hosanagar (2009), Hinz et al. (2011) empirically show that recommendation systems shift demand from niches to blockbuster products. Hosanagar et al. (2014) find that recommendation systems do indeed increase commonality; users purchase more similar products upon the introduction of a recommendation system. In more recent work, Lee and Hosanagar (2019) provide further evidence that collaborative filtering-based recommendation systems decrease aggregate sales diversity while increasing individual consumption diversity.

Given this divergence in the literature, the net effect of recommendation systems on sales diversity remains ambiguous, requiring further testing. A significant roadblock has been difficulty in obtaining field data that provides a contrast between users who can use recommendation systems and those who cannot (Li and Karahanna 2015). Further, beyond sales diversity per se, a resolution to this question may be found in considering interim outcomes, such as product views on retail websites. It is possible for recommendation systems to enhance view diversity differently than sales diversity, while also displaying different effects across mobile and PC channels. In the absence of clear theoretical predictions, here again we allow the empirical analysis to provide some insights.

## 3. Experimental Design

To study the causal impact of recommendation system usage on sales and sales diversity across mobile and PC-based channels, we collaborated with a business analytics company in South Korea that worked with online retailing clients to introduce recommendation system functionality. As part of this collaboration, we conducted two field experiments on the efficacy of recommendation systems within the online retail market across the mobile and PC chan nels. Although the two experiments provided largely consistent results, in the interest of space, we focus on the second experiment here and describe the first experiment in the online appendix. Because the experiments were conducted for two separate clients, there are differences in terms of the research design and data availability between the two experiments. The second experiment we discuss here is more expansive in terms of data availability and scope, allowing us to examine multiple outcome variables associated with the introduction of recommendation systems Hence, we chose to focus on this experiment here.

As mentioned above, the field experiment was hosted by an online retailing website in South Korea that sells cosmetics, accessories, and clothes. The retailer utilizes both PC-based and mobile versions of their site, wherein the products and service features are similar (functionally equivalent, though the layout was slightly different because of screen size limitations) regardless of whether a customer accesses the platform through the PC or mobile phone. The retailer had no other channels, such as a mobile app, during the experimental period. As part of the experiment, we worked with both the analytics firm as well as the retailer in implementing a personalized recommendation feature on the landing page of websites across both the mobile and PC-based pages. Prior to the rollout of the recommendation system across all users on the platform, the retailer agreed to conduct a random field experiment to test the effectiveness of such a system. We were thus able to procure the retailer’s buy-in for conducting the experiment under some specific conditions that were designed around the business constraints, described later

The treatment of interest is the availability of the recommendation panel, which is randomly assigned to customers based on the PC identifier (PCID), a device identifier that is widely used in internet marketing practices. The PCID is a persistent cookie containing an encrypted unique 23-digit string stored on the customer’s device. When a customer visits the website at the start of the experimental period, the customer is randomly assigned to the treatment group (with recommendation panel) or the control group depending on whether the PCID is odd or even. In the treatment group (even PCID), customers are shown the most recent view-based collaborative filtering recommendation panel in a landing page. The recommendation panel shows the recently viewed item as well as a set of recommended items generated through the item-to-item collaborative filtering algorithm, which is the mostly commonly used algorithm in e-commerce (Linden et al. 2003, Hosanagar et al. 2014, Lee and Hosanagar 2019). Customers in the control group do not see recommendations on their landing page; instead they are shown a generic set of best-selling items on the retailing website where the recommendation panel would appear. We ensured that the same number of products are shown across both treatment and control group panels. This design decision was made to alleviate concerns about visual salience that may occur from the presence of a panel of products within the user interface. Since the random treatment is applied at the level of the customer, our analysis is essentially between-subjects.

The experiment was conducted in February 2016, when the treatment (recommendation system versus best-selling items) was turned on for all customers on the retail platform across both channels. Information on customers’ purchase and view behavior on the retailing platform prior to the start of the experiment was available to us, as a way to check for pretreatment trends. The actual period of the experiment was limited to three days because of constraints imposed by the retailer client.<sup>2</sup> During this period of three days, we were able to observe a sample of 18,196 unique customers, based on product views data. Of these 18,196 customers, 9,068 customers are in the treatment group (49.8%), whereas 9,128 appeared in the control group (50.2%), suggesting that the random treatment appears well distributed. Beyond product view data, we collected a series of other outcomes for all these customers, such as sales of recommended products, clickthrough rates, as well as conversion (i.e., whether a purchase occurred or not), so as to provide greater visibility into how mobile and PC users differ within the retail conversion funnel. For each of these customers, information on their pur chase and view behavior on the platform prior to the start of the experiment was available to us, as a way to check for pretreatment trends, which were instrumental in ruling out the effects of channel selection and consumer heterogeneity, described below.

Because one of the goals of this analysis is to understand recommendation system efficacy across mobile and PC users, we need to cleanly identify pure PC-based customers from pure-mobile customers to identify these effects. We use clickstream data across the pretreatment and posttreatment periods to segment the customers into these two categories. Based on clickstream data, we observed 11,623 pure mobile users and 2,567 pure PC users. We note the higher proportion of pure mobile users in the sample, consistent with mobile usage trends in East Asia (Criteo 2016). An additional 546 users were classified as multichannel users in that they used both the PC and mobile device to access content—these were identified by their customer ID as well as inconsistencies in the PCID assigned to them. These users were excluded from the analysis to ensure clean identification across channel use. Finally, we noted 3,460 users who appear to switch channels between the pretreatment and posttreatment periods. We exclude them from the analysis as well but delve deeper into the issue of channel-switchers later in the paper. ## 3.1. Channel Selection and Channel Switching

One of the concerns with the design of our field experiment is the inability to randomize over channel use. Recall that our objective is to examine the efficacy of recommendation systems across the mobile and PC channels. Although we are able to randomize across the recommendation treatment, we are unable to do so for the channel. Ideally, channel treatment should be randomized, such that some consumers are randomly assigned to the PC, whereas others are randomly assigned to the mobile device, with no channel switching. This would lead to the ideal 2×2 factorial experimental design. However, randomizing across channel use is infeasible because most retailers resist limiting access to its channels, even for the purpose of a field experiment (Jung et al. 2019). Moreover, exogenously restricting access to the mobile channel for customers is not possible for any third-party entity (like us) because we cannot mandate mobile or desktop use on the part of the consumer in the field. Therefore, we identify consumers on the basis of their observed channel use on the platform (mobile versus PC users) to carry out our analysis.

The inability to randomize channel use raises two issues in our analysis that are noteworthy. First, it is possible that consumers self-select into a channel that suits their needs, i.e., there are some consumers who, for intrinsic reasons, prefer the mobile or the PC channel to access the retail site. We have limited visibility into why this occurs. However, we can still show that mobile users exposed to the recommendation system show systematically different behavior compared with PC users exposed to the same system. We cannot cleanly establish that it is the channel per se that causes this difference in behavior, rather than some unobservable, idiosyncratic characteristic of the consumer.<sup>3</sup> However, for the manager interested in understanding the effects of channel on sales, the differences across the two channels in terms of consumer usage can be identified cleanly. The only effective way to fully identify the effect of the channel per se, independent of individual idiosyncrasies, is through randomizing the channel, which, as mentioned above, is infeasible in this domain.

The second concern that is raised is the issue of channel-switching, that is, we cannot identify consumers who may choose to switch from the PC to the mobile channel (or vice versa) as a response to the treatment. If this occurs, it would not be possible to attribute the recommendation system’s effect on users in a channel to that channel only; effectively the treatment and the channel would change concurrently for these users. In quasi-experimental studies, these conditions can be handled through the use of pretreatment trends (Bertrand et al. 2004), wherein the behavior of the consumer can be measured prior to the treatment being applied and a difference-indifferences model can be estimated to evaluate the effects of the treatment.

In the field experiment, we randomize the recommendation system treatment but not channel use, for the same reasons discussed above. Our access to pretreatment trends for all customers in the sample allow us to estimate the effects of recommendation systems across channels using a difference-indifferences model that would account for pretreatment trends on channel while randomizing on the recommendation treatment. This is fully consistent with prior work where exogenous shocks have been used to study the responses of individuals or firms that vary along nonrandomized covariates (Angrist et al. 2009). As long as pretreatment use of the channel is uncorrelated with the application of the treatment, it is possible to estimate the moderating effects of the channel on the relationship between the recommendation system treatment and customer-level decision outcomes, including product sales, clickthrough rates, and conversation. Finally, because we have access to pretreatment data on customers, we can also account for channel switching behavior by customers. In the next section, we describe the empirical analyses for the experiment.

## 4. Empirical Analysis

## 4.1. The Effects of Recommendation Systems and Channel in Conversion Funnel

Because we can compare data from the posttreatment period for each consumer to their own pretreatment information, we exploit a difference-in-differences design to examine the contingent effects of recommendation systems usage on customer decision making. The treatment is the availability of the recommendation panel for a randomly selected subsample of customers for a period of three days. To match this period, we consider a pretreatment period of three days as well. As mentioned above, we observed 11,623 pure mobile, 2,567 pure PC, 546 multichannel, and 3,460 channelswitching users. For our analysis here, we focus only on the pure PC and pure mobile users.

Because the time period of the analysis is small, we aggregate the data for the pre- and posttreatment periods, respectively, for each customer and estimate the effect of the recommendation system across channels in a difference-in-differences model. We model customer-level outcomes, such as the views and sales of recommended products specifically, as well as their clickthrough rate and conversion, thereby providing greater visibility into how mobile and PC users differ. The general form of the difference-indifferences model is shown in Equation (1) below:

$$
\begin{array}{r} y _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} P o s t T r e a t m e n t _ {t} + \beta_ {2} R e c o m m e n d a t i o n _ {i} \\ + \beta_ {3} P o s t T r e a t m e n t _ {t} \times R e c o m m e n d a t i o n _ {i} \\ + \beta_ {4} T o t a l V i e w s _ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

The coefficient of interest is $\beta _ { 3 }$ representing the effect of the recommendation system availability across the pre/posttreatment periods. By comparing the estimates of this coefficient across the mobile and PC channels, we assess the differential impact of recommendation systems across the two channels. We estimate versions of this model for five dependent variables: recommended product views (RecViews), sales of recommended products (RecSales), sales quantity of recommended products (RecSalesQ), Clickthrough, and Conversion. Consistent with prior research, we use a negative binomial regression specification for count variables (RecViews, RecSalesQ), tobit regression for Rec Sales to account for the left censoring at zero sales, fractional logistic regression for proportion variables (Clickthrough), and logistic regression for Conversion (Wooldridge 2010). The results are shown in Tables 3 and 4.

The first two columns of Table 3 show the recommendation system’s effect on views of recommended products across the two channels. As is common in difference-in-differences models, the coefficients of interest are interaction terms rather than direct terms of the treatments (Angrist et al. 2009). Therefore, in the interest of space, we focus on the interaction terms across the regressions. The highlighted interaction term shows the effects of the recommendation system treatment are positive and significant for both channels $( \beta = 1 . 2 8 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 ; \beta = \bar { 1 . } 6 1 3 ,$ $p \ < \ 0 . 0 1$ respectively). However, the effect size is much larger for the mobile channel—the presence of the recommendation system renders more views on the mobile channel. Arguably, the ubiquity and convenient access afforded by mobile devices helps explain why mobile users are more active in terms of product views on the retail platform, though we cannot rule out alternative mechanisms in this setting

To summarize, our results from the field experiment show that recommendation systems are more efficacious in the mobile channel compared with the PC channel. When considering various outcome variables, such as Clickthrough and Conversion, mobile users appear to respond with greater effect to the presence of recommendation systems. The literature suggests that these effects could be due to the twin benefits of ubiquity and search cost reductions that are associated with mobile devices and recommendation systems acting in tandem, in spite of the structural constraints associated with small screen size and ergonomic limitations inherent with mobile devices. Although identifying a specific theoretical mechanism is difficult here, the observed effects on sales and conversion rates within the retail sector are noteworthy from a managerial perspective. We conduct further post hoc tests, described next, to consider how the recommendation treatment may further manifest on customer behavior on the retail platform and also to probe potential mechanisms.

## 4.2. Post Hoc Tests—The Role of Product Prices and New Product Discovery

We consider additional tests here to examine how recommendation systems may direct demand toward or away from products, based on their prices. We also study how customers may discover products on the platform because of the recommendation system, to test the argument that the reduction in search costs is driving the observed difference between mobile and PC channels. Although the ubiquity and ease of use provided by mobile devices are not directly testable, we can consider contexts where users may experience relatively higher search costs, serving as indirect tests for causal mechanisms.

We start with considering product prices and ask, do recommendation systems lead customers to view and purchase cheaper or more expensive products on average, across the two channels? Our results reported earlier show higher total sales resulting from the treatment per se, but it is not clear if customers are guided toward more expensive products as a result. To understand this effect, we first collated products in our data set that had at least one purchase across the pretreatment and posttreatment periods. We do not have the full product line offered by the retailer; but anecdotally, the list of products that have been viewed at least once represents over 80% of the product line. We then rank-ordered this list by price (most expensive to cheapest). Because the products are mostly within the retail fashion space, we did not segment them further by specific category. We then identified the products within the top quartile and bottom quartile, that is, the most expensive and least expensive products. Subsequently, we conducted the difference-in-differences estimation, similar to those reported above, across these subsamples of the most expensive and cheapest products, with the dependent variables being views and sales of recommended products.

The results are shown in Tables 6 and 7 and show that the recommendation system’s marginal effect is higher for the more expensive items, leading to a higher number of views and sales, relative to the cheaper items. Because expensive products are more likely to require further cognitive processing, with higher search and transaction costs, it is not surprising that the recommendation system is more effective for these products, compared with cheaper products. For views of recommended products, we could not find any significant differences across the mobile and PC channels, though we note that the marginal effect of the recommendation system is the highest in the mobile channel for expensive products $( \beta ^ { \mathrm { ~ ~ } } = 2 . 0 7 1 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Interestingly, we find significant, and different, effects across the mobile and PC channels for purchase of recommended products. The effect of the recommendation system on sales of relatively expensive recommended products is significant and positive for mobile users $( \beta = 1 . 9 7 7 , p < 0 . 0 \breve { 1 } )$ but has no effect on PC-based users $( \beta = 1 . { \overset { } { 4 } } 9 0 , \mathrm { n s } )$ We also find a positive effect of the recommendation system on the sales of lower-priced items for mobile-based users $( \beta = 1 . 2 0 3 , p < \bar { 0 } . 0 5 )$ but no such effect on PC-based users $( \beta \ : = \ : 0 . 0 7 8 5 , \ : \mathrm { n s } )$ . Viewed together, these empirical results show that the recommendation system helps increase views of highpriced items as well as low-priced items across both channels but leads to higher purchase behavior of both high- and low-priced items only in the mobile channel and not in the PC channel. These results provide some clear implications for retail managers in terms of where recommendation systems, when combined with the mobile channel, have the most compelling return on investment—higher-priced items appear to benefit the most from the reduced search costs and ubiquity associated with these systems accessed on mobile devices.

Because product views can lead to actual conversion, the increased engagement on the mobile device combined with the recommendation system represents a valuable set of assets for the retailer. In case of recommended product sales, we find that the recommendation system significantly increases sales of both new and previously viewed products in the mobile channel, again providing suggestive evidence for the higher levels of engagement that the combination of recommendation systems and mobile channels engender as well as the reduction in search costs. ## 4.3. Recommendation Systems, Mobile Channel, and View and Sales Diversity

In testing for the effects of the recommendation system on product view and sales diversity, we follow recent work by Lee and Hosanagar (2019). As a first step, we calculate the aggregate views and sales for each product on the marketplace. We then use the Lorenz curve and Gini coefficients to study the concentration of product sales across the treatment and control subsamples, as well as channel-specific subsamples. Lorenz curves and Gini coefficients have long been used to measure income inequality and wealth distribution (Gini 1921, Lorenz 1905) and more recently, sales diversity (Brynjolfsson et al. 2011, Oestreicher-Singer and Sundararajan 2012a, Gallino et al. 2016, Lee and Hosanagar 2019).

Figure 1 shows the Lorenz curves and Gini coefficients for the treatment and control samples, as well as based on channel use for the experiment. For view diversity, we find differences in the Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient for the recommendation treatment (Gini = 0.498 for treatment group, Gini = 0.674 for control group) but no differences between mobile and PC channels (Gini = 0.490 for mobile, Gini = 0.471 for PC). The Lorenz curves qualitatively show no differences in sales diversity between the treated and control groups with respect to the recommendation system treatment for the experiment. Indeed, the subsamples have similar Gini coefficients: 0.637 and 0.619. In contrast, the Lorenz curve for mobile customers has a steeper curve compared with PCbased customers $( \mathrm { G i n i } = 0 . 6 1 9$ for mobile, Gini = 0.656 for PC).

Comparing the Lorenz curves and the Gini coefficient are useful but do not provide the means to compute statistical differences. We utilize the permutation test (Good 2006, Hosanagar et al. 2014, Lee and Hosanagar 2019) to test for statistically significant differences in Gini coefficients between groups. In order to produce a p-value, we repeatedly and randomly shuffle the data set to produce null distributions for any test statistics. By comparing statistics from null distributions to the actual test statistics from the real distribution and assessing how many times null distribution statistics exceed the actual distribution statistics, we can determine p-values and infer statistical significance. We use two-sided tests to provide conservative inferences of differences, if any. In our study, we use 1,000 iterations to get an accurate p-value, and the results are shown in Figure 2.

As expected from the raw Gini coefficients, recommendation system usage has no significant effect on aggregate sales diversity $( p = 0 . 2 7 9 )$ , which runs counter to prior studies shown in Table A3 in the online appendix. However, customers who use mobile devices show statistically different sales diversities compared with PC-only customers $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ indicating that mobile-based customers tend to purchase a higher diversity of products than PC-based customers do. In contrast to sales diversity, intriguingly we find that recommendation system usage increases view diversity $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ but the use of mobile leads to very similar view diversities as the PC channel $( p =$ 0.351). We also test the moderating role of channe difference on the effect of recommendation system on sales and view diversity, by creating and comparing specific subsamples. We find no significant interaction effect between recommendation system and use of mobile channel on sales diversity.

The fact that the recommendation system enhances view diversity but not sales diversity, across both PC and mobile channel, is not entirely surprising. Recommender systems help increase visibility for niche products, thereby generating more views (and clicks) on such recommended niche products. However, in terms of conversion rates, the probability of a user buying a niche product is significantly lower than the probability of buying a popular product, as would be expected. Thus, we see that across both channels, a more diverse set of products are viewed but not necessarily purchased as a result of the recommendation system. From a managerial perspective, it is potentially useful to consider alternative methods to convert these views into purchases for niche products—the use of discount coupons or sales promotions specifically aimed at niche, recommended products can help increase their conversion rates. These additional strategies are less needed for popular products that gain from the recommendation system in terms of sales in any event.

We dig deeper into our data set to understand which products benefit from the presence of the recommendation system, across both channels. We, therefore, split the products in the sample into quartiles, based on pretreatment sales data. This allows us to examine how the recommendation system influences sales for the most popular products, relative to lesser popular, niche products. Using data on all customers across both channels, we estimate separate negative binomial regressions for the products in each quartile, with sales quantities as the dependent variable. The results are shown in Table 12 and show a distinct pattern of results. For popular products, that is, those in the top 25% quartile, the presence of the recommendation system across both channels has a positive impact on sales $( \beta = 1 . 5 3 1 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Similarly, for products in the bottom 25% quartile, that is, niche products, the system leads to greater sales $( \beta = 1 . 5 4 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . These effects are not observable for products in the middle quartiles. These results show that although the recommendation system does push sales out toward the edges of the sales distribution within specific subsamples, the cumulative effect is still weighted by the large sales of products in the middle quartiles where there is no discernible effect.

Because the products in the top quartile in general sell more relative to those in the bottom quartile, the marginal effect of the recommendation system appears to emphasize popular products relative to niche products, reinforcing the arguments made by Lee and Hosanagar (2019) that recommender systems enhance individual diversity but may not increase overall sales diversity. The recommendation system does lead to greater view diversity, that is, consumers do browse a greater range of products compared with those who do not have this option. However, this browsing does not lead to serendipitous purchase behavior. In summary, we see an increase in dollar sales and sales quantity from recommendation systems but a muted effect on overall sales diversity. The mobile channel, when viewed independently, appears to be associated with greater sales diversity. This effect is not influenced by the presence of the recommendation system. Thus, mobile users tend to be more diverse in their purchase behavior—a contingent channel-specific result new to the literature.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusion

The benefits of customized recommendation systems in online commerce have been established in the literature as well as in practice: considerable work has argued that such systems reduce cognitive effort on the part of the customer, while also allowing retailers and marketers to direct demand to niche or “long tail” products. Our work in this paper starts from this observation—although extant research provided insights into the value of recommendation systems, we note two significant gaps in the literature. First, there was a paucity of research addressing the use of recommendation systems in the mobile context, an increasingly relevant channel for online commerce (Einav et al. 2014). Indeed, recent research addressing the differences between mobile and PC channels provided strong priors to believe that the efficacy of recommendation systems may be higher in mobile channels, given the higher search and navigation costs (Ghose et al. 2013). Second, the effect of recommendation systems on sales diversity in e-commerce settings remained ambiguous, in terms of their tendency to homogenize or heterogenize the market (Fleder and Hosanagar 2009, Brynjolfsson et al. 2011, Hosanagar et al. 2014, Li and Karahanna 2015). Furthermore, the impact of mobile, as a channel, on sales diversity also remained unexplored. In this study, we address these gaps in the literature.

Our work provides an assessment of the impact of recommendation systems on customer-level decision outcomes in the context of online fashion retailing across mobile and PC-based customers. Through a randomized field experiment, we first show that consistent with prior work, the recommendation system is indeed associated with higher sales quantities, all else equal. Building on this baseline result, we show that these effects are particularly strong in the case of mobile users across the purchasing process, thereby indicating a significant moderating effect of the channel. Mobile users appear to be more responsive to recommendations, show more product views, and higher rates of clickthrough and conversion as a result. Although the definition of a mobile user is potentially complex, we also note that the vast majority of users in our sample primarily use the mobile device to access content on the retail platform, based on the availability of pretreatment data. This validates market predictions about the dominance of mobile in the retail sector (Fulgoni and Lipsman 2016) while also rendering our work here on the role of recommendation systems particularly relevant. The results from the experiment as well as the earlier pilot field experiment reported in the online appendix provide clear and relatively unambiguous evidence of the incremental value provided by recommendation systems to mobile users in particular.

In explaining the higher effectiveness of the recommendation system in the mobile channel, we invoked theoretical arguments pertaining to three interacting factors that are influential here—the impact of search costs, structural limitations imposed by the mobile ecosystem, and the benefits of ubiquity and engagement that mobile devices engender (Bang et al. 2013, Ghose et al. 2013, Maity and Dass 2014). Although each of these factors move in diverging ways, their cumulative effect is clearly positive in our empirical analysis. However, we acknowledge that a more granular identification of theoretical mechanisms would be desirable; we are limited by the constraints imposed by the research site. However, through our post hoc tests, we are able to provide some suggestive evidence of how the recommendation system in the mobile channel is able to offset some of the increased search and cognitive costs on users. Furthermore, we also see some evidence for how the extent to which users treat their devices as personal and ubiquitous helps increase the efficacy of the recommendation system on the retail platform. We thus see that mobile users provide an immediate and obvious uptick in response, not only on sales but also on views, clickthrough, and conversion.

Our results also provide insights to online retailers with significant investments in mobile on the value of recommendation systems even in constrained environments: mobile webpages are limited by screen size, bandwidth issues, and processing power. In such conditions, retailers may benefit from eliminating unnecessary features on their landing pages, while highlighting the value of even simple recommendation systems. In economic terms, the use of recommendation systems contributed an incremental \$7.6 million for the retailer that hosted our field experi ment. The approximate costs of deploying the functionality through the existing mobile infrastructure was roughly \$150,000 (in 2016), showing significant returns on investment, which is why the retailer insisted on extending the system to all customers after three experimental days. For the pilot field experiment reported in the online appendix, return on investment (ROI) figures are similar. The cost of deployment was roughly \$50,000 in 2015, whereas the reported incremental revenues from the experiment alone were \$2.3 million.

In terms of our second exploratory research objective, pertaining to sales diversity (Brynjolfsson et al. 2011, Hosanagar et al. 2014), our results show that these systems appear to have no significant effect either way. The results from the Gini coefficients are statistically indistinguishable between treatment and control groups; though recommendation systems have a positive and significant impact on view diversity, they appear to ultimately increase the sales of both niche and popular product consumptions. Similar results were obtained from the pilot field experiment reported in the online appendix as well. This could be a feature of the specific retailers we work with: neither retailer has the size of product mix observed in firms like Amazon.com or large American retailers like the Gap Inc. In both cases, the target demographics are very specific (“young, urban, fashion-conscious”). Additionally, the experiments were conducted in South Korea, whereas most of the earlier work was conducted in the United States. Further work is needed to examine if these factors influence the effects of recommendation systems on sales diversity.

With respect to mobile consumers, we do observe differences in the Gini coefficients relative to PC users. Even within the target demographic in the experiment, users on the mobile devices show greater diversity in their sales, suggesting that there might be value in designing specific recommendation algorithms for these consumers that are first, personalized to their tastes, and second, designed around the limitations of mobile devices. Indeed, in future work, we are working on the field experiment where we incorporate a customized recommendation system for mobile users (rather than the broad collaborative filtering algorithm used in this study) to gauge the effects on sales and sales diversity. More generally, as mobile retail becomes more ubiquitous and context becomes increasingly important, the ability to show consumers highly customized recommendations will become important as retailers look to increase their sales diversity.

We also discuss the limitations in the work presented here. Although randomization is based on the unique ID of the cookie downloaded onto the consumer’s machine, it is possible to reset this if the customer were to change machines or user IDs during the course of the experiment. This does occur in a small percentage of cases; we remove all such customers from our analysis. Second, as pointed out, we were constrained by the retail platform we worked with in terms of experimental design, data availability, or duration of experimental period. Through a series of robustness tests across the two field experiments, we believe we have addressed most of these limitations. Third, the field experiment was conducted in a specific retail sector (fashion retail and merchandise) in South Korea. Their generalizability to other retail contexts or countries needs to be established through additional empirical work. Fourth, we discuss several mechanisms at play (search costs, ubiquity, structural limitations of mobile devices) but can only identify their net effect in our analysis. The constraints of fieldwork make it difficult to conduct further experimentation to accurately tease out specific mechanisms. This remains an open avenue for future work that we are exploring.

Finally, as discussed earlier, we acknowledge the issue of channel selection as a limitation. To the extent that channel switching may be a problem, we have addressed that through the experiment. However, the residual issue of whether mobile users are fundamentally “different” from PC users, and whether these differences drive the use of the recommendation system, remains unresolved. Unfortunately, this can only be addressed through randomization of channel use, which is infeasible, as has been acknowledged in the literature on the mobile ecosystem (Jung et al. 2019). However, from a managerial perspective, understanding such differences may be relatively unimportant. If mobile and PC users are truly intrinsically different in terms of how they respond to recommendation systems, acting upon these differences in terms of the design and deployment of recommendation systems remains the main insight for managers. Because channel selection is rarely ever in the control of the retailer, not being able to separate the user from the channel may not affect the strategies adopted by the retailer looking to capitalize on recommendation systems across channels. From a theoretical perspective, however, we acknowledge this limitation.

Our work points out several avenues for future research. Clearly, there is potential for significant work in omni-channel use, in terms of understanding the implications of not only mobile device use but also other channels, including both online and offline channels on consumer behavior. Further work on examining the interrelationships between channels is needed to understand how retailers can operate optimally across them. Specific to recommendation systems, as discussed above, more work is needed to examine the changing relationship between recommendation systems and more contextual factors, such as location, industry sector, and multichannel use. There is tremendous potential in mobile ecosystems for advertising, promotions, and targeted sales, all of which are exciting areas for future research in information systems (IS). In this paper, we provide an example of the randomized field experiment that can be deployed to examine the effects of recommendation system use across mobile and PC channels on both customer decision outcomes and market outcomes in the retail sector. There are several other questions of interest at the intersection of mobile platforms, retail commerce, and personalization technologies that are in the sweet spot for IS research; we hope that our work here helps to further this type of research in the future.
