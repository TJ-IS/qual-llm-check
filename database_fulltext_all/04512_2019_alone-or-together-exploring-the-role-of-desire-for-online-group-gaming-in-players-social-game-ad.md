## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online social game addiction Desire for online group gaming Habit Self-regulation Dual-system theory Technology addiction

## A B S T R A C T

Drawing on dual-system theory, this study examines the role of desire for online group gaming in online social game addiction. By conducting a longitudinal online survey, we show that attitude, anticipated enjoyment, group norm, and social identity contribute to the development of desire for online group gaming. Such desire facilitates habit and self-regulation deficiency, which, in turn, fosters online social game addiction. This study contributes to the literature by theorizing a nomological network of desire for online group gaming on online social game addiction and by ofering implications for managing addictive gaming

## 1. Introduction

Online social games, which are facilitated by social networking sites such as WeChat, Twitter, and Facebook, have rapidly become prevalent digital pastimes for online players [1]. Online social games enable online social interaction among players through competitive, cooperative, and competitive-cooperative activities in social game communities [2]. For example, players can develop interpersonal relationships, exchange virtual gifts or coins, and play as a member of guild with their com munity friends [3,4]. Given their socialization features, online social games have gained burgeoning popularity. A serious social consequence of such popularity is online social game addiction [5]. Many reports have highlighted the increasing concerns over young players’ obsessive and addictive gaming. For example, Candy Crush, a Facebook-based and social puzzle game, has 93 million active users who play the game at an average of over 10 times per day [1]. Another famous online social game, Honor of Kings, has more than 200 million registered players, with every player spending an average of nearly 6 h per week playing the game [6]. Players’ addictive online gaming is often associated with various detrimental consequences, such as depression, stress, sleep disturbance, and even death [7,8]. As an increasingly prevalent societal challenge, online social game addiction warrants further research in terms of its antecedents and underlying mechanisms.

Technology addiction has received increasing attention in the information systems (IS) research community [9–16,18,19]. Although previous research has identified a variety of antecedents of user technology addiction, the majority of these antecedents pertain to sociopsychological factors, such as demographics [1], addiction liability [18], perceived enjoyment [12,16], and habit $[ 1 5 , 1 6 , 1 9 ]$ . The impact of social factors (e.g., socialization and group-referent behavior with other users) on technology addiction has been largely overlooked in the literature. One possible explanation is that most studies examined technology addiction in a context in which the behavior is individualreferent and does not usually involve other users (e.g., using smartphones, surfing the Internet, or watching online videos). A review of prior online game addiction literature also suggests that the concept of socialization is often neglected (see Appendix A). We found that only three online game addiction studies have mentioned the role of socialization in online game addiction [8,20,21]. Despite being an insightful start, prior studies did not examine the antecedents of socialization and their underlying mechanisms shaping online game addiction. There is a lack of systematic and theoretical understanding of the role of socialization in the formation of online game addiction.

Motivated by these issues in the technology addiction research, the primary research objective is to develop a systematic and contextualized understanding of online social game addiction. To achieve this objective, we draw on dual-system theory [22,23] to construct a research model that explains online social game addiction. By doing so, we advance the literature in two ways. First, we focus on socialization, which is particularly relevant in the online social game context. By incorporating socialization in the technology addiction models, we provide a contextualized theoretical explanation of online social game addiction, casting a new light on future investigations of technology addictions to social technologies. Second, we focus on dual-system theory to construct a nomological network explaining online social game addiction. Specifically, we emphasize the role of socialization (i.e., the desire for online group gaming) and examine its antecedents and consequences. This study enhances our theoretical understanding of the psychological mechanisms shaping online social game addiction. We believe that understanding online social game addiction is timely and of practical relevance. The results provide practitioners with in sights into intervention and prevention of online social game addiction, which has severe impact to the new generation.

## 2. Theoretical background

We first review prior studies on technology addiction and specify key issues tied to behavioral addictions in the context of online social games. We then proceed to introduce the concept of socialization and dual-system theory [22,23], which are the theoretical foundation of this study.

## 2.1. Technology addiction

Technology addiction is a maladaptive and obsessive pattern of technology use behaviors that conflicts with one’s normal functioning or other important activities [9]. Consistent with Turel et al.’s [9] definition of technology addiction, we operationalize online social game addiction as a maladaptive psychological dependency on online social games, which is typically accompanied by the following symptoms: (1) mood regulation—playing online social games provides mood enhancement and relief; (2) tolerance—a user has to play online social games to a higher degree to induce arousal; (3) relapse—a user cannot easily limit the number of times s/he plays online social games voluntarily; (4) conflict—playing online social games creates a set of interpersonal, intrapersonal, and academic/professional problems; (5) withdrawal—if a user cannot play online social games, then s/he may experience negative emotions; and (6) salience—a user’s behaviors are dominated by playing online social games.

To understand the current status of research on online game addiction, we conducted a literature review on prior online game addiction studies. Our literature review shows several research patterns that motivate the current investigation (see Appendix A). We notice that researchers have adopted diverse theoretical frameworks to explain online game addiction. For instance, social cognitive theory [81] is the most popular theory used in explaining online game addiction. Researchers suggested that personality traits, loneliness, depression, and deficient self-regulation are the key determinants of online game addiction [5,7,8,21,46]. Flow theory [82] has also been used to explain how players’ flow experience is positively related to excessive online gaming [47,48]. Some studies based on motivation theories [83] contended that players’ psychological needs, such as need for curiosity, role-playing, belonging, escapism, and mechanics, foster their addictive online gaming behaviors [17]. Further, some researchers used the opponent process theory [84] and rational addiction theory [85] to examine players’ addictive online gaming behavior [1,49]. Based on our literature review, the role of socialization in shaping players’ online social gaming behavior has not been systematically investigated in the technology addiction literature.

## 2.2. The concept of socialization

The concept of socialization has its stem from the intentional social action literature [26,27,30], which has been widely used to explain the development of socialization and group-referent behaviors. For examples, Tsai and Bagozzi [26] reported that users desire to write articles or share photos with a group of their community friends in online sharing communities. Bagozzi and Dholakia [29] found that users are usually craving for completing a joint project with their community friends in open source software communities. Similarly, Dholakia et al. [27] and Bagozzi and Dholakia [32] argued that users are craving for identifying themselves with small friendship groups and shaping group profiles in online brand communities. The psychological afiliation with a friendship group ofers users collectively constructed desires for socialization and group-referent behaviors with their community friend [31,32]. Fig. 1 depicts the relationship among user, small friendship group, and virtual community.

Previous research hypothesizes that three bases determine a user’s desire for socialization and group-referent behaviors in virtual communities, namely informational, emotional, and social influences [26,27,30]. Informational influence is defined as a user’s cognitive appraisal of group-referent behaviors along a positive-to-negative dimension [29]. It contributes to a user’s desires by ofering them informational justifications and supports for engaging in group-referent behaviors. Emotional influence refers to forward-looking afective re actions toward engaging in group-referent behaviors [29]. Positive anticipated emotions would drive users to imagine the pleasant aspects of group-referent behaviors and eventually foster their behavioral desires to participate in such behaviors. Social influence originates from the normative influences of compliance, internalization, and identifi cation processes: (1) compliance process denotes the influence of a need for approval from important others, such as friends, family, colleagues, and other socially important references in the real world; (2) inter nalization process reflects the congruence of one’s goals or values with a specific small friendship group in virtual communities; and (3) identification process demonstrates one’s self-awareness, afective commitment, and collective self-esteem of her/his membership in the small friendship group [26]. Previous studies show that informational, emotional, and social influences altogether formulate users’ desires for socialization and group-referent behaviors in virtual communities $[ 2 6 , 2 7 , 3 0 ]$

## 2.3. Dual-system theory

Dual-system theory [22.23] has been widely used to explain the etiology of addictive behaviors (see Appendix B). The theory posits that temptation desires can trigger a user’s behavior through two structurally diferent systems, namely the reflexive and reflective systems [22,23]. Temptation desires refer to a user’s cravings for performing a specific behavior [27]. A user’s desire can be conceptualized in two diferent ways: desire for individual-referent behaviors and desire fo group-referent behaviors (see Appendix C). The first type of desire is a personal desire to perform an action alone without the influence of social groups. The second type of desire is a social desire rooted in one’s self-conception of a member of a particular social group and reflects one’s motivation to socialize with the members of social groups. The reflexive system is a habitual, reactive, and largely automatic process [24]. This system drives us to automatically trigger a habitual behavior consistent with our short-term desires without thinking about the consequences of such behavior. Meanwhile, the reflective system is an inhibitory, self-regulated, and controlled process [24]. When a behavior is associated with negative consequences, the reflective system will activate self-regulation to inhibit habitual responses in order to align with one’s long-term goals. The individuals’ day-to-day behavior is an outcome of a “tug of war” between the two aforementioned systems [28]. The balance between the two systems would generate normal and healthy behavior, while their imbalance would yield deviant and ad dictive behavior.

Dual-system theory [22,23] has been recently extended to under stand the phenomena of technology addiction in the IS research community. These studies implicitly uncovered three key principles of the theory. The first principle suggests that technology addiction is an outcome of the reflexive–reflective system imbalance, which is often manifested as a strong habitual tendency to trigger an obsessive technology use combined with impaired self-regulation functions to inhibit such obsessive use [15,25,28]. The second principle contends that the reflexive and reflective systems often interact with each other to influence technology addiction [24]. The third principle posits that the reflexive–reflective system imbalance is gradually triggered by temptation desires [23].

Following dual-system theory, this study operationalizes temptation desire as desire for socialization and group-referent behaviors; deindi viduation efect as a loss of sense of individuality through submerge in a small friendship group; reflexive system as a habitual, largely automatic, and reactive process; reflective system as a self-regulated, inhibitory, and controlled process; and technology addiction as online social game addiction. ## 3. Research model and hypotheses

In this section, we contextualize the relationships between core constructs found in dual-system theory [22,23] to online social game addiction. Specifically, we propose that habit and self-regulation deficiency are positively associated with the online social game addiction. Further, a high level of habit positively moderates the efect of selfregulation deficiency on online social game addiction. Subsequently, we argue that desire for online group gaming enhances players’ habit and self-regulation deficiency for online social games. Building upon the intentional social action literature [26,27,30], we incorporate the informational, emotional, and social influence factors into the model to predict desire for online group gaming. We argue that attitude (i.e., informational influence), anticipated enjoyment $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ emotional influence), subjective norm, group norm, and social identity (i.e., social influence) positively afect players’ desire for online group gaming. Fig. 3 depicts our research model.

## 3.1. Core predictors of online social game addiction

Based on dual-system theory [22,23], we propose the relationships among habit, self-regulation deficiency, and online social game addiction. The theory posits that (1) the reflexive–reflective system imbalance determines technology addiction; and (2) the reflexive and reflective systems often interact with each other to foster technology addiction [15,24]. Thus, we expect that the reflexive–reflective system imbalance and interaction foster online social game addiction.

## 3.1.1. The reflexive system

The reflexive system processes informational cues automatically to activate preexisting behavior with little conscious efort [22]. The IS literature has frequently employed habit to manifest the reflexive system [15,28,39]. Habit refers to the extent to which users tend to use a technology automatically because of learning [40]. Habit formation requires a certain amount of behavioral repetition and specific situational cues [16]. Upon its establishment, habit is performed automatically with minimal mental efort and conscious attention [19]. Thus, a habitual behavior is a reflexive system in nature because of its minimal requirements for deliberate control and cognitive processing [15].

In this study, the reflexive system is operationalized as habit, which refers to a player’s tendency to play online social games with a group of her/his community friends automatically because of learning [40]. A strong habit means that a player views playing online social games with her/his group of community friends as a natural choice. Such habitual tendency may drive the player to overemphasize the short-term thrills and underestimate the long-term risks of her/his gaming behavior [16]. These changes occur through the incentive-sensitization system, in which a habitual player’s brain becomes highly sensitive to the salience of behavioral thrills and pays less conscious attention to the future consequences of a certain behavior [19]. As a result, habitual gaming may further transform into addictive gaming. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H1. Habit is positively related to online social game addiction.

## 3.1.2. The reflective system

The reflective system processes informational cues with conscious cognitive processing and deliberate control to regulate individual be havior [22]. In the IS literature, one of the most frequently studied variables associated with the reflective system is self-regulation defi ciency, which describes one’s failure and state of inadequacy in monitoring, judging, and adjusting her/his behavior [15]. For instance, Davazdahemami et al. [42] and Soror et al. [15,25] employed self regulation deficiency to manifest the reflective system in the context of mobile phone addiction. Turel and Qahri-Saremi [24] and Turel and Osatuvi [28] operationalized the reflective system as a self-regulation deficiency when investigating the nature of social networking addiction. In a similar view, Wang et al. [39] contended that self-regulation deficiency is a typical manifestation of the reflective system in the context of microblog addiction. All of these studies suggest that a strong self-regulation can help users regulate their technology addiction behaviors, while a deficient self-regulation means that individuals are incapable of adjusting their addictive technology use. Consequently, self-regulation deficiency is an appropriate manifestation of the reflective system in which it is consistent with past IS literature and highly relevant to the technology addiction context.

In accordance with prior research [15,28,39], we employ self-reg ulation deficiency to manifest the impaired reflective system. Self-regulation deficiency refers to a player’s failure to adequately judge, monitor, and adjust playing online social games with a group of her/his community friends [8]. This factor comprises the following dimensions: (1) afective irreplaceability, which denotes players’ afective commitment in online group gaming with their community friends that cannot be replaced by any other activities; (2) cognitive preoccupation, which means that players are consciously preoccupied with thoughts of online group gaming with their community friends; and (3) behavioral compulsion, which indicates that players are unable to control their behavioral urge to engage in online group gaming with their community friends [21,39]. Players with deficient self-regulation are vulnerable to play online social games obsessively and addictively [5,7,8]. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H2. Self-regulation deficiency is positively related to online social game addiction.

## 3.1.3. The reflexive-reflective system interaction

Habit and self-regulation may interact with each other and de monstrate the reinforcement efect on online social game addiction. As mentioned earlier, habit diminishes the conscious cognitive processing with which a behavior is performed. After establishing a habit, individuals automatically activate their online group gaming behavior with their community friends because of a prior satisfactory usage experience [40]. They become sensitive to the short-term thrills and pay little attention to the potential negative consequences of their online group gaming behavior [16,19]. Under this situation, players with deficient self-regulation tend to foster addictive online group gaming behavior because they neutralize deviant gaming as acceptable and “rationally” indulge in the game. By contrast, a low level of habit means that players would make an accurate assessment of a behavior. They also acknowledge that addictive online group gaming induces a set of negative problems, such as depression, sleep disturbance, and poor academic/work performance. Therefore, these players may sufer from cognitive dissonance if they continue engaging in addictive online group gaming with their community friends [9]. To reduce such cognitive dissonance, players would try their best to inhibit their addictive online group gaming behavior. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H3. Habit strengths the efect of self-regulation deficiency on online social game addiction.

## 3.2. Desire for online group gaming and the deindividuation efect

Congruent with dual-system theory [22,23], we propose the relationships among desire for online group gaming, habit, and self-regulation deficiency. The theory posits that temptation desire fosters the reflexive–reflective system imbalance [23]. In online social game communities, we investigate one type of such temptation desire—desire for online group gaming. In line with Tsai and Bagozzi [26], we define desire for online group gaming as a player’s craving for playing online social games with a group of her/his community friends. The accumulated desire for online group gaming would change the nature of the reflexive–reflective system to foster addictive behaviors through the underlying deindividuation efect.

Deindividuation efect refers to “the loss of one’s sense of individuality through submergence in a social group” [34, p.239]. This efect captures individual members’ psychological state of categorizing themselves as group members, sharing in-group norms and goals, and behaving similar to other in-group members [35]. Early sociology re search finds that deindividuation efect tends to foster problematic and antinormative behaviors because of a deficient self-regulation and the lack of concern for social comparison [36]. When individuals are deindividuated in a social group, their individual-referent self-aware ness will transform into group-referent membership [34]. They tend to regulate their behaviors based on group norms rather than their internal standards and values [37]. Therefore, any antinormative behavior that the general public regards as unacceptable or problematic may be justified as “acceptable” on the basis of in-group norms and standards. For instance, prior IS studies uncover that deindividuation within online deviant friendship groups would magnify the influence of group norms and eventually lead to problematic and addictive technology use [14,35,38]. In social game communities, desire for online group gaming and the underlying deindividuation efect may distort the reflexive–reflective system balance, thereby making players susceptible to addictive gaming.

Previous studies on habit suggest that frequent repetitions of and positive experiences with a behavior are key conditions for habit development [40]. In social game communities, a strong desire for online group gaming can increase one’s tendency to play games repetitively with a small friendship group [27]. The more frequently a behavior is performed, the more likely it is for conscious cognitive processing to be transformed into automatic responses and habitual use [19]. Further more, the deindividuation driven by desire for online group gaming can result ingroup favoritism, heightened enjoyment, and collective selfesteem in participating in group activities [26]. These satisfactory experiences will drive players to play games repetitively with their community friends $[ 1 6 , 1 9 ]$ . As a result, players with a high level of desire for online group gaming tend to form a habitual gaming behavior with their community friends. Therefore, we hypothesize the following

## H4. Desire for online group gaming is positively related to habit.

Previous research on self-regulation argues that temptation desires may impair individuals’ self-regulatory functions of judging, monitoring, and adjusting their behaviors [50]. In online social game communities, players with a strong desire for online group gaming may deindividuate themselves in small friendship groups [8]. Players in a deindividuated state tend to regulate their in-group behaviors on the basis of in-group norms rather than their internal processes, values, and standards [35,37]. Such tendency diminishes their self-regulation ability to adjust their gaming behavior with their group of community friends. As a consequence, players with a strong desire for online group gaming have a higher tendency to develop self-regulation deficiency. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H5. Desire for online group gaming is positively related to self regulation deficiency.

## 3.3. Antecedents of desire for online group gaming

The intentional social action literature [26,27,30] proposes that informational, emotional, and social influences are the three key prerequisites for one’s desire to engage in group-referent behaviors. In online social game communities, we operationalize informational influence as attitude, which refers to a player’s summary evaluation of playing games with a group of her/his community friends along a positive-to-negative dimension [29]. Positive attitudes mean that players view gaming with their community friends as a beneficial, favorable, and rewarding activity. We denote emotional influence by anticipated enjoyment, which refers to a player’s forward-looking afective reactions and gratifications that are derived from playing online games with her/his group of community friends [43]. A high level of anticipated enjoyment will induce players to imagine the pleasurable aspects of group gaming as fun and enjoyable. Empirical studies have identified attitude and anticipated enjoyment as important bases for a user to participate in group activities in virtual communities, such as open source communities [29], online brand communities [27,30], and online sharing communities [26]. Accordingly, we expect that players with positive attitudes and anticipated enjoyment are more likely to develop desires to participate in online group gaming. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

## H6. Attitude is positively related to desire for online group gaming.

H7. Anticipated enjoyment is positively related to desire for online group gaming.

Social influence is particularly influential in determining one’s desires to participate in group-referent behaviors. The intentional social action literature [26,27,30] examined three modes of social influence, namely compliance process (i.e., subjective norm), internalization process (i.e., group norm), and identification process (i.e., social identity). Subjective norm reflects the social compliance of expectations from important others [29]. These important social references are friends, family, colleagues, and other people they follow in the real world [51,52]. At the early stage of online group gaming, players have limited prior gaming experience with their group of community friends. Thus, they may rely on prevailing subjective expectations significantly from others to formulate their desires to engage in online group gaming. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H8. Subjective norm is positively related to desire for online group gaming.

After a player starts to engage in gaming with a group of her/his community friends, the social influence from in-group members (i.e., internalization and identification) plays a prominent role in determining behavioral desires [26]. The internalization process that underlies group norm reflects the congruence of a player’s values or goals with her/his small friendship group in a social game community [27,30]. In the friendship group, group norm ensures a mutual agreement regarding the consequences of engaging in group activities [32]. A high level of group norm implicitly yields a consensus among members with respect to how and when to participate in group gaming. In this respect, when a player’s goals and values are congruent with those of other group members, s/he tends to have higher desires to participate in online group gaming. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

## H9. Group norm is positively related to desire for online group gaming.

The identification process is further manifested as social identity, which indicates that a player construes herself/himself as a member of a small friendship group in a social game community [53]. Social identity involves the following elements: (1) cognitive social identity, which reflects a player’s cognitive awareness of her/his group membership; (2) afective social identity, which captures the player’s emotional feelings of commitment, attachment, and belongingness to the focal group; and (3) evaluative social identity, which represents the collective or group-based self-esteem derived from group membership [29]. Through the identification process, players will formulate desires to participate in online group gaming by viewing themselves as group members, sharing in-group goals, and acting similar to other in-group members [54–56]. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H10. Social identity is positively related to desire for online group gaming.

## 4. Research methodology

We conducted a longitudinal field survey in China to empirically validate our research model. A longitudinal survey has been proven efective in establishing causality, reducing common method bias, and observing players’ addictive behaviors over time [24,26]. Honor of Kings, a popular multiplayer online social game, was employed as the research context to examine the development of addictive gaming behavior. Details of the research setting, data collection procedures, and measure development processes are discussed below.

## 4.1. Research setting

Honor of Kings is a multiplayer online social game released by Tencent. The game requires players to login with their WeChat or QQ accounts, the two dominant social media apps in China. After logging in, players can group with other game community members to start a 5v5 online battle. Honor of Kings ofers players two basic types of game groups, namely “guilds” and “friends” (see Appendix E). Guilds are formal game groups in which a collection of players are linked to one another with specified names, goals, and norms [57]. Guild members regularly play together in a group to raise their guild rankings and points. They are able to communicate with other guild members through various in-game features, such as guild messengers, guild boards, and guild chatrooms [57]. Meanwhile, friends are informal game groups with no specified names, goals, and norms. Members of these groups include WeChat or QQ friends, general game community friends, and location-based friends. Friend members can quickly form a five-player group to participate in a battle. After the battle, the group will temporarily disappear. Honor of Kings also provides in-game fea tures, such as friend messengers, to facilitate social interactions among friend members. These socialization features make Honor of Kings enjoy massive popularity among Chinese players. As of June 2017, the game had over 50 million daily active users, 200 million registered users, and an estimated quarterly revenue of \$ 1.8 billion [58]. The massive popularity of the game is accompanied with heavy criticism from parents and teachers, who describe the game as addictive for young gamers. The newspaper People’s Daily even describes the game as a “digital drug” to teenagers [6]. These observations show that Honor of Kings is an appropriate research setting for this study.

## 4.2. Data collection

Honor of Kings players were invited to complete online questionnaires at three different instances. Fig. 4 presents the data collection timeline. At time T0 (wave 0), we conducted a pilot study with 33 Honor of Kings players to clarify the instructions and sentences in the online questionnaires. The feedback of these players, such as on the wording and layout of certain questions, helped us improve the final questionnaires. At time T1 (wave 1), we recruited more than 2000 active Honor of Kings players to participate in the first survey. The questionnaire items were used to measure desire for online group gaming and its antecedents, including subjective norm, group norm, social identity, anticipated enjoyment, and attitude. At time T2 (wave 2), six weeks later, the respondents in wave 1 were invited to participate in the second survey. Their responses were used to assess selfregulation deficiency, habit, online social game addiction, and control variables. To encourage participation, we ofered \$5 to respondents who submitted information in both T1 and T2. After completing the questionnaires at both waves 1 and 2, the promised reward was automatically sent to the WeChat accounts of the participants.

We posted the final two questionnaires on a popular online survey platform called Sojump. The URL of the questionnaires was randomly distributed to the participants under the title “Opinion Survey Regarding Honor of Kings Players’ Online Group Gaming Experiences.” We employed the key informant method to help respondents efectively retrieve their online group gaming experiences with the small friendship groups in the social game community. Initially, the participants were required to log in to Honor of Kings by using their WeChat ac counts. They should identify up to, but not necessarily, five game community members with whom they regularly interacted. These members could appear in either their friend lists or guild lists. Then, the participants were asked to picture briefly the images of each group member and recall their previous online group gaming experiences with these members. They answered the questions by following the recal information specific to their online group gaming experiences. The key informant method was proposed by Seidler [59, p.816] to survey “a small number of knowledgeable participants who observe and articulate social relationships for the researchers.” This method was widely adopted to retrieve participants’ perceptions toward group-referent behaviors with their small friendship groups in virtual communities [26,29].

A total of 931 usable questionnaires were gathered at T1, and 627 valid responses were collected at both T1 and T2. WeChat accounts were used to match the respondents’ answers in the two waves of data collection. The average age of the respondents was approximately 22 years, and over 95% of the participants were aged below 30 years. Furthermore, 69.4% of the respondents were males, 72.2% held bachelor’s degrees or above, and 95.7% had a monthly income of US \$1500 or below. In terms of usage frequency and experience, the majority of the respondents reported playing Honor of Kings more than three times per week (85.1%) and having at least three months of game experience (92.5%). We checked for social desirability bias and common method bias. As predicted, these subjective biases were not major concerns of this study (see Appendix F).

## 4.3. Measurement development

All measurement items were adapted from well-established existing scales (see Appendix G). We used the scales from Turel et al. [9] to operationalize the dependent variable, namely online social game addiction. The scales for habit were adapted from Turel and Serenko [16]. Self-regulation deficiency was a formative second-order construct with three dimensions, namely afective irreplaceability, behavioral compulsion, and cognitive preoccupation. The scales for afective irrepla ceability were adapted from Wang et al. [39], whereas the measures for behavioral compulsion and cognitive preoccupation were borrowed from Haagsma et al. [21]. Social identity was also a formative construct with afective, cognitive, and evaluative elements. We followed Tsai and Bagozzi [26] to develop the scales for these social identity dimensions. The measures for desire for socialization, attitude, and group norm were adapted from Tsai and Bagozzi [26], the scales for anticipate enjoyment were adapted from Lowry et al. [43], and the measures for subjective norm were adapted from Lowry et al. [35].

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

This work seeks to understand how desire for online group gaming afects players’ online social game addiction through the reflexive–reflective system. Our empirical results show that strong habit and self regulation deficiency positively influence online social game addiction. Meanwhile, a strong habit will strengthen the impact of self-regulation deficiency on online social game addiction. Furthermore, desire for online group gaming facilitates habit and self-regulation deficiency, which, in turn, fosters social game addiction. These findings confirm the three key principles of dual-system theory: (1) the reflexive–reflective system imbalance fosters users’ technology addiction, (2) these two systems often interact with each other to manifest their roles in addictive technology use, and (3) the reflexive–reflective system imbalance is influenced by temptation desires [15,24]. Our empirical results also support that attitude, anticipated enjoyment, group norm, and social identity positively influence players’ desire for socialization. These findings are congruent with the intentional social action literature [26,27,30], where informational, emotional, and social influences are important determinants in predicting a user’s desire for group-re ferent behaviors with his/her group of virtual community friends.

Based on the above findings, our study confirms that desire for online group gaming and the underlying deindividuation efect are the main drivers of players’ online social game addiction. This indicates that, at time T1 or earlier, a player may log into an online social game and afiliate with a small friendship group (e.g., friend group, guild group, or both) in a game community. The player can either participate in the friendship group actively or be invited by other in-group mem bers passively. After becoming a member of the group, s/he is likely to play the game together with other in-group members and formulate ingroup attitudes, emotions, norms, and identities. These informational, emotional, and social determinants contribute to the player’s desire for gaming with her/his group of community friends. At time T2, the augmented desire for online group gaming deindividuates and immerses the player within her/his small friendship group. Such deindi viduation efect not only leads the player to play the game with her/his community friends repetitively and habitually, but also gradually diminishes her/his self-regulation in adjusting such online group gaming behavior. As a consequence, even if the player recognizes that her/his habitual online group gaming has become addictive, s/he is incapable of regulating such indulgence. In this case, the reflexive–reflective system imbalance, which is manifested as the combination of a strong habit and a deficient self-regulation, further fosters online social game addiction.

At the same time, engaging in online group gaming has rapidly become a prevalent digital pastime for game players in online social game communities [1]. Thus, investigating the antecedents of players’ addictive behavior pertaining to social factors is theoretically and practically significant. This study is among the first to fill this knowledge gap in the technology addiction literature by theorizing and empirically testing the impact of desire for online group gaming on online social game addiction. Our empirical results ofer a nomological framework of desire for online social game addiction with respect to how such desire is formulated by informational, emotional, and social determinants, and how it fosters players social game addiction through the reflexive and reflective systems.

Second, this study advances the technology addiction literature by introducing dual-system theory to the context of online social game addiction. Dual-system theory has been originally employed by sociologists to understand substance addiction, such as overeating, binge drinking, and drug dependence [22,23]. Recently, some IS scholars have started to apply the theory to investigate technology addiction, including mobile phone addiction [15,25,42], Facebook addiction [24,28], and microblog addiction [39]. However, the technology addiction literature yields scanty evidence on the applicability of dualsystem theory to understand online social game addiction [86–89]. Our empirical findings confirm that dual-system theory is also applicable to online social game addiction. Thus, this study can serve as a starting point for future addiction-related research in the online social game industry.

Third, this study contributes to dual-system theory by summarizing and empirically validating three key principles in technology addiction research. These three principles suggest that (1) technology addiction is an outcome of the imbalance between the reflexive and reflective systems; (2) the two systems often interact with each other to influence technology addiction; and (3) temptation desires formulate the reflexive–reflective system imbalance [15,22–24]. The above principles clearly elaborate the relationships among the principal elements of dual-system theory, including temptation desires, the reflexive system, the reflective system, and addictive behaviors. The majority of prior addiction studies have only examined either one or two principles of this theory (see Appendix B), thereby ofering an incomplete understanding of dual-system theory with respect to users’ addictive behaviors. We expect that the three key principles can address such incompleteness and serve as bases for future technology addiction research.

Fourth, this study enriches dual-system theory by integrating desire for online group gaming and the deindividuation efect into the theory. The majority of the previously examined desires pertain to “desire for individual-referent behaviors,” such as desire for alcohol [23] and craving for candy [65]. Few studies recognize the influence of “desire for socialization and group-referent behaviors” when using dual-system theory to investigate addictive behaviors. In online social game communities, we employ desire for online group gaming as a contextual manifestation of desire for socialization and group-referent behaviors. Such desire deindividuates players into a small friendship group in a large game community [26,32]. This deindividuation efect causes players to experience a shift of self-awareness from the individuality to the group [35], which further leads to a reflexive–reflective system imbalance and online social game addiction. Integrating the deindivi duation efect into dual-system theory can provide plausible explana tions for such group-referent addiction phenomenon. Therefore, our work extends dual-system theory from individual-referent addictive behaviors to group-referent addictive behaviors.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

This study also ofers several key practical implications. First, our findings suggest that altering the reflexive–reflective system imbalance can help players recover from online social game addiction. By reflecting on the conceptualization and measures of such imbalance, players can take steps to (1) disrupt their habits and (2) enhance their self-regulation abilities in playing online social games. Several interventions for modifying and breaking habits can be derived from the extant habit literature [41]. For example, reducing gaming frequency and daily gaming time can quickly diminish players’ online social game habits [66]. Furthermore, imposing restrictions on the use of in-game features (e.g., guild messengers, boards, and chatrooms) can also make players escape from a habitual gaming state [16]. Players may also adopt regulatory strategies, such as information- and capacity-enhancing measures, to control their self-regulation deficiency. Informationenhancing measures focus on those players who are unaware of the detrimental consequences of their game addiction and are in need of such information to prevent distorted emotion, maladaptive thoughts, and behavioral compulsion [1]. For example, players could pay attention to game playing statistics and warnings presented at the game interface. Capacity-enhancing measures focus on the eforts of families, educational institutions, and game providers in monitoring players gaming behavior [1]. With the help coming from important external sources (e.g., families and teachers), players can gradually reinforce self-disciplinary and rational regulation abilities.

Second, our study shows that desire for online group gaming gradually leads to a reflexive–reflective imbalance and eventually fosters online social game addiction. Thus, the psychological afiliation of addicted players with their small friendship groups should be reduced and their desire for gaming with their group of community friends should be controlled. Addicted players should be aware of the adverse influences of their small friendship group afiliation and reduce their gaming time with in-group members as soon as possible. When these players want to participate in online group gaming, they can do other group activities instead, such as playing basketball. Such “attention switch” can help these players inhibit their desires for online group gaming [17]. Families and educational institutions can also conduct family conversations and on-campus workshops, respectively, to persuade addicted players to temporarily stay away from their small friendship groups. Game providers may also send emails to addicted players to encourage them to reduce the time and frequency of their online group gaming. They may even temporarily ban these addicted players from in-game talking, cooperating, or grouping with others until the players control their game addiction.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations that ofer opportunities for future work. First, the generalizability of the research findings may be limited because we only investigate one type of online social game (e.g., Honor of Kings). Diferent online social games have unique characteristics and support distinct social interactions that may influence players’ online social game addiction. As suggested by Liu et al. [2], online social games can be classified into casual digital games, team simulation games, and massive multiplayer games. Casual digital games, such as Candy Crush and chess games, support skilled-based matching and competitive interactions where two or more players compete to be the best. Team simulation games, such as Virtual Peace, allow cooperative interactions in which players coordinate with one another in a team to face common challenges. Massive multiplayer games, such as World of Warcraft, League of Legends, and Honor of Kings, ofer both competitive and cooperative interactions where players join groups and compete with one another as a group. To examine the generalizability of our findings, future studies are suggested to extend our research to other online social game contexts. Second, this research only examines desire for online group gaming as a contextual manifestation of desire for socialization and group-referent behaviors. In online social game communities, players may experience various types of social interactions, such as developing interpersonal relationships, grouping with others, and playing as a part of a friendly casual guild. Although engaging in online group gaming has increasingly become the dominant social interaction activity for most players in online social game communities, it may not fully reflect players’ desire for socialization. Thus, future research should further develop the concept of desire for socialization and investigate the role of socialization in the formation of online social game addiction.

## 6.4. Conclusion

This study advances our understanding of online social game addiction by introducing a dual-system theory perspective and examining the impact of desire for online group gaming. Desire for online group gaming, which is formed by attitude, anticipated enjoyment, group norm, and social identity, deindividuates players in their group of community friends. Such deindividuation efect further facilitates players’ habitual group gaming and diminishes their self-regulation toward group gaming, thereby leading to online social game addiction. These findings highlight the impacts of desire for online group gaming on online social game addiction and integrate the deindividuation efect into dual-system theory. Therefore, our work contributes to a better understanding of online social game addiction in particular and dual system theory in general.
