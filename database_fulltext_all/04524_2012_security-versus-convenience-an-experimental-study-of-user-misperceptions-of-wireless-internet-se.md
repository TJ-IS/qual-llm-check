# Security versus convenience? An experimental study of user misperceptions of wireless internet service quality

Byung Cho Kim <sup>a</sup>, Yong Wan Park <sup>b,</sup>⁎

<sup>a</sup> Graduate School of Management of Technology, Sogang University, Seoul 121-742, Korea

<sup>b</sup> Department of Marketing, Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Tech, Blacksburg, VA 24061, USA

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 23 March 2010 Received in revised form 26 May 2011 Accepted 21 August 2011 Available online 26 August 2011

Keywords: Information security Security/convenience tradeoff Security engineering Inference making

## a b s t r a c t

This paper demonstrates that consumers make incorrect inferences about security/convenience tradeoff. We <sup>fi</sup>nd the evidence that consumers tend to infer unobservable security quality from observable convenience and that their inferences are not always correct. In four studies, we examine user perceptions of wireless Internet service quality, with an aim to understand consumers' irrational choice of a dominated product over a dominant option. Our results indicate that consumers make inference in security from convenience using a zero-sum heuristic and that they believe in improving security in return for losing convenience. In a choice setting, we empirically show that security perception, as well as convenience, in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumers' product choices, contradicting the common view of existing literature that convenience is the sole driver of consumer choice. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that spontaneous and extensive education of consumers about security makes a modest impact on their inference making.

## 1. Introduction

As the Internet becomes a crucial part of our lives, more companies use the Internet for business, resulting in the transmission of large amounts of data including countless online transactions. Accordingly, security becomes a critical success factor for online business and electronic commerce. The Obama administration has put a priority on securing cyber space and digital infrastructure, as has been seen in the government's recent cyber security efforts, including creation of a top position charged with protecting online security [10]. Microsoft has been committed to making a joint industry–government effort to <sup>fi</sup>ght against security threats. Despite these efforts made by both the government and the industry sectors, cyber security still remains poor in quality. According to 2008 CSI Computer Crime and Security Survey, computer viruses were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the most frequent security incidents which occurred at almost half (49%) of the respondents' organizations, followed by insider abuse (44%) and theft of laptops or other mobile devices (42%).

While the technical problems have been argued to be the main contributors to poor security, some researchers have a different view. They believe that the problem lies in the economic and behavioral aspect of security. Existing studies with economic perspectives mainly focus on <sup>fi</sup>rm-side security issues such as the effect of security breach announcements on market value [6,33]. Other researchers have established theoretical foundations that explain consumer-side causes of poor security.

The literature on consumer-side security identi<sup>fi</sup>es lack of appreciation of secure and safe systems and the low demand for security to be the main contributors to poor network security [25]. Recently, researchers have paid more attention to psychological aspects of security that may explain consumers' insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient incentive for security. D'Arcy et al. examined the impact of user awareness of security countermeasures on the misuse of information systems and found that the network users' poor security awareness and their misuse of technology result in poor cyber security [11]. Ng et al. identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence a user to practice computer security grounded on a model adapted from the healthcare literature [26].

In this paper, we study the psychological aspect of security from a different angle. We investigate how consumers make inferences about unobservable security quality from observable convenience of information goods and services and how consumers' perceived quality affects their choices. We then examine the impact of educating consumers about security on their inference making process. Finally, we discuss the managerial and policy implications of consumers' perception about security/convenience tradeoff.

Security and convenience are two distinct quality dimensions based on which consumers evaluate information goods, as stated in Bill Gates' famous memo<sup>1</sup> [15]. It is widely believed that there is a tradeoff between security and convenience, that is, enhanced security in return for increased inconvenience. For example, since 9/11, as a process required to increase airport security, every traveler in the United States has experienced a painful and inconvenient security check from simple metal detection to privacy-invasive full body scanners. While this security/convenience tradeoff generally holds for most cases of physical security, advanced technology sometimes achieve enhanced security without losing much convenience for some information goods. One example is Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure (HTTPS) which is a combination of the Hypertext Transfer Protocol with the Secure Sockets Layer/Transport Layer Security (SSL/TLS). The operators who want to create a secure channel over an insecure network such as online banking sites provide an HTTPS connection which signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improves security without even being noticed by most users. A secure virtual private network (VPN) can be another example. A secure VPN provides better security by using encryption based on cryptographic tunneling protocols. All users need to do is click once to turn on the VPN. In most cases, these secure options are offered to users at no additional cost, but providers often struggle with low level of usage unless these secure options are made to be the only available choice.

Grounded on well-established theory in psychology and marketing literature about inference making process, we test our premise that consumers have a tendency to make incorrect inferences about security quality from convenience features. We argue that consumers are in favor of enhancing security in return for losing convenience even though advanced technology could improve both security and convenience without requiring any additional cost for certain information goods and services as in the aforementioned examples. Our study is motivated by consumers' irrational product choice, which is often observed in the information goods market. That is, even when one product is superior to another in both convenience and security dimensions and both are provided at the same price, some consumers choose a dominated product over a dominant option. This phenomenon cannot be explained by existing studies. In this paper, we aim to provide a theory that explains why consumers may prefer a dominated product to a dominant one grounded on the literature of psychology and marketing.

In four studies, with a focus on wireless Internet service, we investigate how consumers make inferences about two different quality dimensions of information goods and services: security and convenience. In Study 1, with a between-subjects design, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that consumers underestimate security quality of a dominant product (i.e., an option with better security and better convenience), but they calibrate their perception when additional information is presented. Surprisingly, there is no difference in convenience rating between a dominant product and a dominated one. In Study 2, we present descriptions of both products to consumers concurrently (i.e., within-subjects design) and ask them to rate security and convenience. The study shows that the dominant product's security rating is lower and its convenience rating is higher than that of the dominated option. We observe the same pattern even when the product description clearly states that security of the dominant option is enhanced. The results support our argument that consumers incorrectly infer unobservable security quality from observable convenience feature by using compensatory inference. In Study 3, we extend our argument to the choice setting. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that consumers' perception of both security and convenience drives their choice. Consistent with the previous two studies, their misperceived security quality leads to the consumers' choice of the dominated product while correctly perceived convenience drives their choice of the dominant one. Interestingly, we found no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact of consumer knowledge about technology on their choice. In Study 4, we investigate whether educating consumers is effective in terms of correcting consumers' misperception of security quality. It turns out that spontaneous and extensive education about security makes an impact on consumers' inference making process. Although the impact is marginal, providing detailed description of security aspect of the product at the time of choice may be an effective way to have them make a rational choice of the product.

The present paper makes contributions to the literature in the following ways. First, our study extends compensatory inference to IT product category. The characteristics of IT products are different from traditional product categories, so the value parity could be violated by advanced technology, which explains why a dominant product and a dominated option co-exist in the IT product market. Second, we <sup>fi</sup>nd the evidence that consumers make inferences about security quality from convenience because convenience is observable but security is not. Even when consumers compare a dominant product with a dominated option, their preferences are affected by security/ convenience tradeoff. Consumers' misperception of security quality would dampen motivations of information goods providers to enhance the security of their products. Third, our results show that both security and convenience drive consumer choice, which contradicts the general view of the existing studies in computer science and software engineering that only con<sup>fi</sup>rms the effect of convenience. The validated impact of security perception, combined with the incorrect perception of security/ convenience tradeoff provides explanation for consumers' irrational choice behavior.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have practical implications for information goods providers and policy makers. First, our results imply that there may exist an optimal level of convenience, which may signal sound security to consumers. Thus, the optimal strategy may be to offer a certain level of procedural complexity instead of perfect convenience which may mislead consumers. Second, not only enhancing security quality but also educating consumers about security so that they correctly perceive and appreciate improved security is a key success factor for business. Finally, from a policy perspective, setting minimum security quality standards would be desirable for certain information goods or subsidizing providers who educate consumers about security may be a welfareenhancing strategy in the sense that information providers can be better motivated for security development.

This paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we review the literature, followed by a theoretical background based on consumers' inference making. Then we present a series of four studies and our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, followed by general discussion. Finally, we provide managerial implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 2. Literature review

While a large volume of literature has exclusively studied information security from a technological perspective, a growing body of literature is looking at information security problems through an economic lens. Anderson argues that failure in providing individual users with the incentive for security leads to poor security [1]. For example, Internet users are concerned only about protecting their machines, not the entire network that can be harmed by exploitation of individual personal computers with insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient protection. August and Tunca examine individual users' incentive under costly patching and investigate different security patch management policies in the presence of negative network externalities [3]. Their results indicate that subsidy-based patching is better than mandatory patching. Kannan and Telang investigate whether markets in vulnerabilities can work better than intermediaries like CERT [21]. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that CERT brings higher social surplus than the unregulated market. Arora et al. model the optimal timing of vulnerability disclosure and show that the vendor's disclosure timing affects both patching speed and quality [2]. Cavusoglu et al. examine the impact of vulnerability disclosure on <sup>fi</sup>rms' performance and <sup>fi</sup>nd that announcements of security breaches hurt the market value of the breached <sup>fi</sup>rms [6]. Cavusoglu et al. investigate how vulnerabilities should be disclosed in order to minimize social loss [7]. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that the disclosure of vulnerability without ensuring patch release may not be socially bene<sup>fi</sup>cial. Telang and Wattal show that vulnerability disclosure negatively affects the market value of software vendors and that vendors' failure to provide timely patches makes situations even worse [33]. Zhao et al. examine the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of certi<sup>fi</sup>cation mechanisms to align the incentive for service providers in terms of improving Internet security grounded on a game-theoretic model [35].

Our paper is closely related to research examining security/ convenience tradeoff of information goods, which has been exclusively studied by computer scientists and security engineers. From the security engineering perspective, existing studies suggest potential con-<sup>fl</sup>ict between convenience and security, implying that improving security is generally accompanied by increased complexity [14,20,27]. Zvrian and Haga study a password mechanism, which is one of the most common security control mechanisms from a convenience perspective and empirically validate the security/convenience tradeoff [36]. Hwang and Verbauwhede examine the design issues around a portable biometric authenticator [18]. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that implementing all on-device biometrics leads to the most secure solution, accompanying sacri<sup>fi</sup>ced performance. Weir et al. examine user perceptions of security and convenience in the context of online banking [34]. They compare three different authentication devices, push-button token, card-activated token, and chip and PIN-secured token and measure user perceptions of convenience and security. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings con<sup>fi</sup>rm that users perceive sacri<sup>fi</sup>ced convenience in return for increased security and that user preference is solely driven by convenience while security does not affect user preference. Given the security/convenience tradeoff and user acceptance, several studies suggest that new security technologies should be designed based on the convenience principle [30,32].

Recently, we observed an interesting phenomenon in information goods and services market, which cannot be clearly explained by existing studies. Thanks to rapidly advanced technologies, convenience is not necessarily sacri<sup>fi</sup>ced to enhance security in certain cases. For example, a wireless network with IEEE 802.1x standard offers much better security by encrypting network traf<sup>fi</sup>cs than an open wireless network. Wi-Fi Protected Access combined with installation of a digital certi<sup>fi</sup>cate allows users to skip log-in process which is a conventional authentication mechanism with the open wireless network. In reality, some organizations provide both options to consumers. As in this example, a dominant option, which is superior both in convenience and security dimensions, co-exists with a dominated option, even at the same price.<sup>2</sup> Interestingly, it is often observed that consumers make an irrational choice of a dominated option over a dominant option. One reason that may explain this striking phenomenon is that consumers may not be capable of evaluating security quality before experiencing it.

Building on prior research in security/convenience tradeoff, this paper aims at understanding consumers' irrational decision making grounded on the theory in inference making process. We examine the case in which one product is dominant over the other in both convenience and security dimensions. In the context of wireless Internet service, we investigate how consumers infer security quality from the information they have and identify the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers' product choice.

## 3. Inference making processes

Consumers make a judgment or a decision based upon information they have. When information about a certain attribute of a product is not available at the time of the decision, they need to make inferences about the missing attribute from other attributes whose information is available. Researchers have exclusively studied the nature of consumers' inference making processes when appropriate information is missing. Prior research suggests that consumers tend to use several strategic approaches to inference making. One approach is to assign an average value based on distributional properties of missing information [12,19,24]. In this case, inferences about missing information do not necessarily have a causal relationship with available information. For example, when consumers make an inference about quality of a new restaurant downtown, they may assign the inferred value on the basis of the average quality of other restaurants downtown. Second approach is evaluative consistency inference, which is to estimate missing information about an attribute from the overall evaluation of the brand [5,8,9,12,13]. For example, imagine that a consumer wants to buy a TV, from either brand A or brand B and that he does not have any information about brightness. However, brand A has better reputation than brand B in the market. If he uses the evaluative consistency inference, he would make inference that brand A is better than brand B in brightness due to better reputation of brand A. The third approach is probabilistic consistency approach, which uses correlation or a causal relationship between a missing attribute and an observable attribute [5,9,12]. If, for instance, quality of a product is unknown, perceived price–quality correlation might provide a cue for quality inference. Consumers might infer that a product with higher price would have higher quality. A number of studies report a variety of attribute covariation: price–quality, brand name–quality, and reliability–warranty [12,29,31]. There are two ways to build a relationship among attributes: theory-based inference and data-based inference [5]. Theory-based inference means that consumers build a relationship from their intuitive beliefs, whereas data-based inference implies that consumers discover a relationship from information about attributes. Broniarczyk and Alba <sup>fi</sup>nd that consumers largely make theory-based inferences rather than data-based inferences [5].

Existing studies focus on attribute-speci<sup>fi</sup>c covariation rather than context-dependent covariation. Attribute-speci<sup>fi</sup>c covariation is formed from the relationship between two attributes, meaning that consumers guess a brand's performance from an attribute from performance in other related attributes. On the other hand, context-dependent covariation implies that consumers <sup>fi</sup>gure out an intuitive relationship from a set of alternatives or decision situation. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Chernev and Carpenter suggest compensatory inference, which re<sup>fl</sup>ects consumers' belief driven by market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency [9]. In general, ‘compensation’ means that an alternative's strength in one attribute can make up for weakness in another attribute. For example, imagine that an LCD TV from brand A is superior in picture clarity, whereas its motion smoothness is poor in quality. If consumers use compensatory strategy, brand A may still have a chance to be chosen because the superiority in picture clarity makes up for inferiority in motion smoothness. If, however, they use non-compensatory strategy, they would reject brand A because the superiority in picture clarity could not make up the weakness in motion smoothness. In the context of inference making, compensatory inference means that consumers make inferences for missing information based on market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, which refers to a general belief that an alternative in the market would have an equal value to its competitors' products. Because the sum of the strengths and weaknesses of an alternative is constant (i.e., market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency), consumers are likely to estimate missing information in favor of equalizing the overall value. Thus, if a market is highly ef<sup>fi</sup>cient, consumers are likely to rely on compensatory inference based on the value parity. This simply means that consumers obtain the value equal to the money paid. The highly ef<sup>fi</sup>cient market can be characterized as a mature and competitive market such as the markets for personal computer and home appliance. Chernev also proposes zero-sum heuristic [8]. The concept of a zero-sum game indicates that a player's gain or loss is exactly counterbalanced by the loss or gain of the other player in the game. Consistent with this idea, a zero-sum heuristic is a function of a dispersion of values across products in the market, irrespective of prior covariation beliefs [8].

When, for example, designing a new product, there might be two distinctive approaches companies could take; one is to specialize in single attribute, and the other is to combine all attributes, called all-in-one. Consider three toothpastes, described by two attributes. Option A is promoting cavity prevention, option B is emphasizing whitening, and option C is claiming effectiveness in both cavity prevention and whitening. When price is constant, consumers would make inferences about perceived performance in cavity prevention and whitening based on the idea that the overall values of these alternatives are equal. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, consumers are likely to believe that perceived performance of option A in cavity prevention is superior to that of option B or C, whereas the perceived performance of option B in whitening is superior to that of option A or C. In addition, consumers infer that option C is better than A in whitening, whereas option C is better than B in cavity prevention. This inference-processing is referred to as a compensatory inference strategy or zero-sum heuristic, and it is based upon consumers' intuition about market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency [8,9]. The belief about market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency would <sup>fi</sup>t in a matured and competitive product category such as automobiles. Chernev uses <sup>fi</sup>ve product categories (laundry detergent, toothpaste, shaving cream, cold relief medicine, and vitamin supplements) to examine zero-sum heuristic, and <sup>fi</sup>nd the results to be consistent across those categories [8].

The present paper proposes that when evaluating an alternative from a choice set, consumers are likely to use zero-sum heuristic for inferring unobservable attributes even though one alternative is dominant over the other. Existing studies examine consumers' decision making in the situation where there is a tradeoff. If it is clear that one alternative is superior to others, consumers' choice process would be quick and easy because they would prefer and choose a dominant option, rather than a dominated option. When consumers know which alternative is superior to others or it is easy to compare one with others, the impact of marketing activities such as an advertisement is limited [16,17]. In most traditional product categories, consumers usually face situations in which they are required to give up one attribute (e.g., performance) in order to get the other (e.g., price). However, information goods such as software and IT service are different from the traditional products in the sense that the swift nature of information technology development allows for both a dominant product and a dominated one to co-exist in the market sometimes even at the same price.

This characteristic of information goods may in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers' inference making process. Although one product may be superior to others in two distinctive dimensions (i.e., security and convenience), consumers may have a wrong perception that a dominant option may perform worse that a dominated option in one dimension based on compensatory inference or a zero-sum heuristic. Especially, convenience would be relatively easier to evaluate than security quality. When consumers look at the description about product features, they could infer how easy it would be to use based on the design and the interface. However, it would be quite dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to estimate the security quality because consumers usually infer security quality level from their experience of the product over time. Therefore, they would evaluate security from the perceived convenience in the manner of compensatory inference or a zero-sum heuristic.

We presume that consumers make inference in security quality from convenience based on compensatory inference making. They use security/convenience tradeoff relationship inappropriately when comparing a dominant product with a dominated product. Different from the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of existing studies, we argue that consumer choices would be affected by not only convenience but also security, and it would explain why consumers prefer a dominated option even though a dominant option is available without paying additional cost.

## 4. Study 1

The objective of Study 1 is to demonstrate how consumers evaluate security and convenience of wireless Internet services. We predict that consumers perceive that enhancing security is accompanied by sacri<sup>fi</sup>cing convenience. That is, a more convenient option may be considered to be less secure than a less convenient option given the same price. Study 1 was implemented in order to test our aforementioned prediction.

Stimulus selection was done as follows. Participants in our study were undergraduate students of a large state university located in the Mid-Atlantic state of the United States, who should be familiar with the product category (i.e., wireless Internet service). Currently, this university provides two different wireless Internet services to the students labeled WLAN and WLSS. Both wireless Internet service options are free for the students, so there is no monetary cost involved. WLAN is an open wireless network, which does not protect traf<sup>fi</sup>c by any encryption technology. WLAN users are asked to go through a login process by providing username and password every time they wish to access the wireless Internet. On the other hand, WLSS allows users to connect automatically without a login process. One time installation of a digital certi<sup>fi</sup>cate authenticates users with the wireless network for a year. WLSS users are no longer required to enter username and password when they access the wireless Internet. Every time the user needs an access to the wireless Internet, the validity of the digital certi<sup>fi</sup>cate on the user's machine is checked without requiring any inconvenience for the user. In addition to the increased convenience, WLSS uses the IEEE 802.1x standard, which provides enhanced security by encrypting network traf<sup>fi</sup>c between user's computer and the wireless access point. Obviously, WLSS is a dominant option, which is superior to WLAN in both convenience and security. What is surprising in this case is the fact that the university has been offering both options for approximately the last two years. Even more striking is the fact that the majority of the students are using the dominated option, WLAN, instead of the dominant option, WLSS, while both options are offered at the same price (free).

In Study 1, we conduct a between-subjects experiment to see whether consumers have misperception of security quality. We used a three-factor (WLAN or WLSS or WLSS with security statement) between-subjects design.

## 4.1. Participants and procedure

The study was administered to a group of students in introductory college of business courses for the undergraduate students. Students were invited to the study which was administered via computer and participation was voluntary. Ninety-<sup>fi</sup>ve students participated in the study and they received extra credit in return for participation. Each participant was randomly assigned to one of the three conditions (WLAN or WLSS or WLSS with security statement). The description of wireless Internet service was presented to the participants (see appendix). Depending upon the assigned condition, participants read only one of the three descriptions. For example, if a participant was assigned to WLAN condition, he was given description, “WLAN: You can access the Internet after login. The login process is necessary every time you want to use the wireless Internet.” On the other hand, WLSS was described as “WLSS: After installing a digital certi<sup>fi</sup>cate on your computer once, you can access the Internet freely without the login process.” Because it might not be easy to estimate security quality, we added another condition, WLSS with security statement, in order to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the inference process. The description of WLSS with security statement was identical to WLSS, except one additional security statement, “Security of WLSS was enhanced.” We expected that comparing WLSS to WLSS with security statement would help us con<sup>fi</sup>rm the inference process more clearly.

Then, participants were asked to rate how secure WLAN (or WLSS) is and how convenient it is, based on 1 to 7 scale (1=Not secure at all; 7=Very secure; 1=Not convenient at all; 7=Very convenient). We also measured the importance of security and convenience (1=Not important at all; 7=Very important). In addition, consumers' knowledge may affect their inference processing. Consumers who have expertise on the information technology might know what the relationships among attributes are rather than novices, so it is worthy to measure participants' knowledge and analyze it as covariate. Thus, we measure their knowledge by asking for the subjects' familiarity, expertise, and interests about general computing and Internet services; for instance, we asked how familiar the subject is with general computing and Internet services, which were ranged from 1 to 7 (1=Not familiar at all; 7 =Very familiar).

## 4.2. Results and discussion

We predicted that consumers would make inference about the relationship between convenience and security, based on the compensatory inference. In detail, they would consider that an option with better convenience attribute (i.e., WLSS) would be less secure than the less convenient option (i.e., WLAN). In addition, it may be possible that the effect could be driven by lack of information about the product characteristics. Although the objective performance of WLSS (dominant option) in security and convenience was obviously better than that of WLAN (dominated option), we expect that consumers would rate security quality of the dominant option lower than that of the dominated option because the dominant option is explicitly more convenient than the dominated option. To con<sup>fi</sup>rm our argument, we added one more condition, WLSS with security statement, which described that security of WLSS (dominant option) was enhanced. If we would observe the expected pattern in WLSS with security statement, it would support our arguments more strongly.

The data showed that participants perceive WLAN (dominated option) superior to WLSS (dominant option) in security. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that there was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in the perceived performance of security (F (2, 92)=5.250; p=.007). To identify differences among WLAN, WLSS, and WLSS with security statement, we conducted Tukey HSD test, and found that the perceived security of WLAN (M=5.226) is better than WLSS (M=4.394; p=.036). However, the difference between WLAN (M =5.226) and WLSS with security statement (M =5.387) turned out not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (pN.10), while WLSS (M=4.394) was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower than WLSS with security statement in security (M=5.387; p=.01). On the other hand, the results of ANOVA also demonstrated that there was no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference among three conditions when the dependent variable was convenience (F $( 2 , 9 2 ) = . 1 3 5 ; p > . 1 0 )$ ). The mean rating of WLAN (=5.742) was not different from the mean ratings of WLSS (=5.788) and WLSS with security statement (=5.903). In addition, there were no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences among three groups in the importance of security (M WLAN=6.097, M Wireless=5.939, M Wireless with security=6.129; F (2, 92)=.250; pN.10) and convenience (M WLAN=5.839, M Wireless=6.212, M Wireless with security=5.710; F (2, 92)=1.293; pN.10).

Fig. 1 shows the mean rating of two quality dimensions of wireless Internet service from the participants, each of whom was under one of the three conditions.

In order to check the impact of consumer knowledge on the inference making process, we conducted ANCOVA with a covariate; we measured familiarity, expertise, and interests about general computing and Internet service. We ran the factor analysis to check whether our items measured one construct, and found that three items compose one factor. Additionally, we tested Cronbach's alpha, and the result indicated that three items were reliable (Cronbach's alpha =.846). Thus, we calculated the average of three items and used it as an index of consumer knowledge. The result of ANCOVA demonstrated that the compensatory inference was quite strong. There was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in security rating among three groups after controlling for the effect of consumer knowledge $\left( F \left( 2 , 9 1 \right) = 5 . 0 5 8 ; p = . 0 0 8 \right)$ Compared to the prior result, the p-value slightly increased from .007 to .008, and it was still strongly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant.

The results demonstrate that consumers estimate the dominant option (i.e., WLSS) less secure than the dominated option (i.e., WLAN). Consumers judged a convenient option as less secure even though the more convenient option was more secure than the other. Moreover, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that there was no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference of importance among three groups, implying that participants' perception about the importance of attributes are not affected by the security statement and that they are generally homogeneous about the two attribute dimensions (security and convenience). However, the results in this study were limited because the data did not support our premise about the convenience perception. Surprisingly, there was no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between WLAN and WLSS when the dependent variable was perceived convenience. The experimental design in Study 1 was a between-subjects design, so each participant was given only one of three options. Because there was no reference for inferring the convenience evaluation, participants judged it based on their intuition or prior experience. This may result in a limited compensatory inference processing. The intuition behind this result can be explained as follows. Since the log-in process is one of the most widely adopted security check both in physical and cyber spaces, participants might grow accustomed to it so that they might not perceive it to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly inconvenient. Especially when there is no information for comparison provided in the between-subjects design, their perceived conveniences showed not to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different between WLAN (dominated option) and WLSS (dominant option).

One of interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings was that participants calibrated their inference or evaluations when we provided additional information about security. WLSS with security statement condition was identical to WLSS condition, except that we explicitly stated that the security quality of the product was enhanced. When comparing perceived securities of two conditions, we found that the perceived security is higher in WLSS with security statement. However, the perceived security of WLSS with security statement was not statistically different from that of WLAN. It means that consumers adjusted their inference when relevant information was provided and they evaluated the products independently. However, the results did not fully support our argument, which is that there was no difference in convenience dimension. The possible explanation for this was that participants were exposed to only one of three product descriptions, and made evaluations independently. Because there was no reference for estimating security and they had to make evaluation based on given information, the compensatory inference processing might be constrained. In the next study, we implemented an additional experiment to test our premises.

## 5. Study 2

The objective of Study 2 was to replicate what we found in Study 1 in a different setting. In Study 1, we showed that consumers make inference that a dominant option, which is superior in both convenience and security attributes, is inferior to dominated option in security. However, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd suf<sup>fi</sup>cient evidence to support our argument that consumers use the compensatory inference. That is, the difference in convenience rating turned out to be statistically insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. Also, consumers underestimated security feature of the dominant option (i.e., WLSS) when additional information about security was not given. The plausible explanation for this partial support in Study 1 is that we presented only one of three product descriptions to participants, making them infer security and convenience features from, only one product without comparing with other options. In this study, we presented a pair of product descriptions (WLAN versus WLSS) to participants (see appendix). With descriptions of both the dominant and the dominated products, consumers would make inferences based on the comparison between a pair of options, presumably based on the belief that the overall values of two products would be same. If consumers would use compensatory inference making, they would underestimate the dominant option and overestimate the dominated option in security dimension equating the overall value. Similar to Study 1, we also added another condition (WLAN versus WLSS with security statement) to test our premise on the inference making process.

Sixty-one undergraduate students at the same university as in Study 1 participated in Study 2, all receiving extra credit for their participation of this study. Students who took part in Study 1 were excluded from this study. The procedure and the questions were identical to Study 1 except that participants were exposed to a pair of product descriptions. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions (WLAN and WLSS without security statement) or (WLAN and WLSS with security statement). Thus, the existence of security statement was the between subjects factor, and the product type (WLAN and WLSS) was the within-subjects factor. Therefore, we used a two (security statement: ‘without’ and ‘with’ condition) by two (products: WLAN and WLSS) mixed design.

## 5.1. Results and discussion

Participants were exposed to both WLAN (dominated option) and WLSS (dominant option). From that exposure, they estimated security and convenience. We predicted that participants would evaluate product attributes using compensatory inference or a zero-sum heuristic when we provided information about both products at the same time because it would stimulate them to make a comparison implicitly.

The results indicated that consumers were likely to use compensatory inference when they faced two products and were asked to evaluate quality. We found there was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between the dominated option and the dominant option when the dependent variable was security $( F \left( 1 , 5 9 \right) = 2 1 . 0 3 5 ; p < . 0 0 1 )$ ). In particular, the mean rating of WLAN (M=5.641) was greater than the mean rating of WLSS (M=4.833) in security. However, the main effect of security statement $( F ( 1 , 5 9 ) = . 0 8 7 ; p > . 1 0 )$ and the interaction effect $( F ( 1 , 5 9 ) =$ $2 . 1 6 6 ; p \mathrm { > } . 1 0 )$ were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, no matter whether the security statement was added, participants believed that WLAN was more secure than WLSS as illustrated in Fig. 2. When the description of WLSS excluded the security statement, the mean rating of WLAN $( M = 5 . 7 3 3 )$ was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater than the mean rating of WLSS $( M = 4 . 6 6 7 ; F \left( 1 , 2 9 \right) = 1 9 . 0 8 5 ; p < . 0 0 1 )$ . When, similarly, the description of WLSS included the security statement, the mean rating of WLAN $( M { = } 5 . 5 4 8 )$ was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater than the mean rating of WLSS $( M = 5 . 0 ; F \left( 1 , 3 0 \right) = 4 . 6 8 6 ; p = . 0 3 8 )$ . In addition, we analyzed the data again with a covariate (i.e., consumer knowledge), and found that there was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in security perception after controlling the effect of knowledge about the general computing and Internet services even though the p-value slightly increased $\left( F \left( 1 , 5 8 \right) = 5 . 3 4 1 ; p = . 0 2 4 \right)$ Overall, these results support our premises as illustrated in Fig. 2.

The data showed that participants judged that WLSS was more convenient than WLAN, no matter whether the security statement was included or not. Study 2 demonstrated how consumers would make inferences about security and convenience. By presenting two options to participants, they compared the dominated option (WLAN) to the dominant option (WLSS) and rated security and convenience of the two options. Although Study 1 did not show the compensatory inference, these results strongly supported our argument. In Study 1, participants rated the dominated option (WLAN) as highly convenient. Thus, the mean value of WLAN did not differ from that of the dominant option (WLSS) in convenient perception. As we previously discussed, a possible explanation for this might be that the login procedure is the most common method in the cyberspace while separate and independent evaluation restricts the compensatory inference processing. In Study 2, we presented two product descriptions to participants, and asked them to evaluate two products. Unlike the result of Study 1, we found that the dominant option was perceived more convenient than the dominated option in both conditions as demonstrated in Fig. 3. It indicated that the compensatory inference making might be prominent when comparing one to the other. Through the comparison, consumers would underestimate or overestimate the perceived performance of products, regardless of the actual performance.

In Study 2, we observed strong compensatory inference when consumers faced two options and compared one with the other. We found that consumers would evaluate the dominant option (WLSS) as less secure than the dominated option (WLAN) in both conditions with or without security statement. Although we explicitly stated that security of WLSS was enhanced, they still estimated that the dominant option was less secure than the dominated option. As we mentioned above, participants in Study 1 were able to judge and infer the product performance only from one product description. The independent evaluation in Study 1 restrained the compensatory inference making, and it resulted in calibrating and adjusting their perception when we presented additional information about the security perception in Study 1. When participants were provided with descriptions of two products in Study 2, they were able to make inference about security from convenience based on comparison between two options equating the overall value. As a result, they underestimated security of the dominant option and overestimated security of the dominated option. In Studies 1 and 2, we con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that consumers would evaluate products based on compensatory inference.

## 6. Study 3

Without suf<sup>fi</sup>cient technical knowledge of or experience with the product, it would be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to evaluate security quality level while convenience is observable and easily comparable. In the previous studies, we found that consumers infer unobservable security quality from observed convenience. Dependent variables in the previous experiments were consumer evaluations of quality dimensions: security and convenience. It is well known that consumer preference is constructive and easily changes depending upon how preference is elicited [4,28]. This means that evaluation might be different from choice, so we extend our argument to the choice setting in the present study.

Participants were asked to imagine that there were two wireless Internet service providers available in the market, and that both services were provided at the same price. Two product descriptions were presented to them (see appendix). Products A (dominated option) and B (dominant option) were described as follows: “Product A — you can access the Internet after login. The login process is necessary every time you want wireless access to the Internet,” and “Product B — after one-time installation of a digital certi<sup>fi</sup>cate, through which security improves, you can access the Internet without login process.” Then, we asked them to rate their perceived security and convenience and subsequently make a choice between them. Also, we measured the importance of each product attribute and their general knowledge about IT products. Finally, participants were asked to state the reasons why they chose one of the two options. One hundred and ten students participated in this study, and their participation was compensated with extra credit. Any students who participated in either of the two previous studies were excluded.

## 6.1. Results and discussion

To examine whether security and convenience perceptions affected the choice of wireless service, we conducted a logistic regression analysis. Before running a logistic regression, it was necessary to recode and create new variables. It turned out that security and convenience perceptions were closely related. When consumers form an attitude or preference toward a product, they would compare one product to the other. It means that evaluation of product A's security is negatively correlated with evaluation of product B's security, and the same logic holds for convenience dimension. When the independent variable is correlated to another, it might confound the result of a regression analysis. To avoid multicollinearity, we checked the correlation between two products in both perceived security and convenience. The correlation between product A and B in security dimension was −.196 (pb.05), and the correlation in convenience was −.359 (pb.01). Thus, we made an index for security by subtracting perceived security of product A from that of product B, and did the same for convenience. The mean value of security index was 1.028, which was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher than zero (t (109)=5.309, pb.0001), and the mean of convenience index was −3.312, which also was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower than zero (t (109)=−18.850, pb.0001). These results were consistent with those found in Study 2. Results showed such that 41.3% of participants (N=45) chose product A (dominated option) and 58.7% of them (N=64) chose product B (dominant option).

To identify what factors would drive consumers' choice, we ran a logistic regression with four independent variables (Security, Convenience, Knowledge, and Gender). The equation we estimated is

The results were also consistent with previous experiments in the sense that compensatory inference making was re-con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in Study 3. We found that security, convenience, importance of security and importance of convenience all had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impacts on the product choice while the impacts of knowledge and gender were not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Consumers who seriously consider the level of security preferred the dominated option (product A) to the dominant option (product B; $\mathsf { W a l d } = 8 . 7 7 5 , \mathsf { p } { < } . 0 1 )$ ), whereas consumers who seriously consider the level of convenience preferred the dominant option (product B) to the dominated option (product A; $\mathsf { W a l d } = 9 . 3 1 4 , \mathsf { p } \mathsf { < } . 0 1 .$ ). The positive coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of security indicated that consumers who perceived product A (dominated option) as the more secure option chose product A over product B (dominant option) $( \mathsf { W a l d } = 6 . 9 0 9 , p \mathrm { < } . 0 1 )$ . Similarly, consumers who perceived product B (dominant option) as the more convenient option chose product B over product A (dominated option) (Wald=12.764, pb.001). An interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding was that knowledge had no impact on the choice. In Study 2, we observed that when adding knowledge as a covariate, the effect was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, but the p-value increased. It implies that although the compensatory inference was robust, knowledgeable consumers might adjust their misperception. In a choice situation, however, knowledge did not have any impact (Wald=0.202, pN.10), meaning that although evaluation of perceived performance for one attribute might be calibrated by their perceived knowledge, the impact of perceived knowledge on the choice would be dismissed (Table 1).

To con<sup>fi</sup>rm what drives their choices, we asked participants to report any reason they chose one of the two products. For a data analysis, we recruited two independent judges and asked them to categorize their reason into four sub-categories; choice-driven by security, choice-driven by convenience, recognizing a dominant option, and other. Among one hundred ten participants, ninety four participants answered this question. The ratio for which two independent judges put a reason into the same sub-category was 90.4% (N=85). There were cases (9.6%) in which two independent judges disagreed (N=9), so those were determined by a third judge. As we expected, 80% of participants who chose product A were driven by a misperceived superiority in security, whereas 68.5% of those who chose product B were driven by a perceived superiority in convenience. One of interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this analysis was that only 11.7% of participants (N=11) knew what the dominant option was, which certainly led to the choice of the dominant option, product B. This indicated that the majority (88.2%) of participants made choices based on their misperception that the dominant option is less secure than the dominated option. We argue that this misperception came from the compensatory inference as we demonstrated in Studies 1 and 2. In addition, this result indicated that knowledge, which was measured based on consumers' level of expertise, familiarity, and interest in general computing and IT products, has no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on the consumers' choice. Interestingly, the content analysis showed that consumers made a rational choice and did not reply on the compensatory inference making only if they con<sup>fi</sup>dently and clearly knew what the dominant option was. We observed that the majority (89%) of consumers still made their choices based on their misperception; particularly con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by the fact that 41% of all participants chose the dominated option which was inferior in all dimensions. Thus, our argument that consumers used the compensatory inference to estimate security quality is strongly supported.

In summary, our results con<sup>fi</sup>rmed our arguments. We extended the compensatory inference theory to the choice setting. The indexes of security and convenience were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from zero, indicating that consumers perceived the dominated option as the more secure option and the dominant option as the more convenient option. Interestingly, the difference between product A (dominated option) and product B (dominant option) in security rating was smaller than the difference in convenience rating. This result could be explained by compensatory inference. When participants evaluated convenience, their judgment would be based on objective performance; one requires a login process while the other does not. Therefore, the difference in convenience was relatively clear. However, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to evaluate security quality directly, so security is inferred from convenience. Because they rely on the inference, not the objective information, the difference in perceived security quality is smaller than the difference in perceived convenience. Study 3 showed that our arguments were robust, not only in evaluations, but also in a choice situation. Also, we found that the impact of knowledge was limited in choice situation. 58.7% of participants preferred product B (dominant option) to product A (dominated option). However, this preference was driven by a perceived superiority in convenience, not a perceived superiority in both attributes. Choices of 89% of consumers were driven by compensatory inference, resulting in 41.3% of consumers choosing product A (dominated option), even though it is inferior in both attributes.

## 7. Study 4

The objective of Study 4 was to investigate how to reduce consumers' bias and correct irrational choice from compensatory inference.

In the previous studies, we showed that consumers made wrong inference that a dominant option was inferior to a dominated option in security despite the actually better security of the dominant option. However, we found that consumers made the right choice when they knew what the dominant option was in Study 3. This result implies that it may be possible to attenuate their misperception based on compensatory inference. If extensive education about security would be provided, the consumers might evaluate the security of an option correctly. In this study, we examine the effectiveness of security education (i.e., providing detailed description of security technology) in terms of correcting consumers' misperception of security quality.

Sixty-six undergraduate students at the same university participated in Study 4, all receiving extra credit for their participation of the study. Students who took part in any of the previous studies were excluded from this study. The procedure and the questions were similar to Study 2 with the exception of providing participants with technical description about IEEE 802.1x standard that is used by the dominant option. Participants were instructed to read the technical description before they faced the product information (see appendix for both the technical description and product information). Then they are asked to evaluate security and convenience features of each of the two options. In addition, we asked them to report their experience of using wireless Internet service on campus. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, participants were required to indicate how frequently they bring their notebook computer and use wireless Internet on campus, which were measured in the following scale: (1) none, (2) 1 or 2 days per week, (3) 3 or 4 days per week, and (4) everyday. Then, we examined how they evaluated security and convenience features depending upon their actual experience. As a result, the actual experience of wireless Internet service usage was the between-subjects factor, and the product (dominant vs. dominated option) was the within-subjects factor. Therefore, we used a two (product: dominant vs. dominated option) by four (actual experience of wireless Internet service usage) mixed design.

## 7.1. Results and discussion

We presumed that participants' misperception about security based on compensatory inference would decrease or be eliminated with security education. Consistent to previous results, there was a signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant difference between a dominant option and a dominated option in their evaluation toward convenience feature (F (1, 62)=124.564; $p { < } . 0 0 1 $ ). They evaluated the dominant option (M=6.435) better than the dominated option (M=2.968) in convenience, but there was no difference among four different usage experiences $( F \ ( 3 , 6 2 ) = 1 . 6 3 9 ;$ pN.10). In addition, the interaction effect was not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (F (3, 62)=.351; pN.10). On the other hand, we found that there was a marginally signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between the dominant option and the dominated option in their evaluation toward security feature $\left( F \left( 1 , 6 2 \right) = 3 . 8 1 2 ; p = . 0 5 5 \right)$ . Participants in the previous three studies evaluated the dominated option better than the dominant option in security because of compensatory inference. In this study, however, the result was reversed even though it was valid at a 10% signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level (Fig. 4). The perceived security of the dominant option $( M = 5 . 5 1 4 )$ was higher than that of the dominated option $( M = 4 . 8 2 6 )$ , but the impact of wireless Internet usage on the security rating turned out statistically not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( F ( 3 , 6 2 ) = . 1 8 7 ; p > . 1 0 )$ . In addition, the interaction effect was not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant either $( F \left( 3 , 6 2 \right) = 1 . 2 0 3$ pN.10).

Study 4 demonstrated that providing proper information to consumers could be one of the solutions to overcome the misperception that comes from compensatory inference. Although the effect was marginally signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at a 10% level, participants evaluated security quality correctly immediately after they read information about security technology. This implies that educating consumers can be effective in terms of correcting consumers' misperception of security. The only concern related to this result is that we asked participants to evaluate products immediately after they were asked to read the technical description. This means that the impact of education might be ampli-<sup>fi</sup>ed. Timing of education could be an important factor, suggesting that an information goods and services provider may want to place an information sheet on the store or include it in the product package. In addition, we found no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact of wireless Internet usage on either convenience or security evaluation. We categorized participants based on their reported usage experience, so the number of participants in each group was not equal (‘none’ group=5; ‘1–2 days per week’ group=20; ‘3–4 days per week’ group=16; ‘everyday’ group=25). However, we believe that it was enough to estimate the impact of actual experience. If non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect was due to the number of participants in each group, the ‘1–2 days per week’ group should have been different from the ‘everyday’ group. We can interpret the result in that usage or experience does not help users better perceive security quality. As a result, consumers might rely on the information provided by a company or compensatory inference based on observable attributes in order to determine security feature.

## 8. General discussion

This paper demonstrates that consumers make incorrect inferences about security quality of information goods and services. Among two quality dimensions of information goods (i.e., security and convenience), it is often dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for consumers to observe security quality while convenience quality is relatively clear. Unlike convenience that consumers can easily evaluate, security quality can be hard to realize until security problems occur. Interestingly, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that consumers misperceive security quality while they correctly evaluate convenience. ## 9. Managerial and policy implications

When consumers misperceive security quality of information goods and services, providers do not have suf<sup>fi</sup>cient motivation to invest in security development. Thus, it is often observed in reality that information goods providers, such as software vendors, play a penetrate-and-patch strategy, that is, they introduce a product as soon as it has a certain level of functionality even though holes in security still remain. Given the nature of the information goods market such as network externality and <sup>fi</sup>rst-mover advantage, it may be optimal to enter the market as soon as possible since consumers can only evaluate visible quality dimension such as convenience without enough appreciation security.

Our study not only provides theoretical background for consumers' misperception of security quality, but also has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant practical value. The results have implications for information goods providers and policy makers. First, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that there may exist an optimal level of convenience, which gives consumers a signal of sound security. Thus, the optimal strategy of information goods providers may be to offer a certain level of procedural complexity instead of perfect convenience which may mislead consumers. Second, not only enhancing security quality but also educating consumers about security so that they correctly perceive and appreciate security is a key success factor. Our results indicate that spontaneous and extensive education may have an impact on consumers' inference making of security quality. From a policy perspective, market regulation such as imposing liability on the provider for any loss due to poor security or setting a minimum security standard would be desirable and welfare-enhancing. Subsidizing providers who educate consumers about security is another possibility. These policy-oriented approaches may provide better motivation for security development.

## 10. Contributions and limitations

Our study contributes to the literature by extending theory in compensatory inference making to the IT product category in which a dominant option and a dominated option can co-exist for a certain period of time due to the fast pace of technology development. In the traditional product categories such as toothpaste and detergent, the value parity might be true and compensatory inference might be a reasonable and effective way to estimate quality attributes. However, since advanced technologies can achieve both better performance and enhanced security without sacri<sup>fi</sup>cing either, the value parity could not be the correct method by which to evaluate product quality. Although Chernev points out that a product could be devaluated by compensatory inference, the case examined in this paper has not been discussed [8]. Our study uses wireless Internet service as stimuli in which one option (WLSS) is clearly dominant over the other (WLAN) in both security and convenience. We <sup>fi</sup>nd the evidence that consumers make inferences about security quality from convenience based on a zero-sum heuristic, which may lead to irrational product choice. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that both security and convenience drive consumer choice while existing studies in computer science and software engineering only con<sup>fi</sup>rm the effect of convenience. Combined with the incorrect perception of security/convenience tradeoff, the validated impact of security quality on consumer choice provides explanation for consumers' irrational choice of information goods and services. Finally, we found evidence that supports the effect of security education in terms of correcting consumers' misperception of security quality.

While signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, this paper has several limitations. First, using undergraduate students as the sample may limit the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. However, given a wide adoption and heavy usage of the information goods and services such as wireless Internet among younger generations, it is important to examine their inference making process and choice behavior. Our results indicate that general knowledge about technology or usage does not have statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on consumer perception of security. It would be interesting to have a sample of security experts and examine the impact of the domain-speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge and experience on their perceptions. Second, the wireless Internet service that we used in this paper is a quite unique example in the sense that both a dominant and a dominated option are provided to users simultaneously at the same zero cost. While our argument about consumers' misperception of security quality based on a zero-sum heuristic and their irrational choice behavior is valid, future research could examine consumers' inference making in a setting with different products. Third, the results were derived based the hypothetical decisions. While hypothetical setting is legitimate and widely accepted by experimental studies in the domain of psychology and marketing, it would be interesting to examine actual choice of consumers. Fourth, some may argue that authentication of login process (WLAN) is better than that of automatic process (WLSS) in which login credential is stored in a digital certi<sup>fi</sup>cate and cached by a browser. However, we believe that the gain from encryption signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly outweighs loss from skipped login process, which led us to conclude that WLSS is in aggregate a dominant option. Finally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd the evidence that security education is effective, but its impact turned out marginal. This result may be due to the technically complicated and non-accessible materials we use to educate consumers. Although we were still able to demonstrate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of education on consumer perception, we acknowledge that the approach to consumer education could be improved grounded on the user education literature [23]. It would be an interesting extension to bring the labeling approach into the domain of security while it has been already applied in the privacy domain by research groups such as CyLab Usable Privacy and Security Laboratory (CUPS) [22]. Future research may consider a labeling approach and study the impact of it on consumers' perception or choice.

Appendix B. Technical description of IEEE 802.1x standard

The information below is the explanation about the IEEE 802.1X standard. Please read the following information carefully.

What is IEEE 802.1x?

“The 802.1X standard is designed to enhance the security of wireless local area networks that follow the IEEE 802.11 standard. 802.1X provides an authentication framework for wireless LANs, allowing a user to be authenticated by a central authority. The actual algorithm that is used to determine whether a user is authentic is left open and multiple algorithms are possible. 802.1X uses an existing protocol, the Extensible Authentication Protocol (EAP, RFC 2284), that works on Ethernet,Token Ring, or wireless LANs, for message exchange during the authentication process. In a wireless LAN with 802.1X, a user (known as the supplicant) requests access to an access point (known as the authenticator). The access point forces the user (actually, the user's client software) into an unauthorized state that allows the client to send only an EAP start message. The access point returns an EAP message requesting the user's identity. The client returns the identity, which is then forwarded by the access point to the authentication server, which uses an algorithm to authenticate the user and then returns an accept or reject message back to the access point. Assuming an accept was received, the access point changes the client's state to authorized and normal traffic can now take place.”

(Quote from Searchmobilecomputing.techtarget.com)

“IEEE 802.1X is an IEEE Standard for port-based Network Access Control(PNAC). It is part of the IEEE 802.1 group of networking protocols. It provides an authentication mechanism to devices wishing to attach to a Local-Area-Network or Wireless Local-Area-Network. IEEE 802.1X defines the encapsulation of the Extensible Authentication Protocol(EAP) over IEEE 802, which is known as “EAP over LAN” or EAPOL, EAPOL was originally designed for IEEE 802.3 Ethernet in 802.1X-2001, but was clarified to suit other IEEE 802 LAN technologies such as IEEE 802.11 wireless and Fiber Distributed Data Interface(ISO 9314–2) in 802.1X-2004.The EAPOL protocol was also modified for use with IEEE 802.1AE(MACSec) and IEEE 802.1AR(Secure Device Identity, DevID) in 802.1X-2010.to support service identification and optional point to point encryption over the local LAN segment.”

(Quote from Wikipedia Website)
