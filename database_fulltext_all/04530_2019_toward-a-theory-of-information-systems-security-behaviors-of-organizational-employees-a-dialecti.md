# Toward a Theory of Information Systems Security Behaviors of Organizational Employees: A Dialectical Process Perspective

Abstract. The various guidelines, procedures, and policies referred to as information systems security procedures (ISSPs) underlie information systems security behaviors (ISSBs) of many employees in organizations. Understanding the reasons for ISSBs—that is, why employees do or do not comply with ISSPs—is an imperative in today’s organizations, given that in formation is a valuable asset. In our study, we observed that employees’ reasons for engaging in ISSBs, such as selecting a password, locking a computer, and using a USB memory device, changed over time. Noting that the dynamic nature of ISSBs has not yet received sufficien consideration in information systems security (ISS) research, we use a predominantly in ductive approach to develop a theoretical understanding of the ISSB change process, sensitized by ideas from dialectics. Our dialectical process view suggests that explanations for engaging in different ISSBs are not static but change over time as individuals seek to deal with, or balance, tensions or contradictory demands. Furthermore, our view suggests that “change triggers” (e.g., new experiences and external events) initiate a process of reevaluating tensions that can, in turn, lead to changes in ISSBs. A number of implications for future research and practice emerge from this dialectical understanding of the ISSB change process.

## 1. Introduction

Consider the following scenario that was inspired by a sequence of events in real life:

Peter works in the sales department of a global energy company where part of his job involves recording sales information and generating reports that are considered critical to the company’s competitive advantage. When Peter started working for the company in 2008, a close colleague suggested that it would be a good idea to lock his computer whenever he stepped away from his desk. Recent news related to security breaches and their negative consequences appeared to support this view, and thus, Peter adopted this practice during that time; this was not too difficult, given that locking his computer did not require much time or effort. However, as time went by, Peter developed a sense of trust in his colleagues and found this practice to be somewhat troubling, given that he didn’t want to unwittingly convey distrust toward his colleagues by locking his computer in their presence. Thus, during his workday, Peter began leaving his computer unlocked on most occasions when he stepped away from his desk. In 2012, however, Peter was promoted to the position of the vice president of sales, a position that demanded higher confidentiality, greater awareness of the dangers of unauthorized use of information systems, and personal accountability for the negative consequences of a possible information systems security (ISS) breach. This prompted him to start locking his computer more frequently. However, Peter still occasionally chose to leave his computer unlocked if he needed to step out of his office to attend to something urgent. One day, Peter found that the company’s security manager had changed the wallpaper of his computer when Peter had left it unlocked. After this incident, he decided to always lock his computer without exception, not only because he was reminded of the importance of locking it, but also due to his conviction that this was clearly expected from him as a professional holding an important position. Currently, even at home, Peter locks his computer as a matter of routine. This practice has become a habit—a pattern of ISS behaviors (ISSBs) without the need for constant evaluation—at least for now.

Information systems security is a critical concern for organizations. ISS refers to the various procedura and technical measures for protecting information and information systems (IS) against unauthorized access to (and modification of) information (Doherty et al. 2009). ISS researchers have directed significant efforts to understanding why some employees fail to comply with organizational ISS procedures (ISSPs; Bulgurcu et al. 2010a, Hsu et al. 2015) pertaining to certain behaviors with security implications, such as leaving one’s computer unlocked (as illustrated in the scenario above). Various industry surveys report that, along with malicious behaviors and technological glitches, negligent or careless employees who do not comply with their companies ISSPs contribute to ISS breaches (Ponemon Institute 2014a, b; SafeNet 2014; CompTIA 2015). For example, CompTIA (2015) reports that such human errors are linked to 42% of ISS breach cases.

Theories frequently applied to examine employee compliance (or noncompliance) with ISSPs include deterrence theory, protection motivation theory, rational choice theory, and theory of reasoned action (D’Arcy and Herath 2011, Karjalainen and Siponen 2011, Crossler et al. 2013, Siponen and Vance 2014). In applying these theories and others, by and large, previous researchers have focused on identifying various (sets of) “independent variables” related to ISSBs that are presumed to be independent of (or the same) over time and security contexts. Yet, as the opening scenario suggests, the dynamics of change, including employees’ shifting reasoning, are clearly relevant to understanding ISSBs, because individuals priorities for enacting these behaviors may continue to change over time as the individuals’ circumstances change. For instance, a user may routinely lock her computer when she becomes suspicious of her environment regarding potential security breaches, and later, this routine can be disrupted, either as an exception or as a repeating pattern, when new task or social priorities emerge.

If the reasons for compliance or noncompliance (ISSBs) change over time and with different situations, then previous research, which focused on static, invariant explanations needs to be complemented with a more dynamic process-based approach (Siponen and Baskerville 2018). Our qualitative study enables us to reach such a novel understanding of the reasons and processes underlying and influencing users’ ISSBs. To the best of our knowledge, this research is among the first to pursue this direction by explicating the mechanisms of change dynamics not captured by the dominant quantitative research designs in past research.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The second section includes a review of the previous literature on ISSBs. The third section describes the research methodology. The fourth section presents the development of our understanding of the process leading to employees’ ISSBs. The fifth section provides a discussion and outlines contributions, implications, limitations, and recommendations for future research The final section offers concluding remarks.

## 2. Problematizing Previous Empirical Work on Employees’ Information Systems Security Behaviors

This section highlights the fundamental differences between the existing ISS research and our approach, thus pointing to the key issues that we seek to address. Previous research (see Online Appendix 1, Table 1.1) pri marily applied theories from other disciplines, such as deterrence theory, protection motivation theory (PMT), and neutralizations, with some adaptation (Siponen and Vance 2014). Typically, these theories are applied in ISS research in a setting where the ISSB (or intention thereof) is modeled as the dependent variable and the explanations are modeled as independent variables (sometimes including moderators). Furthermore, most of the previous ISSB research was carried out using a one-time survey setting, where the study focuses on explaining or predicting ISSBs at a certain point, ignoring longitudinal behavioral changes over time.

The mainstream literature also assumes that the explanations/predictors are static (and fixed a priori) over time and constant across various situations These relationships (e.g., between independent and dependent variables) are typically justified based on a reference theory (e.g., deterrence theory). Different terms are used to refer to models that have the assumptions above in different sciences. In IS, Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991) called such research that assumed a priori fixed relationships “positivistic” (see also Siponen and Tsohou 2018). In health psychology, such models are called continuum or stage-less models (Weinstein et al. 1998). To elaborate on this point, previous ISS studies do not suggest, for example, that sanctions would be explanations in a certain stage but not in another, because the studies did not entertain the idea that the explanations might change over time.

Putting aside terminology issues, such research settings and assumptions discussed above set fundamental boundaries on the applicability of existing ISS research. For example, cases where employees’ reasons for ISSBs keep changing dynamically from one situation to another cannot be easily accommodated by such continuum models. To give an example, deterrence theory shows how the calculation of the severity and certainty (and celerity) of formal or informal sanctions explains employees’ decision to comply with ISSBs, but does not explain how, in certain situations, the decision can be based on other considerations. Our aim is to examine such dynamics, where the reasons for employees’ ISSBs change.

We must acknowledge that some ISS research has examined behavior change. Studies investigating behavior changes have typically used experimental settings (Jie et al. 2009, Albrechtsen and Hovden 2010, Johnston and Warkentin 2010, Johnston et al. 2015) or action research (Puhakainen and Siponen 2010) and have examined the effect of interventions on employees ISSBs, usually with the following research designs: a pretest and an intervention, followed by a posttest (see Online Appendix 1, Table 1.1). Although these studies examine behavior change with a manipulative intervention, they also assume static predictors or explanations of change. Let us clarify this point by using PMT, which explains that the fear of threat is the key reason for users to change their ISSBs (Boss et al. 2015) irrespective of the users’ situations. For illustration purposes, imagine a situation where the fear appeal manipulation is first introduced, and it causes a change from insecure to secure behavior. However, later, the behavior changes back to insecure due to some reason, despite the same level of fear, and new fear appeals do not change the behavior from insecure to secure. Later, a moral persuasion training (“this is our work responsibility”) introduces the change. Such understanding, where moral persuasions is seen to influence ISS-related behavioral change, cannot be accommodated within PMT.

Finally, generally speaking, previous studies assumed that the reasons for compliant ISSBs (e.g., locking the computer) and noncompliant ISSBs (e.g., not locking the computer) are symmetric (Moody et al. 2018). For instance, within PMT (per Boss et al. 2015), one key reason for compliant ISSBs is seen as fear manipulation, and the reason for noncompliant ISSBs is the lack of effective fear manipulation. In turn, deterrence theory explains noncompliant ISSBs by a lack (or inadequate level) of sanction severity and certainty (and celerity). Such symmetric assumptions have no resources to explain cases where the reasons for noncompliant ISSBs are different from those for compliant ISSBs.

Overall, previous ISSB research has focused on finding static explanations and predictors for ISSBs, which are presumed to be independent of (or constant over) time and ISS contexts. Although this body of research undoubtedly has merits, there is a need to consider the changing nature of individuals’ motivations or reasons why individuals choose to comply or not comply with different ISSPs, over time and across changing circumstances. This study represents a first step in addressing this research gap by examining processes of change related to three types of ISSBs.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Data Collection

The case study involved an investigation of a global company headquartered in Finland that employs more than 10,000 individuals and operates in the energy market and the marine industry. Data for this study were primarily collected in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) subsidiary of this company in 2009 and 2014<sup>2</sup> through semistructured interviews. The initial round of data collection was exploratory, given that our goal was to first discern the employees’ point of view concerning the phenomenon. The second round of interviews was geared toward developing a dialectical understanding of change in ISSBs, which is presented (later) in this paper.

To ensure a more representative view of the topic from the stakeholders, interviewees were selected from different organizational positions. Participants were assured of confidentiality (Myers and Newman 2007). Twenty-nine interviews were conducted in the first wave of research, followed by 18 in the second wave. The average interview lasted 56 minutes. Of the 47 interviews, all were recorded and transcribed in text form. The data collection through interviews are line with norms enacted in the IS literature (Sarker et al. 2013). The details regarding the interviewees are presented in Online Appendix 2.

Two researchers conducted the interviews, which allowed for triangulation and offered the advantage of considering multiple perspectives. Using a concurrent process of data collection and data analysis, the first author took the lead in the coding process (see Table 3.1 in Online Appendix 3), while the other researcher(s) engaged in discussions, sorting of ideas, and conceptualization while the data were being collected. The initial interpretations made by the first author served as a starting point for the collaborative analysis process conducted among the three authors, and the final interpretations emerged as a result of a highly collaborative effort within the research team. We believe that the initial individual coding combined with iterative phases of subsequent collaborative sense-making ensured a sufficient level of reliability (i.e., a shared viewpoint) regarding the findings.

## 3.2. Data Analysis

Broadly speaking, this study is interpretive (Lee 1991; Walsham 1995, 2006; Levina and Vaast 2008), and it utilizes a number of aspects from the grounded theory methodology (GTM)—as is common practice in many interpretive studies (Walsham 1995). Although disagreement remains regarding the essence of GTM studies, our work is consistent with the core characteristics highlighted by Birks et al. (2013). Our strategy for theorizing about ISSB-change processes involved interpreting the narratives of the interviews to discern a temporal sequence among activities in line with theories of organizational and individual change processes<sup>3</sup> in the fields of organization studies (Langley and Tsoukas 2010) as well as developmental psychology (Kohlberg 1981). In IS and related fields, a similar approach has been used to theorize about the processes of requirement elicitation (Chakraborty et al. 2010), organizational change (Isabella 1990), radical and accidental organizational changes (Plowman et al. 2007), and organizational adaptation (Dutton and Dukerich 1991).

We used open coding at the sentence or paragraph level, with constant comparative analysis conducted concurrently with data collection (Charmaz 2000, Sarker et al. 2001). In the initial round of interviews, we sought to uncover why and how employees do or do not comply with ISSPs. The identified open codes were placed in high-level categories and organized into a preliminary framework (Glaser 1978, Charmaz 2000). We made a conscious effort not to be guided by any theoretical perspective at this point (Myers and Newman 2007) but sought to suspend our theoretical sensitivity to the phenomenon to immerse ourselves in the participants’ points of view. The iterative analysis of the data collected during the initial interviews (and review comments) alerted us to some interesting and unexpected patterns related to ISSBs (i.e., the underlying tensions that individuals have to confront or resolve as they engage in a given ISSB). Consequently, this led us to use a more directed theoretical sampling to develop a conceptually dense and dynamic perspective on the participants’ underlying reasons for ISSBs and the changes in ISSBs over time.

The tensions between the competing considerations that employees needed to negotiate, as they complied with or failed to comply with different ISSPs led us to view the phenomenon using a dialogical framework that afforded complex and dynamic explanations for changing ISSBs over time as contexts changed. We adopted the notion of dialectics as the broad sensitizing perspective to guide further data collection, analysis, and the development of our process view (Glaser 1978). However, our intention was not to apply the specific (Hegelian) dialectics to the letter, but, consistent with the literature in IS and related disciplines, to appropriate the spirit of the dialectical approach in understanding how employees are influenced by new situational or contextual demands and experiences (i.e., change triggers) and how employees manage tensions encountered via cognitive (i.e., balancing acts) and behavioral resolutions (new ISSBs) particular to the ISSB context (see Section 4). Such use of dialectics, we believe, is consistent with the literature in management and related areas.

Aside from adopting a dialectical perspective on the subject matter, our theoretical sampling was guided by our consideration of the different types of ISSBs that are most relevant. Although organizations face various ISS concerns, a survey of ISS managers (Siponen and Vance 2010) revealed that the top three ISSBs that pose noncompliance challenges are using passwords that are not sufficiently robust, leaving a computer unlocked, and using USB devices without taking sufficient precautions. Furthermore, the following ISSBs are reportedly among the most common causes of ISS breaches: inadvertent actions by trusted insiders (related to all ISSPs in this study); malware (related to USB device behaviors); password compromise (related to password behaviors); and lost, stolen, or hijacked devices (related to computer locking and USB device behaviors; Siponen and Vance 2010, Ponemon Institute 2014a). Thus, these specific ISSBs were used to direct the data collection and analysis in our study.

One objective of our analysis of the second wave of data collection was to interpret employees’ first-hand experiences with ongoing ISSBs and the employees changes over time based on the employees’ narratives rooted in time and place and reflective of persona constructions of meaning (Riessman 1993). Our interpretive approach corresponds to Weick’s (1995) notion of sense-making that depends on the “ability to think in narrative terms: to look back, retrospectively, and ahead, prospectively, so as to construct an understanding of events” (Pentland 1999, p. 717). The interview data collected from each interviewee in the second wave was used to reconstruct the sequential processes of the individual user’s ISSBs and the combined process models for each type of ISSB. The sequential diagrams were used as part of a sense-making process through which we came to understand the data and the relationships between emerging concepts and categories (Charmaz 2006, Birks et al. 2013). These individual models were combined into a detailed integrative process model of employees’ ISSBs (see Figure 3.1 in Online Appendix 3). Following this, a dialectical process view of the employees’ ISSBs at a higher level of abstraction was formulated (as presented in Section 4.2). Finally, our theoretical findings were compared with the existing ISS literature (see Section 5.1) and rule compliance literature (see Section 5.2) to place our per spective in the context of the existing bodies of knowledge (Urquhart et al. 2010).

The iterative data-analysis process continued until an adequate level of theoretical saturation was achieved— that is, once we felt that considering additional data would not result in a new or different understanding, at least for the time being. This process is consistent with interpretive traditions such as hermeneutics, according to which understanding is always provisional and changing, and that a conclusive understanding is an elusive notion. However, the interpretation process may stop when there are no apparent anomalies between the data and the understanding (Sarker and Lee 2006). A key point here is that our interpretive approach sought to be open to a more penetrative understanding, which is not necessarily obtained by aggregating similar views of respondents. We also note that, just as our understanding of a book does not rely necessarily on the number of times a word or concept appears in the book (Lee 1991), our interpretation of data does not rely on sample size, frequency of utterance, and so on. When the part (datum) and the whole (the understanding) are in harmony as a result of a constant comparative analysis process and when adding more data that are available does not seem to change or enrich the understanding, we can assume that a plausible understanding has been achieved (Sarker and Lee 2006).

## 4. Theoretical Sensitivity and Findings

This section highlights our findings regarding employees’ ISSBs. We first discuss dialectics as a sensitizing perspective in Section 4.1. We then introduce and illustrate the dialectical change process of ISSBs in Section 4.2 and Section 4.3.

## 4.1. Theoretical Underpinnings of the Dialectical Change Process of ISSBs

The development of our understanding of the process was influenced by the praxis (Eisenhardt 1989) that was captured in our interview data. As mentioned previously, the first round of interviews revealed implicit tensions encountered by individuals as they enacted ISSBs (which seemed to change over time in some cases). These tensions reinforced the relevance of a process approach and led us to adopt dialectics as a sensitizing device (Patton 1990) to guide further data collection and analysis. The key ideas of our dialectical perspective are discussed below.

Although a large variety of dialectical approaches to study human behavior exists, they share the assumption that the world consists of contradictions and opposing forces that are in constant struggle with one another (Mumby 2005). Thus, dialectical views of organizational or individual change emphasize ongoing conflicts that potentially shape consciousness and action (Benson 1977). Such a perspective is commonly used to understand change that emerges through the resolution of conflicts between opposing forces, which are often referred to as the thesis and the antithesis. A novel response to the contradiction between these two forces is often termed synthesis (van de Ven and Poole 1995), which may lead to a new thesis (or antithesis) over time. Ritzer (1992, p. 160) explains the dialectical approach as follows:

[It] . . . is multi-causal and multi-directional, integrates fact and value, rejects the idea that there are hard-andfast dividing lines between social phenomena . . . looks not only at the present but also at the past and future, and is deeply concerned with both conflicts and contradictions.

Consistent with Ritzer’s quote above, recent interpretations of dialectics have departed from an orthodox Hegelian view of synthesis, questioning the belief that contradictions necessarily lead to a novel positive endpoint in empirical contexts or that a synthesis necessarily solves the dialectical tensions encountered (de Rond and Bouchikhi 2004, Mumby 2005, Kreiner et al. 2015). In fact, contemporary research has defined dialectics in a more flexible sense as “the dynamic interplay and articulation together of opposites [without an] attempt to arrive at some kind of synthetic resolution . . . but rather [exploring] the possibilities that exist in keeping the[se] opposites in tension and play” (Mumby 2005, p. 23).

Several alternative types of responses to tensions have been discussed in the organizational change literature. These responses include selection, separation/vacillation, withdrawal, integration, transcendence, and connection (Gibbs 2009). The different types of resolutions to tensions have been theorized in various contexts, including managing problematic emotional reactions in the organizational context (Pratt and Doucet 2000), contradictory organizational norms and structures (Tracy 2004), organizational identity (Kreiner et al. 2015), and so on. These studies illustrate how encountered organiza tional tensions can be resolved by maintaining contradictory poles (e.g., “selecting one pole as dominant” or “vacillating between different options” as in Tracy 2004, p. 136) or reframing them by taking contradictory poles into account simultaneously (e.g., transcendence) (Baxter 1988; Tracy 2004; Gibbs 2009). Considering these viewpoints in the literature, the present study shows how the responses to the tensions play out in the ISSB context.

We found that, although a handful of empirical studies in the literature apply ideas of Hegelian dialectics (particularly the idea of synthesis in a traditional sense), the majority of conceptualizations of dialectics are adapted to study specific phenomena. The dialectical lens has been adapted to study institutional change (Farjoun 2002, Greenwood and Suddaby 2006), organizational identity development (Kreiner et al. 2015), organizational change (Lourenco and Glidewell 1975, Cule and Robey 2004), interorganizational collaboration (de Rond and Bouchikh 2004, Vlaar et al. 2007), knowledge production (Swan et al. 2010), team interaction (Gibbs 2009), and discursive negotiation (Putnam 2004). Examples of the dialectical approaches in IS include the adoption of telehealth innovation (Cho et al. 2007), systems development and/ or implementation (Sabherwal and Newman 2003, Nordheim and Paiv¨ arinta¨ 2006), postimplementation of IS (Rodon et al. 2011), collaborative appropriations of information technology (Carlo et al. 2012), and the relationship of IS use to trust and control (Gallivan and Depledge 2003).

We note that the dialectical perspective has not been used in ISS to understand ISSBs. As a result, potential insights that can be gained by conceptualizing ISSBs as arising from the resolution of changing opposing motivations or pressures encountered by an individual employee embedded in a context have not yet been put forward.

## 4.2. A Dialectical Process View of Employees’ ISSBs

We next present a high-level perspective of the dialectical change process for ISSBs that emerged from this study (Figure 1), followed by an illustration using the data. We italicize theoretical elements of Figure 1 in the text (where appropriate) to better provide the link between the data and the figure. We also offer a more detailed view of the theoretical elements in Online Appendix 3.

Upon entering a given organizational context, individuals tend to encounter tension regarding their current ISSBs, which functions as a motor for the individuals behavioral changes (van de Ven and Poole 1995).<sup>4</sup> Tensions arise from the competing cognitions (i.e., values, pressures, or interests) that we term the thesis and the antithesis (Benson 1977, Seo and Creed 2002, Robey et al. 2002).<sup>5</sup> We found several instances of competing theses and antitheses within the ISSB context and summarize them in the form of four dialectical tensions below (see also Table 3.2 in Online Appendix 3).

(1) Openness/trust versus suspicion pertaining to the ISS environment is a tension frequently encountered regarding ISS risks; openness/trust refers to high environmental confidence, and suspicion refers to low environmental confidence. For example, an individual might need to balance a value of openness and trust within the organizational environment (ISS risk remoteness; i.e., having a very low chance of ISS risks perceived) with suspicion (ISS risk sensitivity; i.e., being overly conscious about the different risks lurking in the environment) by being vigilant regarding colleagues or other relevant stakeholders. The two poles of this tension are dialectical, because they represent contradictory organizational norms and values: ISSBs often require employees to be suspicious in the working environment when they collaborate with different stakeholders, which creates a clear contradiction with the norms of being open and trusting that may often be touted in the same organization.

(2) Individual versus institutional ISS considerations refer to the encountered tension related to the level at which ISS goals and interests must be privileged when making ISS-related decisions. For example, an employee may encounter high normative vigilance to follow the organization’s ISSPs (a high level of institutional ISS consideration). However, particularly in the absence of clear and repetitive normative cues over time, the employee may prefer to rely on his or her personal initiative and decide to violate an ISSP (organizational-level ISS consideration) due to an increased level of ISS effort burden experiences (an individual-level ISS consideration). Furthermore, the focus behind a given ISSB might have contradictory institutional and individual consequences: Certain ISSBs may be considered potentially beneficial for the organization (i.e., institutional ISS consideration) but simultaneously reduce individuallevel productivity for some organizational members.

(3) Instrumental versus socio-emotional consideration is a tension encountered between instrumental and socioemotional motivations for an ISSB. Instrumental consequences are related to a low affective connection, and social-emotional consequences are related to a high affective connection. In other words, instrumental consequences are related to an emphasis on the material benefits of the ISSBs, whereas socio-emotional consequences are related to the feel-good element, such as maintaining social relationships and roles and serving positive affective needs. For example, individuals frequently need to balance the benefits of compliant ISSBs in terms of ensuring fool-proof protection of business-related critical information or helping others save time and money for the organization (instrumental consequences) with the need to violate an ISSP to help colleagues in urgent need of information and derive satisfaction from the helpful act (socio-emotional consequences).

(4) Immediate versus long-term focus relates to the time horizon prioritized in enacting an ISSB. Immediate and long-term foci can be seen as dialectical due to the opposing/competing time horizons of the expected impacts. In many cases, an advantage related to noncompliance is often realized immediately compared with an advantage related to compliance. For example, individuals, in trying to meet urgent work objectives, and/or in trying to respond to colleagues’ sudden/ urgent requests, work around or violate ISSPs, which may have been designed for the greater long-term good. Such actions appear to have immediate advantages, but these actions potentially enhance the risks of a negative consequence for the individual or to the organization over the long run. The tensions mentioned above were discerned from the case study data, not the ISSB literature. However, there is some scholarly basis for the tensions that we highlight. Although the previous ISS literature has not considered these or other tensions, we found that the tensions discussed above are actually acknowledged in the broader literature. For example, the tension between trust and suspicion is found in philosophical writings, particularly in relation to Ricoeur’s hermeneutics (van der Heiden 2014), as well as critical theory (Brassier 2016). Astley and van de Ven (1983) refer to the tension between individual and collective frames in leading to action in organizations. The tension between individual and institutional (or even societal) considerations is documented in many social situations, including marriage (Amato 2004), and one might argue that the agency-structure dialectic (Jessop 1996) is closely related. The tension between instrumental and socio-emotional (or affective) is also featured widely in the literature on communications, leadership, friendships, and intergroup reconciliation (Galanes 2009, Nadler and Shnabel 2015). This dialectic is somewhat related to what Baxter and Scharp (2016) refer to as “the dialectic of rationality (affectioninstrumentality).” Finally, Dodd and Favaro (2006), among others, allude to the long-term versus short-term tensions that entities continually face and must resolve.

Individuals can encounter tension pertaining to one or more of the above simultaneously in a given situation (e.g., openness/trust versus suspicion, immediate versus long-term). Experiencing a tension can lead them to initiate and, over time, revisit their previous ISS-related judgments (Daniels 2012) through ISSB-specific balancing acts (see Figure 1). Balancing acts involve negotiation between competing cognitions of theses and antitheses at a given time (Skinner et al. 2003, Daniels 2012). We found that tensions between the competing cognitions are resolved through four types of balancing acts: (1) the prioritization of antitheses or theses; (2) vacillation between theses and antitheses, which could be exceptionbased; (3) the compromise between theses and antitheses; or (4) the transcendence between theses and antitheses. In prioritization, encountered tensions are managed through selecting either one of the contradictory poles as dominant (Tracy 2004). Vacillation represents “contextual exceptions to such generally tenable inferences” (Johns 2006, p. 387), an act of fluctuating between ISSBs because there is ambivalence regarding prioritizing any of the ends. Compromising and transcendence go beyond prioritizing or vacillating between a thesis or an antithesis and can result in more innovative options being generated (Scott and Bruce 1994, Skinner et al. 2003). Compromising usually results in a behavioral solution in which neither end is fully satisfied (i.e., both ends are partially prioritized), and transcendence seeks to include and even go beyond both ends of the tension creatively (Tracy 2004).

Furthermore, we find that the balancing acts with different tensions result in a reevaluation of employees present ISSB patterns, potentially leading the individuals to a new ISSB: Prioritization may result in compliance or noncompliance (depending on which end of the tension is prioritized); vacillation can result in moving between compliance and noncompliance because a stable resolution cannot be found; compromising results in partial compliance; and transcendence results in the formulation of a creative solution that is in compliance as a resolution to the tensions encountered (see Figure 1). As a user gradually starts getting comfortable with his or her ISSB, he or she repeatedly applies it, at least under usual circumstances. Such routine reinforcement of a prevailing behavioral pattern, if present, can potentially become a habit, with the individual repeating the same ISSB (as earlier) without engaging in new balancing acts.

The appearance of significant ISSB-specific change triggers (i.e., certain abrupt changes in circumstances) prompts employees to exit this state of routinely enacting a given ISSB and return to a balancing act in which they reevaluate opposing pressures and values/interests within a new context or situation (see Figure 1). A trigger is a key theoretical element in understanding ISSBs and behavioral change, an idea that is often used in process models in the IS literature (Sarker and Sahay 2003, Chakraborty et al. 2010). Change triggers provide “situational opportunities or constraints that affect the occurrence and meaning of organizational behavior” (Johns 2006, p. 386) and help incorporate the role of the context in determining employees’ ISSBs (Swait et al. 2002). We found several context-specific or situationspecific events, activities, and/or experiences that function as opportunities or constraints for employees’ ISSBs. Our data pointed to three types of triggers: environmenta conditions (e.g., organizational changes and ISS threat exposure), situational demands (e.g., social interruptions and task completion imperative), and individual capabilities (e.g., human memory limitations and ISS technology inquisitiveness) that become relevant to a given ISSB situation. We next present interpreted narratives for the dialectical change process of ISSBs illustrating the forms of dialectical tension, balancing acts, new ISSBs, and change triggers (see the empirical illustrations in Online Appendix 3).

## 4.3. Case Narratives

The first narrative, based on the computer locking context, illustrates the following: (1) the presence of several tensions, such as those related to openness/ trust versus suspicion, individual versus institutiona level of goals and interests, and immediate versus longterm focus; (2) prioritization; and (3) an environmental condition as a change trigger, which prompted the reconsideration (and subsequent change) of employees ISSBs. Employees became aware of the company’s computer locking expectation after entering their new context due to the company’s training program and owing to other normative cues such as management’s requests.<sup>6</sup> In one particular case, a department assistant (one of the interviewees) reported experiencing a tension between the company expectation of being suspicious (reflected by her need to be a responsible employee) and her own preference for openness/trust in her work sur roundings (ISS risk remoteness, given the presumed noncriticality of the information assets). In this situation, because this user prioritized suspicion and institutiona interests over her individual preference regarding openness trust in her immediate organizational context, she ended up locking her computer more often when she left her work desk. One time, she left her computer unlocked, due to the situational demand of a task-completion im perative, in which she privileged the short-term goal of completing a task speedily over potential long-term consequences of harm to the company due to secure information being compromised from her computer. Later, she was surprised to see that a colleague had used her unlocked computer during her absence (environ mental condition trigger, personal ISS threat exposure). This created a new tension between her existing openness trust (ISS remoteness in terms of trust in colleagues) and suspicion (being sensitive to ISS risks in terms of her sense of personal privacy), which the employee resolved by choosing the need to eliminate the concrete ISS risk through locking her computer, without exception. The following quote illustrates this prioritization process:

I don’t want others seeing my own laptop. It’s not because I’m hiding but I think it’s . . . giving yourself at work also some privacy. . . . It’s [also] my responsibility at least to show an importance to the confidentiality of my work [regardless] the things that I’m working with are always available also in the common drive for everyone. I think why I [currently] always lock is because when one time when I didn’t lock it, one of my colleagues . . . joked and went to my laptop and started using it without my permission . . . I was surprised to see him using my laptop. I think I became more aware that I should not leave it freely, because I don’t know these people who want to play and joke around . . . I became more not afraid, but more . . . aware that I don’t want to just trust. (Officer009)

The second illustrative narrative, related to the context of scanning USB devices, highlights tensions related to openness/trust versus suspicion and instrumental versus socio-emotional focus. In addition, we see resolution of the dialectical tension in the forms of prioritization and subsequent vacillation, and situational demand as a change trigger. As background for the narrative, we note that one of the ISSPs of the company we studied states that virus scanning of USB devices needs to be done each time before opening a USB device. However, employees had the option of aborting the system-prompted automatic virus scanning when USB devices were used—in violation of the ISSP. In one particular case, an engineer explained how he had in the past tended to scan USB devices to prevent system crashes or technological damage regardless of his sense of environmental confidence in the ISS of the overall IT infrastructure of his organization (thus, prioritizing suspicion over trust). However, on receiving urgent requests from colleagues (i.e., on encountering a situational demand trigger), he failed to comply with ISSPs that required virus scanning of the USB device each and every time. On these occasions, the individual felt it inappropriate to act in way that would seem in opposition to certain social expectations (of helpfulness to colleagues), in order to comply with an ISSP. This created a tension between the socio-emotional and instrumental consequences of the ISSBs. For instance, the interviewee reported feeling obligated to help his coworkers by complying with their requests for ad hoc printing from a USB device as soon as possible (focusing on the socioemotional consequence), although he had concerns regarding malware causing damage or loss (an instrumenta consequence). Although he continued to generally scan USB devices under the usual circumstances as a result of the prioritization of instrumental consequence, for exception requests, vacillation was in evidence, where the user, at times, bypassed the requirement to scan USB devices specified by ISSPs:

I feel that maybe there could be a virus that can harm my computer. [So] when I use your USB in my computer . . . then I have to scan. . . . [However], sometimes I find it hard to say no if a friend from a workshop needs help, such as printing from a USB device in a hurry [without a virus scan]. . . . [Because] for me, it’s important to do extra things for a friend. Also, it’s difficult [for me] to say no to a new employee or a stranger because I want to help people in new situations. It feels good to help, and I myself would appreciate help from others in new situations. It’s a kind of ethical thing. (Engineer019)

Yet another interviewee described a somewhat similar situation leading to vacillation, although the tension encountered was related to the time horizon of the ISSB consequences: immediacy versus long-term focus:

[When I put a USB in my computer], normally it prompts with a scanning. So I let it scan first . . . because USBs can get virus any time . . . after it clears everything, then okay, I open it in my, Explorer. . . . It’s about a few minutes, two to three minutes, something like that. . . . [But] when I am in a hurry, I sometimes skip it. [For example, once] we were in a seminar. All the customers were there, and my laptop was used for the presentation. Most of the presentations had been e-mailed to me already. So that the flow would be smooth. But there were one or two presentations that were missing, and they just gave them to me when the customers were already present. So that time I didn’t really have time to do the scanning, because I was worried that I should start the event right away. Because we don’t want the customers or the people to wait just for this scanning. (Officer016)

The final two narratives highlight tensions and resolutions in password-protection situations. The first narrative shows how a resolution of the tensions occurs through prioritization, resulting in a noncompliant solution, followed by transcendence resulting in a creative solution that was in compliance. For instance, one of the interviewees reported initially confronting conflicting institutional versus individual interests related to passwordrelated ISSBs: an ISS breach concern due to a password being compromised potentially causing grave harm to the organization (institutional interest) and the usability of the password storage medium for the individual and the memorability of the password (individual interest). The ensuing balancing act resulted in the prioritization (selection) of individual interests over institutional interests, leading to noncompliance with the password ISSP initially. The individual chose to write down passwords on a piece of paper as a backup after failing to remember his passwords. After experiencing difficulties with recalling the password (e.g., paper notes got lost or were not available when needed), the user was prompted to reevaluate the tensions, and this round of balancing led to fresh prioritization in favor of, once again, individual interests. The outcome of this iteration of balancing was that the user ended up writing passwords down on a more available (but not more secure) medium—his mobile device that he carried around—compared with paper notes on his desk. Interestingly, in becoming more aware of possible ISS threats related to mobile devices through a discussion with a friend (environmental condition trigger of ISS threat exposure), the individual was prompted to engage in yet another balancing act, this time, leading to transcendence between the conflicting priorities. The individual ended up storing hints about his complex (strong) passwords (i.e., “code words,” such as words related to his personal life or words particular to his native language as an “indirect way of remembering” the actual password) in the organization’s cloud. This solution proved to be easy to use (and accessible from any location) and more effective in securing organizational resources protected by the individual’s passwords. Such an ISSB representing transcendence between the individual and the institutional interests can be seen as a creative way of being compliant with the ISSPs (as opposed to the obvious, noncreative, and potentially infeasible way of memorizing complex passwords). The employee stated the following:

It’s an indirect way of remembering . . . I have the code word for the password [written down]. . . . So if I read that code word, I remember the password . . . I can show those files to you, you will not understand anything. (Officer007)

As the user gradually started feeling confident about this ISSB, it became a routinized behavior.

In a similar scenario, initially, the relevant individual sought to resolve the tension through a compromise, taking the contradictory poles into account simultaneously. He ended up choosing stronger passwords but noting them down in a way that may be considered more (but not wholly) secure (e.g., he stored passwords on his office computer rather than writing the password on a slip of paper and placing it on his table or within a drawer). This new ISSB may be seen as being partially compliant: a solution that recognizes and incorporates the competing priorities into the solution, but neither end is fully satisfied. The passwords could not be accessed easily by others, but the passwords could not be considered to be secure. The emergence of a situational trigger of greater awareness of ISS threats led to a rebalancing act and a resolution. This time, the individual confronted the tension by taking contradictory poles into account simultaneously to produce a creative solution. He recorded his (strong) passwords on a specifically designated password-protected USB device, using the device only in limited environments without an Internet connection. This solution simultaneously made password management less burdensome and, at the same time, more effective in maintaining high-level ISS consistent with the spirit of ISSPs. Although this solution was not perfect (because the USB device was location bound, and we are not clear about the controls at the location where the USB device was physically stored), by and large, we may see this as an example where the individual sought to transcend (not prioritize or even compromise or vacillate regarding) the tension. The following quote describes the situation:

I have my special USB device . . . at home . . . all passwords will be there. . . . So when I connect that USB, first I ensure that computer is out of network. [In the past], I [used to] put the password and zip it with some coding, again putting some password . . . then I kept in the computer. But . . . I found it is also not secure. Because, lot of . . . programs nowadays in the market you can easily break the password from the zip also . . . I am concerned. That risk is always there . . . that kinds of policy at the moment we do not have [regarding storage of passwords on a USB at home] I guess that it is said [in the policy] . . . that you should not write your passwords, and keep it in visible places. . . . That’s in the policy, yes . . . [but] I’m getting older, sometimes I feel difficulties, I forget those passwords. After that I started . . . I have . . . some place to keep those passwords. (Manager001)

A key point that emerges from the data is that ISSBs reflecting prioritization, vacillation, compromise, or transcendence do not necessarily remain the same over time for an employee. Instead, an individual’s method for resolving tensions (and the resolutions themselves) con tinue to change as the individual’s circumstances change For example, an employee who locked her computer yesterday (or every day of the past week) may or may not do so in the future. A given ISSB is often the result of a balancing act between a changing set of opposing considerations within a given (new) circumstance the individual encounters. As Figure 1 shows, the proposed model holds across individuals and across time, and there is no limit to how many times the entire process of balancing occurs, leading to the same (or different) resolu tions. The resolutions are, by definition, temporary, and as new triggers appear, they prompt a new cycle of the entire process of balancing tensions and resolution. We must emphasize that our model does not seek to provide a pattern regarding how tensions themselves evolve over time, but, instead, presents how the development path is determined by individual and situational conditions. In other words, the path is subject to various individual and contextual differences, and overall, this model is consistent with the notion of an emergent rather than technologically or socially deterministic perspective on human behaviors (Markus and Robey 1988).

## 5. Discussion and Contributions

In this section, we elaborate on our contributions to the ISS literature, relate our view to the rule compliance literature, offer some recommendations for future research, and discuss practical implications as well as limitations.

## 5.1. To What Extent Can the Theories Frequently Used in ISS Explain our ISSB Observations?

With respect to our contributions, we first submit that many of the theories frequently used in ISS (e.g., PMT, neutralization, and deterrence theory) are not able to adequately explain our observations—that is, the narratives discussed above—in totality. This does not mean that the existing studies are without value. Instead, the point is that there is a need to complement research that has not focused on explaining how the reasons for ISSBs change over time. We recall our discussion of previous ISSB research, where we note that studies have predominantly tested reference theories from different disciplines, such as criminology, economics, and health psychology, that have specific assumptions because of the contexts in which the theories were formulated originally (e.g., crime, investments, or health threats). In contrast, our model involving the tensions, the balancing acts, the resolutions, and the triggers is derived predominantly in an “inductive” manner by studying the phenomenon of ISSBs (rather than from the phenomena of crime, economics, or health threats).

A fundamental difference between our theoretical contribution and previous theories used in ISS is related to a scientific worldview. Our work examines a complex and emergent relationship, where the agency of the users is given much importance. In contrast, previous research focused on a static and fixed relationship between the independent variables (IVs) and dependent variable (DV), or antecedent conditions and the outcomes

A final point worth highlighting is that our process model is not similar to previous attempts in the literature to combine theories. For example, neutralization techniques and deterrence theory (Siponen and Vance 2010), deterrence theory and PMT (Johnston et al. 2015), and PMT and habit (Vance et al. 2012) have been examined together. However, these studies also assume static and fixed relationships between the IVs and the DV and are unable to explain observations that change over time and circumstances. As an illustration, the Vance et al. (2012) PMT and habit integration study did not examine under what conditions employees reasoning follows PMT, under what conditions the reasoning turns into a habit, and under what conditions the habit fades away.

In this study, we offer a new conceptualization of how ISSBs occur (Figure 1). We inductively discern four key tensions that individuals engage with and seek to resolve dialectically to determine what ISSB they should enact. The tensions are resolved temporarily, especially when the resolution involves prioritization, vacillation, and compromising. Even when the resolution is through transcendence, which would lead to a new solution resulting in inherently greater stability or equilibrium in the process, certain triggers can arise that would initiate a fresh round of balancing of the conflicting priorities.

Regarding the nature of our theoretical contribution, we emphasize that we seek to offer a type II theory, which is typical of interpretive studies (Gregor 2006). Consistent with this type of theory, our contribution is directed not toward “instrumental” utilization but toward “conceptual” utilization by scholars and practitioners (Astley and Zammuto 1992). In other words, we are not trying to specify fixed, static, or “deterministic” relationships between tensions, progression of tensions, and ISSBs (violation or compliance), but a new conceptual way of understanding dynamically how and why people enact certain ISSBs. Although future research may take this broad conceptual understanding and propose or test specific relationships (say, the sequence in which tensions arise, if any, and their consequences), our work represents a foundational step in which an ISSB is seen to result from the engagement with and resolution of one or more of the identified four tensions (see Figure 1). This new conceptualization, we believe, highlights the important role of reflection and agency in ISSBs, both of which have not been adequately acknowledged in the literature.

## 5.2. Situating Our Dialectical Process View of ISSBs Within the Rule Compliance Literature

Consistent with studies that used a grounded approach and published guidelines (Urquhart et al. 2010, Birks et al. 2013, Urquhart and Fernandez 2013), we can relate our findings more broadly to other relevant areas in the literature, specifically to the body of work on rule compliance in the organizational context. We found four common perspectives on individual rule compliance/ breaking behavior within the literature (see the summary in Online Appendix 4). While being sensitive to these perspectives, our study offers a somewhat distinct ontological foundations of an ISSB, characterizing it as (1) a potentially benevolent behavior (rather than necessarily a deviant and purely self-interested behavior), (2) a voluntary behavior based on affirmative individual interpretations and motivations that go beyond extrinsic influences, (3) resulting from a combination of intentional and unintentional motivations to comply or not comply with equivocal behavioral requirements, and (4) changing over time via the weighing of contradictory beliefs and values resulting from situation- or contextspecific needs. This deliberate role of agency in changes related to ISSBs is elaborated next.

First, rule breaking is considered a deviant and purely self-interested behavior of employees who are motivated by negative work-related perceptions (e.g., dissatisfaction or low organizational commitment), opportunism, or even illegal intentions (Vardi and Weitz 2004). A similar perspective has been expressed toward computer abuse (Baskerville et al. 2014) and information systems misuse (Hovav and D’Arcy 2012), treating these activities as illegal, malicious, or unethical ISSBs. In contrast, the present study reveals that employees’ compliance or noncompliance with ISSPs is not necessarily related to malicious or purely self-interested motivations. We found that employees ISSBs can be motivated by benevolent intentions to benefit an organization (e.g., prioritizing organizational tasks over security) or colleagues (e.g., prioritizing altruism over security) rather than be motivated by an intention to harm the company. Our view of ISSP compliance and noncompliance, in many instances, bears some similarity to the phenomenon of “pro-social rule breaking” (PSRB; Morrison 2006) or rule bending (Sekerka and Zolin 2007), in which rules are intentionally violated to promote “the welfare of the organization or one of its stakeholders” (Morrison 2006, p. 6). In relation to dialectics, these types of decisions to break formal policies or rules with good intentions are fundamentally characterized by the perceived necessity to make tradeoffs in order to cope with dilemmas: “a decision not always between right or wrong alternatives, nor between good and bad choices. Instead PSRB (pro-social rule breaking) decisions are often between right and right, or be tween one bad choice and another” (Vardaman et al. 2014, p. 109). Previous organizational literature has seldom acknowledged such a constructive or positive deviant understanding of organizational rule violation, and even fewer empirical studies highlight these patterns in security or related domains (Dahling et al. 2012).

Second, the literature on rule compliance frequently draws upon rational models of employees’ behavior and highlights how extrinsic regulation imposes different costs for an individual when engaging in certain behaviors. For example, Lessig (1999, 2006) offers a framework for studying how individual behaviors are regulated by extrinsic factors that constrain behaviors. Notably, in this framework, the individual is conceived of as a passive recipient of extrinsic constraints (i.e., “a pathetic dot”). Regarding such extrinsic influences, the view common in the literature is that the influence of laws and organizational norms represents the backbone of rule compliance behaviors (Winter and May 2001, Tyler 2006). In these instances, it is assumed that rules are violated because employees are maximizing their own benefits in the absence of sufficient organizational controls (Morrison 2006). Thus, rule compliance behavior is predominantly explained in terms of negative motivations, or fears, related to the negative consequences of getting caught in violation of regulations (May 2004).

In contrast, in the present study, we found that employees experienced a significant level of voluntariness and autonomy in making decisions about complying with ISSPs and that the employees’ ISSBs were seldom directly constrained by extrinsic pressures, such as organizational norms (including influence of authorities) and legal consequences, or extrinsic behavioral reinforcements (such as monitoring or sanctions on noncompliant ISSBs). Instead of being blindly subservient to a socially legitimate, mandatory-like policy, whether it made sense to employees or not within their contexts (Johnson et al. 2006), we found that employees tended to comply with ISSPs based on a personal evaluation of the appropriateness of the ISSPs. Although the deterrence-based approach for explaining ISSBs is dominant in the field of ISS, previous studies support our views via showing that employees’ intrinsic motivation related to personal values offers a better explanation for ISSBs (Son 2011, Li et al. 2014). Organizationally mandated ISSBs were even regarded as inappropriate at times, with certain employees seeing the ISSBs as contradicting social or environmental assumptions (e.g., value conflicts). In summary, the findings of the present study suggest that regulations or sanctions may have a limited role in determining compliance with ISSPs, particularly for ISSBs studied.

This study also offers an understanding of compliance in environments where the influence of normative and deterrent pressures is not clearly established. Specifically, we believe that the insights gained can help strengthen rule compliance based on a better understanding of affirmative and self-regulatory individual interpretations and motivations (May 2004, Tyler and Blader 2005, Son 2011, Li et al. 2014), in predominantly voluntary rule compliance situations. Thus, our perspective can be seen to be similar to that found within microlevel legitimacy studies of “intra-individual” processes involved in institutional change in terms of actively considering individuals’ perceptions and beliefs regarding an entity (e.g., a rule, action, procedure, or idea; Powel and Colyvas 2008, Tost 2011). In short, this study not only elaborates on the issues influencing voluntary rule compliance in a specific context without insurmountable normative pressure but also provides a perspective for understanding the construction of and change in such rule compliance that is seldom recognized in previous empirical literature on the topic in general (Tost 2011) or specific to ISS (Son 2011, Li et al. 2014).

Third, previous rule compliance literature has often assumed that rule violations involve intentional decisions to act in a manner that is inconsistent with organizational policies (Morrison 2006). From this perspective, policies are assumed to have low equivocality (i.e., there is a high degree of consensus regarding the behavioral requirements of a given policy) (Lehman and Ramanujam 2009). We found that employees do not always associate their ISSBs with their noncompliance with ISSPs. In fact, we believe that ISSPs may at times be equivocal (Edelman et al. 1991, Spilker et al. 1999) which is at odds with Lehman and Ramanujam’s (2009) assumption. We found that employees often experience ISSP equivocality in terms of goals and means (Matland 1995) as seen in the case where the rationale for having strong passwords, or the way strong passwords were to be formulated, was not clear to employees.

Fourth, similar to extant research on employees compliance with ISSPs, the broader rule compliance literature has seldom studied how the motivations and behaviors for individual-level rule compliance change over time (Tost 2011). In other words, the literature conceptualizes rule compliance as static. In particular, we did not find any study that explained rule compliance in terms of the consideration of tensions and opposing perceptions and beliefs (theses and antitheses) leading to different behaviors based on specific circumstances. In addition, the rule compliance literature does not recognize the dynamics of behavioral changes that result from situation-specific or context-specific judgments.

In summary, we believe that our dialectical understanding of ISSBs can be particularly useful when: noncompliance is driven not necessarily by malicious intentions but by benevolent ones; ISSBs are not strongly constrained by extrinsic pressures; individuals perceive rules (here, ISSPs) to be equivocal; and we seek an understanding of when, how, and why an employee’s rule compliance might change to one of noncompliance or vice versa. Given these findings, we hope we have been able to demonstrate the value of a dialectical approach to gain a deeper (and arguably a different) understanding of the ISSB change process where context plays an important role (de Rond and Bouchikhi 2004).

## 5.3. Implications for Practice

Previous ISS research presents a comprehensive set of underlying reasons to promote compliant ISSBs. However, this body of research advises us that the same underlying reasons need to be managed to promote compliant ISSBs, irrespective of the situation. As a result, one might argue that extant research advances a “one-size-fits-all intervention approach” (Schwarzer 2008, p. 3). We believe that it is beneficial not only to concretize why compliance with ISSPs occurs (i.e., the factors that may influence an ISSB) but also to emphasize the need to pay attention to the importance of opposing values and pressures, triggers, and methods for resolving, and possibly transcending, the existing tensions. This will likely enable awareness of the dilemmas that individuals confront in ISS-related situations and potentially contribute to the design of better controls for ensuring compliance of ISSBs.

Accordingly, different types of tensions need to be pointed out for employees because these tensions function as a prerequisite for initiating employees’ thinking processes about ISSBs and employees’ behaviora changes. This study highlights four tensions employees need to be reflexively aware of. These tensions are related to environmental confidence (openness/trust versus suspicion), level of goals and interests prioritized (individual versus institutional), motivational driver (instrumental versus socio-emotional), and/or time horizon (immediate versus long-term focus). These tensions are not isolated, and each situation may involve one or more tensions. Moreover, the set of tensions discussed above is not exhaustive, and we can foresee other tensions emerging in different contexts.

The results of this study also help ISS practitioners understand the implications of the different strategies in which employees seek to resolve tensions. Interestingly, the findings show that new ISSBs do not resolve tensions in most cases, except for creating a new solution through transcendence, which occurs infrequently. Therefore, this study suggests that resolving tensions through isolated/unilateral efforts of ISS practitioners or users may not be ideal; mutual information sharing may be needed to identify and resolve tensions and design effective ISSPs. Transcendence is an important method for resolving tensions, not only from the perspective of achieving an improved level of ISS but also because improvements arising from transcendence have previously been associated with individual satisfaction, lower anxiety, or other positive consequences in other research fields (Tracy 2004).

## 5.4. Future Research

Based on these findings, we highlight some areas that need more attention.

First, this study provides a new ISS-specific framework for understanding the content and the logic of a behavioral change process in different professional, organizational, and cultural contexts. Our theoretically and empirically grounded framework involves a multidimensional view of tensions that continuously shape employees’ ISS rationalization and ISSBs. Future quantitative studies can use this theoretical framework to formulate conflicting hypotheses with specified contingencies that would determine which of the competing hypotheses might prevail. The present study provides a foundation for such studies by singling out the situation- and context-specific features that may influence relationships between variables.

Second, future research could study in detail the differences and commonalities of different ISSBs, as well as theorize about the inherent characteristics of different ISSBs. In this context, these characteristics of ISSBs could include, for example, the complexity and frequency of an ISSB, the extent to which an ISSB can become a habit or routine, the recognizability/traceability of an ISSB by others in an organization, mechanisms of technical enforcement, and work fit (i.e., whether the ISSB is connected to users’ normal work tasks and expectations). Future research could also attempt to develop specific theoretical explanations for each type of ISSB (e.g., password behavior versus email encryption behavior). Laudan (1983) notes how scientific theories in the natural sciences typically move from being generic to being specific, with newer theories tending to be particularized to a phenomenon and often more complicated, and we see theorizing about ISSBs taking a similar path.

The present study empirically discerned four types of balancing acts between the conflicting viewpoints resulting from the intraindividual tensions. Future research could further theorize about these balancing acts and additional types of balancing acts and conditions that lead to the different types of behavioral resolutions. Specifically, future research can further theorize about conditions (e.g., organizational and managerial) that can encourage employees to resolve encountered tensions constructively from the viewpoint of ISS (i.e., transcending the opposing poles). This work could be potentially pursued by using literature on innovative behavior (Jabri 1991, Scott and Bruce 1994), regulation of affective experiences in the work context (Power and Dalgleish 1997, Skinner et al. 2003), or managerial communication as a reflexive practice recognizing multiple and contested interpretations of situations (Barge 2004). Instead of the intraindividual perspective of this study, future research can also take an intraorganizational or interorganizational dialectical perspective and analyze conflicts between the different forms of values (Bourne and Jenkins 2013), conflicts resulting from the intersection of national, organizational, and subunit cultures (Leidner and Kayworth 2006), or conflicts between employees’ (individuals or groups) and the organization’s value systems (Tyler and Blader 2005).

## 5.5. Limitations

This study does have some limitations. First, the elements and dimensions of the process model discerned from the data may, in part, reflect the company’s and interviewees’ characteristics. However, the four tensions and the specific triggers are not intended to be viewed as comprehensive foundations for explaining the ISSB change process. In fact, we believe that the emergence of additional instances of the process model elements (e.g., tensions and triggers) does not reduce the contribution of our work, given that it offers a new way to examine and understand ISSBs. We invite further research to develop a more refined set of tensions that are orthogonal and generalizable.

Furthermore, the model we offer may be criticized for not being predictive. As mentioned previously, our methodological viewpoint treats ISSBs from an emergent perspective that is fundamentally different compared with the logic of deterministic and probabilistic models. This viewpoint is consistent with the nature of the study’s sociotechnical focus where behavior emerges “unpredictably from complex social interactions” (Markus and Robey 1988, p. 588). We would like to highlight here that “philosophers of very diverse persuasions continue to agree that a fundamental aim of science is to provide explanations” (Salmon 1989, p. 4; i.e., as opposed to prediction) and note that even theories in physics, such as Newton’s theory of gravitation, and biology, such as Mendel’s theory or Darwin’s theory, are not predictive (Putnam 1974). Furthermore, Shmueli (2010) observes that few if any IS articles published in top journals before 2010 are predictive in nature.

As in the case of similar studies, social desirability of responses and fear of management reprisals may have played a role in shaping the findings. We consciously sought to minimize the social desirability effect related to interviews in multiple ways (van Maanen 1979; see Online Appendix 2). Based on the data collected in this study, we believe that we succeeded in creating an environment in which the employees felt free to explore aloud the contradictions they faced in relation to ISSPs. In many cases, the interviewees specifically discussed their own dilemmas and their own failure to comply with ISSBs.

## 6. Conclusion

Along with malicious attacks and technological glitches, negligent employees who violate ISS policies remain among the most significant causes of ISS breaches (Ponemon Institute 2014a, b). Consequently, the ISSBs of organizational employees have become a major focus for security researchers.

As we delved further into the phenomenon, we realized that employees’ reasons for engaging in different ISSBs (e.g., selecting a password, locking a computer, or using a USB device) change over time as individuals seek to deal with or balance rival motivations or considerations. This realization led us to develop a midrange process theory for employees’ ISSBs, rooted in dialectics. This theory holds that different types of ISSBs arise through balancing acts to deal with certain tensions, and the tensions are often initiated by change triggers. Overall, the proposed dialectic view of the ISSB change process offers a new way of thinking about why an employee might engage in certain ISSBs and why and how the same employee’s ISSBs may change over time. Understanding ISSBs through this dialectic process lens reveals the dynamic nature, as well as a number of subtle complexities associated with ISSBs that can complement previous scholarship. There is clearly more to learn about ISSBs, but we hope that this paper provides a useful foundation for research and can lead to more effective ISS interventions in the future.
