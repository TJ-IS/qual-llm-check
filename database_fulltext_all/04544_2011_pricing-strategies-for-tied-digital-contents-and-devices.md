## a b s t r a c t

Media companies are increasingly offering digital content to consumers. Many of these companies are tying digital content with their proprietary digital devices. In this study, we develop a consumer demand model for digital device and digital content based on a constant elasticity demand function. In modeling consumer valuation of the digital device, we take consumer surplus on digital content into account. We further derive equilibrium prices for digital devices based on an oligopoly competition model with horizontal product differentiation. We analyze the equilibrium prices and how prices affect <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. We <sup>fi</sup>nd product differentiation and the level of product substitutability affect prices. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that content price plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in affecting the price of the digital device. Content price can either increase or decrease the tied digital device price depending on the pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin and demand elasticity of the digital content. We further analyze how content and device prices affect their respective pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and the overall pro<sup>fi</sup>t of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. We extend our model to vertical product differentiation and <sup>fi</sup>nd vertical product differentiation and the level of product quality affect prices.

## 1. Introduction

Media companies are increasingly offering digital content online. Apple Computer is one of the pioneers in selling digital music at its iTunes store. Consumers can buy digital songs and videos starting at just 99 cents a piece [25]. With the high speed Internet, Apple, Net<sup>fl</sup>ix, TiVo and some other companies are providing videos directly to televisions at home [15,22]. As “the last bastion of analog”, according to Amazon's founder Jeff Bezos, books will be digitized as well [11]. The competition in digital books market has been heated. About 2 years after Amazon introduced its popular e-book reader Kindle in 2007. Barnes and Noble announced its digital book reader in 2009 [27]. In 2010, Apple introduced its competing iPad device, aiming to break Amazon's lead in the digital book market [14].

There are different ways to sell digital contents. While some companies provide “free” content with advertising on the Internet, many of the media companies are selling digital content with their proprietary formats and digital devices. For example, Apple sells digital songs in iTunes format using Advanced Audio Coding that can only be played with iPod. If consumers want to stream videos to their TVs from iTunes store, then they have to purchase Apple TV, which costs \$229 [8]. Net<sup>fl</sup>ix has teamed up with LG to introduce its own video player that can play movies on Net<sup>fl</sup>ix's streaming on-demand service [22]. The e-books sold by Amazon are tied to its proprietary Kindle digital reader. Amazon sells digital versions of New York Time best sellers and new releases for \$9.99 [23]. Amazon also provides some books at \$0.00 for its Kindle customers. The new version of Amazon's e-book readers Kindle 2 was initially priced at \$359, which was quite expensive to many customers [23].

In all of the above examples, a <sup>fi</sup>rm makes the sale of its product, e.g. e-books, conditional upon the purchasers also buying another product, e.g. Kindle, from the same <sup>fi</sup>rm. This type of business practice is called tying in economics literature [28]. While Apple's core product is iPod, many companies' main products are digital contents. Although companies can make pro<sup>fi</sup>t through digital devices, one of the goals of tying digital contents with a proprietary device is to help sell content for those companies.

In this study, we aim to address the following questions:

1) How do consumers value tied products such as digital contents that they are likely to buy repeatedly?

2) What are the factors affecting consumers' adoption of an expensive digital device?

3) What are the equilibrium prices for the digital devices?

4) How do the prices of digital contents and devices affect a <sup>fi</sup>rm's pro<sup>fi</sup>t?

The paper is organized as follows. We review related literature in Section 2, followed by the model setup in Section 3. In Section 4, we present our model analysis. We make an extension to the model in Section 5. We discuss our results in Section 6 and provide conclusions in Section 7.

## 2. Prior literature

In a broad sense, digital media belongs to information goods, which are goods whose main value is derived from their digital content [21]. Information goods usually incur large <sup>fi</sup>xed development costs and low production costs. There is a rich literature on information goods from information systems (IS) area, for example, Riggins [18] studied a separating equilibrium for a two-tier fee-based and sponsorship-based information Web site. Lang and Vragov [10] examined a pricing scheme for distributing digital content over centralized versus decentralized networks. Chellappa and Kumar [3] studied how “free” product-augmenting services affect pricing and customer retentions online. Fan et al. [6] developed a model to examine optimal strategies for media providers to utilize the online channel to distribute digital media. Mantena et al. [13] studied vendors of platforms such as video game consoles and vendors of complement goods. Li [12] examined the pricing and distribution channels of digital contents.

The literature on complementary and tying products is mostly from economics. Shaked and Sutton [19,20] developed models for products with vertical differentiation, in which products or services had different levels of quality. Church and Gandal [5] assessed the effect of hardware control of software provision in markets in which the consumption bene<sup>fi</sup>t of a durable or hardware good is a function of the variety of complementary products or software available. Strauss [24] discussed marketing strategies for a manufacturer of a composite product with cross-market network externalities. Chen and Xie [4] examined the competitive implications of asymmetric customer loyalty in a cross-market. They found a middle level of loyalty advantage in the primary product market can lead to an overall pro<sup>fi</sup>t disadvantage. In the IS area, $\mathrm { { A r a k j i } }$ and Lang [2] studied digital consumer networks and producer–consumer collaboration, which used economic modeling to derive <sup>fi</sup>rm strategies based on the complementarities between video game engines and video game content.

Laffont et al. [9] developed a model of unregulated competition between interconnected networks and analyzed the mature and transition phases of the industry in this deregulated environment. In their model, consumers derive their utilities from the networks by using the communication service repeatedly. This demand model is mostly relevant to the problem we analyze. Thus, we use the basic structure of Laffont et al. [9] in modeling consumer demands.

This paper analyzes pricing strategies for a digital device and the tied contents. The results provide interesting implications to companies that compete in this growing market.

## 3. Model setup

While the basic idea of tying is not fundamentally different between information goods and physical products, there is an interesting difference. For physical goods, the two tied products are usually consumed in <sup>fi</sup>xed proportions. For the case of digital goods, applications or content are tied to a platform and the demand for the digital goods are endogenously determined. Following Laffont et al. [9], we model a consumer's demand for digital content as a constant elasticity demand function $q _ { e } = p _ { e } ^ { - \eta } ,$ , where $p _ { e }$ is the price of the electronic content and $q _ { e }$ the quantity of the electronic content a consumer buys. The constant elasticity of the demand function is η, which is assumed to exceed one [9] . Thus, the pricing function is $p _ { e } = q _ { e } ^ { - 1 / \eta }$

When a hardware device is not free, consumers need to take into account the quantities of the electronic content they may consume in order to assess the overall bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of adopting the digital device. Therefore, the utility function for consumers depends on consumer surplus of the consumption. We <sup>fi</sup>rst calculate a consumer's gross surplus, which is:

$$
\int_ {0} ^ {q _ {e}} p _ {e} d p _ {e} = \int_ {0} ^ {q _ {e}} q ^ {- 1 / \eta} d q = \frac {q _ {e} ^ {1 - (1 / \eta)}}{1 - 1 / \eta}.
$$

The consumer's variable net surplus is:

$$
v (p _ {e}) = \frac {q _ {e} ^ {1 - (1 / \eta)}}{1 - 1 / \eta} - p _ {e} q _ {e} = \frac {p _ {e} ^ {- (\eta - 1)}}{\eta - 1}.
$$

Lemma 1. The consumer's variable net surplus of the digital content is a convex function of the price for the digital content, i.e. $\frac { \partial \nu ( p _ { e } ) } { \partial p _ { e } } < 0 .$ $\frac { \partial \nu ^ { 2 } ( p _ { e } ) } { \partial p _ { e } ^ { 2 } } > 0 .$

The proofs for this Lemma and other propositions are provided in the Appendix.

It is not surprising that consumers' surplus strictly decreases in the price of the digital content. In addition, Lemma 1 suggests that as price decreases, consumers' surplus increases sharply because consumers bene<sup>fi</sup>t not only from a lower price but also a higher demand for the contents.

We next analyze the demand for two competing <sup>fi</sup>rms. The analysis generalizes straightforwardly to the case of multiple <sup>fi</sup>rm oligopoly [26]. We follow the Hotelling model [26] and assume consumers are uniformly located on the segment [0, 1]. There are two companies competing with their proprietary devices and contents. They are located at the two extremities of the segment, namely $\alpha _ { 1 } = 0$ and $\alpha _ { 2 } = 1$ . A consumer located at location α will have a utility

$$
v (p _ {e i}) - t | \alpha - \alpha_ {i} | - p _ {k i}
$$

where $t | \alpha - \alpha _ { i } |$ denotes the cost of using a company with location $\alpha _ { i } ( i = 1 , 2 )$ , and $p _ { e i }$ and $p _ { k i }$ are the prices for digital content and device, respectively, from company $i ( \mathrm { i } = 1 , 2 )$

A consumer is indifferent between the two companies' products if

$$
v (p _ {e 1}) - t \alpha - p _ {k 1} = v (p _ {e 2}) - t (1 - \alpha) - p _ {k 2}.
$$

We can solve for the indifference point $\alpha ^ { * }$ that the consumer has the same utility in adopting products from either of the two companies. The consumer demand for the digital device offered by company 1 is therefore

$$
D _ {1} = \alpha^ {*} = \frac {1}{2} + \sigma [ v (p _ {e 1}) - v (p _ {e 2}) ] + \sigma (p _ {k 2} - p _ {k 1}),\tag{1}
$$

and the consumer demand for the digital device offered by company 2 is

$$
D _ {2} = 1 - \alpha^ {*} = \frac {1}{2} + \sigma [ v (p _ {e 2}) - v (p _ {e 1}) ] + \sigma (p _ {k 1} - p _ {k 2}),\tag{2}
$$

where $\sigma \equiv \frac { 1 } { 2 t }$ is an index of substitutability between the products of the two companies.

The demand functions clearly indicate that the consumer's adoption of a company's device and contents depends on (i) the substitutability between two devices, (ii) the variable net surplus of digital contents, and (iii) the prices of the digital devices.

The pro<sup>fi</sup>t function for <sup>fi</sup>rm i is

$$
\pi_ {i} = [ (p _ {e i} - c _ {e i}) q _ {e i} + (p _ {k i} - c _ {k i}) ] D _ {i},
$$

where $D _ { i }$ is the demand for digital device for <sup>fi</sup>rm i, and $q _ { e i }$ is the demand for electronic content for an individual customer of <sup>fi</sup>rm i.

Substituting the demand functions of $D _ { i }$ and $q _ { e i }$ into the above pro<sup>fi</sup>t function, we can get the pro<sup>fi</sup>t functions for both <sup>fi</sup>rms:

$$
\pi_ {1} = \left[ \left(p _ {e 1} - c _ {e 1}\right) p _ {e 1} ^ {- \eta} + \left(p _ {k 1} - c _ {k 1}\right) \right] \left[ \frac {1}{2} + \frac {\sigma}{\eta - 1} \left(p _ {e 1} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} - p _ {e 2} ^ {- (\eta - 1)}\right) + \sigma \left(p _ {k 2} - p _ {k 1}\right) \right]\tag{3}
$$

$$
\pi_ {2} = \left[ (p _ {e 2} - c _ {e 2}) p _ {e 2} ^ {- \eta} + (p _ {k 2} - c _ {k 2}) \right] \left[ \frac {1}{2} + \frac {\sigma}{\eta - 1} \Big (p _ {e 2} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} - p _ {e 1} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} \Big) + \sigma (p _ {k 1} - p _ {k 2}) \right]\tag{4}
$$

Since the competitors are selling the similar digital products, we assume these products have the same elasticity η.

The above model setup assumes a horizontal product differentiation, in which the digital devices do not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly differ in terms of quality. We extend the basic model and analyze the case of product differentiation in Section 5.

## 4. Analysis

We consider a competition model in which <sup>fi</sup>rms 1 and 2 move simultaneously to decide the prices of their respective hardware devices. The prices for digital content from the two companies are given in our model. One reason is that content price is usually determined by the factors beyond the scope of this study. For example, one important factor in determining the content price is that there are physical versions of the same contents. As of now, the market for books, CDs, and DVDs is bigger than their digital counterparts. Publishers and studios want to maintain the price for digital contents in order not to cannibalize the physical product market. For example, the price of 99 cents per song at iTune store is largely based on the wholesale price charged by the labels [1]. In addition, competition can also play an important role in determining the prices.

Taking <sup>fi</sup>rst order conditions for the pro<sup>fi</sup>t functions, we can calculate the equilibrium prices for the Nash equilibrium.

Proposition 1. The equilibrium prices for the two digital devices are as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} p _ {k 1} ^ {*} = \frac {1}{2 \sigma} + \frac {1}{3 (\eta - 1)} \Big (p _ {e 1} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} - p _ {e 2} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} \Big) - \frac {2}{3} (p _ {e 1} - c _ {e 1}) p _ {e 1} ^ {- \eta} \\ - \frac {1}{3} (p _ {e 2} - c _ {e 2}) p _ {e 2} ^ {- \eta} + \frac {2}{3} c _ {k 1} + \frac {1}{3} c _ {k 2} \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} p _ {k 2} ^ {*} = \frac {1}{2 \sigma} - \frac {1}{3 (\eta - 1)} \left(p _ {e 1} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} - p _ {e 2} ^ {- (\eta - 1)}\right) - \frac {1}{3} (p _ {e 1} - c _ {e 1}) p _ {e 1} ^ {- \eta} \\ - \frac {2}{3} (p _ {e 2} - c _ {e 2}) p _ {e 2} ^ {- \eta} + \frac {1}{3} c _ {k 1} + \frac {2}{3} c _ {k 2} \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

It is clear that the equilibrium digital device prices depend on the costs and other competitive factors. The price of a device increases not only in its own cost but also the cost of the competitive device, i.e. $\partial p _ { k i } ^ { * } / \partial c _ { k i } > 0 , \ : \partial p _ { k i } ^ { * } / \partial c _ { k j } > 0 ( i \neq j )$ . This is because a company can raise the price of its own product when the price of the competitor's product increases because of higher cost.

Proposition 2. The equilibrium prices for the digital devices decrease in the level substitutability (σ).

Proposition 2 suggests that the equilibrium digital device prices decrease in $\sigma ,$ the index of substitutability between the two products. A high substitutability between the two devices, which could result from high similarity in terms of functionality and design, leads to a more competitive market and lowers the equilibrium prices. On the other hand, the more different the two products are, the higher the equilibrium prices will be.

The relationship between the prices of digital content and device is less clear. In order to analyze the relationship, we <sup>fi</sup>rst de<sup>fi</sup>ne $P M _ { i } = \frac { p _ { e i } - c _ { e i } } { p _ { e i } }$ as the pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin of digital content for <sup>fi</sup>rm $i ( i = 1 , 2 )$ . We can then prove the following result:

## Proposition 3.

(i) When the profit margin of digital content is lower than a certain level, i.e. $P M _ { i } \leq \frac { 3 } { 2 \eta } ,$ the equilibrium price of a digital device decreases in the price of the tied digital content, $i . e . , \frac { \partial p _ { k i } ^ { * } } { \partial p _ { e i } } \le 0 ( i = 1 , 2 ) .$

(ii) When the profit margin of digital content is higher than a certain level, i.e. $P M _ { i } > \frac { 3 } { 2 \eta } ,$ the equilibrium price of a digital device increases in the price of the tied digital content, i.e., $\frac { \partial p _ { k i } ^ { * } } { \partial p _ { e i } } > 0 ( i = 1 , 2 ) .$

The <sup>fi</sup>rst part of Proposition 3 suggests that when the pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin of electronic content is low, the prices of the digital content and its corresponding device move in opposite directions. The equilibrium price of digital device should increase when a <sup>fi</sup>rm lowers its content price. If the content price is increased, the <sup>fi</sup>rm can then afford to lower the digital price in order to maintain a sustainable demand.

When the pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin of electronic content is higher than a certain threshold, the prices of digital device and content can move in the same direction. Because of high pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin, <sup>fi</sup>rms can even lower their digital device prices with a lower content price in order to compete for a larger market share. As a result, even with a lower content price, the equilibrium prices for digital devices are also lower.

It is also interesting to see that the threshold pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin that determines the directions of price movement decreases in the elasticity of the digital content. It means when the demand elasticity is high, it is more likely that the prices for both the digital device and the content move in the same direction. With a high elasticity of demand, a small change of price will shift a big demand change. As a result, it is necessary for a <sup>fi</sup>rm to move both device and content prices together in order to compete in the marketplace.

Proposition 4. The equilibrium price of digital device increases in the price of the other company's digital content, i.e. $\frac { \partial p _ { _ { k i } } ^ { * } } { \partial p _ { e j } } > 0 ( i , j = 1 , 2 , i \neq j )$

Proposition 4 is quite intuitive in that a <sup>fi</sup>rm could increase its digital device price when the competitor's content price is high and the competition is relatively weak.

The optimal goal of the pricing strategies is to maximize a <sup>fi</sup>rm's overall pro<sup>fi</sup>t. Thus, it is interesting to examine the equilibrium prices from the perspective of <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Thus, we calculate the equilibrium pro<sup>fi</sup>ts of the <sup>fi</sup>rms by substituting the equilibrium prices into the pro<sup>fi</sup>t functions:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \pi_ {1} ^ {*} = \left[ \frac {1}{2 \sigma} + \frac {1}{3 (\eta - 1)} \Big (p _ {e 1} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} - p _ {e 2} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} \Big) + \frac {1}{3} (p _ {e 1} - c _ {e 1}) p _ {e 1} ^ {- \eta} \right. \\ \left. - \frac {1}{3} (p _ {e 2} - c _ {e 2}) p _ {e 2} ^ {- \eta} - \frac {1}{3} c _ {k 1} + \frac {1}{3} c _ {k 2}) \right] \\ \times \left[ \frac {\sigma}{3 (\eta - 1)} \Big (p _ {e 1} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} - p _ {e 2} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} \Big) + \frac {\sigma}{3} (p _ {e 1} - c _ {e 1}) p _ {e 1} ^ {- \eta} \right. \\ \left. - \frac {\sigma}{3} (p _ {e 2} - c _ {e 2}) p _ {e 2} ^ {- \eta} - \frac {\sigma}{3} c _ {k 1} + \frac {\sigma}{3} c _ {k 2} + \frac {1}{2} \right] \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \pi_ {2} ^ {*} = \left[ \frac {1}{2 \sigma} - \frac {1}{3 (\eta - 1)} \Big (p _ {e 1} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} - p _ {e 2} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} \Big) - \frac {1}{3} (p _ {e 1} - c _ {e 1}) p _ {e 1} ^ {- \eta} \right. \\ \quad + \frac {1}{3} (p _ {e 2} - c _ {e 2}) p _ {e 2} ^ {- \eta} + \frac {1}{3} c _ {k 1} - \frac {1}{3} c _ {k 2} \bigg ] \\ \times \left[ - \frac {\sigma}{3 (\eta - 1)} \Big (p _ {e 1} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} - p _ {e 2} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} \Big) - \frac {\sigma}{3} (p _ {e 1} - c _ {e 1}) p _ {e 1} ^ {- \eta} \right. \\ \quad + \frac {\sigma}{3} (p _ {e 2} - c _ {e 2}) p _ {e 2} ^ {- \eta} + \frac {\sigma}{3} c _ {k 1} - \frac {\sigma}{3} c _ {k 2} + \frac {1}{2} \bigg ] \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

The pro<sup>fi</sup>t for each company composes of two parts: one from the digital device $( \pi _ { k i } ^ { * } )$ , and the other from the digital content $( \pi _ { e i } ^ { * } ) .$ . The pro<sup>fi</sup>t functions for these two parts for <sup>fi</sup>rms 1 are as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \pi_ {k 1} ^ {*} = \left[ \frac {1}{2 \sigma} + \frac {\sigma}{3 (\eta - 1)} \left(p _ {e 1} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} - p _ {e 2} ^ {- (\eta - 1)}\right) - \frac {2}{3} (p _ {e 1} - c _ {e 1}) p _ {e 1} ^ {- \eta} \right. \\ \quad \left. + \frac {1}{3} (p _ {e 2} - c _ {e 2}) p _ {e 2} ^ {- \eta} - \frac {1}{3} c _ {k 1} + \frac {1}{3} c _ {k 2} \right] \\ \times \left[ \frac {\sigma}{3 (\eta - 1)} \left(p _ {e 1} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} - p _ {e 2} ^ {- (\eta - 1)}\right) + \frac {\sigma}{3} (p _ {e 1} - c _ {e 1}) p _ {e 1} ^ {- \eta} \right. \\ \left. - \frac {\sigma}{3} (p _ {e 2} - c _ {e 2}) p _ {e 2} ^ {- \eta} - \frac {\sigma}{3} c _ {k 1} + \frac {\sigma}{3} c _ {k 2} + \frac {1}{2} \right] \\ \pi_ {e 1} ^ {*} = (p _ {e 1} - c _ {e 1}) p _ {e 1} ^ {- \eta} \left[ \frac {\sigma}{3 (\eta - 1)} \left(p _ {e 1} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} - p _ {e 2} ^ {- (\eta - 1)}\right) \right. \\ \left. + \frac {\sigma}{3} (p _ {e 1} - c _ {e 1}) p _ {e 1} ^ {- \eta} - \frac {\sigma}{3} (p _ {e 2} - c _ {e 2}) p _ {e 2} ^ {- \eta} - \frac {\sigma}{3} c _ {k 1} + \frac {\sigma}{3} c _ {k 2} + \frac {1}{2} \right] \end{array}
$$

The pro<sup>fi</sup>t functions for the digital device and contents of <sup>fi</sup>rm 2 are:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \pi_ {k 2} ^ {*} = \left[ \frac {1}{2 \sigma} - \frac {1}{3 (\eta - 1)} \Big (p _ {e 1} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} - p _ {e 2} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} \Big) - \frac {1}{3} (p _ {e 1} - c _ {e 1}) p _ {e 1} ^ {- \eta} \right. \\ \qquad \left. - \frac {2}{3} (p _ {e 2} - c _ {e 2}) p _ {e 2} ^ {- \eta} + \frac {1}{3} c _ {k 1} - \frac {1}{3} c _ {k 2} \right] \\ \qquad \times \left[ - \frac {\sigma}{3 (\eta - 1)} \Big (p _ {e 1} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} - p _ {e 2} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} \Big) - \frac {\sigma}{3} (p _ {e 1} - c _ {e 1}) p _ {k _ {1} e} ^ {- \eta} \right. \\ \qquad + \left. \frac {\sigma}{3} (p _ {e 2} - c _ {e 2}) p _ {k _ {2} e} ^ {- \eta} + \frac {\sigma}{3} c _ {k 1} - \frac {\sigma}{3} c _ {k 2} + \frac {1}{2} \right], \\ \pi_ {e 2} ^ {*} = (p _ {e 2} - c _ {e 2}) p _ {e 2} ^ {- \eta} \bigg [ - \frac {\sigma}{3 (\eta - 1)} \Big (p _ {e 1} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} - p _ {e 2} ^ {- (\eta - 1)} \Big) - \frac {\sigma}{3} (p _ {e 1} - c _ {e 1}) p _ {e 1} ^ {- \eta} \\ \qquad + \left. \frac {\sigma}{3} (p _ {e 2} - c _ {e 2}) p _ {e 2} ^ {- \eta} + \frac {\sigma}{3} c _ {k 1} - \frac {\sigma}{3} c _ {k 2} + \frac {1}{2} \right] \end{array}
$$

## Proposition 5.

(i) When the profit margin of digital content is low, i.e. $P M _ { i } \leq \frac { 1 } { \eta } ,$ a firm's total profit and the profit for the digital device decrease in the price of the digital content, ${ i . e . \ } \frac { { \partial \pi } _ { i } ^ { * } } { { \partial p } _ { e i } } { < 0 , \frac { { \partial \pi } _ { k i } ^ { * } } { { \partial p } _ { e i } } < 0 \ ( i = 1 , 2 ) }$ (ii) When the profit margin of digital content is intermediate, i.e. $\frac { 1 } { \eta } \leq P M _ { i } \leq \frac { 3 } { 2 \eta } , ~ c$ firm's profits for both the digital device and content decrease in the price of the digital content, i.e. $\frac { \partial \pi _ { k i } ^ { * } } { \partial p _ { e i } } < 0 ,$ $\frac { \partial \pi _ { e i } ^ { * } } { \partial p _ { e i } } < 0 , ( i = 1 , 2 )$

(iii) When the profit margin of digital content is high, $i . e . P M _ { i } \geq \frac { 3 } { 2 \eta } ,$ a firm's total profit and the profit for the digital content decrease in the price of the digital content, i.e. $\frac { \partial \pi _ { i } ^ { * } } { \partial p _ { e i } } < 0 , \frac { \partial \pi _ { e i } ^ { * } } { \partial p _ { e i } } < 0 , ( i = 1 , 2 )$

From Proposition 5, we can see that when the price for digital content increases, the overall pro<sup>fi</sup>t drops. One major reason is that the demand is quite sensitive to content price.

There are different patterns of price impacts on <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, depending on the level of pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin of digital contents. When the pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin of digital contents is low, i.e. $P M _ { i } \leq \frac { 1 } { \eta } ,$ a lower content price leads to higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t for digital device as well as the overall <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>t. While it is unclear how a lower content price affects content pro<sup>fi</sup>t, it is intuitive that a lower content price can drive up the demand for digital device. The increase of overall <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>t could come from the increased pro<sup>fi</sup>t of digital device.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Adoption of digital contents and devices

While consumers are increasingly consuming digital contents such as music and videos, reading digital books represents a major shift of consumer reading behavior. The digital marketplace is competitive. For example, in addition to Amazon, companies such as Sony, Barnes and Noble, and Apple, have also tried to introduce digital e-book readers. As more books and other contents are being digitized, companies have used tying as a strategy to protect their distribution channel of the contents. Facing different platforms and high prices of the digital devices, consumers have to make adoption decisions. In choosing which device to adopt, consumers usually have to consider the present and future purchases of the contents. We take this into account and incorporate consumer surplus of the consumption of digital contents into the utility function. In this approach, we can capture the adoption process of the digital devices.

We <sup>fi</sup>nd that consumer utility on digital content and the price of digital device play key roles in determining consumer adoption decision. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, consumer utility is very sensitive to content price. As content price decreases, consumer surplus increases sharply because of the combined effects of low price and increased demand. Thus, it is important that <sup>fi</sup>rms price the contents competitively in order to attract consumers to consume digital contents. This result is consistent with the development in the marketplace as companies such as Amazon and Apple all try to sign deals with publishers to make sure digital books from other providers cannot be priced lower than in their stores. It has been reported that consumers are indeed very sensitive to the price digital books [16]. Therefore, companies have to do everything possible to ensure a low price of digital books, which is crucial to ebook adoption.

## 6.2. Competitive strategy

It is interesting to examine how <sup>fi</sup>rms compete to set prices for the digital devices. Based on the horizontal differentiation model, we derive equilibrium prices for the digital devices. In the model extension, we have also examined how quality differentiation affects device pricing.

High product differentiation, which infers a lower substitutability between the products, will lead to higher equilibrium prices. It is therefore imperative for <sup>fi</sup>rms to innovate and make their products as unique as possible. This can avoid direct competition between <sup>fi</sup>rms and soften pure price competition. While Amazon's Kindle and Banes and Noble's Nook are all ebook readers, Apple's iPad is a very different product. iPad not only can be used to read ebooks, but also allows users to play video games, movies, and browse webs. Compared to iPad, Kindle is much lighter. In addition, companies are offering products with different qualities. Amazon's Kindle has two models. The smaller one has a 6 inch display and the bigger one's display is 9.7 inches. iPad also comes with a number of con<sup>fi</sup>gurations, differing in storage size and 3 G availability, with signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different prices. Clearly, companies are using both horizontal and vertical differentiation strategies.

Our results also suggest that content price plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in affecting the price of the digital device. Content price can either increase or decrease the tied digital device price depending on the pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin of the digital content. When the pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin of content is low, with a lower content price, the price for the digital device should be higher. On the other hand, when the pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin for content is high, companies can afford to move prices for both the content and digital device together, making the products more competitive.

The implication for companies is that in pricing digital devices, they have to take both content price and the pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin of the digital content into consideration. In order to attract customers to adopt its platform, a <sup>fi</sup>rm gets to lower its content price as well as its digital device price. However, when the margin for content is low, the company is constrained. Therefore, it is important for companies to lower the cost of digital content. Usually the cost of digital content includes two parts: (i) licensing fee from publishers, and (ii) content distribution and delivery cost. Companies that can negotiate a low licensing fee and can distribute content ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently will have a great competitive advantage. Clearly, <sup>fi</sup>rms can have a huge economy of scale in providing the contents, and can price their products more aggressively later on. Therefore, for new entrants, they have to focus lowering the two types of costs in order to compete effectively.

## 6.3. Pricing and firm profits

Pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for most digital content providers come from both contents and devices. As prices change, pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for contents and devices may or may not move in the same direction. First of all, we <sup>fi</sup>nd <sup>fi</sup>rms should keep their content price low in order to compete effectively. The overall <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>t increases with a lower content price. Content price directly affects the demand for content and consumer acceptance of the digital device. As discussed earlier, a competitive content price is imperative to the success of a content provider.

Second, when pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin for content is low, with a low content price, a <sup>fi</sup>rm can increase its digital device price. The combined effects are that the pro<sup>fi</sup>t for digital device will increase and the overall pro<sup>fi</sup>t increases as well. Further, when pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin for content is medium, a lower content price leads to a higher device price. Overall, the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for digital contents and device increase. Subsequently, the overall <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>t increases. Furthermore, when pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin for content is high, a lower content price leads to high content pro<sup>fi</sup>t as demands for content and device increase. The overall <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>t also increases.

## 6.4. Limitations of the research

There are several limitations of this research. First, we focus on pricing strategies between two companies. There could be other important factors that affect pricing decisions. Factors such as the price of the physical books or CDs may affect the price decisions. Device providers also negotiate content price with publishers. In general, device providers set content prices. However, publishers are increasingly demanding the rights to set ebook prices. Second, we solved the equilibrium prices using a simultaneous game. In practice, companies may enter the market sequentially. It will be interesting to examine equilibrium prices using Stackelberg game. Third, we only consider a duopoly case. When a company has monopoly power, the pricing strategies may be very different, which we leave for future research.

## 7. Conclusions

As digital content providers tie their contents with digital devices, it is unclear the framework in pricing the digital devices. While some consumers perceive the devices as expensive, there also exists quite a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant price difference between different devices. The issues on consumer valuation and acceptance of the devices, and interaction of content price and device price are not all clear.

In this study, we develop a consumer demand model for digital device and digital content based on a constant elasticity demand function. In modeling consumer valuation of the digital device, we take consumer surplus on digital content into account. We further derive equilibrium prices for digital devices based on an oligopoly competition model with product differentiation. We analyze the equilibrium prices and how prices affect <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. We <sup>fi</sup>nd product differentiation and the level of product substitutability affect prices. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that content price plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in affecting the price of the digital device. Content price can either increase or decrease the tied digital device price depending on the pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin and demand elasticity of the digital content. In addition, we analyze how content and device prices affect their respective pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and the overall pro<sup>fi</sup>t of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Moreover, we extend the model to vertical product differentiation and <sup>fi</sup>nd vertical product differentiation and the level of product quality affect prices.

There are many interesting issues for future research. In this study, we model two competing <sup>fi</sup>rms move simultaneously. In the marketplace, there are incumbent and entrant. Thus, we could model the competition using a sequential game rather than a simultaneous game. Further, we could investigate whether tying enhances value for consumers and, if yes, how that changes demand functions and <sup>fi</sup>rm pricing strategies.
