# Winning Back Technology Disadopters: Testing a Technology Readoption Model in the Context of Mobile Internet Services

XIN XU, JAMES Y.L. THONG, AND KAR YAN TAM

ABSTRACT: We investigate how information and communication technology (ICT) service providers can win back disadopters of an earlier generation of technology when a new technology generation appears on the market. Integrating prior research on consumers’ defensive bias, knowledge accessibility, diffusion of innovation, and technology adoption, we developed a model to predict disadopters’ intention to readopt a technology. We postulate that the primary reason for disadoption moderates the impacts of both the drivers of readoption (perceived superiority, effort expectancy, price value of the new technology generation, and social influence) and the characteristics of prior usage experience with the disadopted earlier technology generation (duration of disadoption, tenure with the old generation, and usage intensity of the old generation) on readoption intention. We tested our technology readoption model in the context of mobile Internet services. Data were collected from 274 disadopters of an earlier generation of mobile Internet services before the advent of the third generation (3G) technology. The results supported most of our hypotheses. These findings have significant theoretical and practical implications, especially for firms interested in winning back technology disadopters. Finally, we present an agenda for further research into technology readoption.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: customer win-back, defensive bias, diffusion of innovation, knowledge accessibility, mobile Internet, technology adoption, technology disadopters, technology readoption, UTAUT.

The information and communication technology (ICT) industry rolls out new generations of technology at a very fast pace. These advancements in technology allow companies to provide better quality, more diversified, and more personalized services than before. This trend is most evident in the ICT service market for individual consumers, where service providers keep innovating their service offerings when new generations of technology are introduced. However, the rapid introduction of technology innovations, together with aggressive marketing strategies such as premium pricing, also raises the possibility of inferior performance and expensive billing of the technology-based services, and results in negative consequences. An example is the second generation (2G) of mobile Internet services based on Wireless Application Protocol (WAP). The slow connection and data transfer, tiny and blackand-white display, and expensive billing of 2G services led to the description of WAP as a “Wait-And-Pay” experience, which drove away many mobile Internet users.<sup>1</sup> We call these disenchanted consumers disadopters, that is, consumers who adopted an innovation early but finally gave up altogether on that category of technology—for example, mobile Internet services [26]. Here, a technology category is a general class of technology that satisfies a particular type of user needs. For instance, mobile Internet services is a category of ICT that meets users’ needs to use Internet technology on the move—anytime, anywhere. A technology category can have a number of technology generations—for example, the 2G, 2.5G, and 3G mobile Internet services. In our research, we define disadoption as the action of abandoning the whole technology class/category of mobile Internet services [26, 37]. That is, disadopters are not using any generation of the technology category during the period of disadoption—neither 2G nor 2.5G mobile Internet services. Thus, if consumers switch to another (earlier) generation of the technology, it is not considered a disadoption behavior by our definition.

Disadoption, though not very often seen on a large scale in the later stages of technology evolution, is nevertheless recurrent at the early stage of the evolution of technology categories. These disadoption phenomena are often labeled as “technology failures.” Some prominent examples in the mobile computing arena include the disadoption of the Apple Newton and Microsoft Tablet PC. As an early generation of personal digital assistant (PDA), the Apple Newton was finally abandoned by users mainly due to its poor performance (including short battery life, slow browsing speed, and poor handwriting recognition) and poor value for the money.<sup>2</sup> The Microsoft Tablet PC, on the other hand, was dismissed by users mainly due to the incompatibility of its user interface (UI) design with the mobile context of use—the Windows XP UI based on keyboard and mouse was not a good fit for tasks based on pen or stylus in a mobile setting.<sup>3</sup> Other examples of disadoption include Xybernaut Poma Wearable PC as an early generation of a wearable computing device and Microsoft Passport as an early generation of the federated identity management service.

More importantly, disadoption can have serious consequences for the particular ICT vendor or even the whole ICT industry sector. First, the ICT vendor can suffer a significant financial loss caused by the disadoption because it has invested a large amount of resources in research and development, manufacturing, and marketing of the technology. For instance, it was estimated that Apple sank US\$1 billion into the Newton project.<sup>5</sup> Second, disadoption can delay the diffusion of the innovation of an ICT category and thus the growth of the whole industry sector or its ecosystem. According to Hogan et al. [26], disadopters of ICT are the most damaging to lose because they are essentially those who adopt innovations early and can exert negative social influences on innovation diffusion [55]. Research has shown that disadoption accounts for 75 percent of the total revenue loss to a firm and can even slow the sales growth of the overall industry [26]. As demonstrated in the market for mobile Internet services, users’ disadoption of the 2G WAP services significantly delayed the proliferation of members in the ecosystem of mobile Internet—application developers, device manufacturers, content providers, and so on [77].

While disadoption can be disruptive to the ICT industry in the short run, it can spawn successful innovations in the long run. Learning from the failures, businesses can further innovate by refining existing technologies or even adopt revolutionary designs and introduce radical innovations. For instance, disadoption of the Apple Newton set the foundation for the Palm PDA later, and more recently, the iPhone and other smartphones. The failure of the Microsoft Tablet PC spawned the iPad and other tablet devices such as Microsoft Surface. Mobile Internet service providers learned from their experiences with the 2G WAP technology before introducing the 3G mobile Internet services. These ICT service innovations have been found to enhance strategic outcomes, such as brand equity and customer loyalty [81]. While the diffusion of the whole technology category may restart with the introduction of a new technology generation, ICT service providers still face challenges when they strive to draw disadopters back because, in an environment with rapid ICT evolution, the negative experiences with the older and inferior technology generation may still loom over disadopters’ minds and prevent firms from winning them back. Thus, understanding disadopters’ decisions regarding the readoption of a technology category is crucial not only to a particular firm but also to the success of all the firms that participate in the same market or the ecosystem such as the market/ ecosystem of mobile Internet services.

There is substantial information systems (IS) research on technology adoption with the main focus on adopters’ initial decisions and subsequent use [71, 73, 74, 75] and the related issues such as online attitude and sensing opportunities of innovation [45, 54]. The existing research on mobile Internet adoption has similarly focused on successful adoption rather than disadoption [2, 17, 27, 28, 29, 38, 64]. There is a lack of understanding of ICT readoption induced by a new technology generation. For instance, though the “Wait-And-Pay” experience of 2G WAP services has driven away many mobile Internet consumers, mobile Internet service providers continue to introduce technology innovations, such as the third generation (3G) of mobile Internet services to regain the lost customers. Thus, a pertinent question is: how can mobile Internet service providers win back disadopters of mobile Internet by introducing a new technology generation? There are some studies in the marketing literature on customer win-back. However, the technology element is usually assumed to be invariant and the focus is either on customer value [26] or on the pricing strategy [61]. As discussed above, while the characteristics of a new technology generation can serve to pull disadopters back to the technology diffusion trajectory, disadopters’ negative experiences with the older and inferior technology generation may still linger and prevent them from adopting the new technology generation. Under rapid ICT evolution, the influences of these negative experiences cannot be ignored because the time window between two successive technology generations is rather short. Thus, to effectively address technology readoption, both the drivers of readoption of the new technology generation and the negative experiences with the older technology generation should be incorporated in the research model. Accordingly, this study integrates research on consumers’ defensive bias, knowledge accessibility, diffusion of innovation theory, and technology adoption to investigate how the characteristics of prior experiences with the older technology generation and the drivers of readopting the new technology generation jointly influence disadopters’ readoption intention.

## Literature Review and Theoretical Foundations

## Marketing Research on Disadoption, Defection, and Customer Win-back

While customer acquisition and retention have received considerable attention from marketing researchers, customer win-back is an understudied topic [21, 60]. Lost customers can be categorized into two types—defectors who switch to another company and disadopters who give up altogether on the technology category (e.g., mobile Internet services [26, 37]). Disadoption—consumers’ action of abandoning the whole technology category—is much more damaging to firms than defection because it threatens the growth and even survival of the overall business sector.

Despite the significant value of disadopters, prior marketing research has so far addressed mainly the issue of winning back defectors. For instance, Stauss and Friege [60] offered a solution for customer regain management, which consists of three components—regain analysis, regain actions, and regain control. They identified prior usage experience and the reason for terminating the relationship as the main determinants of customer regain. Griffin and Lowenstein [23] expanded the framework by adding the impacts of defection-proof strategies on customer winback. Their work focused on the strategic and managerial processes for customer win-back with only a brief discussion of the customers’ decision making process. An exception is the research of Thomas et al. [61] on the impact of pricing strategy on customer win-back. They found that prior experience and price differences are the two key predictors of defector win-back. In summary, three important factors have been identified in the marketing literature on winning back defectors: (1) prior experience; (2) the reason for defection; and (3) price.

However, there are limitations in applying the findings of existing research to the context of technology readoption induced by a new technology generation. First, as discussed above, the marketing literature on customer win-back has focused only on defectors—that is, those who switch to a competitor offering a similar service/ product. The reasons for disadoption of the technology category and the variables that can influence readoption have not been thoroughly discussed [26]. Second, the role of innovation in improving the overall performance of the technology category has not been taken into consideration in prior research. While defection may be caused by the inferior service quality or the poor value for money of the service of a particular company [19, 34], a more important reason for technology disadoption is the poor performance of the entire technology generation. For example, disadopters of mobile Internet were so disillusioned with the slow speed and poor usability of mobile Internet services on the 2G network that they used sardonic phrases—such as “Worthless Application Protocol” and “Wait-And-Pay”—to describe 2G services based on the Wireless Application Protocol (WAP). Therefore, the introduction of a new technology generation that addresses the limitations of earlier technology generations may help in regaining disadopters. In summary, the marketing research based on winning back defectors may not be sufficient to explain winning back disadopters, and hence we will need to incorporate other theoretical foundations.

## IS Research on Technology Adoption and Continuance

Technology adoption and use has been studied at different levels—individual users [63, 69, 73], groups [56, 58], organizations [59, 62], and at multiple levels [41, 44, 82]. At the individual level, recent studies examined technology acceptance and use in more specific contexts [8, 9, 30] or based on new theoretical foundations such as neuropsychology [15]. While there is some research on bringing nonadopters along [7, 67] and building an inclusive digital society with disadvantaged users [31], there is little research on how to win back technology disadopters. Research on IS continuance and use either follows the expectation-confirmation framework [3] or combines it with the main predictors of technology adoption [76]. There is also research on postadoption behavior that examines the determinants of IS discontinuance [52]. However, our literature review suggests that there is a lack of understanding of the end users’ technology readoption decision.

Unlike technology adoption decisions that are mainly based on expectancies [73] and continuance/discontinuance decisions that are determined mainly by disconfirmation of expectations [4], technology readoption decisions involve both prospective evaluation of the new technology generation and the retrospective evaluation of the prior experience with the old technology generation. In general, consumer decision making is based on both new information and their prior knowledge [79]. In particular, a consumer’s innovation evaluation process involves both forming expectations about an innovation—the prospective evaluation of the new technology generation in our research context—and assessing satisfaction with an old product—the retrospective evaluation of the prior experience with an older generation in our research context [51]. This prior experience, which is presumably negative, not only led to users’ disadoption of the technology category, but it may also influence their decisions on whether to come back to the technology diffusion trajectory—that is, the readoption decision. For instance, after experiencing the inferior performance of 2G WAP services for too long or paying for the expensive bills once too often, the unhappy consumers finally decide to disadopt the mobile Internet services with negative feelings about their usage experience, for example, “mobile Internet is of poor performance” and/or “mobile Internet is not worth the money.” When a new technology generation is introduced, although its superior performance or the better value proposition may lead to positive reactions from disadopters, their negative experience with the earlier technology generations can still hinder the readoption decision—for example, “I should be cautious about subscribing to the 3G mobile Internet services.” Thus, theoretical exposition is needed to understand the impacts of both the new technology generation and the prior experience with the old technology generations on disadopters’ readoption intention.

## Conceptualizing Technology Readoption

Following the definition of disadoption to mean that users give up altogether on the technology category [26], we define readoption to mean that users restart the use of a technology category by adopting a new technology generation. While technology evolution and disadoption have been widely examined in marketing research [26, 37], the IS research on technology adoption [73] and continuance [3] has not explicitly distinguished the technology generation from the technology category/class. Our study examines the readoption decision regarding 3G mobile Internet services by consumers who had originally disadopted the earlier generations of mobile Internet technology—2G and/or 2.5G.

Research on innovation evaluation and categorization [39, 47, 48, 51] serves as the theoretical foundation for our conceptualization of readoption. Marketing research suggested that consumers always adopt the categorization approach first when evaluating an innovation [51]. That is, they first try to assign the innovation to an existing product, service, or technology category and evaluate the innovation based on their prior knowledge of that category [47]. Even when facing a really new innovation—one that creates or at least substantially expands a category [43, p. 6]—consumers still use categorization to learn about the innovation [39, 47]. In particular, the category to which an innovation is assigned is determined primarily by the first category label that consumers encounter and the degree of their prior knowledge of this category [48]. For instance, when evaluating a digital camera, the category label “camera” will lead consumers to assign it to the camera category (and not to other categories, such as computer peripherals) and make inferences about it based on their prior knowledge of film-based cameras [48]. Following this line of research, disadopters’ decision making about a new technology generation is a readoption decision because ICTs generally evolve by generation/version and the new generation, whether an incremental innovation or a significant/radical one (i.e., a really new innovation), is typically labeled as belonging to the same technology category, and disadopters will refer to their prior knowledge about that technology category. In our research context—3G mobile Internet services —we postulate that the decision is readoption because disadopters will automatically assign this technology generation to the mobile Internet services category due to the labeling effect, “3G,” and consider whether they will restart using the technology category, “mobile Internet services.” That is, the “3G” label will lead disadopters to categorize the new generation with the older technology generations—2G and 2.5G—in the same mobile Internet services category. While the 3G technology significantly expands the landscape of mobile Internet services, disadopters will still consider using 3G as restarting usage of mobile Internet services and their knowledge of their negative experiences with the previous generations of that technology category—the 2G and/or 2.5G technology—can still influence their readoption decisions.

## Theoretical Foundations

Following the above discussion, disadopters’ readoption decision is influenced both by their knowledge about the old generations, with which they have negative usage experiences, and the new technology generation. In the current study, we integrate research on consumers’ defensive bias, knowledge accessibility, technology adoption, and diffusion of innovation theory as our theoretical foundations to study the factors that influence disadopters’ readoption intention.

## Prior Negative Experience: Defensive Bias and Knowledge Accessibility

We posit that the prior negative experience with the old technology generation will generally have a negative impact on readoption intention due to consumers’ defensive bias (e.g., [13]). When a product has failed to meet its promises or consumers’ expectations, consumers will become cautious and hesitant when they evaluate related products or information. Moreover, consumers will develop distrust that also carries over from the failed product to related products [14]. In the context of technology readoption, consumers will generalize the negative experience with the old technology generation to their expectation of the new technology generation. Disadopters tend to be defensive when considering the new technology generation because they found that the older generations failed to meet their expectations and thus developed their distrust of and negative bias against the new technology generation.

We further postulate that the magnitude of the negative impact of prior experience on readoption intention depends on the accessibility of the prior experience—that is, the more accessible the negative experience when consumers evaluate the new technology generation, the greater the impact of defensive bias on readoption intention. We follow knowledge accessibility research [79] to identify and conceptualize three characteristics of prior usage experience—namely, duration of disadoption, tenure with the old generation, and usage intensity of the old generation—that influence the accessibility of the prior experience. Consumer behavior is largely influenced by prior experience that leaves traces in memory. Consumers’ decision making is thus categorized into memory-based (e.g., prior experiences), stimulusbased (e.g., new information), and mixed (a combination of the two) [40]. However, pure stimulus-based decisions are rare, and consumers most often make their decisions based on both prior experience and new information input [1]. In this regard, the prior experience in memory will play a key role in consumers’ decision making. Theories of memory suggest that the accessibility of prior experience in memory depends on the strength of its activation, the strength of its association with the retrieval cues, and the availability of the retrieval cues [49]. As consumers acquire knowledge by learning from their prior experience [1], four factors have been identified to influence the accessibility of knowledge in memory: the association between the knowledge and the retrieval cues, the recency and frequency of the activation and use of the knowledge, and the degree of elaboration or processing of the knowledge [79].<sup>6</sup> In general, in situations where the association between the prior experience and the retrieval cues is stronger, or when the prior experience is more recent and more frequent, or the mental processing of the prior experience by consumers is higher, then the magnitude of its impact on consumers’ decision making will be greater [79].

Consumers’ knowledge about past interactions with the old technology generation is presumed to be an obstacle to readoption in the current research. When disadopters make their readoption decisions, they access their memory for knowledge about their past interactions with the old technology generation and incorporate this knowledge into their decision making. For instance, the prior negative experience with either poor performance or expensive billing of the old technology generation will lead consumers to be skeptical of the new technology generation [50]. When the 3G mobile Internet was introduced, disadopters’ negative experience with 2G WAP services—because of slow connection, poor screen display, and expensive bills—will deter them from readopting mobile Internet services. In this case, the association between the prior experience and the retrieval cues— that is, the introduction of the new technology generation—can be readily established, because the new and the old technology generations belong to the same technology category [49]. The impacts of the prior experience thus depend on the other three factors that influence its accessibility in memory—recency, frequency, and amount of processing. Accordingly, we used the knowledge accessibility research to identify three characteristics of prior experience to influence consumers’ readoption decision in the current study— duration of disadoption, tenure with the old generation, and usage intensity of the old generation—which correspond to the recency, frequency, and amount of processing of the prior experience [79].

## Drivers of Readoption: UTAUT and Diffusion of Innovation Theory

Drawing from technology adoption research—in particular the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT [73])—and the diffusion of innovation theory, we identify and conceptualize four determinants of technology readoption intention—perceived superiority, effort expectancy, price value of the new technology generation, and social influence. As a synthesis of technology adoption research in organizational contexts, UTAUT identified four key factors—performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions—and four moderators—age, gender, experience, and voluntariness—related to the prediction of behavioral intention to use a technology and actual technology use. Performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and social influence were theorized and found to influence behavioral intention to use a technology, and behavioral intention and facilitating conditions determine technology use in UTAUT. Given readoption intention as the dependent variable in the current study, we adapt performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and social influence from UTAUT to our research context as drivers of technology readoption. We did not include facilitating conditions because they were found to influence only the actual behavior in UTAUT, while behavioral intention is the dependent variable in our model. In addition, we include one more driver—perceived price value as an innovation characteristic [65]—that is relevant to the consumer context.

In particular, we first extend the conceptualization of performance expectancy based on the notion of relative advantage in the diffusion of innovation (DOI) theory. DOI explains the process of adopting new innovations in a social system and has been applied in a broad variety of disciplines [55]. While the general model of DOI consists of many elements, two core components of this theory are particularly relevant to the current research—perceived innovation characteristics and innovation adopter category. Innovation characteristics mainly include relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, observability, and trialability [55]. Relative advantage is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being better than its precursor. This notion is the most relevant to our research context where disadopters of an old technology generation (the precursor) are evaluating the new generation (the innovation). Thus, though performance expectancy is originally defined as the degree to which an individual believes that using a technology will help him or her to attain gains in job performance, we replace it with perceived superiority of the new technology generation defined as the degree to which a consumer believes novel and superior contents are embodied in the new technology generation when compared with previous technology generations. Thus, perceived superiority essentially captures the relative advantage of the current generation of the technology relative to the one that was abandoned/ disadopted.

We adapt effort expectancy and social influence from UTAUT to the context of technology readoption. Effort expectancy is the degree of ease associated with consumers’ use of the new technology generation, which corresponds to the complexity of an innovation [55]. Social influence is the extent to which consumers perceive that important others believe they should use the new technology generation. Finally, in addition to the relevant UTAUT factors, we include one more driver of readoption—perceived price value—that is relevant to the context of mobile Internet services for consumers [27]. Price value is generally defined as consumers cognitive trade-off between the quality of the service/product and the monetary sacrifice to use it [16], which corresponds to the cost of an innovation [65].

## The Primary Reason for Disadoption: Diffusion of Innovation Theory

We propose that a disadopter’s primary reason for disadoption is the reflection of his/her innovation adopter category. According to the process model of innovation adoption, the primary reason for disadoption should be the disconfirmation of one or more innovation characteristics—such as relative advantages, complexity, compatibility, and so on—that serve as the key decision-making criteria of innovation adopters [55]. The key decisionmaking criteria or drivers of innovation adoption differs across the so-called innovation adopter categories [46]. Overall, there are five categories—innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards. Moore [46] identified the major decisionmaking criteria regarding innovation adoption of these five categories in the high-tech market. Generally, innovators are technology enthusiasts who appreciate a technology innovation for its own sake. Early adopters are visionaries who demand a fundamental breakthrough from the innovation. Both innovators and early adopters tend to be riskseekers as they can typically bear the financial consequences of a failed technology due to their affluence and they are the pioneers and opinion leaders in the high-tech market. Early majority consists of pragmatists who prefer to make incremental, measurable, and predictable progress. They care about other users’ endorsement of the innovation and are price-sensitive. Late majority consists of conservatives who are against discontinuous innovations and believe far more in very gradual progress and look for extensive endorsement from various sources, including the actual market success. Finally, laggards are skeptics who do not adopt new technologies in the marketplace [46]. In accordance with our definition of disadopters as consumers who adopted an innovation early but finally gave up altogether on that category of technology [26], the categorization of innovation adopters suggests that disadopters would consist of innovators, early adopters, and the early majority because of the timing of their action of adopting an innovation (the old technology generation in our research context).

According to Moore [46], innovators and early adopters will more emphasize the relative advantage of the innovation as they mainly value the technology and its performance. The early majority adopters will be more pragmatic and pay attention to complexity and costs. When an innovation adopter disadopts a technology, his/her primary reason for disadoption will reflect the innovation characteristics he/she values the most, which in turn reflects his/her innovation adopter category. For example, an early adopter of 2G WAP services may disadopt mobile Internet mainly because the technology failed significantly to meet his/her performance expectation. On the other hand, an early majority adopter’s primary reason for disadoption of 2G WAP services may be the lack of economic value of this technology generation. In the current study, we identify two major reasons for disadoption in the context of mobile Internet from our pilot study and the formal survey—poor performance and low economic value.<sup>7</sup> Poor-performance experience refers to consumers who encounter lower-than-expected performance when they use the old technology generation. For instance, consumers encountered poor performance when they used 2G WAP services, such as very slow network connection and data transfer speed, low quality of multimedia services, and poor usability [35]. Low economic value experience refers to consumers who must pay high-priced bills for limited benefits on a periodic basis for using the old technology generation. For example, consumers had to pay expensive monthly bills for the 2G WAP services that were not worth the money [35].

Following the rationale of innovation adopter categories, we postulate that disadopters who choose poor performance as their primary reason for disadoption are innovators and early adopters, while those who choose low economic value are the early majority. Conceptualizing the primary reason for disadoption based on innovation adopter categories has two implications for our research model. First, from the perspective of knowledge accessibility, the primary reason for disadoption is related to the prior experience that is most accessible in a disadopter’s memory, because it corresponds to the disconfirmation of the key innovation characteristics for the disadopter. For instance, when an innovator/early adopter faces a new technology generation, he/she will access his/her memory for knowledge about the technology category and most likely recall the scenarios when he/she encountered the poor performance of the old technology generation. Second, from the perspective of DOI theory, a disadopter, when evaluating the new technology generation, will assign more weight to the key innovation characteristics for his/her innovation adopter category that is reflected by his/her primary reason for disadoption. For example, a disadopter with low economic value as the primary reason for disadoption will pay more attention to the complexity and the costs of the new technology generation because he/she belongs to the early majority.

## Research Model and Hypotheses

Drawing from the theoretical foundations above, we develop our hypotheses in the research context of consumers’ readoption of mobile Internet services. This context sets the boundary conditions of our research model: (1) the two primary reasons for disadoption that we identified, and (2) the bundle-pricing and fixed-term contract strategies of mobile Internet service providers serve as the conditions for theorizing the impacts of tenure, usage intensity, and perceived price value on readoption intention. X

## Characteristics of Prior Experience with the Old Technology Generation

Following consumers’ defensive bias and knowledge accessibility research [79], the three characteristics of prior experience—duration of disadoption, tenure, and usage intensity—are hypothesized to influence readoption intention (see Figure 2).

Duration of disadoption is the length of time from when a consumer stopped using the old technology generation to when the new technology generation is introduced. With the impending release of a new technology generation, the duration of disadoption will have a positive effect on readoption intention due to the lower accessibility of prior negative experience. As suggested by the literature on knowledge accessibility, the strength of the impact of memory-based information on decision making depends mainly on knowledge accessibility—how easily and strongly consumers recall from memory information about an object and then incorporate that information into their decisions [79]. Crowder [12] acknowledged that when individuals retrieve information from memory, recent traces will be stronger than traces that are farther away. Experimental studies have demonstrated that concepts primed—that is, concepts with greater recency—can be more accessible than others and more influential in consumer judgment and decision [24, 25]. Thus, more recent experience will have a stronger impact on consumer decision making than that obtained from interactions a long time ago. In the context of technology readoption, the longer the duration of disadoption, the less accessible the negative prior experience will be, and thus there should be fewer obstacles against readoption when a new technology generation is being introduced. This low-knowledge-accessibility effect should apply to both types of negative prior experiences—poor performance and low economic value, because a longer duration of disadoption should make both types of experiences less accessible to all disadopters—that is, innovators, early adopters, and the early majority [79]. Thus, we do not expect the primary reason for disadoption to have a moderating effect on the relationship between duration of disadoption and readoption intention and hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1: Duration of disadoption will have a positive influence on readoption intention.

Tenure with the old technology generation is the length of time the disadopters had used the old technology generation. We posit that tenure has a negative impact on disadopters’ readoption intention that is moderated by the primary reason for disadoption. Since in the past, the disadopters generally hold the unsatisfactory prior experience in their memory, a longer tenure implies higher frequency of prior usage or bill payments, which reinforces memory of the respective negative experience and thus results in greater accessibility of the negative experience [12, 79]. Moreover, longer tenure can increase the level of distrust in subsequent innovations due to the psychological process of confirmation bias (e.g., [50]). As suggested by Boulding et al. [6], people are inclined to attend to and weigh more heavily information that confirms their prior knowledge and beliefs. The longer the tenure, the more times disadopters’ negative impression of the technology will be reinforced. For example, at the beginning of using 2G WAP services, potential disadopters initially formed a negative impression or belief due to either the low economic value or the poor performance. As time went by, the accumulation and reinforcement of the unpleasant experience would reach a threshold that led to disadoption. Because individuals have different levels of threshold, tenure will differ among disadopters. A longer tenure means a longer period of time and thus more chances for consumers to repeatedly confirm their initial negative beliefs, which strengthen their distrust toward the technology. This distrust in turn can negatively bias consumers’ judgment of the new technology generation and their readoption intention [6]. Hence, in general, tenure will have a negative influence on readoption intention.

Furthermore, we propose that the impact of tenure on readoption intention will be stronger for consumers who disadopted the technology due to low economic value—that is, the early majority. Both the accessibility of prior experience in memory and the magnitude of confirmation bias depend on the number of repetitions of the prior experience [12, 50, 79]. The two major reasons for disadoption imply different relationships between tenure and the number of repetitions of the two types of negative experience. The number of expensive bills paid depends directly on the length of tenure—that is, the longer the tenure, the greater number of payments for the expensive monthly bills because consumers typically pay a monthly subscription fee for mobile Internet services according to a binding contract. Thus, for each unit of increase in tenure (i.e., one month), a payment is made and the low-economic-value experience is enhanced in memory and becomes more accessible. The negative effect of this experience on readoption intention is even more significant for the early majority because they placed more emphasis on cost [46]. On the other hand, the accessibility of poor-performance experience depends on the number of interactions with the old technology generation. Because an innovator/early adopter is financially affluent and can bear the loss incurred by having a long tenure (i.e., a long-term binding contract) with light or even no service usage over time [46], a longer tenure does not necessarily imply more interactions with the old technology generation or greater accessibility of the poorperformance experience. Thus, the enhancement effect of tenure on the accessibility of the poor-performance experience for innovators and early adopters will be less than that of the low-economic-value experience for the early majority. This difference in accessibility of prior experience will moderate the magnitude of the impact of tenure on readoption intention. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2: The primary reason for disadoption will moderate the negative effect of tenure on readoption intention, such that the effect will be stronger for disadopters whose primary reason for disadoption was low economic value.

Usage intensity of the old technology generation is generally the frequency of usage per unit of time. Technology usage has been studied generally as a dependent variable in IS research (e.g., [75]). Usage can be classified into two dimensions— rate and variety [57]. Rate of use is the number of interactions with the object per unit of time. Variety of use refers to the different ways a product or technology is used. In the context of mobile Internet services, service innovations are enabled by each new technology generation and readoption is essentially a meta-decision that involves the evaluation of a portfolio of technology-based services. For instance, 3G technologies supported a variety of new mobile Internet services, such as mobile entertainment services, business/productivity services, and information and content services that were not available in previous technology generations (see Figure 3). Thus, both rate and variety of use are important in determining readoption intention.

We define usage intensity as a composite of both the variety of use and the rate of use in each unit of time and postulate that the primary reason for disadoption will moderate the impact of prior usage intensity on readoption intention. Consistent with the knowledge accessibility literature, usage intensity will also strengthen the negative impact of prior experience through the mechanism of information processing and elaboration [79]. Greater prior usage intensity may increase both the memory of the expensive monthly payments and the number of unsatisfactory interactions with the old technology generation, thus enhancing the accessibility of both types of negative prior experience through a greater degree of elaboration. However, research suggests that higher frequency of usage of product or service may increase consumers’ perceived payment equity, or justifiable pricing [5] because given the price paid, the greater the usage frequency in the life cycle of a product/service, the greater the utilization or exploitation. This is especially true in the context of mobile Internet services, where service bundling strategies are widely applied [81]. Consumers usually pay on a monthly basis a fixed amount of money for a service package, with which they can use a certain number (M) of service units from a portfolio of different types of services. Within the threshold of M, greater usage results in a more justifiable monthly payment [5]. For example, a consumer pays a flat fee of US\$50 to send/receive a maximum of 100 units of multimedia messaging services (MMS) per month. The more MMS he/she sends/receives per month within the limit of 100 units, the greater the price value of the service package. Thus, for the early majority who are price-sensitive, greater usage intensity may lessen the degree of low economic value they perceive from using the 2G WAP services.

In contrast, for innovators and early adopters who place more emphasis on an innovation being both new and superior, greater usage intensity will strengthen the accessibility of the negative experience to a greater extent. Research has shown that a greater amount of information processing can make prior knowledge more accessible in the process of belief formation and change [80]. Greater intensity of prior usage implies that innovators and early adopters process the different usage situations and the different types of services with greater elaboration, thus making the usage experience more accessible when making subsequent decisions, such as readoption. The various usage situations with services of poor performance in the past will lead these disadopters to be cautious and hesitant when the new technology generation is introduced. This caution and hesitancy will be stronger for innovators and early adopters because they are enthusiasts and visionaries of innovation who emphasize the importance of a technology being new and superior. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3: The primary reason for disadoption will moderate the negative effect of usage intensity on readoption intention, such that the effect will be stronger for disadopters whose primary reason for disadoption was poor performance.

## Drivers of the Readoption of the New Technology Generation

Integrating UTAUT and DOI theory, we develop hypotheses regarding the primary reason for disadoption moderating the impacts of four drivers of technology readoption intention—perceived superiority, effort expectancy, social influence, and price value of the new technology generation.

Perceived superiority of the new technology generation is generally defined as the degree to which a consumer believes novel and superior contents are embodied in the new technology generation when compared with previous technology generations. This construct captures the relative advantage of the current generation of the technology relative to the one that was abandoned/disadopted and represents the advancements in the new technology generation and the novel and superior contents as perceived by consumers in any aspects of the new technology generation, including both technological features and the new applications/services the new generation supports. For instance, in the case of 3G mobile Internet services, broadband data capabilities and improvements in device functionalities serve as the superior platform based on which a variety of new services, such as MMS, mobile video phone call services, and mobile business services are offered. In the current study, we essentially adapted the notion of relative advantage to the context of technology evolution within a technology class/category. Rogers [55] defined relative advantage as the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being better than its precursor. In general, this innovation can be either a new generation within the same technology category or a really new innovation that creates a new category and substitutes the one it supersedes. We defined perceived superiority based on the comparison between a new technology generation and previous generations within the same technology category. Thus, perceived superiority is the relative advantage of a new technology generation compared with previous generations. The readoption decision induced by a new technology generation mainly involves the comparison between the new and the older technology generations. The greater the perceived superiority of the new technology generation, the more likely disadopters will reverse their positions and adopt the new technology generation. Thus, perceived superiority of the new technology generation will have a positive influence on readoption intention.

Both effort expectancy and social influence are expected to have positive impacts on readoption. Effort expectancy is defined as the degree of ease associated with consumers’ use of the new generation of mobile Internet services in our research context [74], which corresponds to complexity as an innovation characteristic [55]. In general, the lower the effort expectancy of using a technology, the higher the adoption intention and use [73]. In our research context, the new technology generation may unleash new interfaces and functionalities that demand consumers’ effort to learn about and use the new services. This is especially true for mobile Internet services because of the small device screen and the mobile context of use. Thus, effort expectancy will have a positive effect on readoption intention. Social influence is the extent to which consumers perceive that important others believe they should use the new technology generation [74]. Consumers in general look for others’ opinions or advice—such as word of mouth—when they make purchase decisions. In the context of mobile Internet services, consumers’ friends, colleagues, and even family members serve as the sources of social influence that exert social pressures on consumers regarding the readoption of the new technology generation.

Perceived price value is defined in general as consumers’ cognitive trade-off between the quality of the service/product and the monetary sacrifice to use it [16]. In our research context, this construct characterizes the pricing strategies taken by ICT service providers and corresponds to the cost of service use as an innovation characteristic [65]. When introducing a new technology generation, the service providers can either adopt a premium-pricing strategy that emphasizes profit or a penetration-pricing strategy that targets market share, which in turn results in consumers’ perceived price value of the technology-based services. Perceived price value has been found to have a positive effect on consumer evaluation of services/ products and purchase intention [16]. Thus, we include perceived price value as a positive determinant of disadopters’ readoption intention.

We propose that the primary reason for disadoption will moderate the impacts of the above four factors on readoption intention. Following the notion that the primary reason for disadoption is a reflection of innovation adopter category, those disadopters whose primary reason for disadoption is poor performance are innovators and early adopters, and those whose primary reason for disadoption is low economic value are the early majority. According to Moore [46], innovators and early adopters are enthusiasts and visionaries about technology. When they face a new technology generation, they more emphasize the novelty and the superiority—that is, the perceived superiority of the new technology generation. On the other hand, the early majority is composed of pragmatists who pay more attention to the cost issues related to the new generation—that is, effort expectancy (complexity) and price value (monetary cost). Thus, disadopters whose primary reason for disadoption is poor performance will assign more weight to perceived superiority, while those whose primary reason for disadoption is low economic value will focus more on effort expectancy and price value. Moreover, DOI research has suggested that innovators and early adopters are the pioneers and opinion leaders in the high-tech market, who are not subject to social influence. On the other hand, among the early majority are pragmatists who look for other users’ endorsement of the innovation. Thus, social influence will not have a significant impact on the poor-performance group of disadopters but will affect the low-economic-value group. Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 4: The primary reason for disadoption will moderate the positive effect of perceived superiority of the new technology generation on readoption intention, such that the effect will be stronger for disadopters whose primary reason for disadoption was poor performance.

Hypothesis 5: The primary reason for disadoption will moderate the positive effect of effort expectancy on readoption intention, such that the effect will be stronger for disadopters whose primary reason for disadoption was low economic value.

Hypothesis 6: The primary reason for disadoption will moderate the positive effect of social influence on readoption intention, such that the effect will be significant for disadopters whose primary reason for disadoption was low economic value but not for disadopters whose primary reason for disadoption was poor performance.

Hypothesis 7: The primary reason for disadoption will moderate the positive effect of perceived price value on readoption intention, such that the effect will be stronger for disadopters whose primary reason for disadoption was low economic value.

## Research Methodology

## Sample and Data Collection

An online survey was conducted in Hong Kong one month before the introduction of the 3G mobile Internet services. This setting presented a unique opportunity to conduct a natural field study of the views of disadopters toward the newer technology generation of mobile Internet services. The questionnaire was administered via an e-government portal that is freely accessible to all residents of Hong Kong. We put up a banner advertising the survey on the portal with various incentives, such as LCD TVs, as lottery prizes. To identify disadopters and record their past usage experience, we first emphasized in our survey that stopping usage of mobile Internet services meant not using any mobile Internet services from any mobile service providers. We then asked the survey participants if they were using any mobile Internet services at the time of the survey. If not, we asked them if they had used mobile Internet services before. In this way, we identified the disadopters. We then asked when these disadopters started and stopped using mobile Internet services and their average usage frequency of different services.<sup>9</sup> At the time of the survey (i.e., one month before the introduction of 3G in Hong Kong), there were already many reports about the coming 3G technology and 3G service plans in the mass media and the 3G mobile operator’s marketing campaigns. In fact, Hong Kong was among the first in the world to introduce 3G. In addition, we also provided a brief introduction to the 3G services in our survey. Thus, the disadopters had adequate knowledge about 3G when they answered the survey questions.

A total of 305 disadopters of previous generations of mobile Internet services responded to the online survey. Twenty respondents were excluded from further analysis because they did not answer the questions about their prior experience with mobile Internet services. A question about the respondents’ primary reason for disadoption of mobile Internet servicers was included. We asked the respondents to select their primary reason for disadoption from three options: poor performance, expensive billing, and other reasons. Poor performance and expensive billing (i.e., corresponding to low economic value) were found to be the primary reasons for disadoption from both our pilot study and industry research reports. Only 11 respondents to our survey chose “Other reasons.” Of these 11 responses, 5 did not provide any reason; 3 stated that they were leaving Hong Kong; and 3 indicated both performance and expensive billing as reasons for disadoption. These responses were also excluded from the subsequent data analysis. The remaining sample of 274 respondents was distributed almost equally between male (51 percent) and female (49 percent). The average age of the respondents was around 29 years and their average annual income was around HKD 100,000. The respondents had an average of 10 months of experience with mobile Internet services before they discontinued their usage.

## Measurement of Constructs

The measurement of constructs is presented in Appendix A. Both duration of disadoption and tenure of prior use were measured in months. The measure for usage intensity incorporated both the rate and variety of use. Respondents were provided with a portfolio of different mobile Internet services (i.e., SMS, MMS, ringtone and logo downloads, mobile games, browsing websites, and mobile e-mail) and asked to indicate their average daily usage frequency of each service using seven-point Likert scales. The usage frequency responses were then summed to arrive at a respondent’s overall usage intensity. As the data for the three prior experience characteristics did not follow a normal distribution, we applied a natural log transformation to them. The items for perceived superiority of the new technology generation were adapted from Gatignon et al.’s [20] four items measuring perceived radicalness of technology innovation and used seven-point Likert scales. We added three more items that were related to the performance, diversity, and value of the services supported by the new technology generation. The seven-point Likert scale’s items used to measure perceived price value were adapted from Dodds et al. [16]. Finally, two items using seven-point Likert scales and one item using a sevenpoint semantic differential scale were used to measure consumers’ readoption intention in the case of 3G mobile Internet services. The primary reason for disadoption was measured using a single item with three possible values: expensive billing, poor performance, or other reasons. We indicated to the respondents that “poor performance” referred to the performance of mobile Internet services that was below their expectations, while “expensive billing” referred to monthly bills for mobile Internet services that were higher than they expected. We conducted a pilot test among 30 university staff who had experience with mobile Internet services. As a result, one item (PS4) of the perceived superiority scale was dropped due to its ambiguity and problematic psychometric properties. We then proceeded to conduct the main survey.

## Instrument Validation

Partial least squares (PLS), a structural equation modeling (SEM) statistical technique, was employed to both validate the measurement instrument and test the research model. We used PLS mainly because of the exploratory/early theoretical development nature of our study [11, 21]. In addition, LISREL was employed both for evaluating the measurement invariance between the poor-performance group and the low-economic-value group and for analyzing the model with common method bias controlled [42].

We assessed measurement validity in two ways. First, we assessed how each item was related to its corresponding construct by examining the factor loadings. One item (PS7) of the scale for perceived superiority was dropped due to high cross-loadings with other constructs. We reran the factor analysis and found that all the remaining factor loadings were significant and above 0.70 and the cross-loadings were below 0.30 (see Table 1). Thus, all items loaded more highly on their own construct than on others. Second, we compared the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) with the corresponding correlations among latent variables (see Table 2). Each construct was related more strongly to its own items than to other constructs. Further, the AVEs exceed 0.50 for all the multi-item constructs [18]. Collectively, these results supported the convergent and discriminant validity of the measurement instrument. Finally, from Table 1, the composite reliabilities of all multi-item constructs were greater than 0.70 for the whole sample, and also for the two subsamples, indicating good measurement reliability [18].

Because we tested our hypotheses by comparing path coefficients between the two subsamples classified by the primary reason for disadoption—the poorperformance group and the expensive-billing group—we employed multigroup analysis in LISREL [33] to evaluate the measurement invariance between the two groups. In particular, we evaluated two levels of equivalence—the configural equivalence and metric equivalence. Configural equivalence means that the measurement model across groups has the same factor pattern. Metric equivalence implies that factor loadings across groups are equal—that is, the equality of the scale intervals on which the latent variables are measured. We evaluated both configural and metric equivalence between the two groups. To assess the configural equivalence, we evaluated the global goodness-of-fit index—comparative fit index (CFI)—by estimating the same confirmatory factor analysis model between the two groups without any other constraints imposed ([10]; see also [31]). The CFI was 0.9956, supporting the configural equivalence. Next, we examined the change of CFI when imposing the equivalence constraints on factor loadings across the two groups. The resulting CFI was 0.9949 with a change of 0.0007, which is smaller than the 0.01 threshold [10], thus supporting the metric equivalence between the two groups.

Finally, we performed marker variable analysis to evaluate the common method bias (CMB) in our data following Malhotra et al. [42]. From the correlations among the measurement items for the latent variables, we chose the second-smallest positive correlation (r = 0.03) between two measurement items (SI3 and PV3) as a more conservative estimate. After partialing out this value from all the correlations, we estimated our model using LISREL with both the original correlation matrix and the revised correlation matrix [42]. The results showed no significant differences between the original path coefficients and the reestimated coefficients, thus indicating that CMB was less of a concern in our study. We also examined CMB by including a method factor in our model and compared the factor loadings [36, 53], and arrived at the same conclusion (see Appendix B). We thus proceeded to examine the results for the structural model.

## Results

To test the hypotheses for the moderation effects, we performed t-tests via a multigroup analysis with PLS.<sup>10</sup> We controlled for the effects of a number of demographic variables on readoption intention in our model—age, gender, and income. Age was measured in years. Income was measured in units of HKD 1,000. Gender was measured as 1 for male and 0 for female. Table 3 shows that most of the hypotheses in our technology readoption model (i.e., H1, H2, H3, H6, and H7) were supported. The model accounted for 43 percent of the variance in disadopters’ readoption intention in the overall sample, and explained 45 percent and 42 percent of the variance in disadopters’ readoption intention in the two subsamples—the loweconomic-value group and the poor-performance group—respectively.

The duration of disadoption had a positive impact on readoption intention for the overall sample $( b = 0 . 2 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and the two subsamples (the low-economic-value group: $b = 0 . 2 9 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ ; the poor-performance subgroup: $b = 0 . 2 4 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ supporting hypothesis H1. This finding is consistent with the theory of knowledge accessibility—a longer duration of disadoption will diminish disadopters’ ability to access their memory of the negative prior experience with the older technology generation, which in turn will increase the likelihood of their readoption of the new technology generation. The PLS multigroup analysis found that the difference in the path coefficients between the two subsamples was not significant at the 0.01 level. Hence, a longer duration of disadoption reduces accessibility of consumers’ prior negative experience to the same extent, regardless of their primary reason for disadoption.

The longer the disadopters, who abandoned the technology due to low economic value, had been using the older technology generation, the less likely they would be to consider adopting a new technology generation. This finding is consistent with our prediction based on the rationale of knowledge accessibility and innovation adopter category. The longer the tenure, the more accessible the prior experience of expensive billing is for the early majority who care more about the cost. However, longer tenure does not necessarily produce more frequent poor-performance experience for innovators and early adopters who place more emphasis on the new technology and its superior performance and are affluent enough to bear the expensive bills. Thus, while a longer tenure naturally means a greater number of monthly payments of expensive bills for the early majority, it does not necessarily imply a greater frequency of poor-performance usage for innovators and early adopters.

An unexpected result was the positive impact of usage intensity on readoption intention for the overall sample $( b = 0 . 2 8 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and the two subsamples (the low-economic-value subgroup: $b = 0 . 2 0 , p < 0 . 0 1$ ; the poor-performance subgroup: $b = 0 . 3 7 , \ p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . The notion of use diffusion [57] may offer a potential explanation. Shih and Venkatesh [57] suggested that there is an important postadoption stage of innovation diffusion—the use diffusion, defined as a process where consumers integrate the technology into their lives and use the technology frequently in various ways. An intensive usage pattern—high rate and/or greater variety of use —represents a deep integration of the technology into one’s life, and has a positive impact on the adoption of subsequent technology innovations [57]. Similarly, although disadopters abandoned mobile Internet services previously, a high intensity of prior usage may indicate that they had already found mobile Internet services necessary in their lives, but disadopted it due to poor performance or low economic value. When a new technology generation is introduced, disadopters with intensive prior usage can benefit more from readoption because innovators and early adopters can enjoy the superior performance and the early majority will enjoy the greater value offered by the new technology generation, because they all tend to use a greater variety of applications at higher rates. Thus, usage intensity may have a positive impact on readoption intention. The PLS multigroup analysis found that the difference in the path coefficients was significant at the 0.01 level. While the moderation effect is significant and supported, the direction of the main effect is opposite to that in H3. The impact of usage intensity on readoption intention was greater for the poor-performance group than for the low-economic-value group, because innovators and early adopters more emphasize continuing their prior usage pattern so as to maintain or deepen the integration of the new and superior technology generation into their lives.

Perceived superiority of the new technology generation was consistently the strongest predictor of readoption intention for the overall sample $( b = 0 . 4 2 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and the two subsamples with similar magnitude of impacts (the low-economic-value group: $b = 0 . 4 5 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1$ ; the poor-performance group: $b = 0 . 4 0 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . The PLS multigroup analysis found that the difference in the path coefficients between the two subsamples was not significant at the 0.01 level. Thus, H4 was not supported. One possible reason for the insignificance across subsamples may be that superior performance of the new technology generation also leads to greater price value, given a fixed price level. Thus, the early majority assigns weight to the superior performance similar to that of innovators and early adopters in order to achieve even higher price value of the mobile Internet services.

The difference in the coefficients for effort expectancy across the subsamples was not significant (the overall sample: $b = 0 . 2 3 ; p < 0 . 0 1 ;$ the low-economic-value group: $b = 0 . 2 1 ; p < 0 . 0 1$ ; the poor-performance group: $b = 0 . 2 5 ; p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Thus, H5 was not supported. The insignificant difference may be attributed to the smallform factor on mobile devices. Given the small screens of the mobile devices, ease of use is of great importance to the usability of mobile Internet services [70]. Even innovators and early adopters will assign weight similar to that of the early majority on effort expectancy when evaluating the new generation of mobile Internet services.

The impact of social influence on readoption intention in the low-economicvalue group is significant $( b = 0 . 1 5 ; p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , but not in the poor-performance group $( b = 0 . 0 9 ; p > 0 . 1 0 )$ . The PLS multigroup analysis found that the difference in the path coefficients between the two subsamples was significant at the 0.01 level. These results provide empirical support for H6. This is because, as DOI theory suggests, innovators and early adopters are pioneers and opinion leaders who take risks in their evaluations of the innovation when making readoption decisions. On the other hand, the early majority put weight on external endorsement of the innovation and are subject to social influence [46, 55]. In the context of mobile Internet services, the innovators and early adopters are members of the poor-performance group, while the members in the low-economic-value group are mainly the early majority.

Finally, perceived price value of the new technology generation was also a significant predictor of readoption intention in the overall sample $( b = 0 . 2 0 , p <$ 0.001) and the two subsamples (the low-economic-value group: $b = 0 . 2 9 , p < 0 . 0 1 ;$ the poor-performance subgroup: $b = 0 . 1 5 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Multigroup comparison showed that price value was more of a concern for disadopters who were price sensitive—the low-economic-value subgroup—than those who were performance-oriented—the poor-performance subgroup—supporting H7. Again, this result is consistent with the rationale of innovator adoption categories where the early majority place more emphasis on the cost aspect of the new technology generation as reflected in the price value, while innovators and early adopters care less about the cost or loss incurred by readopting the new generation.

## Discussion

## Theoretical Implications

Our work contributes to the IS literature on technology adoption [66, 72] and continuance (e.g., [4]) by examining an understudied problem—disadopters readoption intention when facing the introduction of a new technology generation (e.g., 3G) within the same technology category (e.g., mobile Internet services). There are three main theoretical implications from our study. First, we integrate research on consumers’ defensive bias, knowledge accessibility, UTAUT, and the DOI theory to study technology readoption. We theorize that the prior experience with the old technology generation forms barriers (i.e., the defensive bias) against technology readoption, and the three characteristics of this experience determine the knowledge accessibility and, therefore, negatively influence readoption intention. On the other hand, drawing from UTAUT and DOI theory, we identify the four key drivers of technology readoption. Our empirical analysis found significant direct impacts of both prior experience with the old technology generation and key characteristics of the new technology generation on disadopters’ readoption intention. In the technology adoption literature, usage experience mainly plays a moderating role in models of technology acceptance and use following the rationale that usage experience improves users’ knowledge structure about the target technology (e.g., [68, 73]). In research on IS continuance (e.g., [3, 76]), usage experience plays an implicit role in the model and is benchmarked against expectation to serve as the foundation for the disconfirmation process. The readoption context differs from both technology adoption and IS continuance in that the disenfranchised consumers have prior negative experience with the old technology generation that led to their disadoption decision. This negative experience is accessible in disadopters’ memory and casts doubts and distrust of the new technology generation in the minds of disadopters. This study thus highlights the importance of incorporating the role of prior experience with the old technology generation in addition to the key characteristics of the new technology generation when studying consumers’ technology readoption decisions.

Second, we provide insights into prior experience with the old technology generation by identifying and theorizing about the impacts of three characteristics of prior experience—duration of disadoption, tenure with the old technology generation, and usage intensity of the old technology generation—on readoption intention based on the theory of knowledge accessibility [79]. In the marketing literature on winning back defectors, the technology is invariant and consumers mainly defect for a lower price. The impacts of prior experience on consumers’ decision making were theorized based on different psychological mechanisms. For instance, in the marketing literature on defection, the impact of duration of defection on restart decisions was posited based on the status quo bias theory [61]. However, in our research context, technology is under rapid evolution and negative prior experience is the result of the low economic value or the poor performance of the previous technology generation. Following the knowledge accessibility literature [79], we have theorized and found that the duration of disadoption has a positive effect on readoption intention because the accessibility of prior negative experience diminishes with elapsed time since disadoption, and that tenure has a negative effect on readoption intention because the accessibility of prior negative experience increases with the length of time the previous technology generation is used. While we have also theorized that usage intensity has a negative effect on readoption intention based on the rationale that greater usage intensity increases the degree of elaboration and thus the accessibility of the prior negative experience, we have found a more profound and positive impact of usage intensity that may be explained by the use diffusion literature [57]. A higher usage intensity can indicate that the earlier technology generation is previously highly integrated into the disadopter’s life, which increases the likelihood of readoption of the new technology generation. Our research thus contributes to the marketing literature by highlighting that the nature of prior experience and the mechanisms through which it influences technology readoption are different from previous research on customer defection and regain [61].

Finally, a major contribution lies with the examination of the moderating role of disadopters’ primary reason for disadoption in our research model. Based on the theoretical foundation of the innovation adopter category, we have identified two primary reasons for consumers’ technology disadoption—poor performance and low economic value—and suggested that a disadopter’s primary reason for disadoption is a reflection of his/her innovation adopter category. We postulate that disadopters due to poor performance of the previous technology generation are innovators and early adopters, while disadopters due to low economic value are the early majority. Accordingly, we have theorized that the impacts of tenure, usage intensity, perceived superiority, effort expectancy, social influence, and perceived price value on readoption intention are moderated by the primary reason for disadoption. This is because different categories of innovation adopter have different decision-making criteria—innovators and early adopters focus more on the novelty and superiority of the new technology generation, while the early majority are more pragmatic and pay more attention to the practical costs and social endorsements. We found that the primary reason for disadoption can moderate the impacts of tenure, usage intensity, social influence, and price value. We also provide explanations for the two unsupported hypotheses in the context of mobile Internet services. Our theoretical development of the moderating effects of the primary reason for disadoption enriches the understanding of the saliency of different factors in affecting different disadopters’ readoption decision. Overall, our research model contributes to DOI theory by studying the readoption decisions after the (dis) confirmation stage of the process of innovation adoption (cf. the process model of innovation diffusion in Rogers [55]).

## Practical Implications

Our research has direct relevance to practice, specifically ICT service providers operating in an industry that is subject to the technology acceleration phenomenon—that is, given the cost of production, the overall performance of ICT improves exponentially over time [78]. Winning back disadopters of ICT is important not only to individual ICT service providers but also to the whole ICT industry because its success can influence the diffusion of an entire technology category, such as mobile Internet services. Our study provides strategic implications for winning back the ICT disadopters. First, if there was a major failure in a previous technology generation, such as the WAP services based on the 2G technology, ICT service providers should differentiate the new technology generation from the previous generation by emphasizing the superiority of the new generation compared with the previous generation. Our study has shown that the superiority of the new technology generation as perceived by disadopters has the greatest impact on their readoption intention. To win back disadopters, marketing communications such as mass media coverage and advertising for the new technology generation need to focus on the superiority of the new technology generation, such as the revolutionary technology advancements and the new and upgraded services so as to differentiate the new technology generation from the previous generation.

Second, our study highlights the impacts of negative usage experience in affecting consumers’ decision making about the new technology generation. Consumers with different prior experience with the technology will exhibit different levels of likelihood of readoption. ICT service providers can use information about the prior experience of disadopters, by examining their past usage history, to identify those with the highest likelihood of readoption. For instance, in the case of mobile Internet services, disadopters with short tenure, long duration of disadoption, and intensive usage frequency are more likely to readopt the new generation of mobile Internet services.

Third, ICT service providers need to take into account the perceived price value and social endorsements of the services supported by the new technology generation, especially if they target the mass market—for example, the early majority. If they overprice the services based on the new technology generation, disadopters will not be encouraged to come back, thus slowing down the diffusion of the new technology generation. Similarly, social endorsements such as positive word of mouth and possibly other social media marketing tools should be used to influence the mass market. Finally, firms need to pay attention to the disadopters’ primary reason for disadoption when designing their win-back strategies. By segmenting the disadopters according to their primary reason for disadoption, such as those who are price-sensitive versus those who are performance-oriented, ICT service providers can then differentiate their marketing strategies accordingly to win back individual segments.

## Agenda for Future Research

While the IS literature has seen many studies on initial technology adoption (e.g., [73]) and technology continuance (e.g., [3]), none has investigated technology readoption. Here, we provide a research agenda for the technology readoption arena by identifying a number of limitations or boundary conditions to this study that provide opportunities for future research.

First, while we have presented a conceptualization of technology readoption in our research context, that is, the case of 3G as a new technology generation within the general technology category of mobile Internet services, we acknowledge that there may be other situations to which the labeling effect and the effect of consumers’ prior knowledge do not apply. This can happen when the new “generation” is totally different from previous generations in the original technology category, such as CD-ROM as a new “generation” of external storage [22]. In such cases, disadopters may not frame their decisions regarding the new technology as readoption. There is a stream of IS research that examines the impacts of various design features on consumer/user decision making [32]. Researchers can follow this line of research to examine how the design features of a new technology generation and/or marketing communications can differentiate it from other generations to the extent that disadopters will not frame their decision as technology readoption.

Second, in the current study we have focused on consumers’ readoption of mobile Internet services considering the ubiquity of mobile Internet today and its great potential to change consumers’ lives [29]. The two primary reasons for disadoption —poor performance and low economic value—are chosen based on our pilot study and industry research reports in the context of mobile Internet services. Moreover, large-scale disadoption typically will not occur in the later stages of technology evolution (e.g., 3G to 4G). Rather, disadoption phenomena are usually caused by “technology failures” that mainly occur with early generations of a technology category. We conjecture that as consumers’ defensive bias mainly applies to the context of product failures, the negative impact of prior experience also applies in the “technology failure” context—a significantly inferior technology generation that fails to meet users’ expectation. This serves as one boundary condition of our research model. We thus call for future research that adapts our research model to the more general context of a technology class with multiple technology generations. In general, ICTs are evolving by generation/version as in hardware/software platforms (e.g., the different generations of CPU and OS) and applications (e.g., the different generations/ versions of ERP software). The research context and thus the boundary conditions can vary—for example, expensive billing or the more general pricing issue may not be a primary reason for disadoption when the context changes from consumer readoption to employee readoption of mobile Internet services. However, the essence of our research model—that the primary reason for disadoption interacts with prior usage experience and beliefs about the new generation to predict individuals’ readoption intention—can be adapted to study end users’ readoption of other technology categories.

Third, we only examined two primary reasons for disadoption—expensive pricing and poor performance of the prior technology generation. Future research can examine other reasons for disadoption, such as the lack of facilitating conditions and the incompatibility of the innovation with users’ value and experiences. Moreover, users may have multiple primary reasons for disadoption—such as both poor performance and incompatibility of mobile Internet services. Future research can examine the differences in the decision making between disadopters with multiple reasons and those with a single primary reason. Furthermore, one of the reasons for disadoption in our study—low economic value—is more associated with mobile Internet services that are priced on the basis of usage. In other contexts, bundling may be the primary pricing strategy adopted by ICT service vendors. That is, a technology and its services are bundled and priced at the point of sale—for example, an iPad along with warranty services and other information services such as cloud services. Both usage-based billing and bundling are mainly under the influence of service providers’ pricing strategy and thus a reflection of the “cost” component of the price value construct. In the current study, we have operationalized this cost component with the term “expensive billing” in the context of mobile Internet services that are priced on a usage basis and have conceptualized it as “low economic value.” This term as used is consistent with the findings from our pilot study and industry research reports at the time of our survey. In other contexts, such as when technologies (and their accompanying services) are bundled and priced together—for example, the price for a technology platform such as an iPad—the price at point of sale is a more accurate reflection of the out-of-pocket cost paid by consumers. Future research needs to contextualize the reasons for disadoption according to the pricing strategy adopted by service/platform providers.

Finally, the current study has focused on the disadoption issue and examined factors that affect readoption intention. Future research can incorporate consumer defection—that is, when consumers switch to another service provider—in the research model to fully understand how usage experiences with an inferior technology generation influence consumers’ subsequent decision making. For instance, disadopters may first switch service providers before disadopting and they may also switch providers when a new superior generation is introduced. This research will offer nuanced insights into the diffusion of service innovation [37] and may offer managerial implications for service innovation strategies of ICT providers. Moreover, attitude strength and attitude change can also be incorporated into the research model to examine how disadopters update their attitude toward the technology category when a new technology generation is introduced, based on which their readoption intention is formed.

In summary, we have provided an agenda for research into readoption to end our discussion. We recommend: (1) examining the conditions for the readoption decision to occur; (2) generalizing the technology readoption model to other contexts, such as other technology categories; (3) doing an in-depth analysis to identify and categorize the reasons for disadoption in the relevant research context; (4) incorporating other endogenous mechanisms into the research model, such as including defection in the model or examining the dynamics of attitude change in users’ decision-making process regarding readoption.

## Conclusion

We investigated how to win back technology disadopters. To this end, we developed and tested a technology readoption model by integrating consumers’ defensive bias, knowledge accessibility research, UTAUT, and DOI theory. This approach takes into account both prior experiences with the previous technology generation (i.e., duration of disadoption, tenure with the old generation, and usage intensity of the old generation) and new information about the new technology generation (i.e., perceived superiority, effort expectancy, social influence, and perceived price value). The empirical results confirmed our conjecture that the primary reason for disadoption, as a reflection of a disadopter’s innovation adopter category, can moderate the impacts of prior usage experiences with the older technology generation and the beliefs about the new technology generation on readoption intention. Our proposed technology readoption model can serve as a basis for future research in the general field of winning back technology disadopters, an important but understudied area of technology adoption research.

## Appendix B. Assessment of Common Method Bias

Following Podsakoff et al. [53], we introduced a method factor with all observed indicators as the manifest variables [36]. We then examined the issue of common method bias by examining the statistical significance of factor loadings of the method factor and comparing the two variances of each manifest variable—one explained by its substantive factor and the other by the method factor. The squared method factor loading is the percent of indicator variance explained by the method factor while the squared substantive factor loading is the percent of indicator variance explained by the substantive factor. If the method factor loadings are insignificant and the indicators’ substantive variances are substantially greater than their method variances, then we can conclude that common method bias is unlikely to be a serious concern. As shown in Table B1, the average substantive variance of the indicators is 0.566, while the average method-based variance is 0.005. The method variance is insignificant with a small magnitude. Therefore, CMB is unlikely to be a serious issue in the current study.
