# Business–IT alignment maturity of companies in China

Leida Chen

College of Business Administration, Creighton University, Omaha, NE 68178, USA

A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 22 September 2008 Received in revised form 3 August 2009 Accepted 25 August 2009 Available online 19 September 2009

Keywords: Business–IT alignment Cross-culture comparison Instrument validation IS in China Strategic IT alignment

## A B S T R A C T

Business–IT alignment has been consistently ranked as the number one concern of IT and business executives. Maintaining the alignment has been found to lead to stronger business performance, higher perceived value of IT and improved IS strategic planning; therefore addressing the issue is both important and timely. The economic growth in China in the last decade has led to greater availability of IT and its widespread use as a critical resource. Despite the increasing interest in Chinese IS issues by both researchers and practitioners, empirical research focusing on human- and organization-related IS issues in China is scarce. Using data collected from 130 business and IT executives from 22 companies in China, 11 of which were multinationals operating in China, we explored several questions in the area of business–IT alignment. An instrument designed to measure the six dimensions of business–IT alignment maturity was cross-validated using a sample of companies in China. Then, the instrument was refined to develop a measurement method that offered better reliability and validity in the context of Chinese companies. The relationship between the alignment maturity dimensions and IS strategic alignment was then examined. Finally, the alignment maturity of companies in China was assessed to provide a snapshot of business–IT alignment in China. A comparison between the 11 Chinese domestic companies and 11 multinational operating in China provided insight into the different IT practices of the two types of companies. Implications for researchers and practitioners are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Business–IT alignment addresses how much IT and business systems are in harmony with one another. Alignment results in a partnership between the CIO and CEO in developing and achieving their strategies and goals. Achieving alignment leads to improved organizational performance [16,19]. Among the few studies that focused on developing an instrument for measuring business–IT alignment, we found that the study of Sledgianowski et al. [18] was the most comprehensive. Their instrument was based on the strategic alignment maturity (SAM) model developed by Luftman [12], which consisted of six maturity factors: communication, competency, governance, partnership, technology scope, and skills. However, other than the work performed in order to develop the instrument, it has not yet been cross-validated. Thus, our study’s first objective was to cross-validate the instrument using a sample of 130 business and IT executives from 22 companies in China.

IT was introduced into China a little more than two decades ago. With China’s growing economy and participation in global competition, IT has become an increasingly critical resource. However, IS management has traditionally focused on technical and operational issues. Similarly, published research on IS issues in China has focused disproportionally on system-related issues, and empirical studies on organizational and strategic issues are scarce [10]. Furthermore, lessons learned from Western organizations may not be readily applicable in China.

As a second objective, we hoped to contribute by adapting the instrument to develop a measure of business–IT alignment that offered high reliability and validity.

IS strategic alignment is to the fit between business and IS strategies orientation. Its importance is regularly discussed in the literature. It has been credited with helping an organization achieve superior business performance while executing business strategies and improving the return on IT investment [2]. Despite its importance, no prior research has been conducted to study of the antecedents of IS strategic alignment. We intended to fill this void by investigating the relationships between the alignment maturity constructs and IS strategic alignment. We also hoped that the study would provide operational recommendations to organizations that intended to improve their IS–business strategic alignment.

The final objective of our study was to assess the current state of business–IT alignment of companies in China using the validated instrument. China is the largest recipient of foreign direct investment. This results in the presence of many multinationals who have experience in several national contexts and with superior organizational capabilities. A comparison of business–IT alignment between the multinationals operating in China and the domestic companies can help us to identify structural factors that may contribute to the difference.

In summary, our study investigated the areas of business–IT alignment and IS research in China by answering four research questions:

RQ 1: Is the instrument developed by Sledgianowski et al. for measuring business/IT alignment reliable and valid for samples other than those used to develop their instrument?

RQ 2: Is the instrument reliable and valid for measuring business– IT alignment in the context of companies in China? How must the instrument be adapted and refined for Chinese companies?

RQ 3: What is the relationship between IS strategic alignment and the alignment maturity constructs (communications maturity, competency/value measurements maturity, governance maturity, partnership maturity, technology scope maturity, and skills maturity).

RQ 4: What is the level of business–IT alignment maturity of companies in China? Do the business–IT alignment maturity levels differ between Chinese domestic companies and multinationals operating in China? If they do, how do they differ? What may have caused the difference?

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Business–IT alignment

Business–IT alignment is an adaptive process rather than an event. Achieving alignment requires an ongoing effort of strategic planning, goal realignment, and implementation of best practice in supporting and shaping business strategies. While most of the literature has focused on the fit between a firm’s IT and business strategy, the alignment issues need to be addressed at strategic, tactical, and operational levels. Strategic alignment helps meet an organizations’ future IT needs; tactical alignment allows the organization to allocate its IT resources effectively; operational alignment ensures effectiveness and efficiency of IT in supporting the organization’s operations on a daily basis. Organizations that have a higher level of alignment are more likely to achieve higher levels of performance and perceived business value from IT.

The importance of alignment has been widely recognized and well documented; however, many companies today are still misaligned, and this has led to disastrous failures, including canceled or redundant projects, systems that do not meet the needs of the business, dissatisfied business users, and high IS costs. As Holland and Skarke [7] suggested, improvement in alignment in many companies today can be attributed to C-level executives’ better understanding of the value of IT and their active participation in IT decision-making.

The top reasons for misalignment included lack of a close working relationship between IT and business, lack of effective prioritization of IT needs, inability of IT to meet its commitments, IT’s lack of understanding of business, lack of senior executive support of IT, and lack of leadership in IT management. The five factors that affected the social dimension of IT alignment identified by Reich and Benbasat [15] included shared domain knowledge between business and IT executives, IT implementation success, communication between IT and business executives, connections between business and IT planning processes, and strategic business plans. Hussin et al.’s [9] research of 256 UK manufacturing firms found that the major factors that influenced alignment were IT maturity, technical IT sophistication, and the CEO’s software knowledge. By applying the theory of knowledge integration, Kearns and Sabherwal [11] provided a comprehensive framework that examined the relationship among integration behavior, alignment, IT planning and projects, and business impact of IT. Their study asserted that business–IT alignment was influenced by IT managers’ participation in business planning and business managers’ participation in strategic IT planning. Furthermore, alignment was found to be positively associated with the success of IT planning and projects, which led to a positive business impact of IT.

Luftman proposed a framework called strategic alignment maturity (SAM) to assess the extent to which business and IT functions align. Focusing on examining organizational behaviors associated with alignment, SAM provides organizations with a tool to evaluate the maturity of their strategic choices and alignment activities and identify areas in which they can achieve a higher level of alignment. Subsequent research included a benchmark study by Luftman [13] that used SAM at 50 Global 2000 companies to create a benchmark for business–IT alignment and identify specific recommendations for improving alignment. Sledgianowski et al. developed and validated an instrument to measure maturity of IT business alignment based on SAM. It uses the six categories in Table 1 for assessing alignment. Based on how the organization scores in these categories, one of the five levels of strategic alignment maturity is assigned to the organization. In order to study the relationship between IS strategic alignment and the six alignment maturity constructs, we intended to examine the research model of Fig. 1. Currently, no comprehensive framework has been used to examine the factors influencing a firm’s IS strategic alignment. The rationale behind our research model is that separate studies have examined links between IS strategic alignment and communication [1], integrated planning behaviors [8], IT resource allocation [17], organizational culture [3], and shared domain knowledge [4]. These constructs are consistent with the alignment maturity constructs; therefore, we believe that by examining the relationship between the six alignment maturity constructs we will develop a comprehensive model of the antecedents of IS strategic alignment.

## 2.2. IS issues in China

As China’s economy continues to grow, enormous investment in IT is being made by companies to boost the productivity of their employees. IT has become widespread in China in the last two decades [5] and become a transforming agent. China identified the development of the IT industry as one of its priorities in the fiveyear economic plan of 2001. But while access to IT has increased, the management of IT still remains a relatively new discipline and China is experiencing a shortage of experienced and skillful IT managers. Even today, empirical studies on human- and organization-related issues in China are scarce. Therefore, studies on organizational issues will provide practical guidelines to improve IS management in Chinese organizations.

Chen et al. identified the three key issues in IS management in China. They were high-level managers’ lack of awareness of IT, technology competence of IS departments, and the role of IS department. IT applications in companies in China are starting to move away from traditional transaction processing systems. Largescale IS such as ERP and supply chain management are broadly implemented today. The success of these systems requires a highlevel of coordination and collaboration between IT and business units. IT issues a country faces are likely to be influence by its economic structure, national culture, political/legal environment, and technological status. The unique Chinese cultural context suggests that studying IS issues in China requires new perspectives. Using Hofstede’s four cultural dimensions, prior research has underscored the distinction between the Chinese and western cultures, with the Chinese culture demonstrating a high power distance, low individualism, average masculinity, and low uncertainty avoidance. Martinsons and Westwood’s [14] research on Chinese cultural characteristics was more specific to IS management. They found that Chinese managers were characterized by reliance on informal information, centralized decision-making, high context communication, and intuitive decision-making. Consequently, Western designed IS which embody Western business practice may result in a misfit when used in traditional Chinese management systems. Western-inspired and developed IT may require drastic reconfigurationin order tobeimplemented and adopted successfully in China [20]. Also, IS management lessons from developed countries may not be readily applicable in China. Therefore, one of our research objectives was to adapt the instrument for measuring alignment maturity for companies in China.

Foreign multinationals operating in China have the advantage of passing the IS management know-how from the parent companies to Chinese domestic companies. Companies we surveyed included both Chinese domestic ones and foreign multinationals operating in China. These two types of companies operate in the same economic and cultural context, yet they may differ significantly in their structures, management styles and IS management practices. Comparing alignment between these two types of companies will shed light on the impact of organizational factors on alignment.

## 3. Research methodology

The data was collected using a survey technique. The research constructs were measured using multi-item scales adapted from the SAM framework.

## 3.1. Instrument development

The instrument consisted of 39 items, listed in Appendix A. The respondent replied to each item by choosing one of the five statements that best described his or her organization. The five statements were designed to represent a continuum from the first signifying the lowest level of alignment maturity and the fifth statement signifying the highest. The respondents were also asked to rate their perception of the organizations’ business–IT alignment maturity by choosing one of the five maturity levels in the SAM framework.

The instrument for measuring strategic IS alignment is shown in Table 2. The items and evaluation method were adapted from Hofacker [6] and Hussin et al. Using a 5-point Likert scale, business executives were asked to indicate the extent to which they agreed with the statements related to business strategy, and IT executives from the same companies were asked to indicate the extent to which they agree with the statements related to IT strategy. A score for IS strategic alignment were then derived from the difference between the ratings from the business and IT executives using the matching perspective recommended by Hofacker.

To ensure the content validity of the questionnaire, the questionnaire was reviewed by two researchers in the area of business–IT alignment. They were asked to comment on the validity of the items and suggest additions, deletions and modifications. Feedback from these experts resulted in minor wording revisions. The questionnaire was then pilot tested with three business executives and three IT executives for readability. All reported that the questionnaire was clear and understandable.

## 3.2. Procedure

The sampling units of this study were IT and business executives, who knew the most about their organizations’ alignment maturity. While alignment is an important issue to any organization regardless of its size, we decided to limit our study to large corporations. First, many items in the instrument dealt with the relationship between the organization’s business and IT divisions, etc. Large corporations were more likely to have established IT organizations and formalized strategic planning and assessment processes. Second, including only large organizations allowed us to control the effect of organizational size when comparing alignment maturity of the companies.

Two hundred fifty IT and business executives from 30 companies in Beijing were contacted, and 154 of the solicited executives from 22 companies agreed to participate in our study. The survey was administered to these IT and business executives. In order to obtain a comprehensive and accurate assessment of the organization’s IT business alignment maturity, at least three business executives and three IT executives from each organization were asked to respond to the questionnaire. The job titles of the respondents ranged from ‘‘Division Manager’’ to ‘‘CEO’’, with the majority being ‘‘General Managers’’ or ‘‘Directors.’’ Twentyfour responses were eliminated from the analysis due to incompleteness, leaving a final sample size of 130.

We also realized that alignment maturity might be exhibited slightly differently in various national settings. Therefore, the second question was, ‘‘How can the current instrument be adapted and refined for the context of companies in China?’’ The new instrument, which consisted of 21 items, showed significant improvement in goodness-of-fit to the data than that of the original instrument.

A review of the deleted items in Model 3 revealed some interesting insights about IS management in companies in China. The great majority of the deleted items could be categorized into three groups: business and IT integration (COMM2, COMM6, GOV6, GOV7, PART5, and SKILLS6), strategic importance of IT (COMP8 and SCOPE1), and culture of change and innovation (SKILLS1 and SKILLS3). The deletion of these items suggested that the companies in China were suffering from lack of business and IT integration, low strategic importance of IT, and lack of a culture of change and innovation.

The third question explored the relationship between the maturity constructs and IS strategic alignment. COMM, COMP, GOV, PART, and SCOPE were found to significantly influence a firm’s IS strategic alignment, suggesting that firms that scored high in these constructs were more likely to align their IS strategic orientation with firm strategic orientation.

The fourth question dealt with assessing alignment maturity of companies in we found that the average alignment maturity of these companies fell between level 2 and level 3 on the maturity scale. One Chinese domestic company even failed to achieve level 2 maturity, 17 companies claimed to achieve level 2 maturity to some extent, and only four organizations, all of which were multinationals operating in China, claimed to have achieved level 3 maturity.

The data from the 22 large companies not only provide a snapshot of the state of business–IT alignment of companies in China but do serve as the starting point of a benchmark against which companies in China can compare their alignment maturity. In addition, no difference was found between the business and IT executives’ perception of their firms’ business–IT alignment maturity.

A comparison was made between the 11 Chinese domestic companies and 11 multinationals; it indicated that there was a significant difference in alignment maturity. Overall, multinationals were found to have a higher level than Chinese domestic companies. A closer analysis found that multinationals scored significantly higher in the COMM, GOV, and SKILLS constructs.

Multinationals operating in China, although highly autonomous, benefit from the IS management know-how and business management practices of their experienced parent companies. Also, the disparity suggests that differences in corporate and management culture of the two types of companies may influence alignment.

Chinese domestic companies should focus on improving in areas where significant deficiencies were identified: facilitating open communication between IS and business units, breaking down traditional hierarchies to establish effective IS governance structures, and developing cross-functional skills for IT professionals. They should also study how multinationals have overcome cultural hurdles and successfully aligned their business with IT.

## 6. Implications and limitations

For researchers interested in business–IT alignment, this study cross-validated an instrument for assessing alignment. The crossvalidation gives researchers confidence in its effectiveness in measuring alignment allowing researchers to study the antecedents, moderator variables, and dependent variables of alignment. The study also enhanced our understanding of the antecedents of IS strategic alignment by examining the relationships between the maturity constructs and ISA. The study adds to the body of Chinese IS research that is empirical-driven and focuses on organizationally oriented issues. For practitioners, the instrument can be used to assess alignment of companies effectively. In addition, the ability to measure a firm’s alignment maturity allows firms to understand the challenges they faces in improving IS planning and management. Our study has created a preliminary benchmark by which Chinese IT managers can compare their organization’s alignment to the best. Also areas of deficiencies that are inhibiting companies in China from achieving alignment were identified.

There were two limitations to the study. First, while 130 executives participated in the study, the sample of 22 companies is relatively limited. Although the sample represents a wide range of industries, they are mainly from the service and technology sector. The sample only includes a small number of companies in other sectors such as manufacturing and construction, which represent a significant portion of the companies in China. Second, the study excluded environmental factors that might affect the alignment maturity of companies in China. For example, external cultural, economic, regulatory, political and industry factors might have inhibited these companies from achieving higher levels of alignment. Indeed, the focus of our study was largely internally oriented.
