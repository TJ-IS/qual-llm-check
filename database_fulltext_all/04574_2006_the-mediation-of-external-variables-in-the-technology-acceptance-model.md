# The mediation of external variables in the technology acceptance model

Andrew Burton-Jones <sup>a,\*</sup>, Geoffrey S. Hubona <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> MIS Division, Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia, Vancouver, BC, Canada V6T 1Z2 <sup>b</sup> Department of Computer Information Systems, J. Mack Robinson College of Business Administration, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303, USA

Received 14 August 2003; received in revised form 27 January 2006; accepted 11 March 2006 Available online 9 August 2006

## Abstract

TAM specifies a pathway of technology acceptance, from external variables to beliefs, attitudes, and system usage. We tested one of its assumptions that the ‘perceived ease-of-use’ and ‘perceived usefulness’ constructs fully mediate the influence of external variables on usage behaviors. Using a survey of 125 employees of a U.S. Government agency we found, contrary to the normally accepted assumption, that external variables could have direct effects on usage behavior over and above their indirect effects. We also found that TAM is significantly and consistently better at predicting frequency than volume of usage. <sup>#</sup> 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Technology acceptance model; External variables; Individual differences; User acceptance; IS usage

## 1. Introduction

In the past decades, there have been large investments in information technology; in 1999, for example, the IT budget of Federal Express was reported to be US\$ 1.4 billion [3]. If companies are to make a return from their IT investments, the new systems must be utilized effectively. Unfortunately, countless systems are never used to their full potential; many simply remain ‘‘unexplored, rejected, or forgotten’’ [45]. Understanding the determinants of system use is therefore a cornerstone of IS research [21].

Of the suite of theories that explain technology acceptance, TAM [17] appears the most popular (see Fig. 1). It explains IT usage as a function of a four-stage process:

 external variables (user training) influences user beliefs about using the system;

 user beliefs influence their attitudes about using a system;

 user attitudes influence their intentions to use a system;

 user intentions determine level of usage of the system.

TAM has been validated over a wide range of systems [35,37], has proven to have reliable and valid constructs [14,23], and routinely explains up to 40% of usage intentions and 30% of systems usage [42].

A meta-analysis of TAM literature identified two aspects of TAM that have received little attention: the role of external variables and the role of different usage measures [38]. Based on a detailed analysis of 22 articles from six journals, Legris et al. found that only 60% of TAM studies considered external variables and there was ‘‘no clear pattern with respect to the choice of the external variables considered.’’ The authors argued that it is important to study external variables, because they are the ultimate drivers of usage. They also found that many studies examined intentions-to-use systems rather than usage. The objective of our research was therefore to provide theoretical and empirical evidence of the role of external variables and usage measures.

Our objective was to test a key tenet: that each construct in the chain from external variables to usage mediates prior constructs in the model. For example, for attitude ‘‘a key principle . . . is that attitudes fully mediate the effects of beliefs on intentions . . . much of its value is foregone if it only partially mediates the impact of beliefs’’ [18]. TAM uses two belief constructs: perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease-of-use (PEOU). However, early tests found that attitude did not fully mediate PU. Other studies confirmed this and ‘‘TAM was refined to exclude attitude . . . because it did not fully mediate the effect of perceived usefulness on behavioral intention’’ [57].

Few studies have tested whether beliefs fully mediate external variables. TAM assumes that external variables predict usage only through their effect on PU and PEOU. While many have tested the relative influence of difference external variables on them [28,31], or the effects of external variables on usage intentions in the absence of PEOU and PU [16], few have tested TAM’s assumption that they fully mediate the effect of external variables on usage. Agarwal and Prasad [2] found that the effects of five external variables on users’ attitudes and usage intentions were fully mediated by PU and PEOU. Similarly, Venkatesh found that PEOU fully mediated six external variables and argued that ‘‘this represents an important theoretical contribution since there has been limited research . . . testing the core assumption of mediation of the effect of other constructs on intention by [PEOU and PU]’’ [58]. However, both of these efforts studied usage intentions rather than usage. We felt that it is too soon to conclude that ‘‘external variables are fully mediated by PEOU and PU.’’

Replicating and extending [12], we developed theoretical and empirical evidence for a direct effect of external variables on self-reported usage over and above the mediated effect of PEOU and PU.

## 2. Theoretical model

Agarwal and Prasad studied the effect of five individual user differences: age, division in an organization, level of education, prior experience, and participation in training. They found that each of these was fully mediated by PU and PEOU. They also asserted that ‘‘if these results are further confirmed in subsequent work, . . . researchers [could] construct simpler models that exclude individual differences altogether.’’ Their results run counter to several theories of social psychology. Therefore, our intent, following [10], was to test the generalizability of their findings. Fig. 2 shows our model.

We tested the relationships between individual difference external variables (system experience, level of education, and age), TAM belief constructs (PU and PEOU), and IS usage. We tested two versions of this model, assessing both: (1) the influences of the individual differences on the usage measures as fully mediated by the TAM belief constructs (i.e. the dashed lines) and (2) the (unmediated) direct influences of these individual variables on usage (i.e. the solid lines).

## 2.1. The direct effects of individual differences on usage

Despite existing evidence, several studies in IS suggest that direct effects are possible. For example, in a study of MBA students, Igbaria et al. [34] found that several variables linked to the ‘‘perceived behavioral control’’ construct had direct effects on use. They recommended that the relationships be tested in the field to determine if they were generalizable. Taylor and Todd [54,55] did not explicitly examine the mediation hypothesis but did find that the strength of the relationships differed, depending on users’ level of experience. In [22], task characteristics were found to have a significant direct effect on IT use. While they did not test its indirect effect, their results suggested a potential unmediated effect.

According to social psychology theories, an individual’s behavior is not just driven by evaluative beliefs and attitudes, but also by subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and habits (see Table 1).

The theory of reasoned action (TRA) proposes that attitudes and subjective norms independently affect behavioral intentions [25]. Subjective norms are beliefs that people who are considered important by the individual think that he or she should behave in that way. Recent field studies confirm the direct effect of subjective norms [30,36,59]. The theory of planned behavior [5] states that individual differences could also directly affect usage through their effect on users perceived behavioral control, which, according to [4], directly affects intentions and behavior over and above attitudes and subjective norms. Several studies support the direct effect of perceived behavioral control on intended and/or actual usage [13,55]. Finally, theories of habit formation suggest that behavior is often affected by habits rather than intent [48]. TAM assumes that individuals rationally calculate the costs and benefits of their actions. However, a recent metaanalysis found that habits directly affected behavior over and above beliefs, attitudes, norms, behavioral control, and intentions [46]. In IS, the strength of habits was confirmed by [60], who found that, after 3 months using an IS, the only significant predictor of later use was prior use; other factors were insignificant.

Prior studies have used age as a proxy for users’ level of perceived behavioral control [41]. Morris and Venkatesh [43] argued that age reduced perceived behavioral control due to lower self-efficacy and cognitive skills (see also [11]). They argued that age increased the effect of subjective norms due to older workers’ greater need for affiliation. Literature also suggests that habits can become stronger with age, because routines become difficult to change [29,40]. Overall, the paths through habits and behavioral control suggest that age negatively affects usage. The direction of the path through subjective norms would depend on the organizational context. P1a is, thus, stated in a nondirectional form.

Mathieson et al. argued that education and system experience could reflect users’ internal capabilities (for example, knowledge of the technology and task). While PEOU may partially mediate this, the theory of planned behavior suggested that education and experience should have a positive direct effect on usage. System experience should also have an additional effect through habit formation. The longer that someone has used a system, the more likely it will become a routine tool and users will not need to assess its PU or PEOU each time they use it, i.e. use became habitual. Increased education and experience should also empower the user, reducing the effect of social norms on their behavior. The direction of the effect would depend on whether subjective norms were favorable or unfavorable towards use of the particular technology. Because the direct effect through perceived behavioral control and habits would be positive but the direct effect through subjective norms could be positive or negative, P1b and P1c are stated in a nondirectional form.

## 2.2. The indirect effect of individual differences on usage

An advantage of testing whether individual differences have a direct effect on usage is that they are expected to have an indirect effect on usage via PEOU and PU. Thus, TAM’s belief constructs can be potent but incomplete mediators [9].

Agarwal and Prasad argued that individual differences imply differences of user learning. TRA, behavioral psychology [51], and social learning theory [8] all support the view that differences in learning lead users to form different feelings about the consequences of using IT [56].

Drawing on attitude theories, Taylor and Todd argued that increased experience with IT allows a stronger relationship to form between beliefs, attitudes, and IT use. Past research has found a large positive effect of system experience on performance using a system [24,50]. Dishaw and Strong found a positive relationship between experience and PU. Agarwal and Prasad also found a positive relationship between similar system experience and PEOU.

Zmud [64] suggested that users’ level of education influenced their success in using IT. Empirical studies also support the benefit of education [20]. This should lead to a positive association between education and PU. Greater education should also increase PEOU by improving users’ attitude and reducing anxiety [33,39], and by providing a stock of knowledge that enables more effective and adaptive learning [7].

Finally, there have been consistent proofs of relationships between users’ age and IT in the literature. Older workers tend to resist change [44] and are, therefore, expected to perceive new IT as less useful, finding it more difficult to learn and use unfamiliar technology [27]. Even if they are willing to adopt new IT, older workers may be less able to appreciate or understand it. We predicted that PEOU and PU were accurate but incomplete mediators. This was not supported empirically by Agarwal and Prasad, although they acknowledged that it was unclear, because they used a proxy for age (job tenure).

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Research method and data collection

Our respondents were 125 staff and professional employees of a large Government agency in the eastern US. A questionnaire solicited users’ beliefs and usage behaviors with respect to two applications: email (MicroSoft’s cc:mail<sup>TM</sup>) and word processing (Corel’s WordPerfect<sup>TM</sup>). Respondents were screened to ensure that they had used the target software applications. Of the 125 subjects, 122 had used the email application and 118 had used the word processing application, resulting in 240 usable responses.

We tested our research model using these processing applications for three reasons. First, they have been examined in prior studies so this allows comparison with prior results. Second, Agarwal and Prasad recommended that the full-mediation versus partialmediation hypothesis be tested over more than one IT.

Email, for example, is conducive to short frequent use, while word processing is more conducive to longer use. Rather than pool results for different ITs, our design allowed us to conduct a detailed analysis for each.

Usage of these applications was strictly voluntary within the organization. All employees had a number of similar alternatives available for use for a considerable period. They could choose their preferred applications and install them from the organization’s LAN.

## 3.2. Operationalization of constructs

Like Agarwal and Prasad, we used the original TAM instruments [19] for PEOU, PU, and usage (see Appendix A).

A pre-questionnaire solicited users’ system experience, education, and age. System experience was the ‘‘total length of elapsed time (in months) using the application.’’ Education was: (1) high school graduate; (2) some college; (3) 2-year associate degree; (4) bachelors degree; (5) some graduate school; (6) master’s degree; (7) doctoral degree. The original self-reported educational data were rescaled: high school graduate = 12 years; some college = 13 years; 2-year associate degree = 14 years; bachelor’s degree = 16 years; some graduate school = 17 years; master’s degree = 18 years; and doctoral degree = 21 years. Age was also self-reported by users.

An important aim of our study was to investigate whether the results of the hypothesized models were consistent across different usage measures. We were unaware of any detailed investigation of this in the literature, in fact many studies used only one measure. We decomposed usage into two sub-dimensions (frequency and volume) that have often been used. Frequency represents the number of times a user said he or she used a system over a period, whereas volume gave time that the user used a system over the same period (see [1,32,49]). While studies have contrasted TAM’s ability to predict usage measured by different methods [52], few studies have examined its relative ability to predict different usage measures. We believed, however, that TAM would explain frequency better than volume. As volume is influenced by more factors than frequency, we expected that the antecedents in a parsimonious model, such as TAM, should explain less variance in volume than frequency.

The result for the proposition was therefore:

(a) support for a direct effect from system experience on IT use for both technologies (P1a);

(b) support for a direct effect from users’ age on IT use on one technology (P1b);

(c) no support for a direct effect on use from users education on either technology (P1c).

For the email application, there is a significant direct effect of system experience on usage volume and frequency over and above its indirect effects through PEOU and PU, supporting P1a. There were no direct effects of age and level of education, however, on either measure of usage, consistent with Agarwal and Prasad’s results. Both models also showed significantly higher explained variance for frequency than for volume, confirming our expectations.

For the word processor application, there were significant direct effects of system experience and age on usage frequency, over and above their respective indirect effects through PEOU and PU, supporting P1a and P1b. Again, however, level of education had no direct effect on either usage measure (P1c). In relation to the usage measures, both models for word processing explained variance in usage frequency better than volume. This was expected and was consistent with the results for email.

To further examine the result of introducing direct versus indirect effects for email and word-processing, Table 7 shows the change in usage variance explained $( R ^ { 2 } )$ resulting from introducing direct effects. Overall, introducing direct effects increased the $R ^ { 2 }$ for usage by 22–250%, providing strong support to the study’s proposition. Finally, we discuss the traditional ‘‘mediated’’ pathways of TAM. Like many past studies, we found that PU was a more powerful predictor of usage than PEOU. These results were consistent across technologies. We expected that experience, education, and age would each significantly affect PEOU and PU, but our results showed strong differences across technologies. System experience had a significant effect on PEOU for email, but had no significant effect on PEOU or PU for word processing. Level of education and age had a significant effect on PEOU for word processing but had no significant effect on PEOU and PU for email.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. External variables and TAM

Our results suggest that TAM’s full-mediation assumption is overstated. We found that external variables can have a strong direct effect on usage over and above their effect on attitudes and beliefs. In the case of email, system experience had a direct effect on frequency $( \beta = 0 . 2 6 )$ and volume $( \beta = 0 . 3 8 )$ . In the case of word processing, both system experience $( \beta = 0 . 1 8 )$ and age $( \beta = 0 . 1 8 )$ had direct effects on usage frequency. Including the direct effect of external variables can significantly improve predictions of IT use (by up to 250%).

Our results for full-mediation versus partial-mediation, however, are inconsistent. The magnitude of the direct effects from external variables differed across usage measures, ITs, and external variables. This is important, because it confirms and extends [12] and suggests that neither a partial- nor full-mediation assumption is warranted. Instead, the effects of external variables appear to depend on the nature of the IT, usage measure, and external variable.

## 5.2. Usage measures and TAM

Our results for different usage measures were consistent and in line with our expectations. Each of our models predicted usage frequency better thanvolume, as expected. Our results corroborate arguments that the usage construct may be more complex than previously recognized. In our study, the variance explained in usage was quite low (0.03–0.25). Our results also showed a steep and consistent drop in explained variance for usage volume compared to frequency.

## 5.3. Expected TAM relationships

Our results showed that the internal TAM model (beliefs ! usage) was consistent across technologies but that the effect of external variables on TAM’s belief constructs was quite IT-specific. This upholds Legris argument that external variables should be studied more systematically.

## 5.4. Limitations

Our study’s internal validity would have been stronger if we had measured the relative strength of each path (e.g., subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and habits) between users’ individual differences and usage. While measuring the relative effect of each path would have provided further explanations of our results, it was not necessary for achieving our objective. A more serious limitation was that we did not control for users’ tasks while using their IT. This made it impossible to investigate task–technology-fit explanations of results. In terms of construct validity, we acknowledge that our IS usage measures could contain measurement error if users had different understandings of what ‘use’ of a system means. For example, self-reported usage is not always an appropriate surrogate measure for actual usage [53]. Further, we did not control for the possibility of habitua usage patterns with other popular email and word processing applications. Also, the explained variance in usage (particularly usage volume) was quite low. Some relationships may therefore reflect greater statistical, than practical, significance.

Finally, although not a limitation per se, there is one last observation: the Venkatesh et al. [61] study integrates most of the prevailing user acceptance models into a Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). And the analytical approach in this study tests the moderating (i.e. interaction) effects among the predictive constructs and measures on subsequent usage behavior. Accordingly, the issue of direct versus mediated effects may be overshadowed by the analysis of these interaction effects among the predictive constructs. In fact, in the Venkatesh et al. study, experience, age, and gender were found to be important moderators of the predictive constructs on usage measures. However, we found no significant moderating effects.

## 5.5. Implications for research

Our results provide evidence that the full-mediation assumption of TAM is overstated. While our result extend the TAM model, we believe the more interesting aspect is that the full-mediation versus partial-mediation depended on the nature of the difference variable and IT being considered.

Second, our results on usage measures suggest that TAM may not be a good model for predicting complex measures of use, though we only measured two aspects of usage.

## 5.6. Implications for practice

PU and PEOU were shown to be important determinants of system usage in the field. This offers management the opportunity to increase systems usage by making systems more usable and useful, for example, through refined systems selection or IT training. Our results also showed, however, that users individual differences, such as age and experience, can have a direct effect over and above the indirect effect on PU and PEOU. Thus, managers cannot ignore user differences, but should tailor their selection and training methods to meet the needs of different users. System experience had a strong unmediated effect on usage in our study. Such usage may be partly habitual. Organizations must therefore assess whether they would prefer their older or more experienced users to remain open to new ideas, new systems, and new ways of working.

Our study also bears implications for the measurement of usage in practice. While predicting IT usage has been a key aspect of IS research, it is also a useful metric to monitor in practice. It gives firms an indication of users’ adoption of IT and the firm’s return on IT investments. Our results suggest that organizations should measure a range of usage measures. We recommend that practitioners adopt a suite of metrics to obtain an overall view of users’ IT acceptance.

## 6. Conclusions

This objective of our study was to contribute to the continued improvement of models of technology acceptance. We investigated two aspects of TAM (external variables and usage measures) that have received little attention. TAM and TRA propose that the effect of external variables is completely mediated by users’ beliefs. We tested this proposition and found that the full-mediation hypothesis is overstated. Our results also suggested that the nature of the relationship should not be considered full- or partial-mediation, but a contingent mediation, depending on the nature of the technology and external variable considered. Our results also highlighted the different (and poorer) results that occur with more complex measures of usage.
