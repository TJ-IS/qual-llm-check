# When Online Reviews Meet Sales Volume Information: Is More or Accurate Information Always Better?

Yang Liu,<sup>a,</sup> <sup>b</sup> Juan Feng,<sup>b</sup> Xiuwu Liao<sup>a</sup>

Abstract. This paper studies how two types of market-generated information, namely, online reviews and past sales volume information, jointly afect consumer purchase decisions as well as firms’ pricing strategies. We build a two-period duopoly model in a market with herding consumers who have diferent preferences and are unsure of the quality diference between products. In addition, a firm’s sales volume is uncertain because of the existence of “irregular” consumers. We find that the impacts of online reviews and sales volume information on firms’ profits are mutually enhancing. The impact of such marketgenerated information depends on both product characteristics (the level of consumers’ misfit for the nonpreferred product) and how consumers a priori perceive the quality difference between the products. Contrary to the conventional wisdom that more/accurate information is beneficial to high-quality firms, as well as consumers, we find that such information can be detrimental to firms, as firms adjust prices to induce, or react to, favorable market-generated information. Accordingly, consumers may not benefit from such market-generated information if the gain from less uncertainty cannot ofset the loss from higher product prices. Such findings ofer guidelines for firms to design better pricing strategies, as well as information policies, such as whether or not to promote more informative reviews.

## 1. Introduction

The advancement of information technology (IT) and the Internet economy makes it easier to both record and convey information about peer consumers’ purchases (e.g., previous sales volume) and opinions (e.g., online reviews), which is not easily accessible in ofline businesses. For example, besides product prices, online stores often present both consumer reviews/ratings and sales volume information (e.g., taobao.com). For those stores for which only consumer review information is displayed (e.g., Amazon, Apple Store), the number of reviews can often be viewed as a proxy for sales volume (Lee et al. 2011, Ghose et al. 2007). The extant literature has already established that consumers prefer products with favorable reviews/high ratings (Chen et al. 2004, Kuksov and Xie 2010, Li et al. 2011). In addition, many empirical studies show that consumers tend to prefer products with a large sales volume (Chen 2008, Cai et al. 2009, Simonsohn and Ariely 2008, Hanson and Putler 1996, Caminal and

Vives 1996). For example, when selecting between two restaurants without knowing their reputations, consumers are more likely to choose the one with more customers (Becker 1991). This phenomenon is known as the herding or crowd efect.

Little research, however, has been performed to study the joint impact of these two types of information. Consider a consumer searching for a tablet on Amazon.com and facing two alternatives, LG and Google Nexus. The LG tablet has a higher rating but lower sales volume and a lower price than the Google Nexus. Which information has a greater influence on consumers? How should consumers choose between these two products? How should firms adjust their prices to maximize profits? Will this decision be diferent if the product is more horizontally diferentiated (books; Figure 1(b)) compared to one that is more vertically diferentiated (tablets; Figure 1(a))?

Online reviews and sales volume information have diferent impacts on consumers. On one hand, online

reviews are believed to help consumers learn more details about products and to reduce information asymmetry between sellers and buyers (Chen et al. 2004, Chen and Xie 2005, Kuksov and Xie 2010). On the other hand, because previous sales volume information reveals the actions of other consumers without including the reasons behind them (Bikhchandani et al. 1992, 1998), herding does not necessarily lead consumers to purchase a “good” product (Simonsohn and Ariely 2008, Zhang 2010). Previous sales volume, for example, may be caused by randomness in early consumer choices (Salganik et al. 2006).

This paper aims to explore the joint impact of online reviews and sales volume information on the strategies of both the firm and consumers. More specifically, for which kinds of products, or under what kinds of market situations, will such market-generated information be influential? Do online reviews help a firm with a large/small sales volume, and if so, under what conditions? How should firms optimally react, in terms of pricing and information policies, when consumers rely heavily on sales volume information? Should the high/low quality firm set higher/lower prices when online reviews are very informative? Under what conditions should firms promote more informative online reviews?

We build a two-period economic model to study the joint impact of online reviews and sales volume information in a duopolistic setting. In this model, we assume the following: (1) Consumers are uncertain about the quality diference between the products, as well as their own location preferences. (2) Consumers’ valuations of the product are afected by both online reviews and previous sales volume information. Specifically, consumers believe that a product that has better reviews, or that has sold better, is more likely to be of higher quality. The more precise the information, the greater the weight that consumers put on that information. (3) There are “irregular” consumers whose purchase decisions are not afected by the comparative prices of the two products. Therefore, firms’ sales volumes cannot be completely controlled by firm prices. We find that online reviews and sales volume information work jointly in influencing consumer purchases as well as firm pricing, and their impacts depend on both product characteristics (the level of consumers’ misfit costs for the nonpreferred product) and how consumers a priori perceive the quality diference between the products. Contrary to the conventional wisdom that more/accurate information helps the high-quality firm, we find that because firms can adjust prices in response to or to induce favorable market-generated information, such information may harm the high-quality firm or even the consumers. More specifically, we find the following in a market with herding consumers: (1) The presence of either online reviews or sales volume information may not help the high-quality firm (or hurt the low-quality firm). (2) The impact of market-generated information on firms’ profit is stronger when there is a more favorable prior belief toward the high-quality firm; moreover, the higher the misfit cost, the higher the impact of online reviews and the lower the impact of sales volume information. (3) More informative reviews, which reduce price competition, are in general beneficial, but can still hurt firms. Consumers may not benefit from such market-generated information if the gain from less uncertainty cannot ofset the loss from higher product prices. Such findings ofer guidelines for firms to design better pricing, as well as information policies, such as under what conditions it is worthwhile to reduce prices initially, and whether or not to promote more informative reviews.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section $^ { 2 , }$ we review the past literature. We present our model in Section 3. In Section 4, we study the impact of online reviews when they coexist with sales volume information, as well as the impact of informativeness. We check the robustness of the results and discuss the managerial implications in Section 5 and then conclude the paper.

## 2. Literature Review

This paper analyzes the impact of both online reviews and sales volume information on consumers’ valuation and firms’ pricing strategies. We discuss the related literature below.

Many empirical studies focus on the impact of online reviews on sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Dellarocas et al. 2007, Duan et al. 2008, and Chintagunta et al. 2010), and three measures of online reviews are most discussed: valence, variance, and volume (Dellarocas et al. 2007). Researchers point out that review valence has a positive impact on sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Chintagunta et al. 2010), while review variance negatively influences online hotel bookings (Ye et al. 2009). In addition, Liu (2006) and Duan et al. (2008) find that review volume may have more explanatory power on sales than the other two measures, and Sun (2012) reports that the impact of review variance depends on the valence. We go beyond these papers by considering a duopoly market in which firms have diferent quality levels to explore the joint impact of online reviews and sales volume not only on sales but also, more importantly, on firm pricing strategies, firm profits, and consumer welfare.

A growing literature studies the information role of online reviews and their impact on firms’ marketing strategies, including advertisements (Chen and Xie 2005), coupons, frills, and keyword advertising (Kuksov and Xie 2010, Lu et al. 2013), and when to disclose online reviews to consumers (Chen and Xie 2008). Chen and Xie (2005), for example, find that advertising is a better response to reviews than pricing when consumers are more horizontally diferentiated. Moreover, online reviews disclose both vertical (e.g., quality) and horizontal (e.g., size, color) information about products, so it helps consumers to learn whether or not the product fits (Chen and Xie 2008, Sun 2012, Kwark et al. 2014). In general, informative reviews may soften pricing competition and improve firms’ profits (Kwark et al. 2014); however, more informative reviews do not always lead to higher prices or more profits when there is a large number of expert consumers who do not need informative reviews (Chen and Xie 2008), or when the repeat purchase behavior exists with low switch risk (from one firm to the other; Lin et al. 2011). Our paper extends this line of literature by providing diferent insights and explanations. For example, we incorporate uncertainty in sales volume information by considering “irregular” consumers and herding consumers. We also study the joint impact of online reviews and sales volume on firms with diferent quality levels.

Because online reviews afect both the pre- and postpurchase behaviors of consumers (Lin et al. 2011), they in turn influence firms’ pricing strategies. Both skimming pricing and penetration pricing are commonly observed. In a monopoly setting, penetration pricing may help a low (perceived) quality product to build a good reputation (Shapiro 1983), or help a seller of niche products to extract surplus from buyers with a high willingness to pay (Bergemann and Välimäki 2006). Skimming pricing is efective when the seller possesses monopoly power, the market is highly diferentiated, and consumers are price insensitive (Noble and Gruca 1999, Khouja and Smith 2007). Researchers find that when incorporating market-generated information, a seller with a higher reputation may price lower than a seller with a lower reputation when there is a suficient number of uninformed consumers (Liu et al. 2012) or when the service level decreases (Ba et al. 2008). In a multiperiod framework, there exists a trade-of between early profit loss by cutting prices (or other marketing strategies) to obtain favorable reviews/ratings and the later profit gain (Kuksov and Xie 2010, Li and Hitt 2010). Others consider the impact of online reviews on pricing strategy in diferent contexts, such as when software piracy exists (Liu et al. 2011), when consumers repeatedly purchase a product (Li et al. 2011), and when the products are sold in multiple retail channels (Kwark et al. 2014). For instance, Liu et al. (2011) suggest that word of mouth leads to a skimming (penetration) pricing strategy when software piracy exists.

Following this line of research, we study firm pricing before and after the market-generated information is available. This paper difers from previous research in that it considers not only online reviews but also sales volume information that might be influenced by a random number of “irregular” consumers who are not influenced by price. We find that such uncertainty can make the “penetration” pricing strategy inefective and that the impact of online reviews depends on how heavily consumers rely on sales volume information.

There is also literature studying the impact of previous sales volume on consumer behavior (Caminal and Vives 1996, Chen et al. 2011, and Zhang 2010). Before the advent of online reviews, Caminal and Vives (1996) considered that a lower introductory price is optimal when consumers are able to infer quality from sales volume information. Nowadays, studies regarding herding and observational learning also indicate that previous sales volume plays a key role in consumers’ decision-making process (Simonsohn and Ariely 2008, Zhang 2010). In addition, Chen et al. (2011) document the impact of observational learning and word of mouth and demonstrate that the impact of negative word of mouth is stronger than its positive counterpart, whereas positive observational learning has a stronger impact than its negative counterpart. We extend this line of literature by incorporating a random number of irregular consumers, to reflect uncertainty in sales volume information caused by randomness in early consumer choices (Salganik et al. 2006). Since sales volume is as easily accessible as reviews/ratings, the joint impact of sales volume and reviews/ratings on consumers and firms cannot be neglected (Chen et al. 2011).

In summary, our model constitutes the first efort to combine both online reviews and sales volume in a framework in which consumers are herding and uncertain about products. We also analyze the joint impact of such information on firms’ pricing strategies and profits in a duopolistic setting.

## 3. The Model

We follow the Caminal and Vives (1996) duopoly model, in which consumers’ valuations are influenced by previous sales volume information. We further extend the model by incorporating consumer reviews. Consider two firms, H and $L ,$ selling two imperfectly substitutable products with diferent quality levels, h and $l ,$ in an online market. The marginal cost of producing each product is assumed to be zero. Without loss of generality, we assume that firm H sells a higherquality product and that the actual quality diference between h and l is $q ,$ which is unknown to consumers. Consumers, however, can infer the quality diference q by the information that they possess.

There are two types of consumers: regular and irregular. Regular consumers purchase from the firm that ofers a higher utility, while irregular consumers purchase based on other reasons that cannot be explained by similar utility functions. For example, they may be loyal customers who are not afected by the price of the competing firm, customers who care about product quality so much that price is no longer relevant, or customers who are simply prompt shoppers or random buyers.

Regular Consumers. Assume that the preference or the true “location” of consumers is uniformly distributed over <sup>[</sup>0, 1<sup>]</sup> (Hotelling 1929), where product h is located at 0 and product l is located at 1. Each consumer demands only one unit of product. Let t represent the misfit cost per unit distance. Thus, if a regular consumer is located at point y, she incurs a misfit cost of yt if buying product h, and a misfit cost of $( 1 - y ) t$ if buying product l.

Regular consumers have prior beliefs about the actual quality diference between the two products, which follow a normal distribution $q \sim \mathrm { N } \bar { ( m , 1 / \tau _ { m } ) }$ These prior beliefs may be based on the product information posted on a website, an advertisement for the product, brand reputation, etc.

Meanwhile, consumers may have some prior knowledge, but are still uncertain about their own location in the market, which means that they are not sure about the extent to which a product fits their needs before they make a purchase. To capture this uncertainty, following Kwark et al. (2014), we assume that a regular consumer believes her location at some point $y$ with probability 0.5, and at any other point on the line with equal probability.<sup>1</sup> So a regular consumer expects the utility diference between the two products to be

$$
E (q) + (\frac {1}{2} - y) t - (p _ {1} ^ {h} - p _ {1} ^ {l}),\tag{1}
$$

where $p _ { i } ^ { i }$ is the price of product i in period $j ; i = h , l$ and $j = 1 , 2 .$ . Moreover, the demand for each product is

$$
D _ {1} ^ {h} = \frac {1}{2} + \frac {E (q) - (p _ {1} ^ {h} - p _ {1} ^ {l})}{t} + u ^ {h},\tag{2}
$$

$$
D _ {1} ^ {l} = \frac {1}{2} - \frac {E (q) - (p _ {1} ^ {h} - p _ {1} ^ {l})}{t} + u ^ {l},\tag{3}
$$

where $D _ { j } ^ { i }$ is the demand for product i in period $j ~ ( i =$ $h , l$ and $i = 1 , 2 )$ , and $u ^ { i }$ represents the demand from irregular consumers, which is a random variable following a normal distribution, $u ^ { i } \sim \Nu ( 0 , 1 / \tau _ { u } )$

We consider a two-period model in which consumers only “live” for one period. In each period, the distribution of a regular consumer’s prior belief about the quality diference $q$ and the distribution of the demand $u ^ { i }$ from irregular consumers are public information to both firms and consumers. Following Caminal and Vives (1996), we assume that consumers can observe the prices of both products in the current period, but not those in the previous period. The rationale behind this assumption is that even though such technologies as price comparison and price tracking are prevalent nowadays (e.g., thetracktor.com, camelcamelcamel.com), it remains dificult for every consumer to accurately monitor the exact price history because of sellers’ “price obfuscation”<sup>2</sup> (Ellison and Ellison 2009), firms’ prevention of price comparisons (Wilson 2010), or the existence of “uninformed” consumers who do not search (Chen and Xie 2008, Xu et al. 2012, Geng et al. 2013). Nevertheless, abundant information existing in the market makes it easier for consumers to correctly infer previous prices, even though they may not be able to accurately observe them.

At the beginning of period $^ { 2 , }$ two additional types of information become available. One is the sales volume of each product in the previous period. Such information can be made directly available on the product website (for example, some websites publish the number of downloads of an app (Apple Store) or software (Download.com) or the sales volume (taobao.com)) or can be inferred from other sources of information such as sales rank or review volume. It is not uncommon for consumers to choose the product that has been bought by more consumers, although the true quality of the product is not discerned (Salganik et al. 2006, Chen 2008). The other type of information is product reviews. Consumers can read the reviews and learn more about both the quality diference and horizontal fit of the products.

The sequence of the game is as follows: At the beginning of period 1, both firms price their products simultaneously, and consumers make purchase decisions. Consumers then ofer truthful reviews of the products that they purchased. At the beginning of period $^ { 2 , }$ observing sales volume information as well as the online reviews, both firms set their product prices and then new consumers in the second period make their purchase decisions accordingly.

## 3.1. A Market Without Market-Generated Information

We first analyze the simplest case without online reviews and sales volume information. This resembles many ofline markets in practice. In this case, consumers can infer the actual product quality diference only through their prior belief, $q \sim \bar { \mathrm { N } ( m , 1 / \tau _ { m } ) }$ . Without any other market information, consumers make decisions based on their prior beliefs. Therefore, firms’ optimal demands, prices, and profits in both periods are

for the H firms:

$$
\tilde {D} _ {j} ^ {h} = \frac {1}{2} + \frac {m}{3 t}, \tilde {p} _ {j} ^ {h} = \frac {t}{2} + \frac {m}{3}, \tilde {\pi} _ {j} ^ {h} = \left(\frac {1}{2} + \frac {m}{3 t}\right) \left(\frac {t}{2} + \frac {m}{3}\right),
$$

for the L firms:

$$
\tilde {D} _ {j} ^ {l} = \frac {1}{2} - \frac {m}{3 t}, \tilde {p} _ {j} ^ {l} = \frac {t}{2} - \frac {m}{3}, \tilde {\pi} _ {j} ^ {l} = \left(\frac {1}{2} - \frac {m}{3 t}\right) \left(\frac {t}{2} - \frac {m}{3}\right),
$$

where $j = 1 , 2 .$

The results show that without any market-generated information such as online reviews and sales volume information, firms’ prices largely depend on consumers’ expectations about product quality (Shapiro 1983). We assume that t is not too large, such that the whole market is covered.<sup>3</sup> Under this condition, both firms are able to set higher prices without much probability of decreasing demand.

## 3.2. Only Sales Volume Information Available

We now consider the case in which only the previous sales volume information is available to consumers at the beginning of period 2. Assume that consumers are herding, i.e., they always prefer products that sold better previously (Chen 2008, Simonsohn and Ariely 2008, Cai et al. 2009).

Sales Volume Information. Besides prior belief $q \sim$ $\mathrm { N } ( m , 1 / \tau _ { m } ) .$ , consumers who enter in the second period can observe the sales volume diference in the previous period, which is determined by the expected quality diference, the unobserved price diference, and the difference in the number of irregular consumers between the two firms. From Equations (2) and (3)

$$
\Delta D _ {1} = \frac {2}{t} (m - \Delta p ^ {\varepsilon}) + \Delta \tilde {u},\tag{4}
$$

where $\Delta p ^ { \varepsilon } = p _ { 1 } ^ { h \varepsilon } - p _ { 1 } ^ { l \varepsilon }$ and $\Delta \tilde { u } = \tilde { u } ^ { h } - \tilde { u } ^ { l }$ . Since consumers cannot observe the previous prices, they can only form expectations about the diference in the number of irregular consumers as $\Delta \tilde { u }$ , where $\Delta \tilde { u } \sim \Nu ( 0 , 2 / \tau _ { u } )$ Here, $p _ { 1 } ^ { i \varepsilon }$ refers to the consumer expectation about the price of product i in the first period (it is an expectation because it is not observable), and ∆u˜ is the diference in the number of irregular consumers between the two firms.

Following Caminal and Vives (1996), sales volume information from the previous period influences the consumers’ current valuations about products. This is also consistent with the herding behavior documented in many empirical studies (e.g., Zhang and Liu 2012, Simonsohn and Ariely 2008, Cai et al. 2009, Chen et al. 2011). Based on the diference in previous sales volumes, consumers infer the quality diference between the two firms to be $q ^ { s } = \Delta p ^ { \bar { \varepsilon _ { + } } } + ( t / 2 ) \Delta D _ { 1 } ,$ which is normally distributed with a variance of $t ^ { 2 } / ( 2 \tau _ { u } )$

$$
\hat {\eta} = E (q \mid q ^ {s}, r) = \hat {x} _ {1} m + \hat {x} _ {3} (\Delta p ^ {\varepsilon} + (t / 2) \Delta D _ {1}),\tag{5}
$$

where $\hat { x } _ { 1 } = \tau _ { m } / ( \tau _ { m } + 2 \tau _ { u } / t ^ { 2 } )$ and $\hat { x } _ { 3 } = ( 2 \tau _ { u } / t ^ { 2 } ) / ( \tau _ { m }$ + $2 \tau _ { u } / t ^ { 2 } )$ , referring to the weight of the prior belief and sales volume information, respectively. It is easily verified that the higher the precision of the information, the higher the weight of this information in shaping consumers’ perceptions about the quality diference. In practice, if firms set prices $p _ { 1 } ^ { h }$ and $p _ { 1 } ^ { l } ,$ , it is simple to show that the sales volume diference can be written as $\Delta D _ { 1 } = ( 2 / t ) ( m - \Delta p ) + \Delta u _ { . }$ , where $\Delta u = u ^ { h } - u ^ { l } . ^ { 5 }$ Plugging in $\Delta D _ { 1 } , ( 5 )$ can be rewritten as

$$
\hat {\eta} = \hat {x} _ {1} m + \hat {x} _ {3} (\Delta p ^ {\varepsilon} - \Delta p + m + (2 / t) \Delta u).\tag{6}
$$

In equilibrium, firms set prices equal to consumers’ expected prices, i.e., $\Delta p ^ { \varepsilon } = \Delta p$ . Thus, the aggregate updated expected quality diference $( \hat { \dot { \eta } } )$ from these information sources can be written as $\hat { \dot { \eta } } = \hat { x } _ { 1 } m + \hat { x } _ { 3 } ( m +$ $( t / 2 ) \Delta u )$

We solve the problem using backward induction. In the second period, since the quality diference is known, the expected demand of each product in the second period is

$$
D _ {2} ^ {h} = \frac {1}{2} + \frac {\hat {\eta} - p _ {2} ^ {h} + p _ {2} ^ {l}}{t}, D _ {2} ^ {l} = \frac {1}{2} - \frac {\hat {\eta} - p _ {2} ^ {h} + p _ {2} ^ {l}}{t}.
$$

Plug in these demand functions into the profit functions $\overline { { { \pi } } } _ { 2 } ^ { i } = D _ { 2 } ^ { i } p _ { 2 } ^ { i } , ~ i = h , l$ . From the first-order condition, we can obtain firms’ optimal prices in the second period

$$
\hat {p} _ {2} ^ {h} = \frac {1}{2} t + \frac {1}{3} \hat {\eta}, \quad \hat {p} _ {2} ^ {l} = \frac {1}{2} t - \frac {1}{3} \hat {\eta}.
$$

In the first period, firms set prices to maximize the total profits in the two periods

$$
\max _ {\{p _ {j} ^ {i} \}} \pi^ {i} = \max _ {\{p _ {j} ^ {i} \}} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {2} D _ {j} ^ {i} \hat {p} _ {j} ^ {i}, \quad i = h, l.
$$

Solving this problem, we obtain Lemma 1. To ensure that both firms stay in the market with a positive price/profit, we assume that consumers’ prior belief about the quality diference and the sales volume diference cannot be too large, i.e., $0 < m < \hat { m } _ { a }$ and $- 3 / \hat { x } _ { 3 } < \Delta u < 3 / \hat { x } _ { 3 }$ , where $\hat { m _ { a } } = \operatorname * { m i n } \{ \hat { m } _ { a 1 } , \hat { m } _ { a 2 } \} , \hat { m } _ { a 1 } =$ $t ( 3 - \hat { x } _ { 3 } \Delta u ) / 2 , \hat { m } _ { a 2 } = 9 t ( 3 - 2 \hat { x } _ { 3 } ) / ( 2 ( 9 - 2 \hat { x } _ { 3 } ) )$ , and $\hat { x } _ { 3 } =$ $x _ { 3 } / ( 1 - x _ { 2 } )$

Lemma 1. With only sales volume information, firms’ equilibrium prices in the two periods are

$$
\text {for the H firm:} \hat {p} _ {j} ^ {h *} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} (\frac {1}{2} t + \frac {1}{3} m) - \frac {1}{3} \hat {x} _ {3} (t + \frac {2}{9} m), & j = 1, \\ \frac {1}{2} t + \frac {1}{3} \hat {\eta}, & j = 2, \end{array} \right.
$$

$$
\text {for the L firm:} \hat {p} _ {j} ^ {l *} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} (\frac {1}{2} t - \frac {1}{3} m) - \frac {1}{3} \hat {x} _ {3} (t - \frac {2}{9} m), & j = 1, \\ \frac {1}{2} t - \frac {1}{3} \hat {\eta}, & j = 2, \end{array} \right.

Lemma 1 suggests that when the sales volume serves as a signal of quality, the equilibrium prices depend on both the consumers’ prior belief and sales volume information. First, the H (L) firm has an advantage (disadvantage) to price high (low) in both periods, because consumers’ prior belief supports the H firm (a positive m<sup>)</sup>. Second, both firms reduce their prices in the first period by $\textstyle { \frac { 1 } { 3 } } t { \hat { x } } _ { 3 }$ to increase the sales volume compared to the “ofline” case. Moreover, the more accurate the sales volume information, or, equivalently, the higher the weight $( \hat { x } _ { 3 } )$ that consumers place on sales volume information, the greater the incentive for a firm to reduce its first-period price to increase its sales volume. In the second period, because this is the last period, no pressure exists to cut prices to generate favorable market-generated information. In other words, the firm that generates a larger sales volume indicates higher quality, and so it sets a higher price.

Lemma 1 also indicates that the presence of sales volume information may not always be beneficial to firms. Given the uncertainty in irregular consumers, the difference in sales volumes does not necessarily reflect the true quality diference between the products, nor is it completely controlled by firms’ pricing strategies. As a result, the H firm may not be able to generate a larger sales volume and thus it can be negatively afected.

## 3.3. Sales Volume Information Combined with Online Reviews

We now consider the situation in which both online reviews and previous sales volume information are available to consumers at the beginning of period 2. In this case, regular consumers entering in the second period infer product quality from three information sources: (1) their own prior beliefs, (2) sales volume information, and (3) online reviews.

Online Review Information. Let r represent the quality diference revealed from online reviews, which is a random variable following a normal distribution, $r \sim \mathrm { N } ( q , 1 / \tau _ { q } )$ . This assumption has two implications: (1) online reviews, on average, reveal the true quality diference between the two firms, and (2) $\tau _ { q }$ captures consumer heterogeneity in the perception of quality diference.

Meanwhile, online reviews also help regular consumers update their location uncertainty. Let γ represent the informativeness of online reviews. By reading reviews, a consumer updates her belief about locating at a point y with probability $( 1 + \gamma ) / 2$ , and at any other point with equal probability. In other words, the more informative the review is, the more precisely can consumers discern their locations in the market.

How do online reviews impact consumer welfare? Intuitively, online reviews are beneficial to consumers because they provide more product information, reduce quality uncertainty, and help consumers make more informed decisions. Because firms can proactively adjust prices in response to online reviews, however, consumers may be worse of if they end up buying the same product at a higher price. For example, Proposition 2 shows that, compared to the case in which there are no online reviews, in general, firms cut prices less in the first period in the presence of online reviews. In the second period, the H firm is able to raise its prices as well.

Corollary 3. The more consumers’ prior belief favors the H firm (the higher the m), the more (less) likely it is that the inclusion of online review information will make the H (L) firm set a higher price in the first period and set a lower price in the second period.

Since the addition of online review information reduces the weight of consumers’ prior belief in their perception of quality diference, the stronger the consumers’ prior belief, the less is the price cut that the H firm needs to attract consumers in the first period; meanwhile, the stronger the consumers’ prior belief, the lower is the impact of the online reviews on improving consumers’ perception about the quality diference. Following the same logic, the more consumers favor the H firm (the higher the m), the less likely it is for the L firm to set a higher price in the first period, and a lower price in the second period, when online review information is included.

## 4.2. The Impact of Informativeness of Online Reviews

Online reviews reveal not only vertical (quality) information but also horizontal or $\dot { \boldsymbol { \mathbf { \mathit { \Omega } } } } ^ { \prime \prime } \mathrm { f i t } ^ { \prime \prime }$ information. A pure rating system uses numbers or 5/10-star scales to measure consumer satisfaction (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Liu et al. 2011). In a text review, however, consumers may reveal why they like or dislike the product in detail (Chen and Xie 2008, Kwark et al. 2014), and thus these text reviews may be more informative than a pure rating system. In this section, we analyze the impact of the informativeness $( \gamma )$ of online reviews on firms’ equilibrium pricing strategies and optimal profits.

Lemma 3 (The Impact of Informativeness on Pricing Strategy). When the informativeness $( \gamma )$ of online reviews increases, both firms are more likely to set higher prices in the second period than in the first period.

The increase in the informativeness (γ<sup>)</sup> of online reviews reveals more horizontal information about the two products. So consumers learn more about the extent to which the products fit their preference and relax the price competition in the second period, which enables both firms to set higher prices in the second period. Meanwhile, realizing that consumers will be more informed, the L firm has a larger incentive to reduce its first-period price, expecting that the quality disadvantage will be ofset by the horizontal information. For the H firm, informative online reviews will raise its prices in both periods (Lemma 2), although the price increase is higher in the second period than in the first period. So, the H firm is more likely to set higher prices in the second period when informativeness increases.

Proposition 4 (Impact of Informativeness on Consumers). In both periods, the more informative the online reviews, the more consumers switch from purchasing the h product to purchasing the l product $( i . e . , \partial \dot { D } ^ { h * } / \partial \gamma < \mathsf { \bar { 0 } } , i = \dot { 1 } , 2 )$

Moreover, more informative reviews can be beneficial only to consumers who buy the l product in the first period, or consumers who switch from buying the h product to the l product in the second period.

Proposition 4 shows that when firms proactively adjust prices in response to online reviews, more informative reviews do not always benefit consumers. Because more informative reviews are usually associated with relaxed competition between the firms, firms then set higher prices (Lemma 3) and thus harm consumers. More specifically, in the first period, more informative reviews harm consumers who purchase the h product, because the H firm cuts prices less; for those who purchase the l product, more informative reviews are beneficial, because they induce the L firm to reduce prices more. In the second period, consumers whose purchase decisions remain the same (compared to the case with less informative reviews) are worse of, because informative reviews reduce price competition between the two firms. Consequently, they purchase the same products at higher prices. In the second period, informative reviews benefit only the consumers who switch to buying the l product.

Proposition 5 (Impact of Informativeness on Firm Profits). In general, both H and L firms benefit from more informative reviews. However,

(1) more informative reviews harm the H firm when online reviews strongly favor the H firm $( i . e . , q > \bar { q } _ { 3 } )$ , consumers’ prior belief strongly favors the H firm $( i . e . , m >$ max $\{ \bar { m } _ { 3 } , 0 \} )$ , the sales volume does not strongly favor the L firm $( i . e . , \Delta u > \bar { u } _ { 2 } )$ , and the impact of the sales volume information is limited $( i . e . , 0 < x _ { 3 } \dot { \leq } \frac { 3 } { 4 } )$ ;

Intuitively, both firms benefit from an increase in the informativeness of online reviews, because they provide more horizontal information about the products and thus relax the price competition between the two firms (Kwark et al. 2014). ## 4.3. The Impact of Accuracy in Sales Volume Information

Because sales volume information is not fully controlled by firms’ pricing strategies, its impact depends on the realization of “irregular” consumers: the firm benefits if it obtains a large enough share of irregular consumers. Intuitively, the more precise the sales volume information, the more reliable the information is. It should then be more beneficial to the H firm. We find, however, that an increase in the precision of the sales volume information may not always help the H firm or harm the L firm. Figures 4 and 5 show that as the precision of the sales volume information increases, firms’ profits may either go up or down, depending on the diference in sales volume, as well as the true quality diference from online reviews.

The more precise the sales volume information, the more reliable the information is, thus the more weight consumers place on the sales volume information. Consequently, firms have a larger incentive to cut their prices and incur larger losses in the first period. In the second period, if it turns out that the firm generates a significantly large sales volume, indicating a higher product quality<sup>9</sup> relative to what the other two information sources do, an increase in the precision of the sales volume information is beneficial, because it places more weight on the more favorable information. ## 5. Discussion and Implications

In this section, we discuss the issue of irregular consumers, the comparison between the case when the expectation of consumers’ belief is correct $( m > 0 )$ and that when it is incorrect $( m < 0 )$ , and managerial implications. Then, we discuss the main managerial implications of our results.

## 5.1. Irregular Consumers

So far, we assume that the number of irregular consumers $( u _ { i } )$ follows a normal distribution. Thus, the diference in the numbers of irregular consumers between the two firms $( \Delta u )$ also follows a normal distribution, i.e., $\Delta u \sim \Nu ( 0 , 2 \tau _ { u } )$ . To elucidate how the number of irregular consumers influences the results, we discuss the changes in the precision level of the random variable ∆u. A high precision (large $\tau _ { u } )$ in ∆u implies that the diference in the number of irregular consumers is more likely to be small, which indicates a higher reliability, and thus a higher weight $\left( x _ { 3 } \right)$ of the sales volume information. Therefore, firms have a larger incentive to enlarge their first-period sales volume by cutting prices. By contrast, a low precision (small $\tau _ { u } )$ implies that the number of irregular consumers is highly uncertain, which reduces the reliability of the sales volume information and discourages firms from cutting their first-period prices. We discuss two extreme cases in the following:

(1) The precision of irregular consumers is close to zero $( \tau _ { u } \to 0 )$ .

In this case, the number of irregular consumers is highly uncertain. This large uncertainty makes the sales volume information inefective. Since $\tau _ { u } \to 0 ,$ we have $x _ { 3 }  0 .$ , which means that consumers do not pay attention to the sales volume information. In the second period, consumers’ expected valuation is determined only by their prior beliefs (m) and online reviews. Therefore, firms have no incentive to reduce prices in the first period. Consequently, the H firm always sets higher prices than the L firm in the second period because it generates better online reviews, on average. If the quality diference is not significant, however, informative reviews can ofset the L firm’s quality disadvantage and improve its second-period prices, as well as total profit.

(2) The precision of irregular consumers is infinite $( \tau _ { u } \to + \infty )$

In this case, the distribution of irregular consumers is $u ^ { i } \sim \mathrm { N } ( 0 , 0 ) .$ , which means that no irregular consumers exist in the market at all. Since $\tau _ { u } \to + \infty ,$ , we have $x _ { 3 } \to 1 .$ , while $x _ { 1 } = x _ { 2 } = 0 . 5 0 ,$ in the second period, consumers’ expected valuation (η) is determined entirely by sales volume information $( q ^ { s } )$ , i.e., $\eta = x _ { 1 } m + x _ { 2 } q \ +$ 1 $x _ { 3 } q ^ { s } = q ^ { s }$ . Since firms believe that the quality diference inferred from the sales volume information is $q ^ { s } = m +$ $\left( \Delta p ^ { \varepsilon } - \Delta p \right)$ , manipulating prices is efective in shaping consumers’ expected valuations.

## 5.3. Managerial Implications

By considering both online reviews and sales volume information, this paper provides a unique perspective to explore the impact of market-generated information on consumers who are unsure of product quality or features and who are likely to be afected by previous consumers’ behavior (sales volume information). It also ofers several guidelines for business practitioners when facing diferent market situations.

(1) Joint Impact of Online Reviews and Sales Volume Information.

Our paper shows that the impacts of online reviews and sales volume information are mutually enhancing. Furthermore, the impact of such market-generated information on firms’ profits is influenced by both how consumers a priori perceive the quality diference and how horizontally diferentiated the products are. Specifically, the impact becomes stronger when consumers have more favorable prior beliefs toward the high-quality firm. In other words, firms’ reliance on online reviews and sales volume information decreases if consumers’ prior beliefs are strong (Corollary 4). Moreover, when selling more horizontally diferentiated products (e.g., books, movies), the impact of online reviews on firms’ profits reduces, while the impact of sales volume information becomes stronger. This indicates that to generate the same profit, the high-quality firm does not need to generate as strong a sales volume signal as in the case when it is selling a more vertically diferentiated product. This, in turn, shows that the high-quality firm does not need to cut its prices so aggressively to generate a higher sales volume (Corollary 5).

When firms design their information policies regarding online reviews, they should consider both the consumers’ prior beliefs and the products’ degree of horizontal diferentiation. In addition, according to Corollary 5, their reliance on sales volume information also depends on the quality level of the products.

(2) Online Reviews and Irregular Consumers.

Our results demonstrate that in a market in which consumers are afected by each other’s behavior (herding), firms need to cut prices initially to enlarge sales volume and increase prices in the second period. In the presence of irregular consumers, however, such price cuts can be very costly or inefective. Online reviews, which on average disclose the true quality diference, are shown to be able to mitigate the negative impact of the uncertainty caused by irregular consumers. Specifically, they help alleviate uncertainty in the sales volume information. Furthermore, since the high-quality firm generates better online reviews, on average, it can aford deeper price cuts than the low-quality firm in the early period and thus benefit consumers.

Proposition 3, however, implies that while online reviews help firms that are not efective at attracting “irregular” consumers, it does not help, or may even harm, firms that possess a large irregular or loyal consumer base. These findings provide guidelines for firms with diferent quality levels and loyal customer bases in promoting online reviews. For example, a firm with a quality advantage and a large loyal customer base does not need to invest heavily in promoting online reviews.

(3) Text Review versus Pure Rating.

In general, it is believed that text reviews provide more information, thus they are more informative than pure ratings. Firms at diferent quality levels, however, do not always need to promote more informative reviews. For example, when the quality diference is suficiently large and the consumers a priori strongly believe it, for the high-quality firm, a pure rating system may be more helpful if the sales volume is highly uncertain, and thus its impact is limited. On the other hand, for the low-quality firm, a text review system may not be optimal if the sales volume information is reliable (Proposition 5).

Firms’ pricing strategies should also be adjusted accordingly under diferent review mechanisms. Informative reviews ofer the high-quality firm larger pricing power that enables it to set higher prices in both periods compared to the case in which only a pure rating system is adopted. The lower-quality firm, on the other hand, should ofer deeper price cuts initially (Lemmas 2 and 3).

## 6. Conclusion

With the advancement of IT and Internet business, consumers in the digital age are able to access more information before they make purchase decisions compared to their peers in the past. Online reviews and sales volume information constitute two examples of information that are easily accessible today, and they afect consumer purchase decisions diferently. While sales volume information may be influenced by random factors and could be misleading, online reviews can provide more detailed information that reduces both quality uncertainty (vertically) and location uncertainty (horizontally). Does more information (online reviews in addition to sales volume information) always benefit consumers? If not, under what conditions? How should firms react to it? By building a duopoly economic model incorporating “irregular consumers,” this paper makes a first attempt to study the joint impact of these two types of information.

We find that, clearly, the impacts of online reviews and sales volume information on firms’ profits are mutually enhancing. As one information source demonstrates a strong signal, the impact of the other information on the firm’s profit increases. Thus, the firm relies less on the other information source to generate the same profit level. Moreover, the impact of such market-generated information depends on both product characteristics (the level of consumers’ misfit costs for the nonpreferred product) and how consumers a priori believe that one product has a significantly higher quality level than the other. When consumers’ misfit cost increases and the product becomes more horizontally diferentiated, online review information has a stronger impact, while sales volume information has a weaker impact, on firms’ profits.

Contrary to conventional wisdom that consumers are better of making more informed decisions, we find that when firms take into consideration the impact of market-generated information and are able to proactively adjust prices, the benefit of more/accurate information may be ofset by higher prices, and consumers may be worse of. The presence of online reviews can better reveal the true quality diference between firms, so the high-quality firm has less pressure to cut its prices in the first period. More informative reviews reveal more horizontal features of firms and reduce competition between firms, thus the low-quality firm is able to set higher prices in the second period. As a result, consumers may be worse of if buying the same product at higher prices.

More/accurate information may not benefit firms, either. First, more information (e.g., online reviews) may harm the high-quality firm while benefiting the low-quality firm when online reviews are less favorable than sales volume information. Second, realizing that consumers rely on market-generated information, firms have a large incentive to cut prices initially to attract more consumers. These price-cutting eforts, however, may not be worthwhile in the second period in the presence of “irregular consumers.” Third, even though online reviews may reveal the true quality difference benefiting the high-quality firm, because informative information may drive some of its consumers to buy from the low-quality firm instead, they actually benefit the low-quality firm.

Our paper makes several contributions to the literature:

First, we strengthen the assertion that firms may not always successfully implement a penetration price strategy under the influence of online reviews and sales volume information (Proposition 1). It is a wellknown strategy for firms to cut prices initially for a low (perceived) quality product (Shapiro 1983) or for a niche product (Bergemann and Välimäki 2006). The uncertainty in sales volume information caused by irregular consumers, however, weakens the efectiveness of firms’ pricing strategies, because firms may not successfully generate suficient sales volume. Fortunately, since online reviews signal the true quality diference on average, they reduce the impact of uncertainty from irregular consumers and help the firm with the quality advantage to set higher prices. When consumers have stronger prior belief toward the highquality firm, the impact of online reviews and sales volume information on firms’ profit is strengthened, and firms rely less on market-generated information. Consequently, they do not need to depend heavily on enlarging sales volume by cutting prices initially. As a result, stronger prior belief reduces the high-quality firm’s reliance on the penetration strategy, especially when the online reviews are so informative.

Second, we incorporate two diferent types of market-generated information and focus on their joint impact. Previous literature has focused mostly on the impact of online reviews and found that online reviews can benefit the high-quality firm in a duopoly setting (Kwark et al. 2014) or harm the firm, because firms need to cut prices to attract positive reviews (Li and Hitt 2010) or because of their informativeness (Chen and Xie 2008, Lin et al. 2011). By considering both online reviews and sales volume information, we enrich this line of literature in the following three ways: (1) we ofer new insights into precisely why online reviews may not be beneficial (for example, when the signals delivered from online reviews are inconsistent with the sales volume information, or when price cuts are inefective in the presence of irregular consumers); (2) our duopolistic setting enables us to articulate the impact of information on diferent types of products (more horizontally diferentiated versus more vertically diferentiated) and on firms with diferent quality levels; and (3) we are able to identify the joint impact of these two types of information and find that the impact of market-generated information depends on both consumers’ prior beliefs of the quality diference and to what degree the products are horizontally diferentiated.

This paper is not without limitations, and future extension is desired. First, in this version, the informativeness of online reviews is treated as an exogenous variable and is independent of the precision of reviews. It would be interesting to relax this assumption and study the scenario in which the outcome of online reviews depends on the price of the product. Second, it would be worthwhile to explore the equilibrium prices in a longer horizon, i.e., more than two periods. Both the first-period and last-period prices of the firms would be similar to those in the two-period case. Prices in the intermediate time periods, however, need to reflect consumers’ perceptions about the quality diference, as well as the interaction between the online reviews and sales volume information from the previous period. It would be interesting to explore the price patterns in such a multiperiod framework. Finally, it would be worthwhile to conduct empirical studies to test the validity of our theoretical findings.
