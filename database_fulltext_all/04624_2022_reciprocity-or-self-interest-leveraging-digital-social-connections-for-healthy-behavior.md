# RECIPROCITY OR SELF-INTEREST? LEVERAGING DIGITAL SOCIAL CONNECTIONS FOR HEALTHY BEHAVIOR<sup>1</sup>

Che-Wei Liu Kelley School of Business, Indiana University Bloomington, Bloomington, IN 47405 U.S.A. {cwliu@iu.edu}

Guodong (Gordon) Gao Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, College Park, MD 20742 U.S.A. {ggao@umd.edu}

Ritu Agarwal Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, College Park, MD 20742 U.S.A. {ragarwal@umd.edu}

In this paper, we examine the role of reciprocity enabled by digital social platforms for offline healthy behavior. Although reciprocity is a fundamental aspect of human psychology, its application in promoting healthy behavior has been limited. We conduct a randomized field experiment with over 1,700 pairs of users on a mobile social network platform. Individuals in the reciprocity treatment group receive a gift from their friends and are asked to return this favor by participating in a running challenge. Their performance is compared to the self-interest incentives widely used in practice. Building on social exchange theory, we argue that reciprocity-based incentives will outperform self-interest incentives with modest reward for motivating behavior change. We find that, on average, reciprocity is stronger than self-interest in inducing exercise behavior by a substantial amount. Furthermore, our results reveal that the magnitude of the reciprocity effect is contingent on the social closeness between senders and receivers. Interestingly, social closeness has an inverted U-shaped influence on the reciprocity effect. The effect is strongest when closeness is moderate, and wanes when closeness is either too strong or too weak. Compared to commonly used self-interest based financial incentives, our findings offer a potentially more powerful avenue for mechanism design in promoting healthy behavior.

Keywords: Reciprocity, self-interest, healthy behavior, social connections, social closeness, social exchange theory

## Introduction

It is widely acknowledged that unhealthy lifestyles such as a poor diet, physical inactivity, and alcohol and tobacco abuse have adverse effects on health and can lead to substantial economic costs (Cunningham et al. 2020; Ding et al. 2016).

To curb the rapid growth in healthcare expenditure, numerous stakeholders, including the government, employers, and health planners are increasingly emphasizing incentivization of healthy behavior (Baicker et al. 2010; Kim et al. 2016). Financial incentives that invoke self-interest motivations have been extensively studied in a variety of settings such as weight loss, smoking cessation, and physical activity (Barte and Wendel-Vos 2017; Charness and Gneezy 2009; John et al. 2011). However, despite the popularity of such incentive programs, empirical findings are equivocal (Gneezy et al. 2011):

although several studies show encouraging results (Halpern et al. 2015; Volpp et al. 2009), others (e.g., Cawley and Price 2013) report dropout rates as high as 68%. Astrella’s (2017) review of the literature from 2000 to 2016 shows that the impact of workplace wellness programs is inconclusive, and Blumenthal et al. (2013) note the ineffectiveness of financial incentive programs provided by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, calling for more investigations on improving the design of such interventions.

In contrast to self-interest based incentives, we propose and test a new mechanism, reciprocity, to promote healthy behavior. Reciprocity is a type of social rule that underscores the importance of repaying what others have provided. It encapsulates a fundamental aspect of human behavior and has been suggested as a universal norm that most societies endorse (Gouldner 1960). We test whether the inclination to reciprocate motivates individuals to engage in healthy behaviors, especially compared to the frequently used self-interest incentive with modest reward. Our work aims to leverage individuals’ existing social connections to trigger reciprocal behavior. Given that social connections can be quite heterogeneous among friends, we further seek to identify the relational conditions under which reciprocity works best.

Surprisingly, despite multiple studies demonstrating the significant role of reciprocity in human behavior (Alpizar et al. 2008; Falk 2007; Fehr and Gächter 2000), the reciprocity mechanism has seldom been incorporated into healthpromoting programs. This is likely due to at least two challenges. One, there are limited means to effectively enable interactions among users, which is a necessary condition for reciprocity. Triggering reciprocity through individuals’ existing social connections can be very costly in an offline setting. Indeed, prior research on reciprocity mostly utilizes a third party who is unknown to the subjects for generating a reciprocal response (see a summary of prior work in Appendix Table A1). And two, it is challenging to study reciprocity in the health context due to limited observability of the outcome. Traditional methods for capturing healthy behavior rely on self-reports, which are not conducive to precise tracking of physical activity. Furthermore, detailed tracking of social connections and physical activity simultaneously can easily become cost-prohibitive for a large sample. As a result, extant understanding of how to take advantage of reciprocity to promote healthy behavior is limited.

We leverage recent advances in information technologies to address the above two challenges. The maturity of wearable devices and smartphones enables precise tracking of physical activity at a relatively low cost. Further, widespread proliferation of online social networks and mHealth platforms has greatly facilitated interactions among individuals. The ease of capturing such interactions makes reciprocity-based incentives more viable today. Additionally, the maturity of GPS technologies on smartphones and sports watches has increased the observability of individuals’ physical activities. When coupled with digital social networks that enable connections at an unprecedented scale, it is now feasible to leverage reciprocity for promoting healthy behavior, as illustrated in recent studies that combine a fitness application with individuals’ online social networks, exploring how this unique context affects users’ physical activity (Gui et al. 2017).

There are three significant differences between our work and prior literature that examines the effects of reciprocity in various contexts such as donations (Alpizar et al. 2008; Falk 2007), labor markets (Falk et al. 1999; Gneezy and List 2006; Kube et al. 2012), and marketing (Maréchal and Thöni 2019). First, as a result of the challenges described above, previous studies have rarely used reciprocity directly to promote healthy behaviors that need to be precisely tracked. Our study extends the scope of reciprocity to health promotion, a major challenge faced by modern society. We take advantage of the capabilities offered by online social networks and digital connections to demonstrate the viability of using reciprocity as a mechanism for healthy behavior change.

Second, studies that explore reciprocity largely do so by manipulating the form of gifts. For instance, Falk (2007) shows that the donations increase by 17% in a small gift treatment and by 75% in a larger gift treatment as compared to a no-gift control. Other work (Kube et al. 2012) suggests that a nonmonetary gift is a more effective trigger for invoking reciprocity than a monetary gift of equivalent value. The subsequent behaviors are used to quantify the magnitude of reciprocity when comparing the gift treatment and the no-gift control. As a result of the treatments examined in these studies, little is known about the relative performance of reciprocity versus the widely used self-interest motivation; we are able to quan tify performance differences.

Finally, most previous literature does not examine the strength of connection between the sender and the receiver on the receiver’s reciprocal behaviors, probably because the sender in prior work has typically been an unrelated third party. In our study, by making a receiver’s online friend the gift giver, we are able to explore how social closeness, the depth of the interpersonal relationship between a dyad, affects the power of reciprocity. Following previous literature, we treat social closeness as social familiarity with another person (Linke 2012), which has been used to distinguish relationship categories, such as a close friend versus a stranger (Aron et al. 1992).

We conducted a large randomized controlled field experiment with over 1,700 pairs of users in an online physical activity community of runners in Taiwan. In the experiment, we encourage individuals to complete a physical activity challenge (e.g., run 30 or 45 km in two weeks). We address two research questions related to the efficacy of reciprocity as a mechanism for healthy behavior change:

(1) Do reciprocity-based incentives compare favorably with the widely used self-interest incentives with modest reward in promoting healthy behavior?

(2) Since reciprocity is a form of a social rule to repay favors, how does social closeness influence the effectiveness of a reciprocity-based incentive?

Previewing our findings, our field experiment yields several novel results. Compared to the traditional self-interest based incentive program, a reciprocity-based incentive initiated by friends leads to a sizeable 31.4% increase in challenge completion rate. Even after controlling for the gift giver’s effect, the reciprocity-based design still outperforms the self-interest incentive with modest reward by 19.8% in challenge completion rate. With these results, our study offers compelling evidence for the value of leveraging reciprocity by using mobile technologies in promoting healthy behavior. We further find that the magnitude of the reciprocity effect is contingent on the social closeness between senders and receivers. Interestingly, social closeness has an inverted U-shaped influence on the reciprocity effect. The effect is strongest when closeness is moderate and attenuates when closeness is either too strong or too weak.

## Prior Literature and Hypotheses

Our work builds on an emerging stream of work that uses mobile technologies to deliver interventions that can motivate healthy behaviors and improve disease management (Free et al. 2013). mHealth systems that leverage the power of mobile devices and social networks offer considerable promise in healthcare. For example, mHealth can help manage chronic conditions such as diabetes and obesity (Dugas et al. 2018; Estrin and Sim 2010; Ghose et al. 2022) or promote preventative healthy behaviors such as physical activity, weight control, and wellness (Ganesan et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2019). Our work is also related to the concept of using gamification elements in a mHealth setting to motivate healthy behavior changes. These elements include leaderboards to motivate physical activity (number of steps) (Hydari et al. 2019), or points to incentivize users to achieve step goals (Patel et al. 2019). Looyestyn et al. (2017) conducted a systematic review to examine if gamification can effectively engage users on online platforms (including in a health context), identifying a variety of gamification elements ranging from points, badges, reward, leaderboard, progress, social comparison, goals, levels, and achievements. Their review concludes that gamification is effective in increasing user engagement in online programs, and calls for more rigorous design to quantify the effect of gamification. Our work departs from these prior studies by proposing a new mechanism, reciprocity, to increase users’ motivation for physical activity and contributes to this stream of work by conducting a field experiment to quantify its effect.

Further, this study fits within a growing body of literature that seeks to directly compare social or behavioral interventions with financial incentives to address the challenge of selfcontrol (Ashraf et al. 2014; Kast et al. 2012). We aim to induce an inclination to repay favors as a motivation for behavior change. Below, we briefly review the stream of work in reciprocity that provides the conceptual background for our study. We then build on a foundation of social exchange theory (SET) (Blau 1964; Emerson 1976; Homans 1961) to propose two hypotheses. First, we discuss why reciprocity might outperform self-interest to motivate individuals healthy behavior changes. Second, we turn to the effects of individuals’ social connections and present arguments for why the strength of social closeness is expected to influence the efficacy of reciprocity.

## Reciprocity

The concept of reciprocity emerges from the theory of gift exchange, which has its roots in literature from anthropology. The classic essay by Mauss and Halls (1954/2000) investigates the gift exchange process in a primitive society that involves a series of actions: to give, to receive, and, more importantly, to reciprocate. One may give a return gift for the purpose of maintaining a social balance or fairness, showing gratitude, or rewarding generosity (Kolm 2006), and sometimes might even overcompensate the cost of the initial gift. Gift exchange engenders reciprocity because it helps agents to signal social distance and distinguish friends from nonfriends.

Individuals reciprocate for three primary reasons: conforming to a universal norm, expressing gratitude, and avoiding psychological distress. First, reciprocity is essential to form human relationships (Gudeman 2001). Such a norm has long been imprinted in individuals and internalized in most societies (Gouldner 1960). Yan (2003) suggests that failure to fulfill the obligations of reciprocity violates ethics and may be viewed as immoral. Second, people reciprocate to show gratitude, an emotional appreciation for received benefits. Komter (2004) describes gratitude as an imperative force that compels us to return the received benefits. Palmatier et al. (2009) show that marketers’ relationship management leads to customers’ gratitude in such a way that it amplifies customers’ purchase intentions. Finally, failure to reciprocate may be costly as it generates psychological distress in the form of feelings of guilt (Becker 1990). For instance, Dahl et al. (2005) show that customers experience guilt when they do not purchase from a salesperson after establishing temporary social connectedness.

In the information systems literature, researchers have viewed open source, online communities, and knowledge sharing through the lens of a gift economy (Bergquist and Ljungberg 2001). Wasko and Faraj (2005) discuss why individuals help strangers in computer-mediated discussion forums even when they know that their help would not be directly reciprocated. Reciprocity between two agents is often difficult because of the anonymous nature of interaction in the online forum. However, when individuals offer advice in such forums, the contributor does not expect direct reciprocation but rather expects the receivers to contribute to someone else someday. This form of reciprocity is labeled “generalized exchange” (Ekeh 1974). Such sharing in the online environment is different from traditional gift exchange (Smith and Kollock 1999); knowledge as a form of a public good in the online forum reflects shared moral obligation for generalized reciprocity and prosocial behavior rather than self-interest (Wasko and Faraj 2000). Similar to Wasko and Faraj (2005), Kankanhalli et al. (2005), in their study of contributions to electronic knowledge repositories used in organizations, argue that reciprocity should be viewed as a benefit because individuals can expect future help from others. The power of reciprocity is expected to be especially strong when an organization does not establish a strong pro-sharing norm.

Lampel and Bhalla (2007) also discuss knowledge sharing as a form of gift-giving in online virtual communities. The authors argue, echoing prior work (Kankanhalli et al. 2005; Wasko and Faraj 2000), that social-status seeking should be an important reason for individuals to contribute opinions and information online. In addition, online peer-to-peer (P2P) barter markets that have gained momentum in recent years further support such interactions. Ye et al. (2018) investigate how reciprocal relationships, manifested through prior interactions, affect transactions in the online P2P barter market. They find that the use of reciprocal relationships provides noteworthy value to the market, especially for avid users, by reducing transaction costs, and resulting in a lower rejection rate.

In the healthcare domain, gift giving behaviors can be commonly observed in the form of incentivized wellness programs in organizations. An incentive wellness program aims to reduce health costs and improve workers’ well-being and productivity by providing inducements (Gubler et al. 2018). In such programs, employers act as gift givers, typically providing monetary incentives to employees (gift receivers). In return, employees participate in such programs, and consequently reduce medical costs for companies. Most wellness programs focus on healthy behaviors, such as exercise, diet, weight loss, and smoking cessation (Astrella 2017; Baicker et al. 2010; Naydeck et al. 2008; Osilla et al. 2012). Two trends in incentivized wellness programs are noteworthy. One, the effects of wellness programs are inconclusive: while Baicker et al. (2010) report benefits in terms of reducing medical costs, Blumenthal et al. (2013) note the ineffectiveness of financial incentive-based programs. And two, the use of wellness programs is accelerating, with online platforms increasingly being used to orchestrate and deliver them (Adjerid et al. 2018; Yan and Tan 2018).

In healthcare, reciprocity is mostly viewed through the lens of social support (Berkman and Glass 2000; Yan 2020), an important component in promoting health. Jou and Fukada (2002) show that individuals whose amount of social support received equals the amount provided are in a balanced status of reciprocity and are more mentally and physically healthy than imbalanced individuals. Abbott and Freeth (2008) argue that trust and reciprocity are two core components of social capital, and thus have potential to protect against chronic stress by reducing anxiety. Chandola et al. (2007) show that lack of reciprocity in close relationships such as among partners or for a parent with a child is associated with poorer selfreported physical and mental health.

## Reciprocity Versus Self-Interest

Although reciprocity has been examined in the contexts discussed above, the relative performance of reciprocity versus the widely used self-interest motivation is less understood. A notable exception is presented in Chung and Narayandas (2017) that compares reciprocity and incentives on salespersons’ performance. The focus of their work is to compare unconditional compensation (gifts) and conditional compensation. Different from their setting, individuals in our treatment and control conditions all receive unconditional gifts to begin with, and our study aims to test whether having a chance to reciprocate to senders can outperform self-interest with modest reward in motivating behavior changes related to physical activity.

Why would we expect reciprocity to be a more useful mechanism than a modest reward for oneself (i.e., self-interest) to motivate behavior change? Gifting is a form of social exchange because it helps to establish and maintain social relationships between a pair of individuals (Belk 1979). Therefore, we draw on SET (Blau 1964; Emerson 1976; Homans 1961), to examine differences between reciprocitybased and self-interest based incentives. According to SET, exchanges between dyads result in economic and social outcomes. A core tenet of SET is that both parties implement a cost-benefit analysis to determine how to interact (Sabatelli and Shehan 2009), seeking to maximize benefits while minimizing costs in the interaction. In other words, people calculate the net worth of a specific relationship by subtracting its costs from the benefits (Monge and Contractor 2003).

A recent study, related to our work, applies SET to examine gift-giving behaviors in a social network service (SNS) platform (Kim et al. 2018). The authors find that the perceived worth, gifting experience in SNS, and the number of friends of SNS affect the frequency of SNS gifting. Likewise, we use SET and its invocation of costs and benefits as drivers of behavior to unravel the differences between alternative incentives. We experimentally control for all costs (the same running challenge for all participants); thus, in our setting, self-interest and reciprocity are distinct only because of the benefits they confer on the individual.

There are clear differences in the benefits of the two mechanisms. In the self-interest mechanism, individuals make a formal contract with the platform by accepting the challenge. Failure to adhere to contract terms would result in a loss of the chance to earn rewards. Therefore, the benefit for the selfinterest group for participants is to earn the rewards for themselves. By contrast, in the reciprocity mechanism, individuals also make a formal contract with the platform when accepting the challenge, except that in this situation, failure to adhere to the contract results in a lost opportunity to reciprocate the reward to the senders. As noted previously, prior research shows that the reasons individuals reciprocate are to conform to the norm, express gratitude, and to avoid psychological distress in the form of feelings of guilt (Becker 1990; Gudeman 2001). Therefore, in addition to earning a monetary reward for a friend, the benefits in the reciprocity group also include a reduction in the burden of psychological debt and an avenue for expressing appreciation.

There are reasons to believe that the benefit of avoiding feelings of guilt, as induced in the reciprocity group, can outperform the modest reward in the self-interest group. In an influential study by Gneezy and Rustichini (2000), a small fine was introduced in day-care centers when parents came late to collect their children. The deterrence hypothesis predicts that the introduction of a penalty should reduce the prevalence of parents who arrive late. Unexpectedly, the number of parents arriving late to pick up their children increased rather than declined. Before introducing the fine, parents faced the disapproval of teachers that might generate a feeling of guilt. However, the introduction of fines represents a price for being late. A sharp increase in the late arrival rate indicates that they would prefer to pay a small amount of money rather than face social or psychological pressure. Studies have also observed a similar crowding-out effect in blood donations (Titmuss 1970) and in other contexts, such as hosting a noxious facility (Frey and Oberholzer-Gee 1997). Janssen and Mendys-Kamphorst (2004) show that introducing financial incentives for individuals who care about social approval would decrease their contributions. This means that the power of financial incentives, especially when they are modest, is inferior to the power of social approval to motivate contributions. In our case, we conjecture that the psychological debt of failing to reciprocate rewards to friends would dominate the desire to earn a modest reward for oneself, motivating more individuals to complete the challenge. Specifically, we propose that:

H1: A reciprocity-based incentive can increase the probability of completing a physical activity challenge more than a self-interest based incentive based on a modest reward.

## The Role of Social Closeness in Reciprocity

Thus far, we have argued that reciprocity will outperform selfinterest with modest reward because of greater perceived benefits. Because reciprocity is fundamentally a relational mechanism, we now turn to theorize about the effects of the nature of the relationship between the reciprocating dyad. The importance of social connections between a pair of users in an online social network is highlighted in substantial research showing that connections are related to behaviors such as adoption, and trust. For instance, by using data on communication among one million customers in a cellular company, Nitzan and Libai (2011) show that individuals are more likely to defect from the service provider if connected to a defecting neighbor, and such effects are influenced by tie strength, indicating that the effects would be positively moderated by the closeness between the focal users and defecting neighbor. Bapna et al. (2017) use a customized Facebook application to explore how social ties can be linked with trust between pairs of users, and find support for such a relationship. They also find that different types of tie strength might not always be effective for users with a different number of social connections (high versus low numbers of Facebook friends), highlighting the nuance of choosing the right metric to represent tie strength on social network platforms.

Despite the growing body of research supporting the effects of social connections on behaviors, the effects of social closeness on the power of reciprocity have not been empirically tested. This oversight is likely because it is costly and hard to leverage individuals’ social connections without the help of modern technologies and social network platforms. In this study, we address this gap and explore how social closeness influences the effects of reciprocity. In a study related to ours, Chen et al. (2009) investigate how the relationship between a sender and a receiver (friends versus strangers) could affect the power of reciprocity. They find, in a laboratory experiment setting using an ultimatum game, that when participants receive favorable treatment, theyreciprocate positively, but the nature of their relationship does not matter. The result is somewhat surprising because, according to Fehr and Gächter (2000), positive reciprocity has long been embedded in the society, and the receipt of favors creates “feelings of indebtedness obliging many people to repay the psychological debt” (p. 161). As noted earlier, failure to reciprocate might generate psychological distress in the form of feelings of guilt (Becker 1990), that is unlikely to be uniformly distributed among all pairs of users. Thus, one plausible explanation for this seemingly conflicting finding may be that measurement of the social relationships between a pair of users in the study by Chen et al. (2009) is too coarse and one needs to treat social closeness as existing along a continuum.

As in Hypothesis 1, we draw upon SET that suggests individuals implement a cost-benefit analysis to determine how to interact with others. While everyone faces the same challenge to earn the rewards in the reciprocity treatment group, their benefits might be different. We argued previously that the benefits to the reciprocity group comprise both tangible (a prize) and intangible rewards (alleviation of psychological distress and expression of gratitude). While the tangible reward is the same, the intangible element is likely to differ based on the relationship between senders and receivers. We conjecture that the desire to reciprocate will be small among remote friends, and will increase with the degree of closeness. Because people reciprocate for long-term outcomes via future interactions (Homans 1961), they have limited aspiration for distant friends with less chance of interactions in the future (Fiske 1992). As a consequence, the receiver’s desire to reciprocate is dampened for distant friends, and will grow as the social closeness increases. For closer friends, receivers feel more indebted toward senders until they have repaid the favors, and worry that failure to do so may endanger future interactions. For a very close friend, however, the perception of indebtedness will likely wane. This is because, for very close friends, the receivers and senders already have an intimate bond. In other words, the receiver believes that the relationship will persist even in the absence of a return gift. Therefore, both the level of gratitude as well as the experience of guilt may not be potent enough to invoke a stronger effect of reciprocity. In addition, due to the more frequent interactions among close friends, the receiver can find other ways to pay back, and the obligation to reciprocate may be attenuated.

To summarize, we propose that there will be an inverted-U relationship between social closeness and the effects of reciprocity. Reciprocity’s effect would be the strongest for receivers who receive gifts from a sender who is moderately close to them. When viewed through the lens of a long-term relationship, moderate closeness would invoke a higher inclination to maintain the relationship compared to a distant one (no desire) or a very close one (no need to do so, because the relationship is already close). For psychological debt, the level of guilt or appreciation would be low for a distant one (do not care) or a very close one (do not afraid to affect relationship). Therefore, while the costs are uniform for all individuals, the benefits gained by repaying a favor would be the strongest for moderately close friends, leading us to propose:

H2: As the closeness between a pair of users increases, the probability of completing the challenge first increases and then decreases, that is, there is an inverted-U shaped relationship between challenge completion and closeness for individuals in the reciprocity treatment group.

## Research Methodology

## Study Method

## Study Context

We conducted a randomized field experiment on a mobile physical activity platform for runners, henceforth referred to as RunningPlatform to maintain anonymity. The platform provides a Twitter-like capability that allows individuals to follow or be followed by others. Using this feature, we are able to find pairs of friends, a necessary condition to test dyadic reciprocity. To ensure the quality of the randomization process, we assign users to groups based on their online identification numbers (user\_id). We divide the online ID by three and use the remainder to assign them into one of the three groups to achieve randomness. Participants in the study are not aware of the existence of our experiment, which helps avoid the well-known “Hawthorne effect” (Adair 1984).

## Experimental Design

To test the effects of reciprocity, we design an experiment that follows a standard gift exchange process that involves a pair of users: to give, to receive, and then to reciprocate. We first describe how we identify user dyads, one of whom serves as a sender and the other as a receiver. We observe individuals running performance two weeks before the intervention date, and use a threshold of 40 km to identify potential senders.<sup>2</sup> When users log into the platform, the system examines their running performance to determine if they qualify as senders. Figure 1 illustrates the process of randomization and the corresponding sample size in each group. Once the system identifies a qualified user, it automatically checks whether the user has at least one qualified receiver or not. A qualified receiver should be the sender’s online friend<sup>3</sup> and should have run less than 40 km in the past two weeks during the pretreatment period. After passing this criterion, a qualified user is directed to a page that describes the information presented in Table 1. On this page, we ask whether they would like to help their friends by forwarding a physical activity related challenge invitation to them. If a sender agrees to forward the message, the system randomly selects a friend who ran less than 40 km in the pretreatment period from the sender’s friend list.<sup>4</sup> This process ensures that the receivers are relatively weak runners. The threshold for differentiating between senders and receivers ensures that the same individual is never selected as both a sender and a receiver, which could potentially create a confound. Furthermore, since weak runners are the ones most in need of motivation, we deliberately select weak runners to receive the challenge. Indeed, it is a common practice to use capable users as a role model to motivate less capable ones because capable individuals can demonstrate how to perform a skill and exhibit that a goal is attainable and desirable (Morgenroth et al. 2015).<sup>5</sup>

To ensure independence across the dyads, each sender is only paired with one receiver. Each sender–receiver pair is then randomly assigned to one of three groups after senders’ consent. We take extra precautions to minimize confounding factors, so that we are able to measure the causal impact of the different messages. First, to prevent any possible influence of the sender on the receiver, we only inform the senders about the receivers’ online identity, but NOT the content of the challenge for the receivers. The sender does not know which treatment group the receiver is in, nor does the sender know whether a receiver accepts the challenge or not, or whether the challenge is completed or not. This design, therefore, reduces the concern that a sender in the treatment group exerts pressure on the receiver in order to get the virtual gold coin. Second, once a pair of users has been decided, the system sends a push notification to the receiver. The title of the push notification is identical for the three groups.<sup>6</sup> Our pairing period is two weeks. Understandably, not all the receivers will log into the platform during the pairing period. In our analysis, we focus on those who log in, as they are the ones exposed to the different message design. Since a receiver gets exactly the same push notification before logging in to the platform and would not know the full message until logging in, the process ensures that there are no systematic differences in the final sample across the three groups (Figure 1 shows the detailed information).

When a receiver logs in to the platform, the receiver is directed to a page with the information presented in Table 2. For receivers in all groups, upon receiving the message, they can choose to accept or decline the challenge.<sup>7</sup> We implement the following experimental manipulations. In the first group (the treatment group), the receiver receives a virtual gold coin from the sender (a gift) and is offered the chance to participate in a running challenge to earn a gold coin to give back to the sender. The virtual gold coin represents a chance to enter a lottery drawing, which provides rewards such as \$200 sports watches, \$150 sports shoes, \$50 T-shirts, and \$30 socks, as shown in Figure 2. We label this group the Frnd-Recip group, for Friend-sender and Reciprocity-based incentive. In the second group (the friend control group), the receiver receives a gold coin from the sender and is given a chance to participate in the same challenge, but in this group, they get to keep the second gold coin for themselves, which doubles the chance of winning the lottery. We call this group the Frnd-SelfInt group, for Friend-sender and Self-interest based incentive. In both groups, the second gold coin earned by completing the challenge is provided by the platform; thus, the only difference between the groups is the beneficiary of the extra coin: the sender (Frnd-Recip group) or self (Frnd-SelfInt group). Our goal is to examine which mechanism will be more powerful in motivating receivers to complete the challenge.

We add a third group (the conventional financial incentive program group), in which the receivers receive a virtual gold coin (gift) from the platform and have a chance to participate in the same challenge to earn a second coin for themselves. This is the Plat-SelfInt group, for Platform-sender and Selfinterest based incentive. We include this group to generate insights on the practical significance of our new intervention compared to a conventional incentive program. Although the Frnd-SelfInt group is the ideal control group to account for the gift giver effect and helps us isolate the effect of reciprocity, most conventional incentive programs do not use individuals’ social connections. Instead, a traditional incentive program would typically offer enrollment rewards from a third-party (similar to a platform gift in the Plat-SelfInt group) and then provide incentives for participants once they complete a challenge. For instance, Acland and Levy (2015) provide a first-week attendance reward and follow-up incentives for gym attendance and Charness and Gneezy (2009) offer a show-up fee and follow-up incentives for gym attendance.<sup>8</sup> By comparing the Plat-SelfInt and Frnd-SelfInt groups, we are able to quantify whether changing who the sender is from a third-party (platform) to one’s friend affects the receiver’s performance. The Plat-SelfInt group thus serves as a basis for comparison, helping us measure the overall effects of a friend’s gift and the effect of reciprocity when compared with the Frnd-Recip group.<sup>9</sup>

Participants in all three groups receive the same challenge. According to the 2008 “Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans,” individuals gain health benefits if they exercise for at least 150 minutes per week. One can gain additional health benefits when increasing aerobic physical activity to 300 minutes. The RunningPlatform typically uses total distance over a period of time to design a challenge. We thus use total distance over two weeks in our design.<sup>10</sup> Based on these guidelines, we aim for the very weak runners to attain the minimum level and moderately weak runners to gain additional benefit by reaching a higher level. Accordingly, we ask receivers who have run less than 25 km (very weak runners) in the pretreatment period to complete a 30 km challenge over a two-week period, and receivers who have run between 25 km and 40 km (moderately weak runners) in the pretreatment period to complete a 45 km challenge over a two-week period. We avoid using a customized goal for each individual because our objective is to promote physical activity that meets recommended guidelines.

## Timeline

We conducted the experiment over six weeks in July, August, and September 2017. In the pretreatment period (July 24– August 6), we recorded all individuals’ running distances to be used for identifying senders and receivers in the next stage. During the pairing period (August 7–August 20), we identified qualified senders and receivers. Once receivers agree to accept the challenge, they undertake the challenge during the treatment period (August 21–September 3). Each period is exactly two weeks.

One day before the treatment period (at 9:00 a.m. on August 20), we sent a reminder message to each of the participants in all three groups about their upcoming challenge. A reminder message is a very common strategy that has been extensively used in prior studies (Acland and Levy 2015; John et al. 2011; Schofield et al. 2015). We followed this strategy to encourage users to complete the challenge. The message communicated the time period for the challenge, the distance they were supposed to run, and the list of rewards. Each group gets unique information about the beneficiary (i.e., the winner of the second gold coin) after completing the challenge. ## Sample Description

We conducted a power analysis to determine the minimum number of participants needed for sufficient power to detect the treatment effect. According to Burtch et al.’s (2017) report of related field experiments, the average Cohen’s d is 0.47 in the power test. This would require a minimum sample of 73 subjects for each group for a desired statistical power of

0.8 in a two-tailed test at alpha = 0.05. A sample of 253 participants is needed for each group if we use a very conservative Cohen’s d of 0.25. Our final sample for each group significantly exceeds these thresholds, as discussed below.

In total, 3,514 users qualified as senders, of which 2,744 agreed to forward the message to their friends (receivers) during the pairing period. Interestingly, a large majority (78.1%) were willing to serve as a sender to encourage their friends to participate in our challenge. ## Results

## Comparison of the Effects of Reciprocity Versus Self-Interest Based Incentives

Upon receiving the message, receivers can choose to accept or decline the challenge. The acceptance rates are 86.3% (Plat-SelfInt group), 82.7% (Frnd-SelfInt group), and 83.9% (Frnd-Recip group), respectively. Pairwise proportion tests show no statistical differences between acceptance rates. Major individual characteristics such as age, gender, tenure, and the number of friends are also statistically similar across groups. Nevertheless, as a further caution against a selection problem, we include the declined users in the analyses. Our dependent variable is Complete\_Challenge, which is equal to 1 when an individual’s total running distance during the treatment period is greater than the total distance of their predefined challenge (either 30 km or 45 km); 0 otherwise. Since declining the challenge indicates no chance to win the second gold coin, we treat this case as a failure to complete the challenge.

We find that 30.6% of individuals in the Plat-SelfInt group, 34.4% of individuals in the Frnd-SelfInt group, and 40.4% of individuals in the Frnd-Recip group complete the challenge. Further analysis using two sample tests of proportions (ztests) shows that the completion rate of the Frnd-Recip group is statistically higher than that of the Plat-SelfInt group (p < 0.01) and that of the Frnd-SelfInt group (p < 0.05). This indicates that the motivation to earn a gold coin for a friend (Frnd-Recip group) is stronger than the traditional incentive program (Plat-SelfInt group) and also greater than the motivation to earn a coin for oneself while controlling the effects of a friend’s gift (Frnd-SelfInt group). We also compare the completion rates between the Plat-SelfInt and Frnd-SelfInt groups and find that changing who the sender is increases the completion rate by 3.8% but the difference is not statistically significant. However, this result helps alleviate the concern that using a friend as a sender may exert a negative effect on motivating behavior change.

We formally test the treatment effect with a Probit model:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text { Complete\_Challenge} _ {i} ^ {*} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} * \text { Frnd } - \text { SelfInt } _ {i} + \\ \beta_ {2} * \text { Frnd } - \text { Recip } _ {i} + \Sigma \beta_ {r} Z _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i} \\ \text { Complete\_Challenge} _ {i} = 1 \Big [ \text { Complete\_Challenge} _ {i} ^ {*} > 0 \Big ] \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where Complete\_Challenge<sup>\*</sup> is the latent variable and Complete\_Challenge is the key dependent variable, indicating whether an individual completes the challenge or not. Frnd-SelfInt and Frnd-Recip each represent Frnd-SelfInt and Frnd-Recip groups, with Plat-SelfInt group as the default, baseline group. $\pmb { Z } _ { i }$ captures both senders’ and receivers characteristics, including pretreatment distance, the number of friends, gender (female), age, tenure, and the number of races participated, and the type of challenge (30 km or 45 km) that the participant was assigned to. $\varepsilon _ { i }$ is the error term for individual observation i.

The results are reported in Table 6 and support Hypothesis 1. In column (1), we find that the Frnd-Recip group’s completion rate is positive and significant (p < 0.01). The marginal effect of Frnd-Recip is 9.6 percentage points. Since the completion rate of the Plat-SelfInt group is 30.6%, the effect of reciprocity leads to a 31.4% increase in the completion rate. By comparing the Frnd-Recip and the Plat-SelfInt groups, we can ascertain how the new incentive design compares to a conventional incentive program. We conducted a Wald test between the Frnd-SelfInt and Frnd-Recip. The results are statistically significant at $\mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 5$ The difference in the marginal effect between the Frnd-Recip and Frnd-SelfInt groups is 6.8 points. Considering that the completion rate of the Frnd-SelfInt group is 34.4%, the effect of reciprocity leads to a 19.8% increase in the completion rate. The result of the Wald test shows that after controlling for the effect of the gift giver, the effect of reciprocity still outperforms an incentive scheme that is based on a self-interest mechanism.

Recall that we treat individuals who do not participate in our treatment as a failure in the main model. One potential concern may be that individuals who decline our invitation were also affected by the message in regard to their physical activity and therefore, treating them as failures might not be appropriate. To address this concern, we release this constraint by treating them in the same way as individuals who participate. As long as their running distance in the treatment period is greater than the challenge distance, we regard them as having completed the challenge. The completion rates after removing the constraint are 33.6%, 36.6%, and 42.4% in the Plat-SelfInt, Frnd-SelfInt, and Frnd-Recip groups, respectively. Table 6 column (2) shows the result. Similar to the main results, the Frnd-Recip group still outperforms the Plat-SelfInt groups (p < 0.01) and a Wald Test supports the conclusion that the Frnd-Recip group outperforms Frnd-SelfInt group (p < 0.05). Thus, our results are robust to alternate measures of Complete\_Challenge.

## The Curvilinear Relationship between Closeness and Reciprocity Effects

We conceptualize social closeness by considering the network structure. The number of common friends between senders and receivers (CommonFriends<sup>13</sup>) has long been used to measure the level of trust and cooperation in relationships (for further discussion, see Aral and Walker 2014; Hong et al. 2018; Peng et al. 2018). To measure CommonFriends, we compare all the friends of a pair comprised of a receiver and a sender and calculate the number of common friends shared by receivers and senders.

0.038, which confirms that there is an inverted U-shaped effect of on completion rate.<sup>14</sup>

Figure 4 illustrates this relationship. We can clearly discern that when senders and receivers are very distant or very close, the challenge completion rates are roughly 34% to 37%, respectively, but when senders and receivers are moderately close, the challenge completion rate rises above 44%. ## Addressing Imbalance in Pretreatment Distances

## Dealing with Imbalance Problem

Our randomization is based on individuals’ online user identification that should be orthogonal to individuals’ characteristics. Indeed, our randomization check (Table 5) indicates that the samples across the three groups are balanced, with only one out of 15 variables indicating a difference that is statistically significant. While a single difference across multiple variables is not unusual in randomization (for a longer discussion, see Bloom et al. 2014), to mitigate any concerns about balance, we examine the variable that shows significant differences. Individuals in the Frnd-SelfInt group run more than the Frnd-Recip group in the pretreatment period. One potential issue is that since runners’ performance is an important factor in challenge completion, the imbalance between the two groups could bias our findings. We, therefore, conduct additional analyses to address this issue. We also note that since individuals in the Frnd-SelfInt group have higher performance than individuals in the Frnd-Recip group, individuals completion challenge rate in the Frnd-SelfInt group should be higher than individuals in the Frnd-Recip group because it is easier to complete the challenge.<sup>15</sup> Therefore, if anything, this tends to underestimate the effects of reciprocity.

We perform two sets of analyses to further alleviate this concern: propensity score matching (PSM) and inverse probability weighting (IPW). We use PSM to match the Frnd-SelfInt and Frnd-Recip groups using individuals’ characteristics to calculate the propensity score in the first stage Probit model, including the pretreatment distance, the number of friends, gender (female), age, tenure, the number of races participated, and the types of challenge (30 km or 45 km) to which the participant was assigned. Before matching, individuals in the Frnd-SelfInt group run 20.13 km in the pretreatment period, which is higher than the 18.59 km of individuals in the Frnd-Recip group in the pretreatment period (p < 0.05). After matching, individuals in the Frnd-SelfInt and Frnd-Recip groups run 18.81 km and 19.29 km in the pretreatment period, respectively. The difference is not statistically significant.<sup>16</sup> We confirm that all the other characteristics are not statistically different in Table 8. Table 9 shows the regression results. In column (1), we see that individuals’ completion rate in the Frnd-Recip group is higher than that in the Frnd-SelfInt group (p < 0.05). The marginal effects show an 8.9 percentage point difference. Considering that the average completion rate in the matched Frnd-SelfInt group is 32.4%, the reciprocity treatment results in a 27.5% increase, which is even greater than what we estimated in the main model (19.8%).

Alternatively, we address the problem of imbalance by employing inverse-probability weighting (IPW). We use IPW to reduce the bias of unweighted estimators. We deployed a two-step approach to calculate IPW estimators: a Probit model to estimate inverse-probability weights by using individuals characteristics; followed by computing the average treatment effects using the estimated inverse-probability weights. After including IPW in the model, we find that the average treatment effect between the Frnd-SelfInt and Frnd-Recip groups is 7.0 percentage points (p < 0.05), which is consistent in magnitude to the results from the main model.

## Performance Comparisons

The purpose of our experiment is to examine if individuals increase their performance in terms of total running distances under different manipulations. Since there is an imbalance in pretreatment distances among groups, directly comparing individuals’ performance during the treatment period between groups might be misleading. As before, we first employ the PSM to find a matched sample. Results in Table 9 column (2) show that individuals’ performance<sup>17</sup> (total running distance in the treatment period) in the Frnd-Recip group is superior to that in the Frnd-SelfInt group when using the matched sample. On average, individuals in the Frnd-Recip group ran 3.06 km more than those in the Frnd-SelfInt group (p < 0.05). Considering the average distance that subjects in the Frnd-SelfInt group ran in the treatment period (26.55 km), this represents an 11.5% increase. Likewise, we employ IPW in the model and find that the average treatment effect between the Frnd-Recip and Frnd-SelfInt groups is 2.26 km.

In the results reported thus far, our findings remain consistent across a variety of models. We further conduct additional robustness checks for alternative explanations.

## Treatment Effects when Compared to Normal App Users

Our goal is to compare the reciprocity mechanism to one of the most commonly used incentives, self-interest. However, for practical purposes, it would be useful to consider an alternative control group, where users simply use the mobile application without receiving any messages related to our treatment. This pure baseline condition can help capture the overall effectiveness of the reciprocity design. To construct this comparison group, we identify individuals who are qualified friends of senders, but have not been chosen as receivers.

Since the receivers are randomly chosen, the unchosen ones serve as a valid baseline control group. In case the treated sample might be different from the normal app users, we use propensity score matching to match the treatment group with this sample using the pretreatment characteristics and show the results in Table 10 columns (1) and (2).

The results indicate that Frnd-Recip group outperforms this group by a substantial margin in terms of challenge completion rate and actual running distance.<sup>18</sup> The marginal effect indicates that the difference of completion rates between these two groups is 29.5%. Since the completion rate of this control group is 11.8%, we see that reciprocity leads to a sizable increase in the completion rate. Moreover, the average running distance for the control group is 19.6 km, the 9.54 km increase of running distance represents a 48.7% boost.

## Potential for Cheating Behavior

While an important aspect of the novelty of this study is our ability to track individuals’ running records using digital devices (smartphones and wearables), there may be a concern related to the potential for cheating. Despite the fact that the platform supports multiple devices, some users may develop a habit of using the traditional method, which is to manually input the distance they run through the web or mobile interface. To the degree these are self-reported, one might question the veracity of the data. To alleviate this concern, we retain individuals who only use digital devices to automatically record running distances and examine their outcomes. There are 9.3%, 9.2%, and 9.0% of individuals in the Plat-SelfInt, Frnd-SelfInt, and Frnd-Recip groups, respectively, who have some manual running records during the treatment period. The proportions are not statistically different. ## Retaining Persistent Weak Runners

A potential concern with our treatment is that we only observe individuals’ performance for two weeks and use this pretreatment performance as a criterion (below 40 km) to find the weak runners as receivers. Since it is possible that individuals’ running behaviors fluctuate across time, we might inadvertently choose a receiver who is actually a capable runner but taking a short break during the pretreatment period. However, this bias, if it exists, should be eliminated by randomization. Still, to address this concern, we trace individuals’ running behaviors back to eight weeks and only retain individuals who registered at least eight weeks before the treatment period. We apply the same criterion and include only those individuals whose average two-week running distance over eight weeks is smaller than 40 km in our subsample. The results of the subsample analyses are shown in Table 10 column (6) and indicate a statistically significant difference. Comparing to the average completion rate in the Frnd-SelfInt group (31.4%), being in the Frnd-Recip group increases completion rates by 22.3%. The results indicate that the treatment effect of reciprocity on persistent weak runners is as strong as the effect on the whole sample.

## Post-Treatment Surveys

Our main analyses have focused on directly comparing the effects of the treatments through observable, objective measures. To gain insights that may further enhance the interpretation of findings, we conducted a post-treatment survey that was distributed electronically to all individuals participating in the challenge immediately after the treatment period. A total of 526 participants completed the survey for an overall response rate of 35.9%. There are 33.9%, 39.5%, and 34.3% respondents in the Plat-SelfInt, Frnd-SelfInt, and Frnd-Recip groups, respectively.<sup>20</sup>

Using the survey data, we first explore what motivates individuals to participate in the challenge. We asked respondents to select all possible reasons from a pick-list of potential motivations in the Plat-SelfInt, Frnd-SelfInt, and Frnd-Recip groups.<sup>21</sup> For the Plat-SelfInt group, the list includes four general motivations, such as finding the challenge interesting and improving health. One additional motivation related to social comparison was added for the Frnd-SelfInt group, and two additional reciprocity related motivations were added for the Frnd-Recip group. In the Plat-SelfInt and Frnd-SelfInt groups, “this challenge is beneficial for my health” ranks as the top reason to participate (62.9% in the Plat-SelfInt and 62.7% in the Frnd-SelfInt groups), followed by “this challenge is interesting” (50.9% in the Plat-SelfInt and 56.5% in the Frnd-SelfInt groups). In the Frnd-Recip group, these two reasons receive an affirmative response from 53.0% (health benefit) and 49.4% (interesting activity) of the respondents, respectively. Besides general reasons to undertake the challenge, we added two items that related to reciprocity in the Frnd-Recip group: showing gratitude (Palmatier et al. 2009) or avoiding guilt (Dahl et al. 2005). There were 74 votes (44.6%) for “I feel guilty toward my friend who sends me a gold coin if I did not win a gold coin” and 107 votes (64.5%) for “I would like to express my gratitude to my friend who sends me a gold coin” among 166 respondents in Frnd-Recip group. The finding that a majority of participants in the Frnd-Recip group undertake the challenge as a way to express gratitude is especially beneficial to this online platform, because it helps strengthen the social connections between senders and receivers, an essential element for long-term health of participants and for the sustainability of the platform.<sup>22</sup>

To the extent that our study is motivated by the need to induce healthy behavior using interventions that are effective, it is important to understand if there are potential negative sideeffects associated with the treatments. One possible sideeffect of using reciprocity to motivate individuals is to incur negative feelings such as stress, frustration, or guilt if receivers cannot complete the challenge. We asked respondents to indicate how they felt during the challenge, including both negative and positive emotions such as finding the challenge stressful, experiencing self-doubt, feeling frustrated, experiencing guilt, and feeling joyful, energetic, and content (Shaver et al. 1987). Independent of their success in completing the challenge, there are no statistical differences between the Frnd-SelfInt and Frnd-Recip groups for both negative and positive emotions. These results alleviate the problem of possible side effects associated with the use of a reciprocity-based incentive.

Finally, in the main analysis, we used an objective measure to gauge social closeness. In the survey, we complement this measure with the “Inclusion of Other in the Self” (IOS) scale, which provides a subjective assessment of the perceived closeness of a relationship (Aron et al. 1992). We ask respondents (who are the receivers) to select the nature of the relationship between them and their sender “friend” in the experiment from seven graphs (see Appendix Figure A3). In each of the graphs, one circle refers to the participant and the other to the friend who sent the participant a gold coin. Different degrees of overlap correspond to different levels of perceived closeness in the relationship, quantified on a scale of 1 to 7. We use this measure to test the inverted U-shaped relationship the social closeness on reciprocity. Table 7 column (2) shows the results. The results are consistent; as before, we find an inverted U-shaped relationship (p < 0.05). Our finding of the key conditioning effect of closeness and the specific form of this effect is consistent across multiple observable and subjective measures, further enhancing confidence in the findings.

## Discussion and Conclusion

## Main Findings and Contributions

Researchers and policymakers are increasingly asking for more evidence in support of effective interventions for enhancing health behaviors (Blumenthal et al. 2013). We proposed a new mechanism, reciprocity, that leverages existing social connections on digital platforms, and empirically tested its effects in a randomized controlled trial with over 1,700 pairs of users. We find that reciprocity outperforms selfinterest with modest reward in terms of challenge completion rates and running distances. We show that a friend’s gift and a reciprocity-based incentive (Frnd-Recip group) can boost performance relative to a conventional incentive program (Plat-SelfInt group) by a striking 31.4%. We further find that, after controlling for the effects of a friend’s gift, a reciprocitybased incentive can still improve the challenge completion rate by 19.8% as compared to a self-interest incentive with modest reward. In summary, for an equivalent financial outlay, invoking reciprocal motivations incentivized more runners to exercise and complete the challenge than the other two groups. Our results provide strong evidence to support the effectiveness of a reciprocity-based incentive.

A key result from this study is that the effects of reciprocity are heterogeneous, conditional on the nature of the interpersonal relationship between the gift exchange dyad. We find an inverted U-shaped relationship of the social closeness between senders and receivers on challenge completion rates. We interpret these results through the lens of relationship maintenance. For distant friends, the aspiration to build a long-term relationship is low and for close friends, a strong relationship is already established with or without completing this challenge to maintain it. In this study, we randomly pick friends as receivers for senders to test the conditional effects of social closeness. In the absence of randomization, a reasonable conjecture is that a sender would choose a friend she/he is close to. However, our results show that such close pairs might not yield the best results because the effects are inferior to moderately close pairs.

Our study makes several important theoretical contributions. First, we extend existing evidence on the effects of reciprocity-based incentives to a novel and consequential context of healthy behavior. It is not clear whether prior findings can be readily generalized due to idiosyncrasies unique to this domain. Healthy behavior requires substantial effort; it is costlier to run 40 km than it is to, say, share a coupon code. Additionally, most existing studies on reciprocity examine activities that are either one-shot or span a short period of time. Indeed, Gneezy and List (2006) point out that the duration of behavior is a gap in the literature and show in a field experiment that reciprocity works only in the first few hours on workers’ efforts. Our results provide empirical evidence that reciprocity can be effective for healthy behavior that takes time and effort to complete. In addition, unlike other contexts that use only monetary incentives or substantial gifts to invoke receivers’ desire to reciprocate, our context combines both incentives (lottery) and senders’ efforts (senders need to run 40 km) to increase receivers’ psychological debts and, in turn, motivate receivers to exercise. Such efforts can be an intangible gift and are unique in the healthcare context because receivers can easily quantify how hard it is for senders to exert those efforts.

Second, we contribute to the literature on incentives in general and reciprocity in particular by juxtaposing reciprocity with the commonly used self-interest based incentive and quantifying differences in efficacy. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to conduct this comparison. In doing so, we advance understanding of an emerging stream of literature by comparing a social or behavioral intervention with a financial incentive (Ashraf et al. 2014; Chung and Narayandas 2017; Kast et al. 2012). With the help of digital technologies and the prevalence of online social networks, our study demonstrates that reciprocity can greatly improve the effectiveness of a conventional incentive program without incurring extra costs, responding to the call for “making incentive dollars go further” (Blumenthal et al. 2013, p. 503). We also provide a foundation for future studies that could examine the synthesized effects when combining multiple mechanisms (e.g., increasing observability from peers, or loss aversion) with a reciprocity-based design.<sup>23</sup>

Third, our analysis of the interpersonal relationship on the effects of reciprocity offers a deeper understanding of the mechanism. Since most studies in previous literature use a third party previously unknown to the subjects to initiate reciprocity (Appendix Table A1), it is not feasible for them to test how interpersonal relationships alter the effects of reciprocity. We use social exchange theory to theorize how social closeness may interact with the effects of reciprocity. Empirical evidence suggests that social closeness plays a key role in human behaviors (Aral and Walker 2014; Bapna et al. 2017; Nitzan and Libai 2011), although findings about the precise nature of interpersonal relationships on reciprocal behaviors are mixed (Falk et al. 1999; Maréchal and Thöni 2019). Our finding of an inverted U-shaped relationship between social closeness and the effects of reciprocity adds further nuance to our understanding of interpersonal relationships, and extends previous studies by treating interpersonal relationships as existing along a continuum; a more realistic assumption.

Our work also yields useful practical implications. Prior work in the information systems literature has largely focused on generalized reciprocityin online forums or knowledge sharing platforms (Kankanhalli et al. 2005; Lampel and Bhalla 2007; Wasko and Faraj 2005). Our research seeks to leverage this capability for the design of an incentive mechanism based on reciprocity. We sought to establish that when there is a need to directly reciprocate to others, individuals are willing to endure some costs (e.g., running hard) to repay others’ goodwill. We show practical implications by discussing how a social network platform can enable direct reciprocity through proper design.<sup>24</sup> The findings also allow practitioners to identify optimal pairs to fully exert the power of reciprocity to achieve precision motivation for each individual.

Furthermore, because our study directly leverages individuals existing social connections, it has the potential to create value for both the platform and for participants. For the platform, a reciprocity-based incentive increases the bond between pairs of users, an essential element for a social-network platform to thrive (Ellison 2007). For individuals, a reciprocity-based incentive not only enhances interpersonal relationships, but might also enable the social support necessary to sustain engagement in healthy behavior in the long run (Kiernan et al. 2012).<sup>25</sup> This benefit to the platform is supported by our additional analysis that shows users who complete challenges have greater interactions with senders than non-completed pairs.

## Limitations and Future Research

We acknowledge some limitations of our field experiment and discuss promising opportunities for future work. The treatment period for our challenge only spans two weeks. This duration might not be long enough to foster a long-term healthy habit, although we note that this observation period is longer and the observed behavior more effortful than what has been the norm in a majority of prior studies on reciprocity. While we believe that the need to reciprocate to friends is essential to maintain the interpersonal relationship, whether the same value of gifts and the obligation to return can be generalized to a longer-period challenge remains an empirical question. Future research could explore if multiple reciprocity-based interventions are necessary in order for long-term habits to develop.

Our study used a virtual gold coin for a lottery drawing to win sports accessories. A lottery represents an uncertain outcome with an associated probability, in contrast to a guaranteed gift such as a water bottle. It is not clear whether the effect of reciprocity would be conditional on the types of gifts or what would be an optimal gift to increase individuals’ performance. In addition, the expected value of the lottery is modest, and our results might change if the incentive becomes more substantial. Our study can be extended by changing the value of the gifts (small versus large) and the types of gifts (monetary versus nonmonetary gifts; certain versus probabilistic gifts).

While we believe that the underlying psychological and economic mechanisms of reciprocity would hold in other contexts, we acknowledge that human behaviors could be strongly affected by many other factors. Completing a physical activity challenge might look good to others, which provides a positive signal for desirable behavior. If our challenge focused on other settings such as conspicuous consumption or smoking cessation, which, in general, are deemed as negative behaviors, the power of reciprocity might be lower. Further, since reciprocity needs social connections to be effective, settings that do not support easy observability of social connections (e.g., offline) or behaviors that are considered undesirable (e.g., bad habits that have privacy concerns) might not be suitable for the application of reciprocity. Even though we are confident that reciprocity can be applied to many other healthcare settings, more empirical studies are needed to examine whether reciprocity outperforms self-interest in fields such as healthy eating, glucose monitoring or hypertension control.

Finally, since the pairs of users in our platform are already online friends, our test of the curvilinear relationship of closeness is skewed to the right because we are not able to observe a pair of complete strangers. Although we cannot find a pair of complete strangers, the limitation might not be a major concern. In this study, we have shown that a moderate closeness between senders and receivers would be stronger than a distant one. It is reasonable to conjecture that the obligation to return favors to total strangers would be even weaker than to remotely close friends. Furthermore, to leverage the power of reciprocity, we need to rely on individuals’ social connections. A pair of complete strangers would not be suitable to apply a reciprocity-based incentive. Future studies might examine whether reciprocity still holds to enhance our understanding of the conditional effect of social relationship.

## Conclusion

This study harnesses the power of modern wearable and mobile technologies, which are increasingly being used in wellness programs (Handel and Kolstad 2017), combined with the unprecedented connectivity enabled by digital platforms. These devices, technologies, and social networks allow us to precisely track users’ behaviors and pair senders and receivers. Leveraging these features, we designed a new incentive based on reciprocity and conducted a randomized, controlled experiment to test its effectiveness. We provided evidence that a reciprocity-based design can substantially improve the effectiveness of a conventional incentive program and if identified appropriately, judicious choice of pairs of senders and receivers would further strengthen such effects. From the perspective of both effective resource utilization and improved outcomes, it is increasingly important to gain a better understanding of how interventions can be more accurately customized for individuals. Our field experiment provides useful evidence for the value of such precision targeting.

## Receivers’ Login Issue During Pairing Period

Our final sample in each group consists of individuals who log in to the platform and read the message regardless of whether they accept the challenge or not. A concern may be that there is a selection bias caused by unobservables that, in turn, causes imbalance across groups. The concern should be minor because individuals are not aware of the event unless they log in to the platform. Since the receivers in all groups do not know about the event in advance, there is no selection problem related to logging in across the groups. Moreover, since the message in the push notification sent to each receiver is exactly the same across groups, receivers should not suffer from a selection problem either.

Nevertheless, we conducted a series of tests to examine any possible selection bias. First, we check the proportion of message reading in each group, which should be similar across groups. Pair-wise two sample tests of proportions (z-tests) among these three groups (64.4% in Plat-SelfInt, 63.6% in Frnd-SelfInt, and 62.0% in Frnd-Recip) show that the proportion of users reading the message is not statistically different across groups. This result provides initial evidence that our sample does not suffer from the selection bias problem. Second, we compare the characteristics of those users who do not login in the pairing period in the Frnd-Recip group and those in the other two groups. A joint test on all characteristics shows no significant difference between the Frnd-Recip group and the Plat-SelfInt group (p-value = 0.37) or between the Frnd-Recip group and the Frnd-SelfInt group (p-value = 0.52). These statistics show that the receivers who do not log in are qualitatively similar across these three groups. Finally, we use the entire sample that includes both individuals who log in and do not log in to the platform to test our previous models (all 2,744 individuals). The results in Table A4 column (1) are consistent with the previous findings in Table 6.<sup>27</sup>

We then test whether the results still hold for the Inverted U relationship for the Frnd-Recip group (Table A4 column (2)); we see that the results are also quite close to the findings in Table 7. The evidence presented here alleviates the concerns of login selection and shows the robustness of our findings.

## Alternative Measures of Closeness

Our theoretical arguments proposed a key role for the social closeness between senders and receivers in the efficacy of a reciprocity-based incentive. We used individuals’ social network structure, the common friends between senders and receivers, as a proxy for closeness. To further ensure the robustness of this index, we reestimate the relationship using alternative measures suggested in prior literature. We measure the number of days senders and receivers know each other (DaysKnowEachOther<sup>28</sup>) to gauge the length of their friendship (Gilbert and Karahalios 2009) as a proxy for social distance. Chen et al. (2009) also use the length of time participants know the other person to measure the level of closeness of relationships between friends. We calculate the number of days since a sender and a receiver first built a social connection on the platform (either following or being followed) until the last date of pretreatment period. Table A5 column (1) shows the results. Consistent with CommonFriends, DaysKnowEachOther shows an inverted U-shaped relationship of reciprocity on completion rate (p < 0.05).

A second alternative to conceptualize social closeness is a multidimensional index. We construct a closeness index (ClosenessIndex), drawing on insights from prior work, that comprises of the interaction between senders and receivers to capture social contexts (Aral and Walker 2014). We create this index as a summative measure of the following variables (each of which is binary): whether a sender and a receiver are in a common online running club, whether a sender and a receiver have attended the same race before, whether a sender and a receiver mutually follow each other, and whether a receiver has pressed like on a sender’s running record in the past half year.<sup>29</sup> The common online running club and common races reflect the shared experience between a sender and a receiver (Aral and Walker 2014). Mutually following each other is a variable constructed from the network structure and has been used to represent the strength of a tie (e.g., Shi et al. 2014). The likes behaviors represent receiver’s positive association with the sender (Kosinski et al. 2013), reflecting receivers’ fondness toward senders.<sup>30</sup> We avoid using the continuous form of the number of likes because it would be affected by the length of the relationship. The range of the closeness index is from zero to four, with zero indicating low closeness and four representing a close relationship. Table A5 column (2) shows the results. The closeness index shows a similar inverted U-Shaped relationship (p < 0.01).

The Pearson correlation coefficient between CommonFriends and DaysKnowEachOther is 0.50 (p < 0.01), and that between CommonFriends and ClosenessIndex is 0.39 (p < 0.01), indicating small to medium strength correlations between ClosenessIndex and the other two variables (Cohen 1988). We see that CommonFriends correlates with these two measures, but not to a large extent. The results suggest that although, as might be expected, the shared friends between senders and receivers (CommonFriends) are positively correlated with other types of interpersonal relationships, they are not indistinguishable from each other. This alleviates the potential concern that all three variables measure the same facet of the social closeness between senders and receivers.

## Concerns Related to Offline Interactions

We conducted our experiment in the setting of an online platform. Although the platform provides detailed information about individuals’ digital social connections and running records, we are not able to observe activities that happened offline. For instance, we are not able to tell whether individuals who fail to reciprocate their friends’ gift would compensate their friends offline, such as buying senders a meal. The unobservability of offline behaviors might lead to an underestimation of our experimental results, which is a typical constraint for a less controllable but more realistic field experiment as compared to a lab experiment. To address this concern, we conduct additional analysis controlling for two variables that capture communications between senders and receivers: proximity closeness and online messages. We use individuals’ GPS locations to capture whether the sender and receiver run in a close region to capture the proximity closeness for offline communications. We also observe whether a sender and a receiver send messages to each other at the targeted platform during the treatmen period. Our results in Table A6 column (1) remain robust after adding these controls.

## Concerns Related to Social Influence

## Network Structure

One lingering concern is that our field experiment, albeit randomized, might suffer from the problem of co-evolution of social influence and network structure. In both offline and online settings, individuals’ social ties have the power to affect behaviors (Aral and Nicolaides 2017; Sun et al. 2019). To estimate treatment effects in a social network is complicated because individuals in the social network are also interacting during the intervention (Phan and Airoldi 2015; Phan and Godes 2018). Such dynamic characteristics might affect the treatment outcomes because participants in the social network platform may not be fully immune to network influence

We use three strategies to address potential threats arising from this concern related to peer influence and behavior observability. First, it may be the case that a user’s behavior is affected by her friends, also known as network effects or peer influence. This problem might be relatively minor in our context because we find that the percentage of receivers’ friends (follower, followee, or mutual follower) who are also in the experiment is only 5.62%. Because only a very small fraction of receivers’ friends is included in the experiment, this reduces the potential fo social influence to be present. Second, we control for individuals’ social network structures, senders’ and receivers’ Eigen centrality, in our main model. The results in Table A6 column (2) remain consistent, indicating that participants’ network structure might not be a major threat in our context. Third, following Eckles et al. (2017), if the experiments were conducted for individuals who have no neighbors, one can safely infer the average treatment effects directly. To mimic this case, we rerun the regressions on a subsample who do not have any friend who is included in our experiment. The results are presented in Table A6 column (3). The results and the Wald Test between the Frnd-SelfInt and Frnd-Recip remain broadly similar to the main results. This analysis, again, partially alleviates the concern of peer influence.

## Observability of Receivers

The second source of potential social influence in this setting is the observability of the receivers’ behavior. Receivers in the Frnd-Recip group might subconsciously believe that whether they finish the challenge or not will be observable to their friends even though, in our experimental design, we avoid informing the senders to minimize such an impact. To the extent that receivers would think senders will know they complete the challenge or not, the treatment effect might not be 100% attributed to the effect of reciprocity. An alternative motivation, “to avoid looking bad” might also drive this behavior. We address this problem with the following three strategies.

First, if expectations of observability and the resultant peer pressure play a significant role in increasing the effect of reciprocity, a reminding message might bolster such pressure. We examine whether a user’s behaviors on reading the messages moderate the reciprocity mechanism on individuals’ physical activity. The results are presented in Table A6 column (4). We find that the interaction term between reading messages and the reciprocity treatment is not significant. This provides indirect evidence that this form of social influence might not be substantial in our setting.

Second, studies have been shown that individuals on a social network platform frequently engage in impression management (Tong et al. 2008). For individuals concerned with the impression they create, exercising hard to reciprocate their friends would avoid their looking bad to senders. This peer pressure effect would be stronger for individuals who care about their impression than individuals who care less. A proxy used in prior literature to capture impression management (Liu et al. 2019) is the number of incoming social ties an individual has. The more followers an individual has, the greater the visibility of his/her actions, and the more likely the individual cares about his/her impression. We use the number of incoming ties and create a variable, HighFollower, which means an individual’s number of incoming social connection is greater than 75% of other users’ incoming ties.<sup>31</sup> The results in Table A6 column (5) indicate that individuals’ propensity to care about their impression is not a statistically significant moderator. This indirect evidence further helps alleviate concerns about the potential confounding effect of pee pressure.

Third, peer pressure may exist not only when the receivers read the messages, but might also happen during the two-week challenge period. When a sender interacts with a receiver during this two-week challenge period, it can create a form of peer pressure for the receivers. If peer pressure plays an important role in moderating the treatment effect, individuals who interact with senders should be more motivated to complete the challenge. We create a variable to capture whether a sender or a receiver has ever pressed Like on each other’s running record during the challenge period as a proxy for the interaction.<sup>32</sup> The results are presented in Table A6 column (6). We find that interaction during the challenge period does not significantly moderate the treatment effect, further increasing confidence that social influence is not confounding the experiment’s findings.

While our analysis cannot fully rule out the possibility of a confounding effect between social influence and reciprocity, the above extensive set of robustness tests suggests that the possible impact of social influence on our findings is minimal. Future studies could follow the method proposed by Eckles et al. to do graph cluster randomization to minimize the impacts of social influence.
