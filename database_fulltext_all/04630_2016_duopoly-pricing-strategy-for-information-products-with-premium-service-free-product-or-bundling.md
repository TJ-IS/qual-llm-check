# Duopoly Pricing Strategy for Information Products with Premium Service: Free Product or Bundling?

ZAN ZHANG, GUOFANG NAN, MINQIANG LI, AND YONG TAN

ZAN ZHANG (zanzhang@tju.edu.cn) is a Ph.D. candidate in the Department of Information Management and Management Science, College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University, China. Her research interests include pricing of information goods and Internet service, and uncertain information processing. Her recent papers have appeared in Knowledge-Based Systems and Information Sciences.

He received his Ph.D. in management information systems from the College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University in 2004. His research interests cover management science and decision support, IT strategy, e-commerce, data mining and business intelligence, and evolutionary computation. ABSTRACT: Many software firms, especially mobile app providers, offer perpetually free basic products to users, but premiums are charged for access to the additional features or functionalities. While the free offering helps capture potential customers, it might cannibalize the sales of premium goods or services. This paper adopts a game theoretical approach to examine the impact of free offering on the competition between two firms in the presence of network effects. The firms can either offer a free core product and a paid service or offer them as a bundle. The core product has stand-alone value and can be used separately but the value-added service has no value without the core product. We derive the market equilibria and present conditions under which the free offering strategy outperforms the bundling strategy. We show that when a firm’s core product has a sufficient advantage in product quality, it is better for this firm to sell the bundle but for the other to use free strategy. However, if the core products are similar in terms of quality, it is optimal for them to use the same strategies. Whether to offer a free product depends largely on the core products’ quality. We also show that the firms may be caught in a prisoner’s dilemma when both adopt the free strategy. Finally, we find that the profitability of the firm that offers a free product always increases in network effects intensity and market size, but this is not the case for the firm that sells the bundle. This study contributes to understanding the behavior of feature-limited free offering in a duopoly setting. Our findings also provide insights into the design of free product and the impact of network effects on the firms’ offering decisions.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: free core product, freemium, information products, network effects, premium service, pricing, product bundling.

Over the past decade, freemium model has been spreading rapidly in software industry. In particular, it has become one of the most prevalent business models of mobile app developers and Internet startups [24]. The model attracts a lot of consumers very efficiently, as it allows consumers to use basic functionality at no cost and to pay an additional fee for access premium features if they wish. According to Gartner’s report, 94.5 percent of global mobile app downloads in 2017 are forecasted to be free, and the free applications with inapp purchases will account for 48 percent of the total revenue, up from 11 percent in 2012 [17]. The success of this business model has been also validated by many of the largest companies across multiple digital fields, from multiplayer computer games such as League of Legends to music services like Spotify, from instant message application software like Skype to online storages like Dropbox. Even the well-known software provider— Microsoft—is now embracing the freemium model,<sup>1</sup> with the aim of attracting millions of free users and monetizing those who need premium services and support in addition to what is offered for free.

Several reasons account for the popularity of freemium models among information products. First, once an information product, such as software, online content, digital music and movies, has been developed, the costs of reproduction and distribution are negligible [23], which makes it possible to reproduce and provide the products unlimitedly. Second, most information products exhibit positive network effects [39], that is, the more consumers in the network, the higher utility a user derives from the product. The presence of network effects enables a company to acquire a large number of users to its free offer. Third, unlike physical products, information products are typical experience goods [31, 37]. Consumers cannot directly verify the quality and other attributes like performance, interface, and features of the product previous to an initial purchase. By experiencing the product, consumers are aware of the true fit of the product.

The practice of free software product offering can be classified into two general categories. The first one is free trial software product with time limitation, which is offered free for a limited trial time. The well-known examples include MathType, allowing users to try the fully functional software for thirty days, and file-compression utility, WinZip, working in full functionality for a forty-five-day trial period. After the trial period, users have to decide whether to buy or not. The second category is a free software product with limited functionality that will never generate direct revenue for the providers. They are provided for free because of other economic benefits such as promoting sales of complementary goods or services. For example, Adobe gives away Acrobat Reader XI for free but charges a premium for Acrobat XI Standard, which offers richer features including writing and editing. Another example of free software with limited functionality is the antivirus software AVG AntiVirus Free,<sup>2</sup> which provides essential antivirus protection free of charge. For more advanced functionalities such as online shield and data safe, customers have to pay a premium for the AVG AntiVirus Pro. In addition, mail software such as MailChimp and Mailjet, and online games like League, provide users free access to the basic product. Most previous studies examine one firm’s decision about whether to offer a free trial (e.g. [8, 9, 12]). The major difference with previous works and hence the contribution to the related literature is that this research focuses on free software with limited functionality rather than limited time in a duopoly setting and in the presence of network effects.

Offering a basic version of information products for free affects a firm in several ways. It helps build up a user-installed base and promotes the adoption of a new product. In addition, offering a free core product helps consumers learn more about the product, which may contribute to boosting the sales of complementary goods or services. However, offering a product free of charge may result in demand cannibalization of the productrelated premium service, especially when the quality of the free product is relatively high, because some consumers may find it sufficient to use only the basic product without any payment and thus do not subscribe to its service. On the other hand, if a firm does not offer a free product but the competing firm does, some of its potential customers may be captured by the competing firm’s free offer, thus resulting in a customer loss. The purpose of this paper is to examine firms’ strategies about whether to give away a core product and only charge for the premium service, allowing users to use the core product separately without having to pay a dime, or bundle the core product with the value-added service and sell them for a single price. Though both strategies are observed in the information products market, it is difficult to tell when a provider should choose one strategy over the other. In particular, we aim to answer the following research questions: First, under what conditions will the strategy of offering a free core product and a paid service be better than the strategy of selling them as a bundle in a duopoly setting? Second, how does the intensity of network effects impact the firms’ offering decisions and the equilibrium solutions? Finally, if a firm considers giving away a free product, what are the benefits from the free strategy, what is the optimal quality of the free product to offer, and what is the optimal price for the associated premium service?

As a marketing tool, free software offering is being adopted by a growing number of providers. It is a common practice for mobile app providers to offer free applications with in-app purchases. Examples of freemium apps include mobile games like Supercell’s Clash of Clans and King’s Candy Crush Saga. However, not all app providers choose to give away their apps for free and charge for premium services. The most prominent is Apple’s App Store, which exhibits games in a category called “Pay Once and Play.” While it is regarded that this new type of game has the potential to shake up the traditional mobile games market, even the landscape of video games, it is unclear whether this kind of shift in pricing can really be a game-changer.

In the software industry, companies also favor free products with value-added service. One notable example is Microsoft, which released Office for iPad in 2014 including Word, Excel, and PowerPoint free of charge, but only with limited functionality.<sup>4</sup> Consumers are granted access to download the Office applications on the iPad for free. But if they want to access more advanced features, such as editing and creating new documents with the iPad, they have to buy into its Office 365 subscription service. Kingsoft, also known as an office suite provider, gives away almost all features of its WPS Office for Android and iOS. Nonetheless, WPS Office for iOS launched its first premium service in 2013—presenter view in PowerPoint when the presenter projects to another screen. A consumer has to pay a premium to access this service.

In the communication market, instant messaging (IM) software is also a typical free product that offers a value-added service to consumers. Tencent’s QQ is made available for free download, but the program’s premium service, such as the VIP service, must be purchased. Providers like Skype also offer premium service: Skype users can use basic functionalities such as video chat and voice calls over IP for free with another Skype client. However, users can also pay for premium service such as physical calls or conference calls.

Other examples involving the strategy of offering a free product are online games. Users can play a game without making an extra purchase, but have to pay a premium during the game to acquire time advantage or access additional features. Nowadays, many of the most popular computer games employ the freemium strategy, which the game designer and former professional StarCraft player Sean Plott regards as the best business model for multiplayer games like League of Legends, Hearthstone, Dota 2. Even though League of Legends is free to play, it generated almost \$1 billion revenues from in-game premium item purchases during the first nine months of 2014, according to SuperData Research [38]. Except for World of Warcraft created by Blizzard Entertainment, all of the top ten grossing multiplayer computer games on SuperData’s list in 2014 are free to play and they make money from in-game spending. A key reason for the popularity of the freemium model among computer games is its ability to acquire and monetize a large user base in new markets.

## Literature Review

There is a growing literature on information product including product compatibility and standardization [14, 26], software versioning and upgrade [3, 10, 43, 44, 45]. Our work is related to the literature on software free trial and to the literature on bundling and network effects.

By examining the duopoly software firms’ strategies about whether to offer a free product, our study complements the literature on a firm’s free trial strategy in a monopoly setting. Conner [7] focuses on the commercial software and its “clone” with a lower quality, the clone in some sense can be viewed as a free trial product by setting its price to zero. She finds that when the network effects are large, it is profitable for an innovator to allow a “clone” of its product. Cheng and Tang [8] extend the models in Conner [7] to investigate the optimal strategy for free trial software with limited functionality. They examine the trade-off between the network effects and demand cannibalization effects. The findings show that the monopoly is better providing the limited version free trial under strong network effects. In another paper, Cheng and Liu [9] analyze two free trial strategies: time-locked free trial and limited version free trial for a monopolistic software firm. They find that the timelocked free trial is more profitable for the firm than the case of no time-locked free trial if the network effects are smaller than the given threshold. In addition, by comparing the profits under the two free trial options, they find that the time-locked free trial generates greater profit if the network effects are moderate, while the limited version free trial dominates in the presence of strong network effects. Another work on free offer of software is Niculescu and Wu [32], which explores two software business models involving a free component under perpetual licensing: feature-limited freemium and uniform seeding. They compare and contrast the business models by developing a consumer valuation learning framework in the context of multiperiod product life and word-of-mouth effects. The focus of most of these studies is to examine under what conditions the firm should introduce the free trial software in a monopoly setting. In contrast to the previous research, we consider the freemium strategy in a duopoly setting, where the firms compete with a differentiated information product consisting of a basic product and a complementary good or service. In our paper, we examine the symmetry case in which both firms offer a free core product and thus profit from the value-added service. In particular, we also investigate the asymmetry case in which one firm uses free strategy, while the other firm uses the strategy of bundling a product and a service.

In addition to studies on optimal pricing strategy involving a free trial, there is also literature investigating software free trial from other distinctive perspectives. For example, Jiang and Sarkar [21] analyze the impacts of free offer on the diffusion of new software for a monopolistic firm with a product, for which the network effects are negligible. Dey, Lahiri, and Liu [12] examine the problem of designing a timelocked free trial by taking the role of consumer learning into consideration. Both articles focus on fully functional software that is given away for a short period time. In empirical research, Lee and Tan [27] study the factors that make some free trial software attract more users than others such as consumer software sampling, product reviews, and types of free trials including a free basic version without a time lock and a fully functional version with a time lock.

Our work is also related to the literature on bundling and network effects. Bundling enables a firm to extract the consumer surplus in a form of price discrimination by selling two or more products in a package [29, 30]. A majority of research focuses on product bundling of information products (e.g. [1, 5, 11, 16, 19, 40, 41]). Venkatesh and Kamakura [40] examine the optimal pricing patterns and bundling strategies under a monopoly by contrasting complements and substitutes from independently valued products. They find that the marginal production cost and the degree of complementarity or substitutability determine which of the three strategies—pure component, pure bundling, and mixed bundling—is optimal. Ferrer, Mora, and Olivares [16] study the optimal pricing of multiple bundles under monopolistic conditions. The bundles in the model are composed of the same product, but the associated services are differentiated by the specific attributes. Venkatesh and Mahajan [41] provide a comprehensive overview of the extant literature on design and pricing of product bundles. In addition, there is a small literature on bundling in the context of network effects examining how the optimal price and profit are affected under network effects. Prasad, Venkatesh, and Mahajan [34] focus on a monopoly offering two products with particular levels of marginal costs and degree of network externality. They analyze the impact of asymmetry or symmetry in network externality and marginal costs on optimal bundling strategy. Most of the works in this field focus on the optimal bundling decision in monopolistic settings.

As a pervasive marketing practice, bundling is often used as a tool of strategic competition. Several articles investigate the bundling strategies in a competitive environment. In this context, Ghosh and Balachander [18] focus on the competition between a generalist firm and two specialist firms, in which the general firm produces products in two categories and each specialist firm produces in only one of the two categories. They find that the generalist firm is able to increase its profit by bundling its products if the two specialist firms do not form an alliance to compete. By extending the bundling model in Bakos and Brynjolfsson [5], which considers the optimality of bundling for a multiproduct monopolist to settings with different types of competition on the Internet, the same authors [6] find that, with nonzero marginal production cost and distribution cost, bundling can create “economics of aggregation” for information goods, even in the absence of network effects. Anderson and Leruth [2] study the competitive bundling strategies for two complements in a duopoly. The results suggest that mixed bundling is a better strategy for a monopoly, while a pure component is an equilibrium strategy in a duopoly. According to these studies, the bundling strategy helps to increase firms’ profits. Although bundling has been widely studied, little research specifically addresses the issue of whether the bundling strategy is still optimal in comparison to the strategy where one component is given away for free so as to boost sales of complementary goods.

The work most relevant to ours is Etzion and Pang [13], which focuses on the competition between two firms that sell a differentiated physical product and can choose to offer a complementary service, where only the service exhibits network effects and is valuable to consumers who buy the product. Our paper differs from Etzion and Pang [13] in three aspects. First, our paper focuses on information products rather than physical products. Hence, given the characteristics of information products, we neglect its marginal cost but instead take its network effects into consideration. Second, our paper includes cases in which the core product can be offered free of charge, due to its negligible marginal cost and positive network effects. Specifically, the free product has stand-alone value so that consumers who do not pay for the premium service also have free access to the core product. These cases are not covered in Etzion and Pang [13]. Third, Etzion and Pang consider the case in which the complementary service is assumed to be provided only in a bundled form with the product. Our model, instead, allows the premium service to be sold separately or bundled with the core product. Hence, given the strategies the two firms adopt, several specific market segments may coexist in the competitive setting. In contrast, in the model presented by Etzion and Pang, consumers in the market are divided into two segments: those who buy the product or bundle (if available) from one firm, and those who buy the product or bundle (if available) from the other firm.

## Model

Consider a market with two firms: Firm 1 and Firm 2, which provide differentiated information products to consumers. Each product consists of two modules: a core product with a stand-alone value and a value-added service module that has no value without the core product. The products are characterized by quality attribute (vertical differentiation) and fit attribute (horizontal differentiation). All consumers prefer high quality to low quality, but the tastes and preferences vary across consumers. The quality determines the intrinsic value that consumers derive from a product, while the fit attribute describes the disutility due to the misfit between a consumer’s ideal product or service and the one she intends to consume.

Our model is developed from the location model in Hotelling [20]. There are N consumers in the market who are heterogeneous in terms of their valuation for the core product and the value-added service. Each consumer demands at most one product from either of the two firms, and consumers are uniformly distributed along a unit line in terms of their preferences, with Firm 1 located at 0 and Firm 2 at 1. A consumer’s location represents her ideal product. If a product does not perfectly match the consumer’s taste, she incurs a misfit cost, which is increasing in the distance between her location and the given firm. The unit misfit cost for using a given core product is λ. Since a whole product contains a core product as well as premium service, a consumer’s misfit cost for the whole product is denoted by βλ, where $\beta > 1$ . In addition, both firms’ bundled products provide an intrinsic value, s, to consumers.<sup>5</sup> The intrinsic value for Firm i’s individual core product is denoted by $\nu _ { i } ~ ( i = 1 , 2 )$ . Thus, an initial simplifying assumption is that neither firm’s whole product is inherently superior, but their core products are allowed to be differentiated in the quality. This assumption enables us to focus on the issues of firms’ strategy choices in terms of whether to offer a free core product and the corresponding pricing policies that arise due to competition. Following previous literature [13, 20, 28, 33], we assume that the two firms compete directly on the marginal customers and thus the market is fully covered (no local monopoly) in this model.

The information products we examine exhibit positive network effects. Similar to Jing [22] and Baake and Boom [4], we model the network effects as a function of product quality and the network size (or user base). A consumer derives an additional utility of γ $( s + Q )$ from using a product of quality, s, where γ is the intensity of network effects, and Q represents the expected size of installed base in a rational expectation equilibrium [7, 25, 36]. The network effects of a product depend on the network size and the product’s intrinsic value, especially for heterogeneous products [15]. It is also supported by practical evidence that products of higher quality create higher network effects. For instance, an advanced version of software (e.g., IM application software) generates more network value than a basic version because the former provides advanced functionality such as receiving more legible documents and saving the chat transcript over the cloud. Table 1 summarizes the notations used in the model.

The firms have two strategies in our model setup: they can offer the core product and service as a bundle, in which case all customers that buy the bundle can use the product and gain access to the service. Alternatively, they can choose to release a free product in order to build up installed base and charge for the premium service. In this case, consumers are granted free access to the core product, while the premium service allows them to use the extra content or features if they pay a fee for it. Given the two optional strategies for both firms, four market configurations are possible, that is, both sell a core product bundled with a premium service, denoted as Case BB, Firm 1 offers a free core product and charges for the premium service, while Firm 2 offers a bundle of core product and service, denoted as Case SB, Firm 1 offers a bundle, while Firm 2 offers a free core product and a paid service, denoted as Case BS, and both offer a free product and a paid service, denoted as Case SS.

## Case BB: Duopoly with Both Offering a Bundle of Core Product and Premium Service

We start with the case in which both firms offer a bundle of core product and premium service to consumers. Under bundling, consumers determine to purchase a bundle from either firm. We plot the consumer surplus functions of the two options in Figure 1.

Out of the two options, consumers choose the one that gives them the maximum surplus. Consequently, the market is divided into two segments such that each segment represents consumers who exercise the same choice. For example, consumers located between 0 and $x _ { 0 }$ choose the bundle offered by Firm 1, as their surplus derived from using Firm 1’s bundle is higher than that from Firm ${ 2 \mathrm { { \dot { s } } } }$ . In contrast, consumers between $x _ { 0 }$ and 1 choose the bundle from Firm 2, while the consumer who is located at $x _ { 0 }$ is indifferent between the bundle of core product and premium service offered by Firm 1 and the bundle offered by Firm 2. By setting surplus function $U _ { 1 } = U _ { 2 }$ , where $Q _ { B B } ^ { 1 } = x _ { 0 } N$ , and $Q _ { B B } ^ { 2 } = ( 1 - x _ { 0 } ) N$ according to rational expectation equilibrium, we get the market division point, that is, the location of the marginal customer,

$$
x _ {0} = \frac {\beta \lambda - \gamma N - p _ {B B} ^ {1} + p _ {B B} ^ {2}}{2 (\beta \lambda - \gamma N)}.
$$

In equilibrium, the two firms choose their prices $p _ { B B } ^ { 1 }$ and $p _ { B B } ^ { 2 }$ to maximize their respective profit. The profit functions for Firm 1 and Firm 2 under bundling can be written as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \pi_ {B B} ^ {1} = p _ {B B} ^ {1} D _ {B B} ^ {1} = p _ {B B} ^ {1} x _ {0} N, \\ \pi_ {B B} ^ {2} = p _ {B B} ^ {2} D _ {B B} ^ {2} = p _ {B B} ^ {2} (1 - x _ {0}) N, \\ s. t. 0 <   x _ {0} <   1. \end{array}
$$

Substituting $x _ { 0 }$ into the profit functions, we find the equilibrium solutions for the competition game. In order to ensure nonnegative demands for both products, the second order condition requires $\gamma N < \beta \lambda$ . This condition suggests that the products network effect on the consumer’s surplus is bounded above by βλ. That is, it is dominated by the consumer’s disutility from using a given product that does not perfectly match her tastes. So the two firms split the market and neither can act as a local monopoly. Similar assumptions can be found in Etzion and Pang [13], Li and Chen [28], and Navon, Shy, and Thisse [33]. If, however, the network effects are strong enough to dominate a consumer’s preference over a product, all the consumers always purchase from a single firm in equilibrium, as will be shown in the following sections.

Solving the first order conditions of the profit functions simultaneously, we get the optimal bundle prices:

$$
p _ {B B} ^ {1 *} = p _ {B B} ^ {2 *} = \beta \lambda - \gamma N.
$$

Accordingly, the resulting demands and profits of the two firms are:

$$
D _ {B B} ^ {1 *} = D _ {B B} ^ {2 *} = \frac {1}{2} N,
$$

$$
\pi_ {B B} ^ {1 *} = \pi_ {B B} ^ {2 *} = \frac {1}{2} (\beta \lambda - \gamma N) N.
$$

From the equilibrium solutions, we find that when both firms sell a bundle of core product and premium service, the equilibrium price increases in customers’ misfit cost. It is intuitive that if users incur a higher misfit cost for using a given product, they become more captive to the firm and thus the firm might be able to raise its price. The equilibrium price also decreases in the network effects intensity of the products, indicating that price competition intensifies with the presence of network effects. This is in accordance with the finding in previous works [26, 33]. If the intensity of network effects increases, the firm needs to cut its price to attract customers.

We now investigate what would happen when network effects are so strong that they exceed consumers’ misfit cost, that is, $\gamma N \geq \beta \lambda$ . In this case, the resulting market structure differs from that when $\gamma N < \beta \lambda$ . When the network effects are strong enough to dominate the cost of misfit, all the consumers patronize a single firm. Assume that Firm 1 sets a very low price so as to capture the entire market. Under this price, the marginal consumers located at point 1 obtain nonnegative surplus, that is, $s - \beta \lambda + \gamma ( s + N ) - P _ { B B } ^ { 1 } \geq 0 ,$ Therefore, the maximum price Firm 1 can charge is $P _ { B B } ^ { 1 } = s - \beta \lambda + \gamma ( s + N )$ . If Firm 1 charges a price slightly higher than this, some consumers will shift to its competitor, thus making Firm 2’s product more attractive, which in turn leads more consumers to shift. Consequently, the outcome is that all consumers will purchase from Firm 2. Hence, we conclude the market outcomes in the following proposition.

Proposition 1 (Market Outcomes for Case BB):

a. If the network effects are lower than the misfit cost for using a bundle of product and service, that is, $\gamma N { < } \beta \lambda ,$ , both firms compete and coexist. The prices in equilibrium are $p _ { B B } ^ { 1 * } = p _ { B B } ^ { 2 * } = \beta \lambda - \gamma N .$

b. If the network effects are greater than the cost of misfit, that is, $\gamma N \geq \beta \lambda$ , only one firm supplies the entire market in equilibrium.

i. In one equilibrium, Firm 1 dominates and the equilibrium prices are $P _ { B B } ^ { 1 * } = s - \beta \lambda + \gamma ( s + N )$ , and $P _ { B B } ^ { 2 * } = 0 .$

ii. In the other equilibrium, Firm 2 dominates and the equilibrium prices $a r e P _ { B B } ^ { 1 * } = 0 ;$ and $P _ { B B } ^ { 2 * } = s - \beta \lambda + \gamma ( s + N )$

## Case SB/Case BS: Duopoly with Only One Firm Offering a Free Core Product

Now we discuss the case where one firm offers a free core product to compete with its competitor’s bundle of core product and value-added service. Without loss of generality, we assume that Firm 1 offers a free core product and charges for the premium service separately and Firm 2 bundles its core product with service. The solution when only Firm 2 offers a free core product can be derived in a similar manner.

A firm will release a free product provided that doing so entails a competitive advantage for the firm. The provision of a free product to the market has two main effects. First, the free product has the advantage of capturing positive network effects from both free users and subscribers of the product-related premium service.

However, it may also lead to cannibalization of partial demand and thus the reduction of profit, as some of Firm 1’s potential customers might find it better to use only the free product without any payment. Second, the introduction of a free product intensifies the competition between Firm ${ 2 \mathrm { { } s } }$ bundle and Firm 1’s free product. On one hand, some of the customers who would otherwise purchase the bundle from Firm 2 if no free product is available may now turn to Firm 1’s free product. In other words, Firm 1 captures some of Firm ${ 2 \mathrm { { } s } }$ customers by giving away its product free of charge. On the other hand, the provision of free product dampens the impact of Firm 2’s price changes on Firm 1’s profit.

In Figure 2, consumers are divided into three segments according to their valuations regarding a product: those located on the left of $x _ { 1 1 }$ are high-end customers who prefer to use Firm $1 \mathrm { { } ^ { \circ } s }$ free core product while at the same time purchasing its premium service, and those on the right of $x _ { 1 2 }$ prefer the bundle of core product and service of Firm 2. Consumers located between $x _ { 1 1 }$ and $x _ { 1 2 }$ find it optimal to take only Firm $1 \mathrm { { } ^ { \bullet } s }$ free core product without its premium service. This fraction of consumers do not contribute to the profit of Firm 1, but they increase the installed base of Firm 1, and thereby contribute to the firm’s network effects. Solving the indifference equation $U _ { 1 } = U _ { 1 f }$ and $U _ { 1 f } = U _ { 2 }$ , respectively, where $Q _ { S B } ^ { 1 } = x _ { 1 2 } N$ and $Q _ { S B } ^ { 2 } = ( 1 - x _ { 1 2 } ) N$ , we get the indifferent point $x _ { 1 1 }$ and $x _ { 1 2 }$ , as follows:

$$
x _ {1 1} = \frac {(s - v _ {1}) (1 + \gamma) - p _ {S B} ^ {1}}{\lambda (\beta - 1)}, x _ {1 2} = \frac {\beta \lambda - \gamma N - (s - v _ {1}) (1 + \gamma) + p _ {S B} ^ {2}}{\lambda (\beta + 1) - 2 \gamma N}.
$$

In equilibrium, Firm 1 chooses the price $p _ { S B } ^ { 1 }$ to maximize its profit from the paid service, while Firm 2 chooses the price $p _ { S B } ^ { 2 }$ to maximize its profit from the bundle. The profit functions for the two firms are:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \pi_ {S B} ^ {1} = p _ {S B} ^ {1} D _ {S B} ^ {1} = p _ {S B} ^ {1} x _ {1 1} N, \\ \pi_ {S B} ^ {2} = p _ {S B} ^ {2} D _ {S B} ^ {2} = p _ {S B} ^ {2} (1 - x _ {1 2}) N, \\ \text {s.t.} 0 <   x _ {1 1} <   x _ {1 2} <   1. \end{array}
$$

Figure 2. Market Segmentation When Only Firm 1 Offers a Free Core Product

To ensure the concavity of the profit functions, it is required that $2 \gamma N { < } \lambda ( \beta + 1 )$ We will examine the scenario in which this condition is violated later. Solving the first order conditions of the profit functions, we derive the optimal prices:

$$
p _ {S B} ^ {1 *} = \frac {1}{2} (s - v _ {1}) (1 + \gamma), p _ {S B} ^ {2 *} = \frac {1}{2} (\lambda - \gamma N + (s - v _ {1}) (1 + \gamma)).
$$

From the optimal prices in equilibrium, we obtain the demand for Firm 1’s premium service and the demand for Firm 2’s bundle of core product and additional service:

$$
D _ {S B} ^ {1 *} = \frac {(s - v _ {1}) (1 + \gamma)}{2 \lambda (\beta - 1)} N, D _ {S B} ^ {2 *} = \frac {1}{2} \frac {\lambda - \gamma N + (s - v _ {1}) (1 + \gamma)}{\lambda (\beta + 1) - 2 \gamma N} N.
$$

Accordingly, the two firms’ profits are:

$$
\pi_ {S B} ^ {1 *} = \frac {(s - v _ {1}) ^ {2} (1 + \gamma) ^ {2}}{4 \lambda (\beta - 1)} N, \pi_ {S B} ^ {2 *} = \frac {1}{4} \frac {(\lambda - \gamma N + (s - v _ {1}) (1 + \gamma)) ^ {2}}{\lambda (\beta + 1) - 2 \gamma N} N.
$$

At the equilibrium prices, the conditions for Firm 1 to have positive demand for the premium service and for Firm 2 to have positive demand for the bundle should be satisfied $( \mathrm { i . e . , } 0 { < } D _ { S B } ^ { i } { < } N$ for $i = 1 , 2 )$ . In addition, in order to guarantee the effectiveness of the free product strategy, the demand for only Firm 1’s free product is required to be positive $( \mathrm { i . e . , } 0 { < } Q _ { S B } ^ { 1 } - D _ { S B } ^ { 1 } { < } N )$ . These requirements are equivalent to the constraint on the indifference points (i.e., $0 { < } x _ { 1 1 } { < } x _ { 1 2 } { < } 1 )$ . Given our choice of parameters, it is straightforward to show that $D _ { S B } ^ { 1 } { > } 0$ and $D _ { S B } ^ { 2 * } { > } 0$ . Hence, to ensure nonzero measures of consumers who use only the free basic product from Firm 1, we require that:

$$
v _ {1} > s - \frac {\lambda (\beta - 1) (\lambda (1 + 2 \beta) - 3 \gamma N)}{2 (1 + \gamma) (\beta \lambda - \gamma N)}.\tag{5}
$$

According to the equilibrium results, we can analyze the effects of various parameters on the equilibrium prices and profits. An increase in the intensity of network effects always has a positive effect on Firm 1’s equilibrium price and the demand for its paid service. This happens because the strategy of releasing a free product helps to reduce the direct competition with its competitors’ bundled product. The free offer of Firm 1 helps dampen the impacts of Firm 2’s price changes on Firm 1’s profit from the premium service. By contrast, as the intensity of the network effects increases, Firm 2’s price might be reduced, especially when the market size is large enough. With large network effects, some potential customers of Firm 2 might derive more utility from using Firm 1’s free product, thus making it possible for them to switch from purchasing the bundle offered by Firm 2 to using the free product offered by Firm 1. As a result, the release of a free product by Firm 1 has a negative impact on the market share of Firm 2. Under this circumstance, Firm 2 should reduce its price so as to capture customers that would otherwise switch to the free product of Firm 1.

When the condition $2 \gamma N { < } \lambda ( \beta + 1 )$ is not satisfied, with the presence of strong positive network effects, consumers who would purchase Firm 2’s bundled product now switch to Firm 1’s free core product. Consequently, Firm 2 is driven out of the market by the introduction of the free product and Firm 1 becomes a monopoly. The equilibrium prices are $p _ { S B } ^ { 1 * } = ( s - \nu _ { 1 } ) ( 1 + \gamma ) / 2$ and $p _ { S B } ^ { 2 * } = 0$

We now discuss the scenario in which the region $[ x _ { 1 1 } , \ x _ { 1 2 } ]$ in Figure 2 is empty. When Condition (5) does not hold, all the potential customers of Firm 1 prefer its whole product over the individual free core product and thus no user takes the free product offered by Firm 1. Intuitively, if the intrinsic value of the free product is relatively low, which from another perspective implies that the premium service has sufficiently high intrinsic value, all users that take the free product from Firm 1 will be incentivized to purchase its premium service. In this scenario, Firm 1’s free core product together with the paid service competes head-to-head with Firm 2’s bundled product. It turns out that in equilibrium, each firm supplies half of the market, that is, $D _ { S B } ^ { 1 * } = D _ { S B } ^ { 2 * } = N / 2$ . The price of Firm 1’s premium service and the price of Firm 2’s bundled product are the same as that in Case BB where both firms adopt the bundling strategy, so that the equilibrium prices are $p _ { S B } ^ { 1 * } = p _ { S B } ^ { 2 * } = \beta \lambda - \gamma N$ . As a result, the strategy of releasing a free product converges to the strategy of bundling the core product and premium service. Proposition 2 summarizes the market outcomes under Case SB.

Proposition 2 (Market Outcomes for Case SB):

a. If the network effects are relatively low $( i . e . , 2 \gamma N { < } \lambda ( \beta + 1 ) )$ , the two firms compete and coexist.

i. When the region $[ x _ { 1 1 } , \ x _ { 1 2 } ]$ in Figure 2 is not empty, the equilibrium prices are $p _ { S B } ^ { 1 * } = ( s - \nu _ { 1 } ) ( 1 + \gamma ) / 2$ and $p _ { S B } ^ { 2 * } = ( \lambda - \gamma N + ( s - \nu _ { 1 } ) ( 1 + \gamma ) ) / 2$

ii. When the region $[ x _ { 1 1 } , \ x _ { 1 2 } ]$ in Figure 2 is empty, Firm 1’s strategy of offering a free product converges to the bundling strategy and each firm serves half of the market. The prices in equilibrium are $p _ { S B } ^ { 1 * } = p _ { S B } ^ { 2 * } = \beta \lambda - \gamma N .$

b. If the network effects are relatively high $( i . e . , 2 \gamma N \ge \lambda ( \beta + 1 ) )$ ), Firm 1 serves the entire market, while Firm 2 fails and exits the market. The equilibrium prices are $p _ { S B } ^ { 1 * } = ( s - \nu _ { 1 } ) ( 1 + \gamma ) / 2$ and $p _ { S B } ^ { 2 * } = 0$

## Case SS: Duopoly with Both Firms Offering a Free Core Product

Now we analyze competition in the presence of two free products. When both firms offer a free core product and a paid premium service separately, consumers with a lesser taste for the product choose between using either free offer while in contrast, consumers with higher taste purchase premium service from Firm 1 or Firm 2. Accordingly, consumers are divided into four segments: those located on the left of $x _ { 1 1 }$ use the free core product and pay for the premium service from Firm 1 and consumers located on the right of $x _ { 2 2 }$ use the free core product and purchase the premium service from Firm 2. The consumers located between $x _ { 1 1 }$ and $x _ { f 1 2 }$ do not value Firm 1’s additional service highly enough and only use its free core product, while consumers between $x _ { f 1 2 }$ and $x _ { 2 2 }$ use only Firm ${ 2 \mathrm { { \dot { s } } } }$ free core product. In this case, Firm 1 sells its premium service to a fraction $x _ { 1 1 } N$ of the consumers, while Firm 2 sells its service to a fraction $( 1 - x _ { 2 2 } ) N$ of the consumers. Consumers located between $x _ { 1 1 }$ and $x _ { 2 2 }$ have no contribution to the revenue of either firm, but they increase the installed base, thus contributing to the network effects of the product they use. As the number of free users expands, the utility that a customer derives from consuming the service in conjunction with the core product also increases, which results in a higher demand for the premium service. The corresponding market segmentation is shown in Figure 3.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \pi_ {S S} ^ {1} = p _ {S S} ^ {1} D _ {S S} ^ {1} = p _ {S S} ^ {1} x _ {1 1} N, \\ \pi_ {S S} ^ {2} = p _ {S S} ^ {2} D _ {S S} ^ {2} = p _ {S S} ^ {2} (1 - x _ {2 2}) N, \\ \text { s.t. } 0 <   x _ {1 1} <   x _ {f 1 2} <   x _ {2 2} <   1. \end{array}

According to the concept of rational expectation equilibrium, we have $Q _ { S S } ^ { 1 } = x _ { f 1 2 } N$ and $Q _ { S S } ^ { 2 } = ( 1 - x _ { f 1 2 } ) N$ in the surplus functions. Accordingly, we have the indifferent points as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} x _ {1 1} = \frac {(s - v _ {1}) (1 + \gamma) - p _ {S S} ^ {1}}{\lambda (\beta - 1)},   x _ {f 1 2} = \frac {1}{2} + \frac {(v _ {1} - v _ {2}) (1 + \gamma)}{2 (\lambda - \gamma N)}, \\ x _ {2 2} = 1 - \frac {(s - v _ {2}) (1 + \gamma) - p _ {S S} ^ {2}}{\lambda (\beta - 1)}. \end{array}
$$

From the above indifferent points, we find that the size of a firm’s user base largely depends on the network effects and the intrinsic value consumers derive from its free product. The free product with a higher quality is more attractive and thus captures more users. If the two firms’ free products are the same in terms of intrinsic value, the location of marginal users at point $x _ { f 1 2 }$ is just equal to 1/2 by setting $\nu _ { 1 } = \nu _ { 2 }$ , implying that each firm derives a market share that is equivalent to half the entire market potential.

The optimal prices are solved by differentiating the two firms’ profits with respect to prices. The equilibrium prices and demands for the two firms’ premium services are:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p _ {S S} ^ {1 *} = \frac {1}{2} (s - v _ {1}) (1 + \gamma), p _ {S S} ^ {2 *} = \frac {1}{2} (s - v _ {2}) (1 + \gamma), \\ D _ {S S} ^ {1 *} = \frac {(s - v _ {1}) (1 + \gamma)}{2 \lambda (\beta - 1)} N, D _ {S S} ^ {2 *} = \frac {(s - v _ {2}) (1 + \gamma)}{2 \lambda (\beta - 1)} N. \end{array}
$$

The profits at the equilibrium prices are given by:

$$
\pi_ {S S} ^ {1 *} = \frac {(s - v _ {1}) ^ {2} (1 + \gamma) ^ {2}}{4 \lambda (\beta - 1)} N, \pi_ {S S} ^ {2 *} = \frac {(s - v _ {2}) ^ {2} (1 + \gamma) ^ {2}}{4 \lambda (\beta - 1)} N.
$$

When a firm releases a free product to the market, it does not profit directly from the free offering but from the associated paid service. The free offering acts as a means for the firms to compete for consumers rather than a means for price competition. It helps to dampen the effect on a firm’s profit from the changes in the price charged by the competing firm and lessens price competition between them. Suppose first that the release of free product in this case is an effective strategy for each firm. At the equilibrium prices, it is straightforward that $D _ { S S } ^ { 1 * } { > } 0$ and $D _ { S S } ^ { 2 * } { > } 0$ The condition for $0 { < } Q _ { S S } ^ { i } - D _ { S S } ^ { i } { < } N$ (for $i = 1 , 2 )$ is given as follows:

$$
v _ {i} > \frac {\lambda (\beta - 1) v _ {j} + s (\lambda - \gamma N)}{\beta \lambda - \gamma N} - \frac {\lambda (\beta - 1) (\lambda - \gamma N)}{(1 + \gamma) (\beta \lambda - \gamma N)} \text {   for   } i, j = 1, 2 \text {   and   } i \neq j.\tag{6}
$$

According to the equilibrium solutions, when the free strategy is effective, a firm’s profit increases in the intensity of network effects and in the intrinsic value of its premium service $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \ s - \nu _ { i } )$ , and decreases in consumers’ misfit cost. A firm enjoys a larger market share and a higher profit if its product matches users’ expectations perfectly. Intuitively, when a free core product and a paid service are provided separately, only consumers with lower misfit cost in using the associated service would pay a premium for it. In addition, a firm always benefits from an increase in the network effects intensity. This is in sharp contrast with the result in Case BB where the network effects intensify competition between the firms.

Now we examine the scenarios in which the constraint $0 { < } x _ { 1 1 } { < } x _ { f 1 2 } { < } x _ { 2 2 } { < } 1$ in Case SS is not satisfied. It can be readily verified that $x _ { 1 1 } { > } 0$ and $x _ { 2 2 } < 1$ according to the equilibrium solutions, reducing to the situation where $x _ { 1 1 } { < } x _ { f 1 2 } { < } x _ { 2 2 }$ does not hold.

1. When $x _ { f 1 2 } \leq x _ { 1 1 }$ , no consumer will use only the free core product from Firm 1 without paying for its premium service. The equilibrium in this scenario has the same properties as that in Case BS where Firm 1 sells the bundled product and Firm 2 offers a free core product and a paid service.

2. Symmetrically, when $x _ { 2 2 } \leq x _ { f 1 2 }$ , no user will take Firm 2’s free product without purchasing its premium service. The equilibrium in this scenario is the same as that in Case SB, where Firm 1 offers a free core product and paid service and Firm 2 sells them in a bundled form.

3. When $x _ { 2 2 } \leq x _ { 1 1 }$ , neither firm captures free users. The firms’ strategy of offering a free core product in some sense converges to the strategy of bundling the core product with premium service for both firms in Case BB. Thus, the firms have equal market share, which is equal to 1/2, and the firms profits are $\pi _ { S S } ^ { 1 * } = \pi _ { S S } ^ { 2 * } = ( \beta \lambda - \gamma N ) N / 2$

a. If the marginal points in Figure 3 satisfy $x _ { 1 1 } { < } x _ { f 1 2 } { < } x _ { 2 2 }$ , the prices in equilibrium are $p _ { S S } ^ { 1 * } = ( s - \nu _ { 1 } ) ( 1 + \gamma ) / 2$ and $p _ { S S } ^ { 2 * } = ( s - \nu _ { 2 } ) ( 1 + \gamma ) / 2$

The firm would benefit from selling a bundle of product and service provided that the quality of its basic product is superior to its competitor’s. A higher value of core product means a lower value of the associated service. This finding is consistent with empirical observations. A good example in the database management system is perhaps the competition between Microsoft’s SQL Server and Oracle’s RDBMS. However, it is commonly believed that Oracle takes a lead in product quality and delivers better performance. Another example is in the market for multiplayer online role-playing games, such as World of Warcraft and Star Wars: The Old Republic. The former game charges a subscription fee for players whereas the latter allows players to play for free and pay a premium only if they want access to additional features.

When the intrinsic values consumers derive from the basic products are similar and not very large, that is, when the values of $\nu _ { 1 }$ and $\nu _ { 2 }$ fall in region $0 { - } a { - } e { - } d$ labeled SS, the corresponding region contains an outcome that each firm considers offering a free product regardless of the decision of its competitor, because they can obtain more profit than when bundling the core product with service (i.e., $\pi _ { S S } ^ { 1 * } > \pi _ { B S } ^ { 1 * }$ and $\pi _ { S S } ^ { 2 * } > \pi _ { S B } ^ { 2 * } )$ . This explains why some firms switch from the traditional strategy to freemium. For example, the provider of MailChimp announced it was going freemium in $2 0 0 9 . ^ { 6 }$ Since the e-mail applications are similar in terms of functionality and deliverability, offering a free plan to consumers could bring in potential paying customers who need to send more e-mails per month. Other e-mail applications such as Mailjet also allow users to deliver a limited volume of e-mails without paying a dime.

However, once the intrinsic values of the basic products exceed ^v, offering the bundle of product and service can also be part of the equilibrium. Given that the other firm bundles the product, the best response would be to use the bundling strategy as well, as each firm is better off selling the bundle than releasing a free product to the market. Though two equilibria that correspond to parameters falling within region $h { - } b { - } e { - } c$ coexist, the equilibrium that does not involve introducing a free product is payoff dominated. This finding is summarized in Proposition 5.

Proposition 5 (Dominant Equilibrium): When two equilibria prevail in which both adopt the strategy of bundling core product and service or both adopt the strategy of releasing a free product, the firms will make greater profits in the former equilibrium.

The reason for Proposition 3 can be understood by noting that the free strategy lessens the competition between the product-related premium services of the two firms. When both firms consider providing a free product to the market, the free strategy leads to a direct competition between the two firms’ free offer, which essentially works so as to dampen the effect of changes in the price charged by one firm on the other firm’s profit generated from the paid service. As shown in Figure 4, when the values of the core products’ quality fall in region $h { - } b { - } e { - } c ,$ multiple equilibria prevail. Inspection of the payoffs in Table 2 shows that in this region, offering a core product free of charge is less profitable for a firm compared with selling the bundle of product and service. Consumers who have a high preference for a firm’s core product, even if no product is given for free, are still willing to purchase the bundled product as long as the core product is attractive enough. However, if the firms increase the quality of their free products to a relatively high level, the free offering would cannibalize potential sales of their premium services. Under this condition, the dominant strategy for a firm is to sell a bundle irrespective of its competitor’s action.

In many cases the equilibrium is not necessarily Pareto optimal, because there exist some nonequilibrium outcomes of the game that would be better for both firms. The following proposition presents the condition under which the firms are caught in a prisoner’s dilemma, in which each firm chooses to offer a free core product and a paid service, even though selling them as a bundle would generate higher profit for both firms.

Proposition 6 (Prisoner’s Dilemma Condition): Define the threshold value $\hat { \nu } =$

$s - \frac { \sqrt { 2 \lambda ( \beta - 1 ) ( \beta \lambda - \gamma N ) } } { 1 + \gamma }$ for $i = 1 , 2$ When both firms offer a free basic product, if the intrinsic value of Firm i’s basic product is larger than $\hat { \nu } \ ( i . e . , \ \nu _ { i } > \hat { \nu } )$ Firm i would be better off if neither firm were to offer a free product.

The term s in the threshold represents the intrinsic value that consumers derive from the whole product, while the second term in the threshold can be seen as a critical value for the quality of service. Thus, the threshold value ^v is the quality difference between the whole product and premium service. Proposition 6 implies that when the intrinsic value of Firm i’s core product exceeds ^v, the firm would earn greater profit when neither firm offers a free product than when both do. Intuitively, if a firm’s free product is attractive enough to users, the potential customers who would have purchased the bundle had the firm not offered a free product may now find it more beneficial to use only the free product without any payment. In this scenario, the firm that offers a free product would suffer potential customer loss. Proposition 6 also implies that if the service quality is very low $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , the value of $s - \nu _ { i }$ is small), the potential consumers would rather use the free product than pay a premium for the less attractive service. As a result, the free strategy yields lower profit relative to the bundling strategy, under which the firm can capture consumers who are sensitive to the quality of the core product and would pay for it. In the shaded region $h { - } b { - } e { - } c$ in Figure 4, the two firms’ core product quality is higher than the threshold ^v. In this region, both firms would be better off when neither offers the core product for free. The firms will be caught in a prisoner’s dilemma if both of them offer the free core product in equilibrium. However, in the region $t { - } h { - } k { - } n$ where only Firm 1’s core product has a higher quality than ^v, Firm 1 would be better off when both firms sell a bundle than when neither does. Likewise, in the region $u { - } m { - } g { - }$ h, where only Firm ${ 2 \mathrm { { } s } }$ core product quality exceeds ^v, Firm 2 would be better off when both firms sell a bundle. Hence, if consumers derive great value from the firms’ core products, the bundling strategy dominates the free strategy.

## Network Effects Intensity and Firms’ Decisions

To investigate how the changes of network effects intensity affect the firm’s decision in terms of whether to release a free product, and thus the equilibrium outcomes, we examine how the thresholds for the intrinsic value of core product given in Proposition 4 change with the intensity of network effects.

It is easy to find that ${ \partial \hat { \nu } } / { \partial \gamma }$ is always positive. Given the condition $\gamma { < } \lambda / N$ in Case SS, the threshold $C \nu _ { j } + D$ always increases in γ when $\lambda / N { < } \gamma _ { 0 } .$ , but if $\lambda / N { > } \gamma _ { 0 } , C \nu _ { j } +$ D first increases in γ and then decreases in it, where

$$
\gamma_ {0} = \frac {3 \lambda N + 2 N (s - v _ {j}) + N ^ {2} - \sqrt {N (\lambda + N) (4 (s - v _ {j}) (\lambda (\beta + 1) + 2 N) + N (N - 4 \lambda \beta + 5 \lambda))}}{2 N (N + v _ {j} - s)}.
$$

When the initial network effects intensity is not high and the change is not large enough, the threshold value for the quality of core product at which the firm should release a free product increases. Therefore, in the presence of a small intensity of network effects, a firm needs to improve the intrinsic value consumers derive from its free product so as to make the release of a free product a profitable strategy. With an increase in the intensity of network effects, the region of equilibrium SS in Figure 4 increases in size, while the region of equilibrium BB decreases in size since the threshold ^v increases as γ grows. By contrast, if the intensity of network effects is relatively high, a further increase in γ enables a firm to lower its free product’s quality as the high network effects partially compensate for the quality reduction of the free product, and thus the firm can move more functionalities and features to its premium service. Due to the relatively high network effects intensity and the resulting high intrinsic value of paid service, the firm can always make more profits by releasing a free product irrespective of the decision of its competitor.

Notice that if the intensity of network effects is very weak, the region of equilibrium BB would expand to a maximum size, while the region of equilibrium SS would shrink but not vanish. This theoretical finding indicates that compared to products with relatively high network effects, relatively few products that display weak network effects are given away for free. This result is evident in the market for software such as computer diagnostic tools and personal firewall software. These software packages are stand-alone, single-user products and thus generate weak direct network effects from the products’ installed base. The major part of the value that users derive is independent of the numbers of the products’ adopters. Hence, few of them are provided with a free basic version. In contrast, a free offering is more prevalent in the market for communication software (e.g., Skype, LINE, MailChimp, IncrediMail) and online computer games (e.g., League of Legends, Dota 2). A growing number of providers give away their basic products for free to induce users to experience and consequently purchase the paid complementary goods or services.

## Network Effects Intensity and Profits

Proposition 7 states how a firm’s profit changes due to an exogenous change in the intensity of network effects.

Proposition 7 (Impact of Network Intensity on Profits):

a. When both firms offer a free core product and premium service, the network effects intensity has a positive effect on the firms’ profits. An increase in the product’s network intensity increases the equilibrium prices and the demands for the premium service.

b. When neither firm offers a free core product in equilibrium, the firms’ profits always decrease as the intensity of network effects increases.

c. Define the threshold value $\begin{array} { r } { \bar { \nu } _ { 1 } = s - \frac { N ( \beta \lambda - \gamma N ) } { \lambda ( 1 + \beta ) + N ( 1 - \gamma ) } } \end{array}$ . When only Firm i offers a free core product in equilibrium, it always benefits from an increase in the network intensity γ. While for Firm $j ,$ an increase in γ has a negative effect on its profit if the intrinsic value of Firm i’s free product $\nu _ { i }$ is higher than $\bar { \nu } _ { 1 }$ , but a positive effect when $\nu _ { i } < \bar { \nu } _ { 1 }$

Proposition 7(a) shows that with the introduction of a free offering, each firm obtains more profit in the presence of increased network effects intensity. The equilibrium outcomes in Case SS show that when both firms use free strategy, the optimal price and demand for premium service grow in response to an increase in the network effects intensity. Consequently, a firm is always profiting from the increase in the network effects. This result contrasts with that in Etzion and Pang [13], according to which a firm’s profit may decrease in the intensity of network effects when both firms offer a complementary online service. Nevertheless, it is consistent with the popularity of free offerings of utility applications, such as instant message applications and multiplayer game applications, because this category of information product tends to have relatively higher network intensity. Taking the free-to-play multiplayer online games League of Legends and World of Tanks as an example, it is evident that the former has a larger user base and much higher network effects. Therefore, the former game, League of Legends can bring more profits than the latter, which is in also accordance with the market report by SuperData Research [38].

In contrast, if neither firm provides a core product for free, the intensity of network effects always has a negative impact on the equilibrium profits. As the network intensity increases, a firm’s optimal price decreases due to the intensification of price competition. However, the equilibrium demand for each firm’s bundled product remains unchanged due to the symmetry in the firms. As a result, a reduced price results in a lower profit.

An important insight from Proposition 7(c) is that when only one firm provides a free product, this firm can always achieve greater profit by enhancing the product’s network effects. The introduction of a free offering increases its user base due to the increasing network effects, which in turn pushes up the demand for its premium service and thus the firm’s profit. Whereas, for the firm that does not use the free strategy, its profit decreases with an increase in the network intensity if the intrinsic value consumers derive from the competing firm’s free product is higher than a given threshold value. Under this condition, consumers who place a high value on the core product prefer to take the competing firm’s free product rather than purchase its bundled product. With an increase in network intensity, more and more consumers switch to the free product, resulting in shrinkage in its market share and consequently reduction in its profit.

According to Figure 5, when the intensity of network effects is lower than $0 . 1 8 \ ( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \gamma < 0 . 1 8 )$ , the equilibrium strategy is that both firms take the SS strategy. As the network intensity increases but is not higher than $0 . 6 ( \mathrm { i . e . , } 0 . 1 8 < \gamma < 0 . 6 )$ , the profit that Firm 1 derives in Case BS is higher compared to that derived in Case SS or Case SB (i.e., $\pi _ { B S } ^ { 1 * } > \pi _ { S S } ^ { 1 * } = \pi _ { S B } ^ { 1 * } )$ . ## Market Size and Profits

One of the strategic reasons for a firm to give away a free product is to develop a consumer-installed base of the product. By capturing a large use base, the firm can generate revenue by selling the associated premium service. The following proposition describes the impact of an increase in the market size on firms’ profits.

Proposition 8 (Impact of Market Size on Profits):

a. If both firms offer a free core product and charge for premium service in equilibrium, a firm’s profit always increases in the market size N.

b. Define the threshold value $\bar { \gamma } = \beta \lambda / ( 2 N )$ . When neither firm offers a free core product in equilibrium, a firm’s profit increases in the market size N if the intensity of network effects is lower than -γ. The result is reversed when $\gamma > \bar { \gamma }$

c. Define the threshold value $\begin{array} { r } { \bar { \nu } _ { 2 } = s + \frac { 4 \gamma ^ { 2 } \dot { N } ^ { 2 } } { \lambda ( \beta + 1 ) ( 1 + \gamma ) } - \frac { 3 \gamma N - \lambda } { 1 + \gamma } } \end{array}$ . If only Firm i offers a free core product and charges for premium service in equilibrium, the profit of Firm i always increases in the market size N. While for Firm j, an increase in N leads to a reduction in the profit if the intrinsic value of Firm i’s free product $\nu _ { i }$ is larger than $\bar { \nu } _ { 2 }$ , but to a rise in profit when $\nu _ { i } < \bar { \nu } _ { 2 }$

When a firm offers a free product, it always benefits from an increase in the number of potential consumers, which contributes to promoting product adoption. This possibly explains why so many products are introduced to the market for free and only the complementary goods or services are charged for.

However, if only one firm uses the free product strategy, the quality of its free core product has an important impact on the competing firm, especially when the free product’s quality is very high. In this scenario, the more consumers in the market, the more potential customers of the bundled product captured by the free product, thus resulting in a decrease in the competing firm’s profit generated from the bundled product.

When both firms adopt the bundling strategy, the equilibrium profits do not necessarily increase in the market size with the presence of network effects. The firms will benefit from an increase in the size of market only when the network effects intensity is lower than a given threshold value. Otherwise, as the intensity of network effects rises, the firms have to cut their prices to attract customers due to the fiercer competition, but the increase in demand is not sufficient to offset the lost profit due to the reduction in price. However, if the product exhibits no network effects, an increase in the market size can always lead to higher equilibrium profit. This is because the price is independent of market size but the demand increases in it.

We illustrate how a firm’s profit changes and thus the market equilibrium switches with the change of market size in Figure 6. In performing this numerical analysis, we use the same numerical examples discussed previously except for the parameter value for market size. In particular, we set $\gamma = 0 . 5$ and $N \leq 3 5 , 0 0 0$ , such that the conditions required in the cases are satisfied. Figure 6 shows that when the market size is smaller than 10,000, Firm 1 gains greater profit in Case BS relative to other cases. In addition to Firm 1, Firm 2 also profits more in Case BS since the two firms are symmetric in this example. As a result, BS is the equilibrium strategy when $N { < } 1 0 , 0 0 0$ . When the market size varies between 10,000 and 20,778, both firms derive higher profit by taking SS strategy. Thus, the equilibrium switches from Case BS to Case SS. If, however, the market size increases such that it is larger than 20,778, two equilibria emerge: an equilibrium where both firms take SS strategy and an equilibrium where both take BB strategy. Though both are feasible, the two equilibria yield different profit for the firms. When the market size is larger than 20,778 but does not exceed 31,723, Figure 6 shows that the firms get greater profit under Case BB. By contrast, if the market size exceeds 31,723, both firms are better off taking SS strategy than BB strategy. Hence, the equilibrium shifts to Case SS.

In addition, we compare the firms’ profits in a fully covered market and an uncovered market to show whether they are better off acting as a local monopoly.

## Case SB/Case BS: Local Monopoly with Only One Firm Offering a Free Core Product

Suppose that only Firm 1 offers a free core product and charges for the premium service. The consumers indifferent regarding whether the free core product and premium service are provided by Firm 1 are located at $\begin{array} { r } { x _ { 1 1 } ^ { l } = \frac { ( s - \nu _ { 1 } ) ( 1 + \gamma ) - p _ { S B } ^ { l 1 } } { \lambda ( \beta - 1 ) } } \end{array}$ , while the consumers indifferent regarding whether to use Firm 1’s free product or not to use it are located at $\begin{array} { r } { x _ { 1 } ^ { l } = \frac { \nu _ { 1 } ( 1 + \gamma ) } { \lambda - \gamma N } } \end{array}$ . ## Case SS: Local Monopoly with Both Firms Offering a Free Core Product

When both firms provide a free core product in an uncovered market, the market divides up into five segments, including the segment of consumers who purchase the premium service offered by either firm, the segment of those who use either free product, and the segment of consumers who use neither product. The consumers indifferent regarding whether to pay for Firm 1’s premium service or to use only its free product are located at $\begin{array} { r } { x _ { 1 1 } ^ { l } = \frac { \overline { { ( s - \nu _ { 1 } ) ( 1 + \gamma ) - p _ { S S } ^ { l 1 } } } } { \lambda ( \beta - 1 ) } } \end{array}$ , and those who are indifferent regarding whether to use Firm 1’s free product or not to use it are located at $\begin{array} { r } { x _ { 1 } ^ { l } = \frac { \nu _ { 1 } ( 1 + \gamma ) } { \lambda - \gamma N } } \end{array}$ Symmetrically, the consumers indifferent regarding whether to use Firm ${ 2 \mathrm { { \dot { s } } } }$ free product or not to use it are located at $\begin{array} { r } { x _ { 2 } ^ { l } = 1 - \frac { \nu _ { 2 } ( 1 + \gamma ) } { \lambda - \gamma N } } \end{array}$ , and those who are indifferent regarding whether to use only Firm 2’s free product or to purchase its premium service are located at $\begin{array} { r } { x _ { 2 2 } ^ { l } = 1 - \frac { ( s - \nu _ { 2 } ) ( 1 + \gamma ) - p _ { S S } ^ { l 2 } } { \lambda ( \beta - 1 ) } } \end{array}$ . Given the consumers’ choices, the firms choose their prices $p _ { S S } ^ { l 1 }$ and $p _ { S S } ^ { l 2 }$ to maximize their profit. The profit functions for Firm 1 and Firm 2 are $\pi _ { S S } ^ { l 1 } = p _ { S S } ^ { l 1 } x _ { 1 } ^ { l } N$ and $\pi _ { S S } ^ { l 2 } = p _ { S S } ^ { l 2 } ( 1 - x _ { 2 } ^ { l } ) N _ { \Omega }$ , respectively. Differentiating the profits with respect to prices and solving for the two prices, we get the equilibrium prices $p _ { S S } ^ { l 1 * } = \textstyle { \frac { 1 } { 2 } } ( s - \nu _ { 1 } ) ( 1 + \gamma )$ and $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { S S } ^ { l 2 * } = \frac { 1 } { 2 } ( s - \nu _ { 2 } ) ( 1 + \gamma ) } \end{array}$ . Thus, in equilibrium, the values of $\dot { x } _ { 1 1 } ^ { l }$ and $x _ { 2 2 } ^ { l }$ are $\begin{array} { r } { x _ { 1 1 } ^ { l } = \frac { ( s - \nu _ { 1 } ) ( 1 + \gamma ) } { 2 \lambda ( \beta - 1 ) } } \end{array}$ and $\begin{array} { r } { x _ { 2 2 } ^ { l } = 1 - \frac { ( s - \nu _ { 2 } ) ( 1 + \gamma ) } { 2 \lambda ( \beta - 1 ) } } \end{array}$ The profits of the two firms are $\begin{array} { r } { \pi _ { S S } ^ { l 1 * } = \frac { ( s - \nu _ { 1 } ) ^ { 2 } ( 1 + \gamma ) ^ { 2 } } { 4 \lambda ( \beta - 1 ) } N } \end{array}$ and $\begin{array} { r } { \pi _ { S S } ^ { l 2 * } = \frac { ( s - \nu _ { 2 } ) ^ { 2 } ( 1 + \gamma ) ^ { 2 } } { 4 \lambda ( \beta - 1 ) } N } \end{array}$

The constraint for the market structure to be realized in this case is $0 < x _ { 1 1 } ^ { l } < x _ { 1 } ^ { l } < x _ { 2 } ^ { l } < x _ { 2 2 } ^ { l } < 1$ , where $x _ { 1 } ^ { l } < x _ { 2 } ^ { l }$ is required such that the firms do not compete directly for the marginal consumers. Checking for this constraint yields the condition that $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { s ( \lambda - \gamma N ) } { ( 2 \beta - 1 ) \lambda - \gamma N } < \nu _ { i } < \frac { \lambda - \gamma N } { 1 + \gamma } - \nu _ { j } } \end{array}$ for $i , j = 1 , 2$ and $i { \neq } j$ and $\lambda - \gamma N { > } 0$

Comparing the equilibrium outcomes in the uncovered market with those in a fully covered market, we find that the firms’ profits from the premium service are not changed whether or not they act as a local monopoly. The reason is that the free products dampen the impact of price change of one firm on the profit of the other firm.

To investigate the firms’ optimal strategy choice and whether they are better off in acting as a local monopoly, we plot the firms’ profit under different cases in both a fully covered market and an uncovered market when network intensity varies. In this example, the condition for both firms to act as a monopoly by taking SS strategy is not satisfied, so Figure 8 does not reflect Firm 1’s profit under Case SS in the uncovered market. In addition, given the values of other parameters, the firms can play as a monopoly only if the intensity of network effects is relatively weak $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \ \gamma \leq 0 . 7 2 2 2$ in this example). In Figure 8, we find that when the network intensity is lower than 0.7222, the two firms are actually better off taking BB strategy under which they play as a monopoly relative to other strategies in which they compete directly for the marginal consumers or act as a monopoly. However, if the value of network intensity exceeds 0.7222, it is infeasible for the firms to play as a monopoly. Hence, they compete directly for the marginal consumers.

## Conclusions

We analyze duopoly firms’ product offering strategies in the presence of network effects. In our model setup, a firm can give away its core product that has a stand-alone value to consumers for free while charging for the associated value-added service that allows consumers access to premium content or features, or it can bundle the core product with premium service and sell them for a single price. Though previous literature on free trials examines the effect of the introduction of free software on commercial software, it does not provide insights on cases in a competitive market. This paper extends past research by adding competition.

We find that when a firm gives away its core product for free, it always benefits from an increase in the product’s intensity of network effects. This finding is in accordance with previous work [8] in a monopoly, which suggests that under strong network effects, it is more profitable for a firm to offer a free trial product. The release of a free product attracts potential customers and thus builds up a large user base, which results in large network effects. This in turn pushes up the demand for product-related premium service. In addition, the free strategy leads to a direct competition between the free product and the competing firm’s product. This effect essentially works to dampen the impact of changes in the price charged by the competing firm on the profit of the firm that provides a core product for free. If the two firms take different strategies, for the firm that chooses to sell a bundle, an increase in the intensity of network effects does not necessarily have a positive effect on its profit. Whether it can profit from the increasing network effects intensity largely depends on the intrinsic value consumers derive from its competitor’s free product. We also show that if the firms choose to use the bundling strategy regardless of their competitors’ action, then both firms can achieve more profits than in other cases—in other words, this equilibrium is Pareto optimal. By contrast, if both firms adopt free strategy equilibrium, they might be caught in a prisoner’s dilemma. In other words, although under certain conditions, the strategy of offering a free product is an equilibrium strategy, the firms would be better off not doing so, especially when the intrinsic value that consumers derive from their free products is very large.

We also examine the firms’ strategy choice and pricing choice when they play as a local monopoly. The results show that if a firm adopts the bundling strategy, the profit it derives in the uncovered market is different from that in the fully covered market. By contrast, if a firm takes the free strategy, its profit from the premium service remains unchanged. However, by playing as a local monopoly, the firm can acquire a larger installed base. We also find that when the network intensity is relatively weak, both firms are better off using a bundling strategy under which they play as a monopoly relative to other strategies in which they compete directly for the marginal consumers or act as a monopoly.

One possible direction for future research is to examine the impact of introducing functionality limited free software on the diffusion of a new product in a competitive market. Intuitively, a free offer provides a source of product diffusion by word-ofmouth effect. A free offer of one product may have a positive or negative effect on the diffusion of other products. Another possible direction is for duopoly software firms to consider premium service quality decisions, which could lead to useful product design implications. It is also of interest to examine a two-stage model where an entrant uses the freemium strategy to compete for customers with the incumbent that sells a bundle of product and service.
