# Searching for information in a time-pressured setting: experiences with a Text-based and an Image-based decision support system

Mansoor Aminilari<sup>1</sup>, Ramakrishnan Pakath<sup>\*</sup>

DSIS, School of Management, C.M. Gatton College of Business and Economics, University of Kentucky, Lexington, KY 40506-0034, United States

Received 1 January 2003; accepted 1 February 2004 Available online 24 July 2004

## Abstract

Searching for the right information and making quick, accurate decisions within time-pressured settings is often non-trivial. We contrast the relative efficacies of written English (Text) and a more concise, compact communication mode (Image) for information search and decision making by using a financial incentive scheme to apply implicit time pressure on subjects. We found that, while Image users earned as much as Text users, they achieved this earnings parity by following speedier but less accurate strategies. We conclude with thoughts on possible refinements to our work that could steer subjects in the ideal direction of fast, accurate, lucrative decisions with languages like Image. <sup>D</sup> 2004 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information gathering/search strategies; Decision-making strategies; Time pressure; Symbolic languages; Multimedia systems; Exante DSS evaluation; Induced value theory

## 1. Introduction

Searching for information is routine in many decision-making situations. Prior research (e.g., Refs. [1,8]) has discussed practices relating to the acquisition and use of information in organizations. Ackoff [1] suggests that managers typically suffer from a shortage of decision-relevant information and a simultaneous overabundance of irrelevant information. Feldman and March [8] observe that organizations over-invest in information generally, while also gathering additional information when specific, important decisions are in prospect. These observations, unfortunately, still hold true in today’s internetworked business enterprises.

Sometimes, information search must be performed in a sequential manner. The decision-maker (DM) relies on the outcomes of prior search efforts to either guide subsequent search direction or cease information acquisition and begin decision making. Often, a DM is also faced with a time bound with added incentives for earlier decision task completion. Incentives may directly accrue to the DM (e.g., a cash bonus) or may be indirect (e.g., the firm involved snags a lucrative order). The compulsion to not merely meet, but to beat, a pre-defined timebound could result in the creation of <sup>b</sup>time pressure<sup>Q</sup>.

Acquiring decision support information is subject to costly errors of two kinds: over-acquiring and incurring excessive informational costs; and underacquiring and incurring excessive risks of decisional error. The presence of time pressure could exacerbate either kind of error, particularly the latter. To mitigate such errors, a DM must implement an effective <sup>b</sup>information gathering and decision making strategy’’ that would also account for any resource constraints (e.g., a time bound) and incentive mechanisms (e.g., a reward/penalty for early/tardy completion) in place. In some circumstances, a DM must device and implement an appropriate strategy in real time. In others, suitable strategies have already been defined—the DM must learn/memorize, store, recall and implement the appropriate strategy. As a third possibility, a DM may opt or be forced to pursue both strategies. An instance is when the DM attempts recalling and implementing a pre-defined strategy but resorts to improvising/innovating when the attempt fails.

Our interest here lies primarily in the kind of scenario just exemplified. In such situations, both the means and the times available for strategy learning, storage, subsequent recollection and application influence how successful a DM is at ensuing decision making episodes. With such considerations in mind, in this paper, we examine the impacts of two different information sources, direct monetary incentives and induced time pressure on a DM’s ability to learn, recall and apply predefined information gathering and decision making strategies. Each predefined strategy is an optimal strategy defined for a particular situation. Many such optimal strategies, applicable in different circumstances, are made available for study beforehand. Thereafter, instances of such circumstances are randomly generated and the DM must either recall and apply the appropriate strategy or devise a strategy that could be suboptimal. As such, the strategy applied would either converge with the optimal one or deviate from it to some degree. We use the extent of deviation and the decision-making time to determine the reward/penalty that accrues to the DM.

Our focus in this work is on two kinds of information sources for providing decision support information: conventional, written English text and a special, symbolic language that we have devised, called <sup>b</sup>Image<sup>Q</sup> (where <sup>b</sup>Image<sup>Q</sup> does not necessarily connote <sup>b</sup>iconic<sup>Q</sup>). The efficacy of a symbolic alternative to English for factual information recall in a time-pressured, incentive-driven setting was first reported in Marsden, Pakath and Wibowo [15–17]. Our work is an extension in that we focus on both factual information recall and information search/ purchase strategy recall in such a setting.

Our interest in examining an alternative to written English for such tasks is based on the following considerations. Written English dominates much of human–computer interaction but some of its features could manifest themselves as drawbacks in specific communication settings. First, English must usually be processed word by word, sequentially, from left to right and top to down. A suitably constructed language that relaxes either or both of these requirements, to whatever extent possible and feasible given a particular communication context, could result in speedier information processing. Second, English is amongst the most complex of languages with exceptions to its rules being considerably more in number than the rules themselves. A recent research study [19] argues that this complexity could be why there are twice as many identified dyslexics in English-speaking countries than in those (e.g., Italy, Spain, Finland, (the former) Czechoslovakia and Japan) with a one-to-one relationship between letters and sounds. Likewise, in a visual medium, a language with just a single interpretation for each of its constructs should result in enhanced comprehension speed and precision. This feature is especially desirable in today’s largely multi-cultural, global, business and social exchanges. Third, business telecommunication is increasingly shifting to mobile devices (like palmtops and web phones) that tend to have smaller displays than conventional laptop, desktop, and larger equipment. Yet, the use of mobile gadgets like web phones in the US lags that in Japan and Europe. Amongst the reasons being posited is that Americans (and, perhaps, other English-dependent individuals) are uncomfortable with the small displays that necessitate relatively larger amounts of screen scrolls for processing English text. A language that is more compact than English and yet is as expressive for a given context could be more readily deployed on modern mobile units.

The specific language alternative that we examine is intended to mitigate each of the above drawbacks of English. While it is by no means a general substitute and could certainly be improved, it and languages like it may prove to be more appropriate under the right circumstances. In this research, we describe one such circumstance where brief messages involving limited vocabulary are used for information exchanges in a time-pressured setting. Such exchanges are common in many real-world contexts such as stock trading, emergency medical treatment, and competency testing. Consider daily stock trading. A day trader must monitor information about stocks of interest and successfully classify each stock (buy, sell, hold) within a small duration in order to increase net equity. The trader must make quick, accurate decisions. If (s)he takes an undue amount of time with one stock, the consequences could include not only lost income, but also lost investment. However, undue haste and resultant mistakes could also be equally devastating. The trader must be adequately supported in making this tradeoff and a decision support system (DSS) equipped to quickly and effectively convey cutting-edge information using a compact, yet precise and unambiguous, vocabulary set would help alleviate both types of risks. The broad question that we examine here is whether DMs equipped with an Image-based DSS do as well as, or better than, those using a Text-based system in settings like this.

Our research platform contains two sets of software modules. Sections 3 and 4 contain illustrated descriptions of key features of the two modules used by subjects in our actual experiment. Section 5 briefly describes the two modules that we used to train subjects in the appropriate use of the preceding, actual experiment module set. In order to ensure the sufficiency of the experiment-specific features/settings of the platform, a number of individuals critiqued the platform in three separate experimental pre-test stages. Section 6 provides an overview of this process. In Section 7, we discuss the definition and operationalization of a measure called <sup>b</sup>deviation from optimality<sup>Q</sup>. ## 2. Literature review

Given that our work is an extension of Refs. [15– 17], we, too, draw upon prior research on task performance under time constraints and time pressure, ex-ante evaluation of DSSs and alternate media, and the <sup>b</sup>induced value theory<sup>Q</sup> approach to DSS evaluation. Since Ref. [15] presents an exhaustive review of the literature in each of these areas, we encourage the reader to peruse this work rather than replicate that effort. As such, our intent here is three-fold. First (Section 2.1), we discuss key features and findings of Refs. [15–17] as it stimulates this effort. Second (Section 2.2), we present a brief review of representative literature on information purchase/search strategies, an issue that is not addressed in Refs. [15–17]. Third (Section 2.3), we present a brief update to the extensive review of literature on task performance under time constraints and time pressure in Ref. [15] by merely citing some of the more recent work. Our intent is that our update taken in conjunction with the more detailed effort in Ref. [15] should provide the necessary background for the ensuing discussions in this paper.

2.1. Literature on alternate information sources and time pressure

Marsden, Pakath and Wibowo [15–17] consider decision-making contexts where a support system user relies upon information that appears in the form of short, precise message <sup>b</sup>capsules<sup>Q</sup>. These capsules seek to quickly, accurately and concisely convey information concerning a decision problem of interest. The nature of the information <sup>b</sup>source<sup>Q</sup> used by the system (which combines an information <sup>b</sup>mode<sup>Q</sup> and an information <sup>b</sup>flow rate<sup>Q</sup>), coupled with the complexity of the information conveyed, may result in appreciable differences in user performance. The focus of this work lies in examining this issue in an exploratory study.

The study makes two contributions. It first describes the design and construction of a generic experimental platform that facilitates the <sup>b</sup>ex-ante<sup>Q</sup> evaluation of a number of systems belonging to the decisional class in a controlled laboratory setting. The platform enables one to simulate essential features of a system using the Text, Image and/or Audio modes in combination with one or more of three flow rates, labeled normal, slower and faster. One may provide information concerning different types of tasks. Secondly, the study describes the application of a customized instance of the platform to an exploratory study intended to assess subject performance (using a variety of metrics) across modes, flow rates and task complexity levels, in the presence of temporal and monetary pressures. These experiments, like the present work, rely heavily upon the postulate of non-satiation due to Vernon Smith [21] and his work on induced value theory in experimental economics. In doing so, the study ties accruable rewards (positive and negative) to both time and quality in a unique manner, by tracking performance with regard to both speed and accuracy. Few existing multimedia systems-related studies employ the induced value theory in this manner.

## 2.2. Representative literature concerning information purchase/search strategies

The literature on human information search/purchase strategies in single-media settings (e.g., oral or written text) dates back several decades and spans multiple fields such as cognitive psychology, economics and statistics. We suffice by presenting a concise overview of a small sampling of such research primarily because few simultaneously deal with the issues of alternate communication media, time pressure, strategy recall and search in the presence of monetary incentives tied to both performance quality and speed. We focus primarily on experimental design-related issues rather than on experimental findings given the wide chasm between the goal and setting that we use and those used in the reviewed works. This focus also reveals that all of the reviewed studies suffer from the same subject stimulus-related pitfalls (articulated by Smith [21]) that were documented in Ref. [15].

Pitz [20] examines information-seeking behavior in a task in which subjects, after purchasing one or more chips from two bags, must choose the <sup>b</sup>right<sup>Q</sup> bag. Three groups of 30 subjects each were allowed up to 5, 10 and 20 purchases, respectively, before having to make a decision. A correct choice resulted in a 5-cent reward, while an incorrect one yields a 3-cent loss. No time bounds or time pressure were involved. Wendt [23] conducted an experiment where subjects were asked to select the predominant color in a two-colored wheel given information about which color a pointer came to rest upon following a wheel spin. A total of 32 undergraduate students participated where each subject made a total of 180 estimates. A point system was use to keep track of rewards during the experiment. This was then converted into a monetary reward ranging between US\$2.50 and US\$3.50. No explicit time bounds or pressure-inducing mechanisms were applied. Most subjects completed the experiment in between 1.5 and 2 h.

Hershman and Levine [10] examine decisionmaker behavior with respect to an optimum purchasing policy, which maximizes expected value in a hypothetical military reconnaissance problem context. A subject must decide which one of two binomial hypotheses generates the data presented. One free sample starts the process. Subjects possess the option of either making a decision on the basis of this information or purchasing one additional sample at a stated price and then deciding. Overall 330 trials took place. The two players scoring the highest total points received prizes of US\$10 each. A subject received all pertinent information in text mode on a computer, which also provided feedback to subjects. Levine, Samet and Brahlek [14] investigate the effect of limitations on the amounts of available information and resources necessary to purchase information on the decision maker when acquiring information from up to three sources. The frequency with which they query each source is a function of the constraints mentioned. The study uses one mode and makes no mention of any kind of incentive mechanism.

Connolly and Gillani [6] examine human performance in tasks involving information acquisition and use. Two experiments were conducted involving 60 engineering student subjects in each. Subjects acted as analysts for an imaginary company that sold predictions of football game results to clients. To help make these predictions, a subject could purchase assessments from one or more <sup>b</sup>football experts<sup>Q</sup> on retainer to the company. The company paid cash rebates to its clients when its predictions differed from actual game results. While information purchase is wholly textual, prediction errors are displayed using both graphs and tables. No time constraints are mentioned and the only motivators used are the information purchase and erroneous prediction costs. Connolly and Thorn [7] propose that, when informational costs increase, a decision-maker must balance costs against improved decisional accuracy. Through laboratory evidence, the study asserts that humans often perform poorly in this regard. Subjects may assess each item of information independently of other purchases in a computerinteractive game involving sales distribution centers. Three experiments were conducted using undergraduate engineering and policy and management students. In two of the three experiments, two kinds of indirect incentives are used—information purchase costs and penalties for errors. In the third, subjects received course credit in addition to a monetary reward for participation. No time constraints are mentioned.

## 2.3. Update to literature on task performance under time constraints and time pressure

Since the literature review in Ref. [15] pertaining to studies on task performance under time constraints or time pressure, several other studies have emerged in this category (e.g., Refs. [2,4,5,9,12,13,22]). However, as reported in Ref. [15], we too were unable to locate any that simultaneously span the dimensions of creating a time pressured decision setting, controlled ex-ante system evaluation in a laboratory setting, and the application of the induced value theory where sufficiently sized monetary rewards and penalties are closely tied to subject performance.

## 3. The experimental platform and the <sup>b</sup>Memorize Patterns<sup>Q</sup> software module

The platform consists of a Visual Basic 5.0 application running under Windows NT on identical Dell Dimension XP5 D200 PCs equipped with 15-in. NEC monitors. These stations were housed within the controlled environment of the School of Management Research Laboratory, C.M. Gatton College of Business and Economics, University of Kentucky. The platform contains a main menu that offers controlled access to two sets of software modules. The first set, called the Experimental Platform Tour, contains two modules that tutor a subject on the mechanics of using the second module set and help him/her become proficient at using the platform for the actual experiment. The second set, called Actual Experiment, also contains two modules but these pertain to participation in the real experiment. As their names signify, a subject must complete Experimental Platform Tour before invoking Actual Experiment.

In the following subsections, we discuss various aspects of the first of the two modules, called <sup>d</sup>Memorize Patterns<sup>T</sup>, in the Actual Experiment module set, with a view to exposing key features of our symbolic language. We begin with a brief overview of an experimental session to facilitate these discussions. (Discussions on the second module, <sup>d</sup>Perform Experiment<sup>T</sup>, are found in Section 4 and a synopsis of <sup>d</sup>The Experimental Platform Tour<sup>T</sup> module set is in Section 5).

## 3.1. Overview of an experimental session

An experimental session is made up of multiple experiments. The session begins with a subject reviewing a stack of <sup>b</sup>cards<sup>Q</sup>, on screen by invoking a Memorize Patterns module. This stack is a means for exposing a subject to a set of <sup>b</sup>patterns<sup>Q</sup>. Each pattern describes how to <sup>b</sup>classify<sup>Q</sup> or slot systemgenerated objects (called <sup>b</sup>samples<sup>Q</sup>) based on known and acquired information about its characteristics.

Thus, a pattern prescribes an optimal information gathering and decision making strategy for a particular sample type. The stack also provides for testing a subject thoroughly on his/her understanding of the patterns.

Once a subject completes the stack review, (s)he moves to a Perform Experiment module and attempts applying the learnt patterns to classify each of a set of randomly generated samples. For each, the subject must determine whether to acquire any further information about the object apart from what is already known and, if so, what pieces and in what sequence. The necessary knowledge is embodied in one of the several patterns the subject has already been exposed to in the Memorize Patterns module.

Ideally, the subject should rely on recalled pattern knowledge to guide him/her in this <sup>b</sup>information search and decision making<sup>Q</sup> endeavor. However, a subject is free to pursue any other strategy, including guessing. Our reward/penalty matrices (discussed in Section 9) are set up such that only the optimal strategy yields a monetary reward and any deviation from it results in a commensurate monetary penalty being assessed. Further, we tie both rewards and penalties to strategy-execution speed with quicker execution of the optimal strategy eliciting greater rewards and quicker execution of a non-optimal one eliciting greater penalties (to encourage subjects to try and succeed.) Thus, the matrices are intended to exercise sustained, implicit time pressure on the subject.

Some of the subjects used a Text-based DSS for the experiment, while others used an Image-based system. Both kinds of systems are identical in their support capabilities and other features, except that one performs key interactions with subjects using written English while the other utilizes the new symbolic language. In what follows, we discuss the creation of categories of objects, the creation of patterns (and associated object classification rules) for different object categories, and the packaging of object features, patterns and other information in Text and Image.

## 3.2. Object categories and patterns for object classification

We created four categories of sample objects by associating sample ID numbers with each object and assigning specific samples to specific categories. Sample numbers 1 through 10 belong to the Zero View category, samples 11 through 20 to the One View category, samples 21 through 30 to the Two View category, and samples 31 through 40 to the Three View category. The significance of sample numbers and object classes is made explicit in the ensuing discussions.

A <sup>b</sup>pattern<sup>Q</sup> tells a subject if (s)he must acquire any information for the sample under consideration, and if so, what information, in what sequence, and, based on such information, how to classify the sample. Each pattern is expressed in rule form. The <sup>b</sup>premise<sup>Q</sup> of a rule describes what condition(s) must be met for the action in the <sup>b</sup>conclusion<sup>Q</sup> to be taken. The condition(s) indicate the appropriate information acquisition strategy for a sample and the action stipulates how to classify it. This stipulation is some function of the condition(s) (i.e., is a decision making strategy based on available information). Each of the four categories of samples mentioned above is associated with a specific classification pattern. Thus, we utilize four classes of patterns called Zero View, One View, Two View and Three View patterns.

## 3.2.1. The Zero View pattern

The simplest pattern has only one condition—the freely available sample number for the object. The subject can acquire this costless information any time after the object is randomly generated (as it is always displayed for each object). The classification formula for a Zero View pattern requires a subject to categorize the object as belonging to the same class as its sample number. This is shown pictorially in Fig. 1.

## 3.2.2. One View, Two View and Three View patterns

As a pattern’s premise becomes more complex, additional characteristics of the sample are required to classify it. These additional characteristics describe different <sup>b</sup>views<sup>Q</sup> of the object. An object has as many as three views. Each view is characterized by the number of <sup>b</sup>pins<sup>Q</sup> it contains. A view may have between 1 and 12 pins, depending on the sample in question.

A One View pattern draws not only on the freely available information (i.e., the sample number) but also on information purchased about a particular, sample number-dependent, view in order to correctly classify the generated sample. Similarly, a Two View pattern requires the conditional purchase of information concerning two particular views. Together, the Zero View, One View and Two View patterns prescribe different partial information acquisition strategies for correct object classification. A Three View pattern, on the other hand, requires the purchase of complete information (i.e., about all three views) for making a correct decision. In each case, as with the Zero View pattern, a different decision making formula prescribes what decision to make based on available information.

## 6. Refining the experimental setting and platform

The research platform described in Sections 3–5 is the result of extensive developmental efforts spanning a period of about 2 years. Steps were taken to ensure that the general experimental environment was convenient, consistent and secure, and to ensure participant privacy, freedom from distractions and the avoidance of any form of interactions between participants. Such efforts apart, we also elicited feedback on our platform’s features from a total of 49 individuals across three stages of pre-tests, over a period of about 5 months. Each pre-tester participated in a session lasting approximately 2 h at a time and day when (s)he felt (s)he could best concentrate on the task at hand. The feedback obtained enabled us to introduce several improvements in the product. We summarize these efforts in the following paragraphs.

The first of the three pre-tests came after we had expended considerable developmental efforts ourselves. As such, we were able to offer a fairly refined and polished product for initial critical review. We had three systems development experts (two MIS PhD candidates who were also faculty members at two other universities and one full-time professor of DSIS at the University of Kentucky) each critique both the Text and Image versions of the platform. All three participants volunteered because of their prior experience with systems analysis and design, in general, and interest in our research, in particular. As such, we did not offer any gratuity for their participation. Each pre-tester evaluated the system on 3 separate days. The first author conducted an ongoing interview with each as s(he) examined and criticized each platform version in turn. The feedback obtained allowed us to make further improvements (see Ref. [3] for details) to the four modules.

Following refinement of the platform based on expert feedback, we had five College of Business and Economics professional/secretarial staff members evaluate it. We asked this group to focus solely on the ease-of-use of the platform user interfaces and to not worry about any other aspects. Our intent was to make the platform as facile as possible for our subjects. Three were randomly assigned to the Text DSS and did the pretest in one session. The remainder worked using the Image DSS during a different session. These volunteers put down their impressions on an open form. As a token of our appreciation, we presented each with attractive mementos from the local Gateway computer store. These efforts allowed us to make a few additional interface improvements (see Ref. The third and final pre-test involved a group of 41 University of Kentucky faculty possessing at least a Ph.D. degree (representing 14 programs with 17 participants coming from outside the College of Business and Economics). ## 7. Measuring deviations from optimal behavior

The optimal information gathering and decision making strategy for a sample is a function of the sample number and any purchased information as depicted in tree form in Figs. 1–4. Given that the system permits a maximum of six information purchases prior to final decision making, the optimal strategy for a particular (say, Two View) problem may be depicted as a seven-element vector, OS=hView C,

View A, Box 12, Null, Null, Null, Nulli, which states Acquire information on View C first, followed by information on View A and choose Box 12. The <sup>b</sup>null<sup>Q</sup> entries indicate that no further informational or decisional clicks are required if executing the optimal strategy. As with an optimal strategy, we can represent any strategy that a given subject deploys using a similar scheme. For instance, the subject’s strategy for the above Two View problem might be, SS=hView B, View A, Box 10, Null, Null, Null, Nulli. Unlike an OS where one of the seven elements must be a decisional click (followed by Nulls, if necessary), an SS might also take on values like hView A, View A, View B, Null, Null, Null, Nulli (i.e., a user may not make a final decision).

Then, we may compute the <sup>b</sup>distance<sup>Q</sup> between an OS and an SS as follows. Consider each of the elements in OS in turn, beginning with the leftmost element, until the first Null element is encountered. Compare each non-Null element in OS with each non-Null element in SS beginning with its leftmost element and proceeding in sequence from left to right. If an element in OS does match an element in SS, record the distance between them as: jlocation index of element in OS–location index of element in SSj. If no match is found, we record the distance as 8 (i.e., maximum number of locations plus one). We terminate the process upon encountering the very first Null element in OS. Sum up the distance measures for each of the non–null elements in OS to obtain an overall <sup>b</sup>extent of deviation<sup>Q</sup> score for SS. The outcome of each match process could produce a distance score of 0 (i.e., <sup>b</sup>yes, matched at correct location<sup>Q</sup>), 1 (<sup>b</sup>yes, but matched at a location 1 locations away<sup>Q</sup>), 2 (<sup>b</sup>yes, but matched 2 locations away<sup>Q</sup>), . . ., 6 (<sup>b</sup>yes, but 6 locations away<sup>Q</sup>) or 8 (<sup>b</sup>no match found<sup>Q</sup>). Thus, in the case where OS=hView C, View A, Box 12, Null, Null, Null, Nulli and SS=hView B, View A, Box 10, Null, Null, Null, Nulli, the distance measures are as follows: 8, for element 1; 0, for element 2; and 8, for element 3. We add the individual element distance scores to obtain an overall deviation score of 16 for the example SS. We use the deviation score for a given SS, along with the time taken for its completion, to assign a monetary reward or penalty to a subject for using that strategy. We describe, next, the manner in which time is factored into our calculations.

## 8. Determining strategy execution time bounds

The data gathered from the 17 Image mode and the 19 Text mode Stage 3 pre-testers who completed all four modules were used, among other things, to help us determine three strategy execution time windows for use in creating suitable reward/penalty matrices for use in our experiments. We deliberately relaxed the settings on our <sup>d</sup>time remaining<sup>T</sup> clocks for each of the four modules to prevent them from timing out during the pre-test. At the same time, pre-testers were urged to work as quickly and accurately as possible through each module. We stressed that the prime purpose of the pre-test was to establish performance standards for our actual subjects. As the pre-testers worked their way through each module, the software would trap and record a variety of user navigational and performance-related data including the time spent in each module.

We used the Stage 3 pre-test Module 3b time data to develop three strategy execution time bounds, T1, T2 and T3, such that T1<sup>b</sup>T2<sup>b</sup>T3. With 19 Text DSS pre-testers and 3 samples of each problem category, we have a total of 57 subject strategy (SS) execution time data values. Likewise, the 17 Image DSS pretesters resulted in a pool of 51 such values. Given our desire to emphasize both speed and accuracy in strategy execution, from each set, we extracted the time values for those SSs with an associated deviation score of zero (i.e., the optimal strategy (OS) was executed perfectly for these samples).<sup>2</sup> We sorted the resultant observations in each category in ascending sequence. For each sorted set, we extracted three execution time values corresponding to the observation at 33.33% from the beginning (i.e., smallest value), at 66.67% from the beginning and at 100% from the beginning (i.e., the largest value) of the set, resorting to linear interpolation where necessary. We then averaged the eight observations (i.e., four for Text and four for Image) at each cutoff point to obtain a single average execution time value for that point, regardless of DSS or problem category. The resultant values were used as T1, T2 and T3. Thus, T1 indicates that one-third of all pre-testers (regardless of mode and complexity level) completed a problem successfully in T1 seconds or less, on average. T2 and T3 may be similarly interpreted. ## 11. Experimental design and findings

Our final subject pool contained the following composition of doctoral students: Accounting (1), Agriculture Economics (9), Marketing (2), Management (2), Economics (7), Finance (8), DSIS (10), Electronic Engineering (2), Spanish (2), Art (1), Statistic (1), Political Science (5), Ed. Psychology (1), Math (1), Biology (1) and History (1). Subjects were randomly assigned to the treatment sequences to ensure an equal number of participants (i.e., 27) in each mode. Before beginning the session, each subject was asked to read and understand a handout entitled, <sup>b</sup>Assessing Your Monetary Rewards and Penalties<sup>Q</sup> (Appendix A). The system generates three sample classification problems to help a subject <sup>b</sup>warm up<sup>Q</sup> for the actual test problems. Thereafter, the system generates 12 test problems, 3 from each of the 4 complexity levels (Level 0, Level 1, Level 2 and Level 3). All problems are generated randomly, so that instances from the four levels are presented in no particular sequence.

For each subject, we gathered a variety of performance data. Of particular interest to this paper are three such measures: strategy execution time, strategy deviation score and reward earned. These correspond to the time taken for subject strategy execution, the deviation score for the strategy executed (a measure of strategy accuracy), and based on the applicable reward/penalty matrix and the preceding two measurements, the appropriate reward or penalty value for the executed strategy. Because each of these actuals is a bounded quantity, and because the upper bound for the deviation score is different for different complexity levels, we converted each into a proportional value in the interval [0, 1]. Consider a Level 0 problem as an example. Here, actual strategy execution time is contained in [0, 50], strategy deviation score is contained in [0, 8] and reward earned lies in [2.158, 2.158]. Thus, a {strategy execution time, strategy deviation score, reward earned} value of {5, 0, 2.158} would yield the proportional equivalent {0.10, 0.00, 1.00} and a value of {15, 8, 2.158} would yield {0.30, 1.00, 0.00}.

Given 3 modes, 4 complexity levels, 27 subjects per mode and 3 observations per subject per complexity level, we had a total of $2 7 ^ { * } 3 { = } 8 1$ three-variate observations for each mode-complexity level combination. The experimental setup allows us to analyze the data for interaction effects and main effects by modeling the experiment as a two factor, completely randomized, fixed effects MANOVA model. Factor A is the mode with two levels: Image and Text; Factor B is the complexity level with four levels: Level 0 through Level 3; and each mode-complexity level combination is a cell. For multivariate data, the MANOVA is preferable to a series of repeated t-tests or univariate ANOVA tests (e.g., Ref. [11]).

Apart from requiring intra- and inter-cell data independence (which requirement our data set satisfies), the two-factor MANOVA model also requires that observations in each cell be multivariate normal with equal covariance matrices (i.e., requires multivariate homoscedasticity). In practice, researchers generally assume that the data are multivariate normal if the number of cell observations is large and multivariate homoscedasticity if the observations is each cell are approximately equal. Given n=81 observations per cell, we would be in-line with customary practice in both regards. Yet, we opted to also analyze our data using tests based on a powerful non-parametric version of the two-factor MANOVA. The procedure does not rest on such assumptions and is also applicable in cases involving discontinuous distributions, unequal number of cell observations, and for both small and large sample sizes. If both procedures lead to the same conclusions, our test findings may be regarded as being robust with regard to departures from the conventional parametric assumptions.

## 12. Discussion

Our results suggest that the Image mode subjects are likely to perform as well as or better than Text mode subjects in earned reward terms. This is encouraging given the emphasis on the performancebased financial incentive mechanism as the primary driver underlying the experiment. Our findings also tell us that, in our experiments, Image subjects tended to execute quicker, less accurate strategies with the reward gains from speedy strategy execution offsetting the losses resulting from any inaccuracy. While quicker strategy execution is indeed desirable as are higher rewards, we would have preferred that strategy accuracy not be sacrificed in the process. In hindsight, the following aspects of our experiment present themselves as possible explanations for the observed behavior (and as possible areas for improvement at subsequent attempts at similar experiments).

First, given its novelty, we would have liked to have spent more time and effort in training our subjects in the use of the Image mode to increase fluency. Ideally, training should have encompassed more than one session with intervals between the sessions to help subjects absorb and digest the new language. Unfortunately, practical considerations (both time-related and experimental integrity-related) prevented us from doing this and we had to restrict ourselves to offering a single 3.5-h training session (i.e., for completing Modules 1, 2 and 3a). Our subjects completed the training within 1.70 h, on average. Second, during training, participants were not subject to explicit or implicit time pressure of any kind. This, again, was a conscious choice based on available time and the intent of the training session— to foster platform, experiment and language fluency in our subjects. Given that experimental integrity considerations required us to ask subjects to directly proceed to the actual experiment and its timepressured environment, it is possible that the language’s novelty, lack of sufficient exposure and subject fatigue may all have contributed to negatively impacting performance with Image with regard to strategy accuracy.

Third, we would have preferred showing each of the decision trees shown in Figs. 1–4, on a single screen to help subjects better visualize search paths and outcomes. We were unable to create legible renderings of each tree in each mode given available infrastructure and, instead, broke each tree into multiple fragments spread across many screens (i.e., the multiple pattern cards in the card stack). While we did offer pencil and paper for our subjects during training to allow them to reconstruct each tree from its fragmented form (and we observed that the stronger performers did do so), we did not explicitly ask subjects to map the information on the cards to decision trees (we allowed subjects to choose the manner in which they assimilated the card stack). Regardless, we would have preferred to not burden them with this added step during training. Thus, perhaps, a different approach to training could produce better strategy execution accuracy with Image than currently observed.

Fourth, it is possible that the Image mode, as defined, was inherently too complex for our subjects. However, we have no evidence in support of this possibility both from our pre-testers and our actual subjects. While one may conjecture that perhaps the actual subjects were coerced into silence by their fear of exclusion from the experiment, this explanation does not apply to our large pool of unpaid pre-testers. Fifth, it is possible that our monetary incentives were insufficient to motivate our subjects. Recall that our reward mechanism allowed our subjects to earn as much as three times the prevailing minimum wage rate (US\$5.15/h) based on performance, including a guaranteed show-up fee of US\$5.00. Our subjects, most of whom were teaching and research assistants at the University of Kentucky, were earning between 1.5 and 2.9 times the minimum rate (depending on their status in the program) from the university and some likely had other sources of income. While Vernon Smith’s [21] principle of non-satiation states that rational individuals prefer more money to less, the real world imposes real limits on how much an experimenter has available for subject payment. Sixth, it is likely that the lack of context sensitivity played a role.

Perhaps for the kind of language that we investigated, sensitivity is a pre-requisite for successful learning and use. If this is true, it would be inappropriate to use Smith’s Induced Value Theory as a basis for experimentation on man–machine interaction languages. We are not convinced that this is the case, though, and are inclined toward viewing training approach and training time as the likely explanations for the outcomes observed. Finally, what we currently have is relatively slower but more accurate strategy execution with Text and relatively speedier but less accurate execution with Image. It may be possible to restructure the payoff matrices to try and encourage the more desirable behavior of speedy, accurate execution of strategies. How to suitably structure the matrices to try and get close to this ideal is an issue for subsequent research.

## 13. Concluding remarks

This paper summarizes our efforts at designing and executing an experiment aimed at contrasting the relative utilities of two kinds of human–machine interaction languages in a time-pressured information search and decision-making setting. We began this paper by providing a motivation for our work and a review of relevant literature. We then described key features of our experimental platform in terms of its two main software modules—the Experimental Platform Tour and the Actual Experiment—and their respective sub-modules. We then summarized how we improved, fine-tuned and established platform parameters for the actual experiment, using a threestage pre-testing process. The resultant, highly refined end product was used in a carefully designed and executed experiment intended to contrast the virtues and limitations of the two communication modes—Text and Image—vis-a\`-vis one another, in terms of three performance measures: strategy deviation score (i.e., an indicator of strategy accuracy), strategy execution time and reward earned (which is a function of the preceding two measures). Our findings suggest that subjects using the Image mode are likely to equal or even better Text mode subjects in terms of rewards earned. However, they would likely achieve this result by executing speedier (hence, more lucrative) but less accurate (hence, less lucrative) strategies than Text mode subjects. We concluded our analyses with a discussion of possible explanations for the outcomes observed, which, in turn, are pointers to what may be addressed in future research to obtain the more desirable result of faster, more accurate, more lucrative strategy execution using Image. We are currently exploring some of these avenues in ongoing research.

14. Coupled with the US\$5.00 show-up fee, your maximum possible earnings would be US\$30.90 (thrice the minimum wages for 2 h), US\$20.60 (twice the minimum wages for 2 h) and US\$10.30 (the minimum wages for 2 h).

(A) The Window 1 maximum penalty is the same as the Window 1 (maximum) reward.
