# ADVERSE SELECTION IN B2B SECONDARY MARKET ONLINE AUCTIONS FOR IT EQUIPMENT: AN EMPIRICAL ANALYSIS <sup>1</sup>

Abdullah Alhauli College of Business Administration, Kuwait University Safat, KUWAIT {Abdullah.Alhauli@ku.edu.kw}

Wedad J. Elmaghraby Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland College Park MD, U.S.A. {welmaghr@umd.edu}

Anandasivam Gopal Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University SINGAPORE {anandasivam.gopal@ntu.edu.sg}

Online business-to-business auctions for used IT products have emerged as a viable market for finding a second life for these products, rather than having them end up in landfills as e-waste. As part of the growing “secondary market” landscape, these online B2B auctions are significantly affected by adverse selection since uncertainty about product quality from their first life remains in place. We study how these adverse selection costs may be identified and reduced in online B2B auctions for mobile phones using a proprietary data set for pallets of iPhone devices. We focus on the differences between carrier-locked and unlocked iPhones, and the degree to which the jailbreaking of devices may lead to adverse selection costs. We first show that uncertainty with respect to the possibility of jailbreaking-to-unlock induces significant adverse selection costs in this market. We identify a clear method that these adverse selection costs may be reduced through policies implemented in the primary market. We find that adverse selection costs exist with respect to jailbreaking-to-unlock, by comparing prices obtained for locked and unlocked devices, as well as pallets where this information is not disclosed. However, when some of the uncertainty surrounding jailbreaking-to-unlock is removed by virtue of an exogenous policy change implemented by Verizon in the primary market, i.e., to sell all iPhones as factory unlocked, adverse selection costs are significantly reduced. Our work has significant implications for enhancing the efficiency of secondary markets for IT products, by virtue of highlighting the connections between primary and secondary markets. Managerial and theoretical implications that emerge from this work are discussed in the paper.

Keywords: Secondary markets, adverse selection, primary markets, Verizon, jailbreaking, online B2B auctions, mobile phones, iPhones, econometrics

## Introduction

Consumer spending on new IT products in the United States in 2019 was estimated at \$1.69 trillion, according to IDC, representing an increase of 5.3% over the previous year.<sup>2</sup> A large part of this spending was devoted to mobile phones, computers, and communication equipment that are largely durable and can provide value for many years. However, as these devices are replaced by newer models, these used technologies enter business-to-business (B2B) and business-toconsumer (B2C) secondary or liquidation markets. These markets process the resale of products that have passed through the primary market in some form (Elmaghraby et al., 2018; Lee & Whang, 2002), either as customer returns, unsold inventory, or from buyback programs (Tibben-Lembke, 2004). The efficient functioning of these secondary markets is desirable since these “older” products are functional and continue to provide value (Pilehvar et al., 2017). Moreover, their second life prevents them from ending up in landfills where they can cause environmental damage (Tibben-Lembke, 2004).

A recurring issue faced by secondary markets for IT products, such as computers and mobile devices (Pilehvar et al., 2017) pertains to uncertainty about product quality (Ghose, 2009). Specifically, secondary markets are prone to adverse selection because of uncertainty about the quality of products sold therein, as shown by a significant body of evidence in the context of used durable goods (Akerlof, 1970, Garicano & Kaplan, 2001; Overby & Mitra, 2014). Uncertainty about quality manifests as adverse selection costs: reduced prices relative to conditions where full information is provided to buyers (Dewan & Hsu, 2004). Adverse selection can also lead to market failure, which, in the case of IT equipment, leads to greater e-waste and environmental degradation (Tibben-Lembke, 2004) as well as lost opportunities for the sale of equipment in other settings where demand exists (Neto et al., 2016).

Potential solutions to adverse selection in secondary markets have largely focused on providing information at the point of sale—these include quality attestations, warranties, third-party certifications, signals, and relational measures that build trust (Overby & Mitra, 2014; Ozpolat et al., 2013; Klein et al., 2016; Pavlou et al., 2007). However, this focus on the point of sale misses the influence of factors from the primary market that can help reduce uncertainty in the secondary market. Arguably, information from the primary market can have a positive effect on prices in the secondary market as well. Prior literature on the interconnectedness between the primary and secondary markets (Arunkundram & Sundararajan, 1998; Chen et al., 2013) have discussed how the secondary market negatively affects demand in the primary market. However, the reverse is also possible— decisions in the primary market taken by critical players, such as the makers of original equipment or firms that are important value-added resellers, can positively affect market efficiency and prices in secondary markets. Our empirical research question therefore is: To what extent do product design and feature decisions by key players in the primary market (such as original equipment makers and telecom service providers) reduce adverse selection in the secondary market for the same set of IT products? In this paper, we provide empirical validation of this effect within B2B secondary markets for iPhones.

In many typical B2B secondary markets for IT products, pallets of equipment are sold to a set of pre-registered business buyers through an online auction platform. The auction platform acts as an intermediary, connecting big-box retailers that are interested in moving older or used products from their shelves/warehouses, to buyers who bid for these pallets. The platform does not handle any of the pallets since they are shipped directly from the retailer to the eventual buyer. Bidders are themselves resellers, and include flea market operators, eBay power sellers, and independent stores that vary in bidding activity and purchase volumes (Pilehvar et al., 2017). Since this equipment is sold “as-is” by retailers, it rarely comes with clearly specified quality assurance in terms of functionality and appearance (Elmaghraby et al., 2018), leaving residual uncertainty about the devices in the pallets.

In our empirical analysis, we focus on how policies implemented in the primary market impact the residual uncertainty about one specific aspect of the products being sold, thereby reducing adverse selection costs. We focus on Apple iPhone devices; in the U.S. alone, secondary market auctions for mobile phones were roughly \$25 billion in 2017. In the secondary markets, the quality of mobile phones remains uncertain on many dimensions—software, physical condition, presence of warranties, and feature sets. In our work here, we focus on one source of uncertainty—whether the device runs the risk of being jailbroken. Jailbreaking is an (unauthorized) process by which the user is able to gain root access to the phone. Users jailbreak iPhones for two main reasons (Cheng, 2010)—to gain greater control over the device and bypass the “walled garden” model used by Apple (Wolk, 2009), and to untether the device from a specific carrier (“unlocking” the device). Unlocked phones can thus operate on any carrier that offers similar technology (such as GSM). Unlocked phones are more valuable, as they are not “locked-in” to a single carrier, and command higher prices in the primary market.

In the U.S. market, both locked and unlocked iPhones are sold in the B2B secondary markets in pallets. However, unlocked iPhones can manifest in these markets in multiple ways. First, they may have been sold as “factory unlocked” in the primary market. Alternatively, they may have been unlocked by a specific carrier (such as Verizon or AT&T). Finally, they may have been unlocked as a result of jailbreaking. Jailbreaking is not uncommon—5% of all iPhones in 2010 were found to be jailbroken (22.8M devices), rising to 11.2% in 2013 in the U.S. alone (Fitzgerald, 2010; CoderProof, 2013). While factory unlocking and carrier-facilitated unlocking do not affect the intrinsic quality of the phone in any way, jailbreaking-to-unlock results in the loss of manufacturer warranties and maintenance support from the device maker and renders the jailbroken devices more susceptible to malicious software, resulting in potential future software issues (Miller, 2011).<sup>3</sup> Thus, while all iPhones may be jailbroken for reasons of control, the risk of obtaining a jailbroken phone is amplified for unlocked devices that cannot be verified as factory unlocked or carrier-facilitated unlocked. When a potential buyer in the secondary market encounters a pallet of unlocked iPhones, it is impossible to verify whether the pallet is free of jailbroken ones. This uncertainty introduces adverse selection costs, i.e., buyers will reduce their bids for such pallets (Dewan & Hsu, 2004). Detailed information about the phones and their provenance may help but is unfortunately rarely provided in these markets (Pilehvar et al., 2017). As a result, the usual mechanisms by which adverse selection may be resolved, such as warranties, signals, and certifications, do not apply here.

Interestingly, some of the adverse selection costs associated with unlocked phones would be reduced if it were established that all such devices were safely unlocked, without the need for jailbreaking. As part of a deal with the Federal Communications Commission (FCC), Verizon announced in 2012 that all 4G LTE devices (starting with iPhone 5 models) would be “factory unlocked,” i.e., they would be operable on other networks as well. Other carriers did not adopt this policy. This policy implemented in the primary market inadvertently resulted in eliminating the need to jailbreak these phones for the purpose of unlocking them, thereby alleviating some residual uncertainty in the secondary market. We empirically examine how this exogenous policy change affected prices for Verizon phones, relative to those offered by other carriers, as well as the magnitude of adverse selection costs. Our empirical analysis addresses two questions: first, is there evidence of adverse selection costs associated with jailbreaking-to-unlock, reflected in the prices of unlocked iPhones? Second, does the policy change reduce these adverse selection costs for unlocked Verizon phones, as would be expected from a reduction in uncertainty?

Our analysis finds that, unlike the traditional literature on adverse selection, which emphasizes signals, certification, return policies, and seller reputation to tackle adverse selection (Ozpolat et al., 2013; Ghose, 2009; Overby & Mitra, 2014; Klein et al., 2016), appropriate policies from the primary market can have a salubrious effect in the secondary market. We conduct our analyses using a proprietary dataset on B2B auctions obtained from a leading online intermediary B2B secondary market platform. Our sample contains auction data of iPhone pallets spanning the period from January 2014 to July 2017. We restrict the sample to auctions of two different iPhone generations that span the policy change—the iPhone 4 and iPhone 5 models, leading to a sample of 8,179 unique auctions.

A model-free comparison of prices shows the presence of adverse selection costs—unlocked phones are priced lower than locked phones (\$46.90 versus \$63.70 for iPhone 4 models; \$88.30 versus \$94.50 for iPhone 5 models) when the phones cannot be guaranteed to be factory unlocked or carrierfacilitated unlocked. This price ordering stands in stark contrast to the ordering found in the primary market, where unlocked phones command a price premium over locked phones. However, this was not the case with Verizon iPhone 5 models in the secondary market, which were guaranteed to be carrier unlocked. In fact, these devices commanded a significant premium, with an average price of \$137.40. Based on this preliminary evidence, we investigated auction-level prices across all of the iPhone auctions hosted by sellers on the B2B auction platform. We first tested for the presence of jailbreaking-to-unlock adverse selection costs, by comparing pallets where the locked/unlocked status of the phones was not disclosed to pallets where this information was provided. Subsequently, we considered prices for Verizon iPhone 5 pallets, where phones did not need to be jailbroken for the purpose of unlocking, to see whether prices were higher, relative to similar models offered by other carriers where the uncertainty from jailbreaking-to-unlock remained.

Our regression results, reported later, provide clear support for the presence of adverse selection costs (Dewan & Hsu, 2004)—locked phones generated higher prices when the risk of jailbreaking-to-unlock remained in unlocked phones. These risks remained in place when the pallet was marked as unlocked and when no information was provided about the status of the phones in the pallet. However, when the uncertainty pertaining to jailbreaking-to-unlock was removed through Verizon’s policy change, prices for unlocked phones in the secondary market rose significantly, matching the price structures observed for unlocked phones in the primary market (Felin & Zenger, 2014).

Our work here makes two primary contributions to the literature. First, we extend prior work studying adverse selection in secondary markets, especially for IT products, by showing that the ill effects of information asymmetry may be partially handled by policies from the primary market. In our case, the policy may not have been specifically implemented for the benefit of secondary markets but capitalizes on the extent to which primary and secondary markets are interlinked (Lee & Whang, 2002; Ghose, 2009). Second, we contribute to the literature on the secondary markets of durable goods that create environmental problems. Well-run secondary markets, as well as programs to refurbish and remanufacture products, remain critical to reduce e-waste (Tibben-Lembke, 2004; Neto et al., 2016). Policies from the primary market, based on the complete lifecycle of IT products, can help ensure robust secondary markets while also limiting e-waste (Kumar & Putnam, 2008).

From the perspective of implementing policy that can help create sustainable reverse logistics programs, we show the value of forward-looking policies that can benefit both primary and secondary markets. For carriers like Verizon and AT&T, our work primes them to consider the downstream implications of their strategic decisions and the benefits thereof. For online secondary market auctioneers like our research partner, our work shows how they can benefit from providing complete information, thereby ensuring better market outcomes (Pilehvar et al., 2017). In the next section, we provide details about two important aspects of our work— the B2B secondary market context and jailbreaking, before delving into the empirical analysis.

## Secondary Markets for IT Products

The rapid rate of new product development of IT products results in contexts where multiple generations of the same technologies are available to users concurrently in the market (Carrillo, 2005). For instance, it is easy to observe sales of new iPhone devices as well as a robust secondary marketplace for multiple generations of used but functional previousgeneration iPhones. A particularly visible form is the B2B online auction (Pilehvar et al., 2017), wherein pallets of equipment are auctioned to a set of pre-registered buyers. Since many pallets in secondary markets are idiosyncratic and nonstandard, auctions are a preferred market mechanism to facilitate price discovery (Pinker et al., 2003). While B2B secondary markets possess the benefit of moving large quantities of goods at a relatively fast pace, they are often affected by adverse selection, where quality of the product is not discernible (Neto et al., 2016). Since these products are in their second life and sold “as-is,” they rarely come with clearly specified quality information with regard to functionality and appearance. Some of this uncertainty could be resolved by disclosing more product information (Lu et al., 2017). However, retailers looking to dispose of their products quickly tend to not invest time in generating this detailed information (Pilehvar et al., 2017). Thus, these markets are characterized by significant adverse selection, due to the inability to clearly indicate product quality (Akerlof, 1970).

Several mechanisms can be used to reduce the effects of adverse selection. Third-party quality attestation or certification is one mechanism that can help verify quality prior to purchase (Ozpolat et al., 2013). Alternatively, quality signals can be issued by the seller to the market (Ghose, 2009; Overby & Mitra, 2014). Sellers can also offer warranties, trial periods, generous return policies, and post-sales service contracts (Overby & Mitra, 2014; Peterson & Schneider, 2014). Finally, intangible assets like reputation and trust can help reduce the effects of adverse selection (Dimoka et al.,

2012; Pavlou et al., 2007; Kirmani & Rao, 2000). All of these function at the point of sale. Paradoxically, these mechanisms are less likely to be used in B2B secondary market auctions that we study due to the minimal investment made in the flow of goods from the primary to the secondary market. It is here that policies from primary markets may help reduce the costs of uncertain product quality and thereby enhance market efficiency. As an empirical exemplar, we use the context of iPhones and the role of jailbreaking, described briefly next.

## Jailbreaking and Adverse Selection Costs in Mobile Phones

The market for used iPhones is large and active, with multiple generations of phones sold on secondary markets at the same time (Elmaghraby et al., 2018). Since phones that go into B2B pallets can come from several sources (buy-backs, returned products, unsold inventory), there is significant variability in their quality. Retailers provide some baseline information about the state and generation of the phones in the pallets; Retailers “self-report” the quality of the phone along generic quality classifications (e.g., A, A/B, B, B/C, C, D, or salvage). These details are provided in Figure A1 in the Appendix. These classifications are meant to briefly describe the cosmetic issues and functionality of the devices and are broad in scope to minimize the burden on sellers as they create similar pallets, without providing details about specific devices. Among the specific details omitted is how unlocked phones within the pallet obtained their status, and hence, whether or not the pallet contains phones that are jailbroken-to-unlock.

Mobile devices in the US that are locked to a single carrier can only be used with that carrier, such as Verizon or AT&T, and represent a closed platform ecosystem at the carrier level (Boudreau, 2010) and hence inherently increase switching costs (Cho et al., 2014). Alternatively, devices can be unlocked and usable on multiple carriers, depending on the user’s preference. Unlocked phones allow users to switch carriers with ease (Zhu & Zhou, 2012) and, as a result, are more expensive in the primary market.<sup>4</sup> In an ideal secondary market, the price premium on unlocked phones should remain in place, since these allow the buyer greater choice in terms of carriers. However, all unlocked phones are not the same. As mentioned earlier, phones can be safely factory unlocked or carrierfacilitated unlocked. Phones that are unlocked in this manner retain all relevant warranties and provide a baseline level of performance that is associated with a smartphone that has not been tampered with.

However, this is not the case when a phone is unlocked through jailbreaking. Jailbreaking is a process by which restrictions placed on the phone by the device maker, in terms of “root” access, installation of noncompliant apps, security and privacy restrictions, and carrier access, are removed. Jailbroken phones can thus be freed from carrier restrictions imposed by the manufacturer (Apple, for instance) and the carrier (Verizon, for instance) (Miller, 2011). Regardless of why a device is jailbroken, it results in the loss of manufacturer warranties and maintenance support from the device maker, thereby lowering its value. Jailbroken devices are also more susceptible to malicious software resulting in potential future software issues

When a buyer encounters an unlocked iPhone pallet, it is not clear how the devices were unlocked. Not all unlocked iPhones are jailbroken, and not all jailbroken phones are unlocked, since users can jailbreak their phones in order to exert greater control over the device per se (Cheng, 2010). However, unlocked phones carry a heightened risk of having been tampered with due to the possibility of jailbreaking-tounlock, thereby creating an adverse selection problem. This problem manifests in two ways. First, if the status of the phones in a pallet is not disclosed, the buyer is uncertain regarding the degree of jailbreaking risk, leading to reduced valuations for the pallet (Akerlof, 1970; Klein et al., 2016; Ghose, 2009). Second, if the pallet is clearly labeled as containing unlocked phones, the buyer cannot be sure whether the phones are jailbroken, factory unlocked, or carrier-facilitated unlocked. This factor exists independent of other damage that may exist in the pallet of phones. Therefore, the final prices are likely to be lower for unlocked pallets in the presence of adverse selection costs, everything else being equal. While the risk of jailbroken devices exists even in locked pallets, the uncertainty attributable to jailbreaking-to-unlock is not present.

Thus, the adverse selection costs that result are likely to be higher in auctions where no information is provided and for unlocked phones. However, the incremental uncertainty associated with unlocked devices would be selectively removed if all unlocked devices were determined to be factory unlocked by default, thereby reducing adverse selection costs. The exogenous policy change implemented by Verizon in the primary market provided exactly this information to the secondary market, starting with iPhone 5 models which would be factory unlocked. Other carriers, in contrast, continued to offer a mix of locked and unlocked phones in the primary market. Thus, the policy change allows us to gauge first, adverse selection costs from the potential presence of jailbroken-to-unlock devices, and second, the reduction in these costs due to the new policy. We describe the context and research site in some detail next.

## Research Context: B2B Secondary Market Online Auctions

Our research site is a leading online intermediary B2B platform that specializes in running online auctions. The platform serves as a shopfront for big-box retailers who create their own virtual “shopfronts” on the platform; to maintain consistency, all retailer “shopfronts” are generated using a consistent user interface. The platform only hosts the auction process, while pallets are shipped directly from the retailer to the buyer. Neither the platform nor the bidder determines what goes into a pallet, and the platform does not bear responsibility for the condition of the contents (Pilehvar et al, 2017). Each pallet contains products that are typically of the same category (e.g., mobile phones, laptops) but not necessarily identical. More importantly, neither the platform nor the seller invests any effort in restoring any of the technology items on sale to factory conditions. Bidders are not able to physically inspect the pallets in advance and have to rely on information disclosed on the auction site and data gleaned from the primary market.

In the specific case of mobile phones, pallets are typically formed of devices that are of the same operating system, brand, and generation (e.g., Android, Samsung, Galaxy) with occasional exceptions. Since we focus on iPhones, pallets here are typically formed of a number of iPhones of the same generation (iPhone 4 or iPhone 5), but with varying features. However, all phones in a pallet typically are either unlocked or locked to a particular carrier, in order to facilitate easy processing of the merchandise for the retailer as well as the bidder.<sup>5</sup> This sorting is carried out at the retailer’s site, as part of the pallet preparation process agreed upon between the platform and retailer firms.

The platform supports a fairly standardized template for all auctions hosted on its site. Individual sellers vary in the degree of information they populate (Table 1 illustrates examples of auctions and a snapshot of the associated information content provided on the auction page).

## Data and Methodology

We used a proprietary dataset obtained from our research partner site and focused our study on auctions for iPhone 4 and iPhone 5s. We focused on pallets of pre-used iPhones that included devices of a single generation so as to minimize extraneous variation in the pallets. All iPhones in such pallets were either locked to a specific carrier or unlocked, with no mixed pallets. However, there is variation across pallets in terms of the physical condition of the phones and other attributes such as memory size and carrier. Our sample had 8,179 unique auctions from January 2014 to July 2017, items were sold by 9 major retailers and the auctions included 2,929 unique bidders. For each auction, we collected information pertaining to the retailer, the number of units in each pallet, the starting and final prices of the auction, the number of bidders, and the date and time of the auction. Additional information on the pallet is available on the auction listing, such as the physical condition of the devices, their models and storage capacities, and the network carrier. Our sample included phones from four carriers: Verizon, AT&T, T-Mobile, and Sprint, with the largest proportion from Verizon (39.61%) with the other three carriers making up the remainder. All four carriers were also associated with locked and unlocked device pallets in the sample. Given our focus on unlocked devices, we describe this variable in more detail below.

The sample included auctions where the locked/unlocked status of devices in the pallet was disclosed by the retailer, as well as auctions where this information was simply not disclosed. When the status was disclosed, all phones in a pallet were either unlocked or locked with respect to a particular carrier, as mentioned earlier. In the majority of the cases (6,907 auctions), no information on this status was provided while the remaining (1,272) auctions provided this information. The distribution of pallets across these states (no information, locked, unlocked) is depicted in Figure 1 for clarity. The omission of this information in the auction listing may be due to retailers not having this information easily available or may reflect a conscious choice by retailers to withhold this information. However, this propensity to not disclose is observable across all carriers as well as both generations of iPhones across time periods in our sample. Further, all four carriers were associated with locked and unlocked pallets in the sample. While we cannot clearly state why some retailers chose not to provide information in this context, this does not affect our results because of the information provided by the Verizon policy change, as discussed below.

The unit of analysis in our baseline analysis is the auction, while the dependent variable of interest is auction final price, normalized by the number of devices in the pallet, i.e., final price per unit, consistent with prior research (Pilehvar et al., 2017; Elmaghraby et al., 2018). We study whether auction final prices are affected by whether devices in the pallet are locked, unlocked, or undisclosed. The average final price per device in our sample was \$116.01 (sd = \$68.80), with the distribution of prices in the sample positively skewed. Therefore, we took the natural logarithm of the final price to symmetrize the residuals. An estimate of how much adverse selection influences prices in this market could be estimated by simply comparing final prices across the three types of pallets (locked, unlocked, undisclosed). However, such a comparison cannot fully account for unobservable factors that may drive the presence of locked or unlocked (or undisclosed) pallets in the market. Therefore, a better identification strategy requires an exogenous shock that removes the influence of any strategic decisions by retailers to offer pallets of a specific type for auction. As discussed earlier, this exogenous shock was provided by Verizon’s policy change. Thus, in addition to representing our central argument for the beneficial effects of primary market policies on secondary market efficiency, the policy change also provides us with a clean identification strategy (Cook et al., 2002). Since the policy change occurred with Verizon iPhone 5 phones, we restricted our dataset to two specific iPhone generations (iPhone 4 and iPhone 5) that spanned the exogenous policy change. In the case of iPhone 4, all four carriers carried and sold both locked and unlocked phones. We constructed our analyses in stages, as described below.

## Empirical Analysis

## The Presence of Adverse Selection Costs

Before establishing the positive effects of Verizon’s policy change, we first tested for the presence of adverse selection costs in our sample. We did this in two ways—first, we compared pallets where information was disclosed to those where no information was provided. Second, we compared across (disclosed) locked and unlocked pallets. Our intuition is as follows: when no information on the status of the pallet is provided, the bidder faces greater uncertainty surrounding the phones and their status (locked or unlocked), since some proportion of the phones could easily be jailbroken. If the pallet is unlocked, here again, this source of uncertainty concerning jailbreaking-to-unlock the phone remains. It is only in locked pallets that no residual concerns about jailbreaking-to-unlock exist, all else being equal. Thus, we would expect pallets where no information is provided to generate lower prices, all else being equal, compared to those pallets where some information is disclosed (Goeree and Offerman, 2002). Similarly, when we compare locked to unlocked pallets where jailbreaking-to-unlock can exist, we would expect lower prices for unlocked pallets. We thus proceed with our analysis along these two lines of reasoning.

## The Impact of Verizon’s Policy Change on Adverse Selection Costs

In studying the effects of the policy change, we return to Equation (1), where undisclosed pallets are compared to those where information is provided. As per the new policy, all Verizon iPhone 5 devices are factory unlocked, thereby eliminating an important reason to jailbreak them. Therefore, post-policy change, for B2B buyers considering Verizon iPhone 5 pallets, the lack of disclosure should have no effect on bidders’ willingness-to-pay, thereby reducing the discounting of prices associated with unlocked phones. As a result, the final prices for Verizon iPhone 5 pallets should be higher than the final prices for unlocked iPhone 5 pallets offered by other carriers, which should continue to see some price discounting since jailbreaking-to-unlock continues to be a risk. The policy change allows the identification of information disclosure on final prices by exogenously reducing information asymmetry in the market for Verizon iPhone 5 devices.

To test this, we introduced an interaction term between Model<sub>ij</sub>, Verizon<sub>ij</sub>, and Undisclosed<sub>i</sub> to the specification in Equation (1). This provides a way to establish how prices for Verizon iPhone 5 pallets may deviate from the norm, consistent with difference-in-differences models (Bertrand et al., 2004). Since the regression includes a three-way interaction, we included all two-way interactions in the model, with the results shown in Columns 2 and 3 of Table 3. In Column 2, the two-way interactions show a consistent effect associated with lack of disclosure; the net effect of lack of disclosure on final prices is negative. Since our main interest is in the three-way interaction in Column 3, we caution against interpreting the results of the model in Column 2.

To understand the effect of the new policy on Verizon iPhone 5 pallets, we look at the three-way interaction in Column 3. This three-way interaction is part of the “cube view” of eight possible cells that exist when interpreting three-way interactions of categorical or dummy variables (Aral et al., 2012); here, we are interested in the coordinates (1,1,1), which map to the set {Verizon, iPhone5, Undisclosed}. Paradoxically, this three-way interaction is negative (-0.458, $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , showing that prices are lower when no information is provided. To calculate the true marginal effect of the Undisclosed variable here, we contrast predicted prices across different sets of relevant iPhone pallets. Using the coefficients from Column 3, we see the predicted price for a Verizon iPhone 5 device, where information is disclosed, is \$163, while a comparable Verizon iPhone 5 device where no information is provided is \$134. This \$29 difference in final prices is counterintuitive, since disclosing the status of the phones has no diagnostic value. These results are also shown in diagrammatic form in Figure 2.

A possible explanation for this counterintuitive finding could be that bidders were not fully aware that Verizon iPhone 5 devices were factory unlocked. In this case, this effect would be expected to fade over time through information diffusion or observed behavior on the auction platform. We therefore examined final prices over time to examine this possibility. We split the sample into Verizon (2,818 auctions) and non-Verizon (5,361 auctions) subsamples. Further, to see if bidding behavior changes over time, we divided these subsamples into two periods of two years each. Within each subsample, we interacted the Undisclosed variable with Model 5 to examine price trends for iPhone 5 models when no information is provided. The results are shown in Table 5, with the first two columns for Verizon and the remaining for non-Verizon carriers. In the interest of space, we show the fully interacted model for the four subsamples.

The results in Column 1 for Verizon in 2014-2015 show the counterintuitive negative interaction term (-0.272, $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { p } } <$ 0.10). The effect of the disclosure of the status of the unlocked iPhone 5 device from Verizon caused the predicted price to significantly increase from \$152 to \$181 (a difference of \$29, statistically significant). However, in the second time span of 2016-2017, this negative coefficient loses significance entirely, showing that bidders bid similarly for iPhone 5 models whether or not their status was disclosed. The effect size in this time period is roughly \$8 (\$127 to \$135) and not significant. In contrast, the results for the non-Verizon carriers retain negative interactions in significance and magnitude (Columns 3 and 4). The difference in predicted prices per unit in 2014-2015 is \$23 and remains roughly equivalent in 2016-2017 (\$20). These results show that bidders learned about the Verizon iPhone 5 policy change, and its implications, over time and responded as expected.

## Placebo Tests for Falsification

The analysis reported above relies on the exogeneity of Verizon’s policy change, and changes in the associated risks of jail-breaking-to-unlock. Therefore, we consider Verizon

Model 5 phones to be “treated”, with the “control” sample composed either of Verizon Model 4 phones or all phones offered by the three other firms. The observed effects of the Undisclosed variable in Table 3 and the Locked and Model 5 variables in Table 4 therefore form the core of our analysis. However, it is possible that we captured spurious correlations here that are not linked to the Verizon policy change per se but represent other unobservable factors (Bertrand et al., 2004). To test for these spurious correlations, we conducted a set of placebo tests to examine how robust the estimated treatment effects are with respect to the results in Tables 3 and 4, specifically with respect to the Undisclosed, Locked, and Model 5 variables.

We follow prior work (Burtch et al., 2018) in conducting the placebo tests through random implementation of the treatment variable. We started with the estimates shown in Table 3, Column 1, where the estimates of Undisclosed on final prices are shown for the full sample. We then generated a set of new pseudo-treatments by randomly assigning the Undisclosed status to auctions within the sample of 8179 auctions, but retaining the ratio of treated to untreated (control) observations. By definition, these new random treatments should be unrelated to final prices. The results from this analysis are shown in Table $^ { 6 , }$ Column 1. As expected, the coefficient of the placebo variable Undisclosed\_Rand is insignificant. We repeated this random assignment process 1000 times and store the estimated beta coefficients for the Undisclosed placebo variable. We then plotted these coefficients, shown in Figure 4. As shown, the mean of the coefficients estimated is close to zero, with a standard deviation of 0.016, relative to the coefficient estimated originally (-0.707, $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Subsequently, all interaction terms with this randomly generated treatment variable are also insignificant and close to zero. This placebo test allowed us to establish some robustness for the estimated effect of lack of disclosure on final prices.

We now focus on the results in Columns 1 and 2 of Table 4, which show the variation in the effect of the Locked variable across locked and unlocked phones within Models 4 and 5 respectively. The differences in the coefficients were attributed to the fact that Model 5 Verizon phones were less risky since they were all factory unlocked. We applied the same placebo analysis by randomizing the Locked variable, with the results shown in Columns 2 and 3 of Table 6. As expected, both coefficients are close to zero. The plots of the estimated coefficients in Figures 5 and 6 also show no systematic effect. We thus conclude that the coefficients estimated in the original analyses were not based on spurious correlations or other unobservable factors.

## Robustness Checks: Bidder-Level Analysis

We finally consider the results in Columns 4 and 5 of Table 4, where we show that unlocked Verizon iPhone 5 models enjoyed a premium due to the change in Verizon’s policy. Thus, the focal result here is the interaction term between Verizon and Model 5 variables (0.155, $p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ . As a placebo test, we randomly generated values of the Model 5 variable, and included this variable as well as interaction with Verizon in the regression. The results are shown in Columns 4 and 5 of Table 6—both the direct effect of Model 5 and the interaction term are insignificant and close to zero. The plots of the coefficients of the interaction term across 1000 random draws, shown in Figure 7, also show no significant effect. In summary, the placebo tests across the important specifications in our analysis provide clear support for the fact that the Verizon policy changes are indeed associated with the results we reported earlier. <sup>9</sup>

The results reported thus far are centered at the auction level but do not provide visibility into bidder-level behavior. For robustness, we considered bidder-level analysis; we studied bidders who bid on both disclosed and undisclosed iPhone pallets within the same time period and contrasted their bidding behavior across these pallets. Bidder-level analyses help dispel concerns about bidder self-selection and unobservable biases (bidders choosing to participate in only disclosed or only undisclosed auctions) while accounting for bidder idiosyncratic behavior that is not fully observable in auction-level analysis. We first identified bidders who issued a bid on comparable auctions within the same calendar month, i.e., bidders who participated in auctions where the pallet status (locked or unlocked) was disclosed as well as those where this information was omitted. Intuitively, this resembles a quasi-experimental setup where the same bidder’s behavior is observed across pallet types. To simplify the analysis, we considered three periods where the sample of bidders identified in this manner was large enough for statistical analysis: January, May, and November 2016. Across these months, there were 8,912 observed bids by bidders across auctions. If a bidder submitted multiple bids in a single auction, we only included the last submitted bid, reflecting maximum willingness-to-pay. We included control variables that are similar to those used in Equations (1) and (2), as well as bidder fixed effects and a variable that indicates the order of each submitted bid within an auction, to capture potential order effects (Pilehvar et al., 2017). Finally, to capture time trends, we separately estimated models for each of the three months to examine the effects of information diffusion. The dependent variable is the natural log of the value of each submitted bid; we estimated an OLS regression.

We separate the analyses for Verizon (Table 7) and non-Verizon carriers (Table 8) to capture the effects of the policy change. The first column of each table combines all three months, while the remaining three columns provide monthwise results. A three-way interaction for Verizon (non-Verizon) iPhone 5 models is included, as before. Across both tables, we see the negative effect of nondisclosure of status information, as expected. Interestingly, the three-way interaction showing the specific impact of the Verizon policy change is significant and negative, but smaller in magnitude for the Verizon analysis (-0.533, p < 0.05) than the non-Verizon analysis (-0.993, p < 0.05). As before, the Verizon results are counterintuitive, since providing this information is nondiagnostic, given the new policy. However, when we consider the three months separately, this effect for Verizon auctions fades away; in November 2016, the interaction term is largely negligible indicating that information about the policy change has completely diffused. However, the effect remains in place for non-Verizon auctions, suggesting that the adverse effects of lack of information persist. Effectively, individual bids across Verizon and non-Verizon iPhone 5 pallets clearly diverge over time as information about the policy change diffuses within the community, as shown in Figure 8. These results provide additional support for the beneficial effects of primary market policies in reducing adverse selection costs, but at the bidder level.

## Conclusion and Implications

Secondary markets are a significant station in the lifespan of electronic equipment—they are instrumental in giving durable products a second life as well as reducing e-waste (Thibodeau, 2013). However, these markets suffer from adverse selection, i.e., residual uncertainty about the quality and functionality of the products that are sold there. Resolving these uncertainties has often required mechanisms at the point of sale, such as guarantees, warranties, and certifications. However, there is an alternate way in which adverse selection can be reduced— by adopting appropriate policies in the primary market for durable goods. By definition, primary and secondary markets for durable goods are linked (Guide et al., 2003). These links can be used to incentivize manufacturers and retailers of technology products to consider adopting policies in the primary markets that can continue to benefit the secondary markets. In this paper, we provide an example of such a salubrious effect by showing how a policy change in the primary market for iPhone devices by Verizon reduced adverse selection in the secondary market by reducing the uncertainty associated with jailbreaking-to-unlock.

Adverse selection costs were significantly reduced for iPhone devices in their aftermarket as a result of Verizon’s policy change, consistent with prior theory in information asymmetry in durable products (Dewan & Hsu, 2004, Ghose, 2009).

Our analysis allows us to estimate the extent to which adverse selection may be mitigated, based on the specific iPhones market we study. In 2014, the unit price for a used unlocked Verizon 16 GB iPhone 5 was approximately \$200 in the B2C secondary market.<sup>10</sup> In our models, the predicted price for a similar device in the B2B secondary market was \$141. These devices were factory unlocked, and hence carried no risk associated with jailbreaking-to-unlock. It is reasonable for B2B prices to be lower than B2C prices to allow B2B buyers to generate margins. The comparable iPhone 5 predictive price from non-Verizon carriers in our model, where factory unlocking was not guaranteed, was \$75. The price differential of roughly \$66 (\$141 versus \$75) represents an indirect measure of adverse selection costs borne by sellers.

Beyond these costs, it is also evident from our analysis that B2B buyers continue to leave value on the table. In 2013, 11.2% of the phones in the U.S. were jailbroken; since this figure includes all phones, it serves as a conservative estimate of the proportion of a pallet of unlocked phones that may have been jailbroken. The current average cost to repair an iPhone is approximately \$89, including, for example, battery replacement and repairing screen responsiveness issues, which are common device performance issues resulting from jailbreaking.<sup>11</sup> Therefore, when faced with a pallet of unlocked devices and rounding up this proportion of jailbreaking to 15%, a reasonable reduction in willingness-to-pay for a pallet of such devices should reflect this partial discounting (price reduction proportional to 15% of pallet size × \$89 in repairs). However, bidders systematically underbid by \$66 per unit, thereby overestimating the risk of jailbreaking and creating inefficiencies in the market.

Do bidders who actually win pallets realize that they may be underbidding for pallets by overestimating the risks of jailbreaking? Bidders who win unlocked iPhone 4 or non-Verizon iPhone 5 pallets could perhaps examine the actual pallet and reevaluate their bidding strategy. We ran some model-free analysis to consider this possibility. We considered bidders who bid in both locked and unlocked auctions and won pallets in unlocked auctions, leading to a relatively small sample. We correlated their average bidder willingness-to-pay against the number of times they won in locked or unlocked auctions, excluding Verizon iPhone 5 auctions where there was no risk of tampering. We found that the average price (bid) for locked phones was relatively steady over time. In contrast, bidders who won multiple unlocked phones tended to submit higher bids over time. This upward trajectory of bids may potentially be explained by bidders initially overestimating the potential losses associated with jailbroken phones but learning over time through winning, causing them to reestimate their future bids. In our rough analyses, we found that bidders tended to bid higher for unlocked phones after their fourth win. These analyses are available upon request. We leave a more rigorous examination of these dynamics associated with winning auctions on adverse selection to future research.

While it is plausible that Verizon’s policy change was introduced without much consideration of its (shown) salubrious effects on secondary market valuations, the primary message of our work here—namely, the interconnectedness of primary and secondary markets and the ability of changes in the primary market to positively impact secondary market prices in the presence of adverse selection concerns—has been strategically acted upon in a wide range of industry settings. For example, the diamond industry has adopted blockchain certification in the primary market in the hopes of providing valuable information to customers regarding the product’s origin, thereby positively influencing (re)sale values further downstream (Sumkin et al., 2021). The blockchain certification acts as a “single, tamper-proof digital record for diamonds” (Krawitz, 2019) to ameliorate concerns surrounding “blood diamonds” and other unconscionable supply chain practices. Within the IT industry more specifically, hardware producers are often associated with the risk of malicious circuitry (hardware Trojans) in chip design. To counter these risks and enhance the current as well as future performance of the hardware (and as a result, their future resale value), chip designers have explored a range of methods to identify “backdoor” entry points and fortify them from attack.<sup>12</sup> These initiatives in the primary market help enhance resale in the secondary markets by ruling out the possibility of tampering.

While the above examples describe situations where steps have already been taken to tamper-proof products in the primary market, thereby helping in the secondary market, we believe there are many opportunities for more to be done. As the need to reduce the environmental effects of e-waste becomes more pressing, it will be necessary to consider strategies that help keep older technologies around for longer than otherwise expected. Interestingly, even governments are participating in these efforts; for example, the French Government is requiring technology makers like Apple and Microsoft to provide a “repairability score” for products that can then be used to extend the life of these products (Beres, 2021). Similarly, Google is implementing the notion of “circularity” in their servers and devices, ensuring that these used technologies can reenter the secondary market with adequate quality and performance assurances.<sup>13</sup> These initiatives have been designed to ensure that secondary markets for these products have more complete information, thereby enhancing their efficiency. These links between primary and secondary markets can thus be used to enhance value in both markets—our work here provides evidence for such an effect but within the context of iPhone jailbreaking.

Our work is subject to certain limitations. First, while we theorize about jailbreaking-to-unlock, we did not directly observe phones that are jailbroken. Nevertheless, the changes in prices we observed from the exogenous policy change across multiple analyses are fully consistent with the expectations of jailbreaking. Second, strategic decisions by sellers could have played a role in the prices we observed. However, we did not have the visibility into seller data to identify why sellers chose to auction locked versus unlocked phone pallets. Third, we studied iPhones, arguably the most well-specified mobile device in the IT industry. Although other brands (e.g., Samsung) may be perceived differently by bidders, we believe that our work provides some insight into how firm policies in the primary market could help to address adverse selection in the B2B secondary market.

There are several managerial implications that emerge from our work here. For policy makers considering sustainable reverse logistics programs in the IT industry, we show the value of forward-looking policies that can benefit both primary and secondary markets. For carriers like T-Mobile and AT&T that sell both locked and unlocked devices, it is useful to consider the downstream implications of factory unlocking when designing strategies in the primary market (Lee & Whang, 2002; Guide & Li, 2010). For secondary market auctioneers, we show that withholding information, whether intentionally or by omission, has a negative effect on prices. We quantify such adverse selection costs that are borne by sellers in the market. Knowing these costs is useful since it places an upper bound on how much sellers may be willing to invest in remedying this quality uncertainty in terms of investing in “factory unlocking” devices before placing them on auction sites. As mobile devices become increasingly durable, they can remain in circulation longer (Pilehvar et al., 2017), making the efficient functioning of secondary markets a desirable objective. From a market efficiency perspective, our work thus calls for a holistic approach to policies from the primary market, which can have far-reaching ramifications.
