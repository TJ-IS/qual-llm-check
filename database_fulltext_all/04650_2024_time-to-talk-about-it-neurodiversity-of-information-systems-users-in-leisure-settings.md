# Time to Talk About It: Neurodiversity of Information Systems Users in Leisure Settings

Ofir Turel,<sup>1</sup> Antoine Bechara<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of Melbourne, Computing and Information Systems, Australia, oturel@unimelb.edu.au <sup>2</sup>University of Southern California, Department of Psychology, USA, bechara@usc.edu

## Abstract

There is a growing trend to accept, understand and cater to people with heterogenous mental health levels, as captured by the term “neurodiversity.” Population studies demonstrate growth in neurodiversity facets such as depression, anxiety, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Thus, samples of information systems (IS) users are likely neurodiverse. While studies started examining neurodiversity effects mostly in professional contexts, possible effects of IS users’ neurodiversity in leisure settings have been largely overlooked. Here, we suggest that we need to consider such possible effects more systematically. This is important because neurodiversity facets can manifest in changes in cognitive emotional processing, social reactions, and decision-making which can alter user responses to IS. Thus, ignoring the neurodiversity of users can affect the accuracy and generalizability of user behavior models; it can disadvantage large segments of users. To support these ideas, we first developed a theoretical model that explains how neurodiversity can affect the decision-making of IS users. Next, we conducted three studies (n1 = 400, n2 = 381, and n3 = 280) that first replicated common IS models in leisure settings and then theoretically and empirically integrated neurodiversity facets into these models. Results show that (1) typical IS user samples are neurodiverse, and (2) integrating neurodiversity facets into user behavior models can be informative, lead to greater inclusivity, and afford more nuanced theoretical and practica insights. Thus, we call for a more systematic inclusion of neurodiversity facets in behavioral IS research.

Keywords: Neurodiversity, Inclusivity, Privacy, Technology Use, Online Reviews, Mental Health

Lior Fink was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on January 25, 2023 and underwent two revisions.

## 1 Introduction

Neurodiversity reflects natural variation in human brain function and structure that is viewed as normal rather than as an impairment (or disorder) (Loiacono & Ren, 2018). This perspective implies that mental health challenges that have been traditionally viewed as disorders <sup>1</sup> exist on a continuum rather than as a dichotomy dividing the population into “normal” people vs. people with a disorder (Chapman, 2021). It suggests that all people are simply mentally different and that society should accept and embrace such differences (Armstrong, 2015). In line with this more humanistic trend, we use neurodiversity terminology here and view mental disorders as mental differences on a continuum that reflects neurodiversity.

Our literature review of the Association for Information Systems (AIS) journals, affiliate journals, and conferences (See Appendix A) indicated that (1) neurodiversity research in information systems (IS) is in its infancy, with only 15 papers since 2017 (mostly conference proceedings) mentioning neurodiversity, and (2) research on the topic tends to focus mostly on the possible integration of neurodiverse talents into the workforce (e.g., Loiacono & Ren, 2018), with a few exceptions that focus on neurodiverse students (Soror et al., 2023), children (Zolyomi & Schmalz, 2017) or the general population (Jia et al., 2022). Two notable research gaps emerged from this review. First, with the current emphasis on professional contexts (e.g., work), there is limited understanding of how the neurodiversity of IS users in leisure settings (e.g., using e-commerce, social networking sites, or online review platforms for nonwork purposes) can affect user behavior in common nonwork contexts. Second, while providing a great departure point, existing studies have treated neurodiversity rather narrowly, focusing mainly on autism and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Thus, there is room to theorize and examine how additional neurodiversity facets, especially prevalent ones, affect the way users interact with technologies.

We posit that these are important gaps to address for two reasons. First, the general population is neurodiverse along many facets; almost one in five American adults (19%) experiences mental challenges (The American Psychiatric Association, 2018). One very common neurodiversity facet is depression. Research has shown that the prevalence rate of major depressive disorder in the US is 7.1% (National Institute of Mental Health, 2019), but minor depression, i.e., depression that is lower on the continuum and often does not prompt people to seek treatment, is 5.17 times more prevalent (Cuijpers et al., 2004). Indeed, across 24 undergraduate student samples (n = 48,650), the prevalence of some level of depression on a continuum (regardless of formal diagnosis) is 30.6% (Ibrahim et al., 2013). Another very common neurodiversity facet is anxiety<sup>2</sup>: 31.1% of US adults have had debilitating levels of anxiety at some point in their lives (National Institute of Mental Health, 2017a). These prevalence rates imply that it is likely that IS users, including those in leisure settings, are neurodiverse. This heterogeneity, however, has been typically overlooked. This means that users who are not in the middle of neurodiversity continuums may have been marginalized and remain unaccounted for by IS research.

If the general population is neurodiverse, it seems likely that samples of users of IS in leisure settings would also be neurodiverse. We wish to test this idea because its validation underlies the importance of the topic and paves the way for future research on neurodiversity in IS users. Thus, our first research question is:

RQ1: Is there neurodiversity among users of IS in leisure settings?

Second, if there is neurodiversity among IS users in leisure settings, ignoring it significantly limits the ability of the field to develop precise and inclusive models of IS user behaviors. Neurodiversity is rooted in psychobiological underpinnings that alter people’s cognitions, emotions, decision-making processes, and behaviors (Bishop & Gagne, 2018; Mäntylä et al., 2012; Murphy et al., 2001). This means that overlooking neurodiversity may have produced biased results because people vary in cognitive and emotional processing abilities, as underpinned by their neurodiversity facets. As a community of researchers that believes in and wishes to increase diversity (Burton-Jones & Sarker, 2021), we have done little to understand the nuances, try to help, and be more inclusive of a large portion of our constituents—users in leisure settings (beyond those in organizations; see, for instance, Loiacono & Ren, 2018) who are not in the middle of neurodiversity continuums. Thus, our second research question is:

RQ2: (a) does the neurodiversity of users of IS in leisure settings matter, i.e., does it affect common models of IS user behavior, and (b) through what mechanisms?

To address these research questions, we replicated three IS research associations with typical samples of IS users in leisure settings. These associations were carefully selected based on their foci: threat avoidance, reward seeking, and social reactions. We chose these because the brain processing in such situations can be influenced by specific common facets of neurodiversity. To address RQ1, we used the samples to capture neurodiversity as manifested in significant variation in neurodiversity facets. We then addressed RQ2a-b by theoretically integrating specific neurodiversity facets on a continuum (anxiety levels, depression levels, and

ADHD levels) into the tested models. We examined if this integration adds value by either informing or shifting existing knowledge and typical results.<sup>3</sup> We chose to focus on specific neurodiversity facets that are (1) relatively widespread, (2) exist and can be measured on a continuum, and (3) for which there is a systematic theoretical basis for integration into IS user behavior models. These choices, of course, do not cover all facets of neurodiversity. Nevertheless, they can provide sufficient evidence and serve as a reasonable departure point for a more careful integration of neurodiversity into models of IS user behavior. In all studies, we avoided the pitfall of formally diagnosing people. Instead, in the spirit of neurodiversity, we treated all users’ scores on the selected neurodiversity facet scales as indicators of the magnitude of the challenges they face, thus capturing broad neurodiversity.

Specifically, in Study 1 (n = 400, two-wave online survey) we integrated anxiety (also known as “anxiety disorder”) levels into an internet privacy concern model in the context of e-commerce (Hong & Thong, 2013). In Study 2 (n = 381, two-wave online survey) we integrated depression (also known as “depressive disorder”) levels into the commonly examined association between satisfaction and use (Bhattacherjee, 2001), in the context of social networking sites (SNS). Lastly, in Study 3 (n = 280, online experiment) we integrated ADHD levels into a model of reactions to online reviews (Yin et al., 2014).

Overall, the studies demonstrate that integrating neurodiversity facets into behavioral IS models in leisure settings can be beneficial and informative, leading to greater inclusivity and to more nuanced insights. Consequently, we argue that the field should move the needle from the current state of mostly ignoring sample heterogeneity in neurodiversity facets to at least controlling for such heterogeneity and, ideally, to accounting for the theory-based effects of neurodiversity facets on IS user cognitions, affect, and behaviors. This would create a more inclusive research field by allowing system developers to improve systems design decisions, IS scholars to have a better understanding of the specific mechanisms that explain how and why neurodiversity matters and to help ensure that the understanding is based on strong theoretical and empirical evidence rather than common stereotypes.

## 2 Background

Neurodiversity can encompass any natural heterogeneity in the facets of mental health. Thus, we start by developing a general framework that allows for the integration of neurodiversity into IS user behavior models, including in leisure settings. Because there are numerous facets that can fall under the neurodiversity umbrella, we acknowledge that this framework may need to be adjusted for different contexts. Nevertheless, we believe it is important, necessary, and reasonably generalizable. We later build on this framework to theorize the specific effects of three facets of neurodiversity on common IS user models.

Note that there is a long tradition in the field of IS, which looks at the impacts of individual traits in the context of IT use (Sun & Zhang, 2006). These models provide rich insight into how individual traits, people’s cognitive and affective states, and characteristics of IT jointly impact the psychological processes and outcomes associated with IT use. Neurodiversity facets can be similar to other individual differences in their ability to alter the ways people process cognitions and emotions. However, there are three points that make the focus on neurodiversity facets interesting, novel, and important. First, neurodiversity represents a relatively overlooked battery of individual differences; how these differences affect a broad range of IS user behaviors has yet to be examined. Second, we have a moral obligation to examine and understand the effects of neurodiversity on IS user behaviors because without this, we underserve large segments of the user population—i.e., those individuals not in the middle of neurodiversity facet continuums. Third, traditional individual differences (e.g., computer self-efficacy) tend to be neurotypical. Neurodiversity facets, in contrast, are rooted in deviations from common psychoneurological processes. That is, while all individual differences (neurotypical or not) serve to inform cognitive processes, neurodiversity variables inform them through specific psychoneurological processes, as depicted in Paths 1 and 2 in Figure 1, on which we elaborate in the next subsection. The mechanisms through which neurodiversity affects user behavior have received little attention in IS research. Thus, there is a need to more systematically consider how neurodiversity can be integrated into models of IS user behavior.

## 2.1 Neurodiversity Effects on User Cognition, Affect and Behavior

To understand the general mechanisms through which neurodiversity can affect IS user behavior, we first note that motivation theories suggest that humans seek to obtain rewards and avoid aversive states (Carlezon Jr. & Thomas, 2009). Consequently, people’s decisionmaking and behavior can be modeled as a sequence of cognitive-emotional processing steps aimed at obtaining rewards or avoiding aversive states (Bishop & Gagne, 2018; Gagne et al., 2020). Thus, when faced with a decision, people try to estimate the type of outcomes the action (or inaction) may lead to (including emotions), the outcome probabilities, and the effort they need to exert to obtain the outcomes. To this end, they recall past actions and outcomes and use this information to mentally simulate the cognitive-emotional effort and outcomes associated with the new action (or inaction). These estimates produce a composite value of action assessment. This value assessment can drive one of three prototypical behavioral choices: approach (take action to obtain the stimuli), avoidance (take action to avoid the stimuli), and inaction (avoid action and decision-making). These behavioral choices, in turn, lead to outcomes such as obtaining the reward, aversive outcomes, or not obtaining the reward nor obtaining aversive outcomes. The outcomes, in turn, are processed by the actors to update their belief system: the expected rewards, efforts, risks, and emotions that serve as an input for cognitive processing are modified based on these outcomes.

Neurodiversity can affect people’s cognition, affect, and behavior through the systematic alteration of the abovementioned steps. Though each neurodiversity facet may affect different steps in different directions, there are common threads and overarching neurodiversity influences, which we cover in Figure 1. These include changes in (1) people’s cognitive processing (Path 1 in Figure 1) and (2) the way people update their belief system or “learn” (Path 2 in Figure 1) (Bishop & Gagne, 2018; Gagne et al., 2020). Effects on cognitive processing (Path 1) include the alteration of people’s motivation and their ability to recall specific information, a biased emphasis on specific events (e.g., those with negative valence), limits in one’s ability to process and integrate emotions into decision-making (which can bias the expected value of actions), and the estimated effort cost. Moreover, neurodiversity can affect people’s ability to process and learn from the outcomes of their choices (Path 2), which can further bias the cognitive processing of decisions (Bishop & Gagne, 2018; Gagne et al., 2020). As we explain later, these ideas also apply to IS users, including in leisure settings, because IS users engage in decision-making that involves possible rewards (e.g., when using a social networking site), threats (e.g., when sharing private information), and emotional reactions (e.g., when reading online reviews). Next, we briefly explain each one of the neurodiversity facets this study focuses on. We then use the overarching theoretical mechanisms and model (Figure 1) to explain the specific decision-making alterations caused by higher levels of the examined facets of neurodiversity.

## 2.2 Anxiety

Anxiety reflects excessive worrying and preoccupation with potential negative future events, without necessarily facing real threats (Borkovec et al., 1983). The avoidance theory of anxiety suggests that excessive anxiety is rooted in persistent worrying about future bad events that are probabilistic and rarely happen in reality (Borkovec et al., 2004). Thus, high anxiety levels manifest in individuals exhibiting an exaggerated processing of uncertain or probabilistic negative future events (Mueller et al., 2010). Consequently, such individuals tend to show more avoidance of behaviors or cues that may signal punishments and/or reward omissions, relative to individuals with lower anxiety levels (Mueller et al., 2010). Overall, people with higher anxiety levels have heightened awareness of possible future punishments, exaggerated probabilities of unlikely aversive events and outcomes, and lower ability to update their beliefs after positive outcomes, compared to those with lower anxiety levels. Thus, anxiety can influence cognitive processing and decision-making in the domain of threat avoidance because it leads to inflated threat perceptions and overweighted threat integration into decision-making (Bishop & Gagne, 2018).

The IS literature has emphasized trait anxiety, which captures the tendency to be anxious when confronted with problems. This represents a normal variation in people’s response to stressors (Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002). In contrast, the anxiety we focus on here is an individual difference in problematic anxious responses to any life event (not necessarily a stressor), which tends to be debilitating and manifest in conscious physical symptoms (e.g., panic attacks). Indeed, a meta-analysis indicated that trait anxiety has a 0.59 correlation with anxiety disorder (Knowles & Olatunji, 2020), which means they share only 34% of the variance. Nevertheless, IS studies on trait anxiety can be informative, as they show that normal variation in trait anxiety can influence how people react to computers (Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002) and that the invoked anxiety can drive people to seek support, disengage, and reduce IS use (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2010). Thus, if the anxiety we capture here produces similar if not more severe anxious reactions, we can assume it will also drive threat avoidance behaviors.

## 2.3 Depression

Depression, in contrast to anxiety, is associated with reduced sensitivity to rewards (Eshel & Roiser, 2010). Individuals with high levels of depression tend to experience low positive affect from pleasant events and high negative affect in response to unpleasant events (Kasch et al., 2002). Depression is also associated with hyposensitivity to rewards in probabilistic contexts that involve both reward and punishment outcomes (Kunisato et al., 2012). For example, individuals with high levels of depression often avoid social gatherings to avoid the risk of having a negative experience (i.e., increased avoidance of punishment) at the cost of potentially obtaining social rewards. Indeed, studies employing probabilistic learning tasks showed that participants with higher depression scores do not exhibit learning deficits (Path 2 in Figure 1) but show a diminished tendency to base their decisions on the likelihood of obtaining rewards (Path 1 in Figure 1) (Kunisato et al., 2012). Consequently, when facing potential rewards, individuals with high levels of depression are less likely to act (Ottenbreit & Dobson, 2004). They instead seek escapist actions that shift attention from selfreflection (Leary, 2003). Thus, depression can affect cognition and decisions in the domain of reward seeking by reducing reward perceptions and underweighting the integration of reward expectations into decision-making (Bishop & Gagne, 2018).

The IS literature has focused on negative affectivity, which is the degree to which people feel a broad range of negative emotions ranging from hostility to guilt, across situations (Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002). Notably, negative affectivity differs from depression, as they have a 0.39-0.51 correlation (Watson et al., 1988), which means they share only 15%-26% of the variance. Nevertheless, the IS literature on negative affectivity can be informative. Studies have demonstrated that individual differences in negative affectivity can influence the way that users react to IS (Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002) and lead IS users to feel strain (Ayyagari et al., 2011). Such results allude to the potential of individual differences in depression to influence reactions to IS, especially in the domain of reward seeking.

## 2.4 Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is often linked to symptoms of inattention, impulsivity, impaired inhibition, and hyperactivity (Davidson, 2008). While these can be relevant for decision-making in various contexts, these main symptoms are not our focus here. Instead, we follow the idea, as supported by numerous recent studies, that ADHD is also associated with difficulties in several social functions, such as peer functioning (including peer rejection and social isolation), which may be a result of inappropriate social behavior such as frequently interrupting and not listening to others carefully (De Boo & Prins, 2007; Guevremont & Dumas, 1994; Hinshaw, 2002). The difficulties in social and interpersonal functioning associated with ADHD have been attributed to overlapping deficits with autism spectrum disorders (ASD), which are characterized by specific impairments in empathy (Nijmeijer et al., 2008). While most studies on ADHD focus on children, more recent studies suggest that adults with subclinical levels of ADHD also show reduced emotional empathy (Groen et al., 2018). Hence ADHD levels can affect empathic reactions in cognitive and decision-making processes (Braaten & Rosén, 2000). That is, in IS use contexts that induce empathic reaction, people high on the ADHD continuum may find it difficult to respond to empathyinducing cues and develop empathy compared to people with lower levels of ADHD.

Notably, ADHD has been the focus of recent IS studies (See Appendix A). For instance, research suggests that it can negatively affect online learning and produce stress (Soror et al., 2023) and that people with high levels of ADHD might have attributes that can benefit the workplace but also challenge typical communication norms in the workplace (Loiacono & Ren, 2018). Research also shows that technology can support safe socialization for people with ADHD (Zolyomi & Schmalz, 2017). Here, we suggest expanding insights on ADHD by integrating it with behavioral IS models that emphasize empathic reactions.

## 3 Study 1

The objective of Study 1 was to integrate anxiety levels into an IS model that focuses on threat avoidance in leisure settings. We chose the internet privacy concern (IPC) model in Hong and Thong (2013) because it captures a common (but certainly not the only) threat avoidance process in IS use contexts. Consistent with Hong and Thong (2013), we did not focus on a specific website. Instead, we focused on all e-commerce websites a person interacts with to capture the more general and overreaching privacy concerns that people have when interacting with such sites.

## 3.1 Theory: Study 1

The base model predicts that as IPC increases, trusting beliefs decrease and risk beliefs grow. Given that these hypotheses are explained in detail in the original study (Hong & Thong, 2013), we outline and briefly justify them in Table 1 (Labeled as H1.1 and H1.2), which also provides construct definitions.

Here, we focus on the theoretical integration of anxiety levels into this model. Based on Figure 1 and the effects of anxiety on decision-making (Bishop & Gagne, 2018), we expect an elevated integration of privacy concerns in people with higher levels of anxiety. This is because two aspects of sharing private information are particularly difficult to estimate: the probability of adverse outcomes, such as misuse of the information, and the harm (negative “value”) caused by this outcome. Importantly, individuals can vary in how they weigh outcome probabilities and value (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). One’s level of anxiety biases the estimation and weighting of probabilities and outcome values (Bishop & Gagne, 2018). Specifically, people with high levels of anxiety (1) often fail to adjust cognitive and affective processing weights based on positive experiences (Gagne et al., 2020) and (2) typically have much better recall of negative experiences than positive ones (Muris & van der Heiden, 2006). These two processes make anxious people more sensitive to threats (Gagne et al., 2020) and cause them to mentally simulate negative outcomes more frequently and intensely than others when developing beliefs and attitudes (Bishop & Gagne, 2018).

Applied to our context, this means two things. First, anxious IS users will likely have better recall of negative events—causing them to miscalculate (overestimate) risk—and are also likely to have elevated fears of something going wrong compared to people lower on the anxiety continuum. This systematic bias is captured in Path 1 in Figure 1. In our context, this will lead to elevated privacy concern and risk beliefs, and reduced trust beliefs because such assessments rely on evaluated probabilities and magnitudes of harms (Bélanger & Carter, 2008). Moreover, anxious individuals are less likely to learn from positive outcomes and update their belief system, meaning that their expectations regarding negative outcome probabilities and severity will remain inflated (Bishop & Gagne, 2018). In the context of internet privacy, as reflected in Path 2 in Figure 1, this means that anxious individuals will not adjust their fearful worldview, despite the fact that in all or virtually all internet use instances their information has not been misused. This is consistent with the ideas that (1) people do not always have the ability or motivation to engage in effortful translation of privacy concerns into trust, and (2) effortful processing depends, among other things, on cognitive resources and processing abilities (Dinev et al., 2015), which in our case are attenuated by anxiety levels. Hence:

## H1.3: Anxiety levels will be positively associated with B internet privacy concerns and (b) risk beliefs and negatively associated with (c) trust beliefs.<sup>4</sup>

Second, anxiety levels can affect the weighting of risk reflections because anxious people tend to overemphasize risks in their mental calculus, which leads to summative cognitive appraisals (Bishop & Gagne, 2018). Therefore, users with higher levels of anxiety will overweight IPC in their cognitiveaffective processing, compared to others. In Figure 1, this is reflected in the Path 1 effect on the composite value of action, which is affected by expected outcomes as well as the weights given to them. Hence, it is reasonable to expect that anxiety levels will increase the weight people place on concern facets (IPC in our case) in their trust and risk calculus, as concerns are more salient than benefits for anxious people. Thus:

H1.4: Anxiety levels will moderate (strengthen) (a) the positive association between internet privacy concerns and risk beliefs, and (b) the negative association between internet privacy concerns and trust beliefs. Simply put, the positive association between IPC and risk beliefs will be increasingly positive for people with higher levels of anxiety and the negative association between IPC and trust beliefs will be increasingly negative for people with higher levels of anxiety.

## 3.2 Participants and Procedures: Study 1

Participants were recruited in 2019 from a population of American university students who were not taught by the researchers. The inclusion criteria were >18 years old and e-commerce site use history. The study was posted on a course portal and students received 1.5 extra credit points for voluntary participation. We recruited students because they tend to be normally functioning individuals who vary in neurodiversity facets (Blanco et al., 2008) and are likely to use ecommerce (Lei et al., 2020). Of the 454 invitees, 400 (88.1%) met the inclusion criteria and completed the study. The average age in the sample was 22.79 (18- 54, SD = 4.20); 51.7% of participants were female.

The study employed two online surveys, administered one week apart. The first survey first screened for ecommerce use status (“Do you use e-commerce websites, such as Amazon.com, Walmart.com, Target.com, clothing retailers, grocery stores, online music stores, etc.?”) No exclusions were made. Next, it captured user demographics (age, sex [female = 0, male = 1]), followed by the IPC scale. In the second survey, we captured anxiety levels with items from DASS-21 (Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995b) as well as trust and risk beliefs (Hong & Thong, 2013).<sup>5</sup> We used DASS-21 because it is a commonly used, valid and reliable scale for capturing anxiety levels on a continuum (Antony et al., 1998; Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995a; Norton, 2007) across cultures (Oei et al., 2013). All scales and subscales were found to be reliable (See Appendix B, including survey items, descriptive statistics, and reliabilities) and had reasonable convergent and discriminant validities (see Appendix C). Hence, we used averages of multi-item scales in analyses. To operationalize a score for the third-order factor structure in Hong and Thong (2013) we used averages of firstand then second-order scales.

## 3.3 Results: Study 1

To address RQ1, we analyzed anxiety scores in the sample. We used the classification system in Lovibond and Lovibond (1995b), which is based on clinical studies. The anxiety scale produced index scores through summation, which were then multiplied by 2 for scoring. As per Lovibond and Lovibond (1995b), we used the following cutoffs: 0-7, 8-9, 10-14, 15-19, and 20+ as indicators of normal, mild, moderate, severe, and extremely severe anxiety, respectively.

This classification is used in clinical settings as a preliminary screening tool, as it is only suggestive of neurodiversity facets that require treatment; a full diagnosis requires interviews and a closer examination of individuals’ challenges. The distribution of anxiety levels is given in Table 2. The mean was significantly above zero (p < 0.001) and the 95% bias corrected confidence interval for the standard deviation (0.54; 0.82) did not include zero. Thus, we concluded that there was reasonable heterogeneity in anxiety levels. The scores were in line with normative scale scores. For instance, the DASS-anxiety score<sup>6</sup> in a sample of the general adult UK population was 0.83 (Henry & Crawford, 2005), in university students in nine countries it was 0.61 (Zanon et al., 2021), and in US students it was 0.56 (Zanon et al., 2021). The classification results suggest that only half of the sample was fully free of anxiety symptoms (normal). In fact, 50% of the sample suffered from at least mild anxiety, and 23.25% suffered severe anxiety or worse, with 12.75% experiencing extremely severe anxiety. This is in line with other samples indicating that 40% of individuals suffer from at least mild anxiety (Beiter et al., 2015).

To address RQ2a-b, we used a two-step approach in the PROCESS macro and SPSS 28. The PROCESS macro runs a sequence of ordinary least squares models in SPSS, which allows for deep inference regarding moderation and mediation models. The PROCESS macro uses a default of 5,000 bootstrapping resamples to generate confidence intervals for direct, indirect, and conditional effects (Hayes, 2017). In the first step, we regressed IPC on anxiety level. In the second, we estimated Model 1 in the PROCESS macro which included anxiety levels as the moderator, IPC as the independent variable, and trust or risk beliefs, respectively, as the outcome. The models controlled for age and sex and used centered variables for the interaction. There was no concerning collinearity among the predictors (VIF = 1.025). Given that there were relatively small correlations among the constructs and in different directions (see Appendix B), the risk for common method bias (CMB) was small.

Results are given in Figure 2. First, replicating Hong and Thong (2013), IPC reduced trusting beliefs (supporting H1.1) and increased risk beliefs (supporting H1.2). Second, anxiety had both direct and moderation effects. It increased IPC (supporting H1.3a) and risk beliefs (supporting H13.b), and reduced trust beliefs (supporting H13.c). Moreover, anxiety levels strengthened the IPC effect on risk and trust beliefs, supporting H1.4a and H1.4b, respectively.

## 3.4 Discussion: Study 1

Addressing RQ1, the findings demonstrate neurodiversity in typical IS user samples, with heterogeneity in anxiety levels. Next, we expected anxious IS users to overemphasize negative events, overestimate risk, and fail to update their beliefs despite evidence of low risk, which together, lead to reduced trust and elevated risk assessments. Addressing RQ2a-b, we found support for these ideas. The two models show that anxiety levels can influence IS user behaviors in the domains of risk and trust in at least two ways.

First, they directly affect user cognitions by driving users to overemphasize risks. Second, they increase the effect of IPC on risk beliefs (this effect became stronger for people with higher levels of anxiety) and on trusting beliefs (this effect became more negative for people with higher levels of anxiety).

Taken together, the results demonstrate that the effects in Hong and Thong (2013) are stable in direction but not in magnitude because the magnitude is contingent on one’s anxiety level. This supports the Dinev et al. (2015) idea that in some situations, people will engage in a less effortful development of trust and risk beliefs based on privacy concerns. Here, we show that given neurodiversity along the anxiety facet, people might engage in overweighted cognitive and affective processing of privacy concerns, which alters their trust and risk judgments. We also show that the levels of concerns and risks are elevated among people with higher levels of anxiety. Importantly, risk judgments are also relevant in other IS use contexts, such as cybersecurity and online reviews. Thus, what we demonstrate here in the privacy context can pave the way for a better integration of neurodiversity facets such as anxiety levels into many models of IS user behavior.

The results in Panel B of Figure 2 further show that males had lower trusting beliefs compared to females. One possible explanation, which we cannot support or refute, is that males and females use different ecommerce websites. Another possible explanation is that e-commerce websites lack the trust building cues that males seek by providing insufficient means for building hierarchy and, in contrast, include more trust building cues that females seek, with features for building relationships (Awad & Ragowsky, 2008). A third possible explanation is that males and females differ biologically in trust processing in the brain (Riedl et al., 2010).

Overall, we conclude that while prior IPC models are not biased at mean levels of anxiety, they ignore the nuances of IPC effects and risk, concern, and trust magnitudes among people with different anxiety levels. This approach marginalizes large segments of the population with no anxiety symptoms as well as those with very strong anxiety symptoms who might have different IPC effects regarding their cognitions and behaviors and who might benefit from systems that better cater to their psychological needs.

## 4 Study 2

The objective of Study 2 was to integrate depression levels into an IS model that focuses on reward seeking. We chose the satisfaction → system use association (Bhattacherjee, 2001) because it captures a common and important (but certainly not the only) reward-seeking process in leisure IS use contexts. This association is at the core of IS user behaviors; satisfaction not only explains use behavior indirectly, through increasing use intentions, but also directly (Bhattacherjee & Lin, 2015). For this study, we focused on SNS as instances of hedonic systems, the voluntary use of which is motivated by satisfaction (Casaló et al., 2017). Our choice of this association was guided by theoretical arguments on how depression can alter cognitive and affective reward (or enjoyment) processing and weighing abilities (Bishop & Gagne, 2018).

## 4.1 Theory: Study 2

The base model predicts that satisfaction increases system use. Given that this is explained in the original study (Bhattacherjee, 2001), we outline and briefly justify it in Table 3, which also provides construct definitions.

Here, we focus on the theoretical integration of depression levels into this model. Depression acts through the same mechanisms as anxiety does to change people’s cognitive-emotional processing, but in the opposite direction: higher depression levels push people to favor inaction in the domain of reward seeking (Bishop & Gagne, 2018). Specifically, people with higher levels of depression share the same impaired learning mechanism as people with high anxiety levels (Gagne et al., 2020). The difference is that they misjudge rewarding behaviors by failing to encode positive experiences and update their beliefs accordingly. Moreover, anhedonia (inability to derive pleasure from typically enjoyable activities) is a key feature of depression (Bishop & Gagne, 2018). It happens because depression prevents recall of positive experiences and increases the mental “playback” of negative experiences. Applied here, it implies that people with higher levels of depression will not be satisfied with generally positive use experiences to the same extent as people with lower levels of depression because one’s level of satisfaction is informed by the recall of prior experiences (Bhattacherjee, 2001). Hence, through recall and mental simulation processes that are different from those of people with lower levels of depression, people with higher levels of depression produce a lower appreciation of rewarding experiences (Path 1 in Figure 1). Thus:

H2.2: Depression levels will be negatively associated with satisfaction with a social networking site.<sup>7</sup>

There are two competing views about how depression informs future rewarding behaviors. Both views rely on Path 1 in Figure 1, which suggests that neurodiversity facets can alter people’s perceptions of outcome values, outcome probabilities, and effort cost evaluations, which in turn, alter people’s composite value of action evaluations (action motivation) and consequent behavioral choices. Specifically, the first view suggests that people with higher levels of depression will use fewer positive cues, such as satisfaction, in their cognitive and affective processing determining use for three reasons. First, their ability to recall positive experiences and feelings is impaired (Bishop & Gagne, 2018), such that they will not recall positive satisfying events as well as people with lower levels of depression. Second, depression manifests in reduced future reward estimates (MacLeod, 1996), such that in the presence of satisfaction signals, people with higher levels of depression will be less likely than people with lower levels of depression people to use these signals for assigning high value to future use actions (Bishop & Gagne, 2018). Lastly, the perceived effort associated with a behavior, including presumably using a system, is inflated among depressed people and may receive a higher weight compared to other considerations (e.g., satisfaction) in one’s cognitive and affective processing (Bishop & Gagne, 2018). Hence:

## H2.3 (alternative a): Depression levels will moderate (weaken) the positive association between satisfaction with a social networking site and the use of this site.

A competing perspective stems from escape theory, which suggests that people engage in enjoyable behaviors such as eating, to overcome, forget, or cope with negative states (Heatherton & Baumeister, 1991). This theory implies that when facing constant negative affect (depression), people will be motivated to reduce the focus on themselves and turn their foci onto external elements (Paxton & Diggens, 1997). This is what coping theory defines as emotion-based coping (Carver et al., 1989). While ineffective in the long run, this coping approach drives people to narrow their focus of attention onto relevant cues and the actions that allow them to escape reality (Paxton & Diggens, 1997). Applied here, it implies that people with higher levels of depression will have a narrow focus that likely emphasizes cues, such as satisfaction, that allow them to implement their escapist coping strategy. Other cues may be pushed outside of their narrow cognitive focus (Blackburn et al., 2006). In Figure 1, this manifests in higher weights given to positive emotions and outcomes by people with higher levels of depression, which leads to a stronger motivation to pursue the rewarding behavior, leading to higher levels of the behavior (Path 1 effect). Hence, satisfaction will be more informative for use decisions among people with higher levels of depression levels. Thus:

## H2.3 (alternative b): Depression levels will moderate (strengthen) the positive association between satisfaction with a social networking site and the use of this site.

If H2.3a is true, it means that SNS use in people with higher levels of depression is biased through misguided reflective-affective processes that involve the inability to incorporate expected rewards into one’s mental calculus. If H2.3b is true, it means that the coping perspective is dominant in guiding SNS use through the narrowing of one’s motivation to pursue escapism. Furthermore, if H2.3b (i.e., the coping perspective) is dominant, issues related to sex differences will arise, because males and females differ in how they cope with depression (Kleinke et al., 1982; Nolen-Hoeksema, 1987). Specifically, females “are more likely to internalize and ruminate on their condition, whereas [males] are more likely to engage in externalizing or distracting activities” (Wang et al., 2019, p. 6). Such sex differences in coping with depression likely stem from socially learned and enforced gender-role stereotypes that drive females more than males to focus on themselves rather than on other factors when coping with negative states (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1987). Such serotypes appear in childhood and are further reinforced through life (Nolen-

Hoeksema et al., 1986). Applied here, it means that males are more likely than females to respond to depression symptoms using an escapist approach (external focus) via SNS. This represents nuanced differences in Path 1 in Figure 1, which manifest in a three-way interaction. Hence:

H2.4: The moderation effect of depression levels on the positive association between satisfaction with a social networking site and the use of this site will be stronger for males than for females.

## 4.2 Participants and Procedures- Study 2

A different sample was collected in 2019, using similar procedures to those used in Study 1. The inclusion criteria were >18 years old and current Instagram use. We sampled university students because they are likely to vary in levels of depression (Ibrahim et al., 2013) and are likely to use Instagram (Alhabash & Ma, 2017), which is a common SNS that we focused on here. Of the 474 invitees, 381 (80.4%) met the inclusion criteria and completed the study. The average age in the sample was 22.73 (18-44, SD = 3.91), and 53.0% of the sample were females. The study employed two online surveys, administered one week apart. The first survey screened for Instagram use (“Do you use Instagram?”) No exclusions were made. Next, it captured user demographics (age, sex [female = 0, male = 1]), followed by satisfaction with Instagram, adapted from Limayem et al. (2007). The second survey captured depression levels using items from DASS-21 (Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995b) and the extent of Instagram use over the last week, adapted from Turel and Bechara (2016). We used DASS-21 because it is a commonly used, valid, and reliable scale for capturing depression levels, including in nonclinical samples (Sinclair et al., 2012). All scales were found to be reliable (See Appendix B) and had reasonable convergent and discriminant validities (see Appendix C).

## 4.3 Results- Study 2

We repeated the procedure we used in Study 1 to address RQ1 and examined heterogeneity in depression scores. We again used the classification system from Lovibond and Lovibond (1995b), this time with the following cutoffs: 0-9, 10-13, 14-20, 21-29, and 28 + as indicative of normal, mild, moderate, severe, and extremely severe depression, respectively (see the distribution in Table 1). The mean was significantly above zero (p < 0.001) and the 95% bias corrected confidence interval for the standard deviation (0.56; 0.67) did not include zero. Thus, we concluded that there was heterogeneity in depression levels. The mean was in line with normative scale scores, though slightly higher. For example, a mean score of 0.63 was obtained in a nonclinical sample in the US, and means up to 0.89 were obtained in other countries (Zanon et al., 2021).<sup>8</sup> The classification results suggest that only 58.79% of the sample was fully free of depressive symptoms, and 41.21% of the sample had at least mild levels of depression. They further show that 10.24% suffered from severe depression. These numbers are in line with national samples reflecting a prevalence of major depressive disorder of 9.9% in people with some college education (Hasin et al., 2018). Likewise, smaller student samples revealed a 33% rate of at least mild depression and 11% rate of severe depression (Beiter et al., 2015).

To address RQ2a-b, we estimated three models. First, we regressed satisfaction on depression level in SPSS. Second, we estimated Model 1 in PROCESS to obtain moderation effects across the sexes. In the third step, we used Model 3 (three-way interaction) with the PROCESS macro to estimate sex moderation of the moderation effect estimated in the prior step. All models included age and sex as controls; in Model 3, sex was a moderator and not a control variable. Interactions terms were based on centered continuous variables. There was no concerning collinearity among the predictors (VIF = 1.036). Given that there were relatively small correlations among the constructs, with some being nonsignificant and others differing in direction (See Appendix C), we concluded that the risk of common method bias (CMB) was small.

Results are given in Figure 3. They support the idea that satisfaction increases use (H2.1). Importantly, they show that depression levels can influence IS models that focus on reward seeking in at least two ways. First, depression reduced satisfaction, supporting H2.2. Second, it increased the effect of satisfaction on Instagram use, supporting H2.3b (and rejecting H2.3a). For each unit increase in the level of depression, the effect of satisfaction on use became stronger by 0.17. Importantly, at low levels of depression (-1SD from the mean), the effect of satisfaction on use was not significant. In this zone, use was guided by other considerations. The effect of satisfaction on use was significant only at average and high (+1SD) levels of depression. Lastly, the significant positive three-way interaction suggests that the moderation effect of depression levels is stronger for males than for females, Supporting H2.4. This implies that the satisfaction effect on use is conditional on one’s level of depression and biological sex. As can be seen in Panel B of Figure 3, at low levels of depression, satisfaction drives Instagram use in females only. In contrast, at high levels of depression, satisfaction drives Instagram use in males only.

## 4.4 Discussion: Study 2

Addressing RQ1, our results suggest that typical IS user samples are heterogeneous in depression levels and include a non-negligible proportion of people with at least some symptoms of depression and quite a few with severe depression or worse. Addressing RQ2a-b, the models show that accounting for neurodiversity in terms of depression matters in leisure IS use contexts. While in line with our theory, depression did not affect SNS use directly, it did so by reducing the satisfaction people get and increasing the satisfaction effects on use. The coping-driven moderation effect was pronounced in males but not females, which highlights sex-based differences in coping strategies, and more broadly in neurodiversity effects (Path 1 in Figure 1). Taken together, such findings highlight the intricate insights afforded by including the neurodiversity facet of depression in common models of IS use behavior.

There are several noteworthy conclusions. First, the satisfaction effect on use replicates and is in line with, for example, results in Bhattacherjee and Lin (2015) who report a path coefficient of 0.2 Our results (β = 0.15) are in line with their results. Second, the two-way and three-way moderation results show that by ignoring the neurodiversity of IS samples in depression, we obtained results that are accurate for “average” people but are not inclusive of all users. For instance, the common knowledge that satisfaction drives use was true only for people with average to high levels of depression symptoms, and this effect was also dependent on the user’s sex. For people with no depression symptoms, satisfaction does appear to be a driver of system use, presumably because such individuals do not need to cope with ongoing struggles. They may be motivated by other factors (e.g., social connectedness needs) (Karahanna et al., 2018) or automatic use of the system in a habitual manner (Limayem & Cheung, 2011) regardless of an individual’s level of system satisfaction. Thus, system developers may benefit from not blindly aiming to increase satisfaction because the effects of these efforts will vary according to users’ depression symptom profiles and the biological sex.

## 5 Study 3

The objectives of Study 3 were to integrate ADHD levels into an IS model that focuses on empathy and to demonstrate neurodiversity effects in experiments, beyond surveys. We chose the experiment in Yin et al. (2014), because empathy may be a key mechanism with which people judge and act upon online reviews (Min et al., 2015). At the same time, higher levels of ADHD have been associated with impaired empathic processing abilities (Groen et al., 2018). Thus, there was a theoretical basis for integrating ADHD levels into the Yin et al. (2014) model.

## 5.1 Theory: Study 3

The base model predicts that empathy at least partially mediates the elevated effect of anxiety-embedded reviews compared to anger-embedded reviews on review helpfulness. Given that the base model hypotheses are explained in detail in the original study (Yin et al., 2014), we outline and briefly justify them in Table 5, which also provides construct definitions.

Here, we focus on the theoretical integration of ADHD levels into this model. Adult ADHD is characterized not only by hyperactivity and attention deficits but also by psychosocial problems (Canu & Carlson, 2007), including issues around social cognition—that is, the ability to understand others’ intentions and emotions (Uekermann et al., 2010). Indeed, studies show that adults with ADHD symptoms exhibit reduced emotional affect processing abilities (Miller et al., 2011) and specifically reduced empathic abilities (Abdel‐Hamid et al., 2019; Groen et al., 2018). Consequently, ADHD manifests in a limited ability to perceive emotional information and translate it into empathy and empathic responses (Uekermann et al., 2010). In Figure 1, this ADHD effect is reflected in Path 1—specifically in the reduction of emotional reactions to stimuli that underlie outcome value estimations. This is in line with feelingsas-information theory, which suggest that emotions inform outcome evaluations (Schwarz, 2012); when people’s ability to integrate emotions into decisions is impaired, they make suboptimal decisions (Bechara et al., 1999). Applied to our context, it means that people with higher levels of ADHD will find it more difficult than others to feel empathic toward reviewers and consequently exhibit lower levels of empathy compared to others. Hence:

## H3.3: ADHD levels will be negatively associated with empathy.<sup>9</sup>

Because people with higher ADHD levels tend to be less effective than others at integrating emotional cues into feelings of empathy (Uekermann et al., 2010), it is reasonable to expect that the ADHD effect described in H3.3 happens in part through limiting people’s ability to integrate empathy-relevant cues from reviews into their cognitive-emotional processing (Path 1 in Figure 1). This is in line with ideas that people with higher levels of ADHD react biologically differently to emotional cues (Maier et al., 2014; Viering et al., 2021), which makes them less emotionally expressive (Lugo-Candelas et al., 2017). Here, it means that people with higher levels of ADHD will be less able than others to feel empathic in response to the same empathy-relevant cues that others receive. Hence, their translation of empathy-relevant cues into empathy is expected to be weaker. Thus:

H3.4: ADHD levels will moderate (weaken) the positive association between anxietyembedded reviews and empathy.

## 5.2 Participants and Procedures: Study 3

A different sample was collected in 2019, using similar procedures to those used in Studies 1 and 2. The inclusion criteria were >18 years old and online review use history. We recruited university students because they likely vary in ADHD levels (DuPaul et al., 2001; Mortier et al., 2015) and likely use online reviews (Koh et al., 2010). Of the 328 invitees, 280 (85.3%) met the inclusion criteria and completed the study. The average age in the sample was 22.52 (19-43, SD = 3.19); and 51.8% of the sample were females.

The study employed an online experiment followed by a survey. It first screened for online review experience (“Do you at least sometimes read online product reviews?”) No exclusions were made. Next, it captured user demographics (age, sex [female = 0, male = 1]) and ADHD levels using items from the Adult ADHD Self-Report Screening Scale for DSM-5 (ASRS-5) (Ustun et al., 2017). This scale is valid and is commonly used in clinical practice for initial screening (to “suggest” ADHD) before a full clinical screening commences (Anbarasan et al., 2020).

Next, we followed the same procedures as in Yin et al. (2014)<sup>10</sup> and presented the cover story that we were training an artificial intelligence program (See Appendix B). We asked participants to read five reviews. Our focus was on three negative reviews, but we inserted positive review fillers between them to avoid revealing our focus on negative reviews. The negative reviews were presented in a random order. In a between-subjects design, half of the participants (50.7%) were randomly assigned to an Anxiety condition (coded as 1), in which the reviews conveyed anxiety, and the other half (49.3%) were assigned to an Anger condition (coded as 0), in which the reviews conveyed anger. Following Yin et al. (2014), we conveyed the emotions at the beginning of the reviews, at the end of the reviews, and in both locations. After each review, we asked participants to rate their empathy toward the reviewer and the helpfulness of the review using the scales in Yin et al. (2014). The scales were found to be reliable (See Appendix B). The only exception was α(for 3 review averages) = 0.646. Because removing items did not improve it and to ensure completeness and comparability with Yin et al. (2014) reasons, we decided to retain all items. The scales had reasonable convergent and discriminant validities, except for ADHD (see Appendix C). The ADHD scale had a two-dimensional structure, with one factor capturing hyperactivity and another attention issues. This is consistent with findings about the factor structure of ADHD scales that tend to capture hyperactivity and inattention separately (Reuter et al., 2006). Nevertheless, the combination of these subfactors is a good predictor of ADHD symptoms (Adler et al., 2006). In our case, these two factors loaded on a single factor (see Appendix C). Since we focus on ADHD outcomes that do not specifically pertain to hyperactivity or attention deficit, in line with prior studies using this scale (Adler et al., 2006), we chose to use all items by averaging them to capture the ADHD levels in the sample.

## 5.3 Results: Study 3

We repeated the procedure used in Studies 1 and 2 to address RQ1 and examine neurodiversity in ADHD scores. A cutoff of 14 was used to separate unlikely ADHD from “suggestive of ADHD” cases (Anbarasan et al., 2020; Kessler et al., 2007). The sample (M = 1.53, SD = 0.61) included 241 unlikely ADHD cases (86.1%) and 39 cases with scores that were suggestive of ADHD (13.9%). The mean was significantly above zero (p < 0.001) and the 95% bias corrected confidence interval for the standard deviation (0.56; 0.65) did not include zero. Thus, we concluded that there was nontrivial variability in ADHD scores. The mean was in line with normative scale scores, though slightly higher; for instance, multiple measurements in Kessler et al. (2007) produced means between 1.22 and 1.48. The classification results are in line with prior studies that found 17.3% “suggestive of ADHD” prevalence in a US sample, which is a much higher prevalence than clinically diagnosed adult ADHD (around 4.4%) (National Institute of Mental Health, 2017b). The gap is reasonable, as the scale points only to probable ADHD, and many people, despite symptoms, have not been tested for adult ADHD (London & Landes, 2021).

To address RQ2a-b, we estimated Model 7 in the PROCESS macro. The model included age and sex as controls. Interactions of continuous variables were centered. There was no concerning collinearity among the predictors (VIF = 1.0006 to 1.021). Given that there were relatively small correlations among the constructs, many of which were nonsignificant and different in direction (e.g., r = 0.05 [nonsignificant] between ADHD level and helpfulness vs. -0.08 between ADHD level and empathy [nonsignificant]), we concluded that the risk for common method bias (CMB) was small.

## 5.4 Discussion: Study 3

Addressing RQ1, we show that there is a nontrivial variability in ADHD scores in typical IS research samples: about 13% of a US sample has “probable ADHD,” and there was significant variability in ADHD scores in the whole sample. Addressing RQ2ab, the models show that variability in ADHD matters. Without considering it, our results are in line with Yin et al. (2014). The empathy mediation is not significant when ignoring ADHD or when considering it at average ADHD levels. Nevertheless, when ADHD heterogeneity is considered, we show that the effect may be significant; it depends on ADHD levels. In line with the views of ADHD as a barrier to empathic processing, people with lower levels of ADHD had no such barriers, felt empathic toward the reviewer, and consequently felt that anxiety-embedded reviews were helpful (more so than anger-embedded reviews). In contrast, using the same view of ADHD and empathy, people with high levels of ADHD had barriers to feeling empathic toward the reviewer and, consequently, their helpfulness perceptions were not impacted by reviews that conveyed anxiety.

Overall, these insights have important implications. They indicate that ignoring heterogeneity in ADHD can lead to correct conclusions about empathic responses for users with average levels of ADHD only. These conclusions become incorrect when considering neurodiversity in ADHD in typical IS user samples. In a broader sense, they show that ADHD levels can affect models of IS user behavior that involve empathic reactions. We hence call for embedding ADHD levels into additional models in domains that involve empathy (e.g., cyberbullying). This could enrich our understanding of important IS phenomena and help us, as a discipline, to better cater to people at various points on the ADHD continuum.

## 6 General Discussion

Diversity refers to differences among people along any relevant dimension, equity refers to giving people of all types equal opportunities, and inclusion captures the extent to which people of all types feel welcomed and able to fully participate in activities (Burton-Jones & Sarker, 2021). The IS community has started tapping into such diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) issues, in domains such as sex inequality (Gupta et al., 2019), neurodiversity (Boyd et al., 2018; Jia et al., 2022; Loiacono & Ren, 2018), and racial (Payton et al.,

2021), regional, language, research topic, and seniority inequalities (Windeler et al., 2020). In this study, in line with the broader neurodiversity movement (Sonuga-Barke & Thapar, 2021), we extend the known neurodiversity effects to (1) behavioral models of IS users in leisure settings and (2) relatively unexplored neurodiversity facets, such as depression and anxiety. We also highlight key mechanisms through which neurodiversity can influence IS user behavior, including (1) direct effects on user cognitive-emotional processing (by making it challenging for some users to process cognitions and emotions) and (2) the modulation of the effect of cues, cognitions, and emotions, as well as downstream cognitive-emotional processing and behaviors (by driving over- or underweighting of specific reflections and emotions in decision-making). These manifest in Path 1 in Figure 1. Moreover, neurodiversity facets can influence learning from experience through the alteration of encoding mechanisms and emphases. This manifests in Path 2 in Figure 1. Taken together, the model in Figure 1 highlights the high-level ways through which neurodiversity facets can influence IS users’ information and emotion processing, value assignment to actions, and consequent behaviors.

By testing the ideas in this model as applied to specific neurodiversity facets in specific IS use contexts, we demonstrate the general need to consider neurodiversity in IS user behavior models. In essence, our findings show that as a discipline, we have traditionally catered to users in the middle of neurodiversity continuums, despite the large and growing heterogeneity in users’ neurodiversity facets. To the extent that we want to serve all sexes, races, nationalities, and ethnicities—for example, through developing systems and work practices that acknowledge and cater to their specific needs (e.g., Cyr et al., 2009; Krasnova et al., 2017)— we, as a discipline, should also strive to acknowledge and cater to our neurodiverse user base, including in nonwork settings.

The first step in doing so is to better understand neurodiverse users’ needs and reactions to the systems we develop, to develop a theoretical framework that allows integrating neurodiversity into IS user behavior models (see Figure 1), and to acknowledge that not all users fit the same “mentally average” profile. This need is acute because, as we show in three studies, there is great heterogeneity in typical IS user samples in neurodiversity facets. Study 1 showed that 50% of the sample presented at least some level of elevated anxiety, Study 2 showed that over 40% of the sample presented some level of depression, and Study 3 showed that about 14% of the sample met basic criteria for adult ADHD. The studies further indicate that neurodiversity, at least in three common facets— namely anxiety, depression, and adult ADHD—can influence (though not always fully nullify) common associations in models describing IS user behavior. That is, common behavioral IS knowledge is not absolute; it may be contingent on users’ levels of neurodiversity. By ignoring neurodiversity as a theoretical explanatory source, the field caters to an “average” user and does not afford equal access and benefits to those users who are at the extreme ends of neurodiversity continuums.

Specifically, Studies 1 and 2 demonstrate that overlooking neurodiversity can lead to biased estimates for certain groups. In Study 1, we show that the translation of privacy concerns into risk and trusting beliefs is contingent on one’s anxiety levels. In Study 2 we show that depression levels reduce satisfaction with Instagram but also increase the translation of satisfaction into use. We further show that the narrow cognitive focus on escapist technology use is dominant in males with high depression scores. Overall, Study 1 and 2’s findings indicate that the conclusions we reach as a discipline are often correct but only when dealing with people in the middle of neurodiversity continuums. In contrast, these conclusions may be imprecise when applied to the lower or upper extremes of neurodiversity continuums. They also unravel the important role of neurodiversity in behavioral IS theories, especially as applied to nonwork contexts. They imply that practices such as reducing privacy concerns and increasing satisfaction through system design may not be globally efficacious. Thus, our discipline’s tendency to overlook neurodiversity limits the equity and inclusion of neurodiverse populations. The findings of Studies 1 and 2 ultimately show that more nuanced models can be obtained when considering the mechanism through which neurodiversity can influence the cognitiveemotional processing and decision-making of IS users.

Study 3 goes a step further and demonstrates that omitting neurodiversity considerations can lead to imprecise conclusions about neurodiverse users. Specifically, it replicates Yin et al. (2014) and shows that without considering ADHD levels, anxiety emotions in reviews do not drive empathy. However, when ADHD levels are considered, we show, in line with views of ADHD as a barrier to empathic responses, that people high in ADHD symptoms fail to empathize with anxious reviewers, but people low in ADHD symptoms effectively empathize with anxious reviewers.

Ultimately, the findings imply that at least some aspects of neurodiversity can provide a possible explanation for inconsistent past findings (e.g., the association between IPC and trust, see Dinev et al., 2015); for boundary conditions for common associations, such as between satisfaction and use (Bhattacherjee & Lin, 2015); and for the nonsignificance of associations in some cases (Yin et al., 2014). Moreover, our study extends the battery of individual differences that can explain user behavior in nonwork settings from the neurotypical to the neurodiversity space. This is (1) a needed extension to enable fairer treatment of more diverse groups of IS users and (2) a timely extension, as we show here, because of the extent of overlooked neurodiversity in typical IS user samples. Importantly, the overarching model proposed in Figure 1 can serve as a basis for the future integration of neurodiversity facets into models of IS user behavior. We hope that future studies will extend the range of examined neurodiversity facets, which would allow system developers to improve systems design decisions, IS scholars to further improve the understanding of the mechanisms that underlie neurodiversity effects, and the IS research community to better understand and cater to people at different points on neurodiversity continuums based on systematic research rather than common stereotypes.

In sum, in this study we (1) demonstrate that IS users in nonwork settings are neurodiverse, (2) demonstrate that this neurodiversity can have direct and moderation effects on common IS user behavior models, and (3) pave the way for further integration of neurodiversity into relevant IS models and contexts. We thus call for more research on this topic and for improved inclusivity of neurodiverse people in behavioral IS research models. Note that our findings do not imply that IS scholars should always blindly account for neurodiversity in IS models. Instead, we call for more careful consideration of neurodiversity and its theoretical integration in behavioral IS models when the model involves cognitive, affective, and/or social cue processing aspects that may be impaired by specific neurodiversity facets.

Several limitations of the study are noteworthy and relevant for future research agendas. First, we only focused on three neurodiversity facets and their effects on specific IS phenomena; we also measured them using one approach. Future research could expand the examined facets and their measures, as well as the phenomena they affect. The overarching model presented in Figure 1 could be used as a departure point for thinking about the processes that might be influenced by neurodiversity facets in contexts beyond those studied here. Second, people may be high in multiple neurodiversity facets, as these can be comorbid (Katzman et al., 2017). Thus, the simultaneous effects of such facets should be considered and examined in future research. This also means that the effects we observed here should be interpreted with caution, as we did not control for possible effects of comorbid neurodiversity facets. Third, we call for system design studies that can translate insights on neurodiverse users, like those ones obtained here, into impactful action. It would be interesting to consider which design elements are helpful for catering to people at different points on the continuums—as well as how and why. Fourth, while the use of student samples here was appropriate given the types of systems and behaviors we examined (Compeau et al., 2012), we call for replication with different sampling frames and systems. Fifth, in all studies, we used sex as a proxy for gender and, hence, treated gender as binary. Future research should consider accounting for a broader set of genders (Turel, 2022) and for implicit gender identities (Serenko & Turel, 2021). Lastly, it would be interesting to consider the benefits that people higher on the continuums bring to IS use contexts, not just their limitations. While such benefits have been considered for the workplace (Loiacono & Ren, 2018), future research could examine whether certain forms of neurodiversity may be beneficial for IS use in leisure settings, and, if so, how, why, and under what conditions.

## 7 Conclusion

Overall, the findings demonstrate the need for and utility of considering the neurodiversity of IS users in nonwork settings as a source of information that can help the field better address its DEI objectives and allow developers to better cater to their neurodiverse user base. As such, we invite IS scholars to consider, when relevant, a more systematic inclusion of neurodiversity measures and theories in studies that focus on user responses to IS. We also invite system developers to acknowledge the fact that not all users are mentally alike or “typical.” This understanding can lead to more equitable and inclusive system development practices.

Table C3 demonstrates reasonable convergent and discriminant validity in Study 1 (Except for ADHD levels), with (1) composite reliability (CR), factor loadings, and square root of average variance extracted (AVE) scores of at least 0.7, and (2) the square root of AVE exceeding interconstruct correlations for each examined construct. ADHD levels presented reasonable loadings, and a reasonable square root of AVE. However, both were slightly below 0.7. To examine the reasons for this, we ran a principal component analysis and found a two-factor solution. The first manifested in items 1-4 (loadings of 0.60-0.76), which relate to hyperactivity. The second manifested in items 5-6 (loadings of 0.80-0.85), which relate to attention issues. This is understandable given the multifaceted nature of ADHD. The two dimensions loaded on a single factor (loadings = 0.81). Because both facets are needed for capturing ADHD, we retained both. Hence, the overall mean of the scale can reasonably represent the two facets of the concept, and ultimately ADHD levels.
